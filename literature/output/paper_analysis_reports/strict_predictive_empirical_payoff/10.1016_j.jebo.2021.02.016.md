Authors: Mantilla, C., Zhou, L., Wang, C., Yang, D. H., Shen, S. P., & Seabright, P.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables in the public goods game are group total earnings (efficiency), individual earnings, and contributions. Punishment behavior and trust game outcomes are also analyzed, but efficiency and group earnings are explicitly reported and analyzed (see Table 7).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that in public goods games with punishment, ethnically mixed groups experience lower contributions and lower group earnings than homogeneous groups. This is primarily because participants are less likely to punish co-ethnic free-riders when out-group members are present, reducing the deterrent effect of punishment. The effect is strongest in unbalanced mixed groups (3+1 composition), where efficiency losses are largest. The study provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing institution is undermined by in-group favoritism in diverse groups, leading to lower efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or even reversed in ethnically mixed groups due to in-group favoritism in punishment assignment. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the group composition (homogeneous vs mixed) is a critical moderator: punishment is less effective (or even harmful) in mixed groups because in-group members are less likely to punish each other, reducing deterrence and lowering contributions and earnings. The game uses standard PGG parameters (4 players, 5 rounds, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=2, punishment impact=5, no chat, no rewards, anonymous punishment). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between group types with punishment enabled. The findings suggest that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment in mixed-ethnicity groups may not increase efficiency and can even decrease it relative to homogeneous groups.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods group has 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public goods game is repeated for 5 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: each player can allocate any amount (0-10 points) to the public good.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each point contributed yields 2 points to the group, divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment (disapproval card) costs 2 points to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment reduces the punished player's earnings by 5 points (punishment impact per unit cost = 2.5).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 5 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, players are informed about the individual contributions of other group members and their ethnicity.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: punished players are told how many disapproval cards they received, but not who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report a control condition with punishment fully disabled, so the direct effect of enabling punishment versus no punishment on efficiency is not measured; all groups have punishment enabled.
- The main moderator studied is group ethnic composition (homogeneous vs mixed); other game design dimensions are not experimentally varied.
- The sample is rural, non-student, and from a specific region in China, which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- The number of rounds is limited (5), so long-term dynamics of punishment and cooperation are not observed.
- The experiment does not include a reward mechanism, so findings are specific to punishment-based enforcement.
- Some session-level confounds may exist due to field setting and matching protocol, though balance checks are reported.
