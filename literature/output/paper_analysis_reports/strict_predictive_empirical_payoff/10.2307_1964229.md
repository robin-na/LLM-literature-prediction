# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: COVENANTS WITH AND WITHOUT A SWORD - SELF-GOVERNANCE IS POSSIBLE
Authors: OSTROM, E., WALKER, J., & GARDNER, R.
Journal: American Political Science Review
Year: 1992

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average net yield as a percentage of maximum possible net yield
- group earnings
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- defection rate
- frequency and targeting of sanctions
- frequency of communication
- agreement formation
- punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is net yield (efficiency) as a percentage of the maximum possible. Defection rate and sanctioning frequency are also reported, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a CPR game with 8 players and a quadratic production function, baseline efficiency (net yield) is low, especially in high-endowment settings. Allowing communication (covenants) alone increases efficiency substantially, with repeated communication leading to near-optimal outcomes. Introducing a sanctioning mechanism (the sword) alone increases net yield before costs, but after accounting for the costs of punishment, efficiency gains are minimal or negative. When both communication and sanctioning are allowed, especially when the group can choose the sanctioning mechanism, efficiency is maximized and defection rates are minimized. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus highly context-dependent: punishment alone is not efficient, but when combined with communication and endogenous institution-building, it is highly effective.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments depends critically on the presence of communication and the way the punishment institution is implemented. If the game design allows for communication and endogenous choice of punishment, enabling punishment can dramatically increase efficiency. If punishment is imposed without communication, efficiency gains are small or negative after accounting for punishment costs. The control efficiency (without punishment) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect of punishment is positive only when paired with communication and institutional choice. Game design dimensions such as player count (8), number of rounds (20+), absence of chat in baseline, continuous contribution, and the specific punishment cost/tech parameters are all relevant for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 8 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments typically have 20 rounds, sometimes more (up to 25+ in some treatments).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (face-to-face discussion) is allowed in some treatments and not in others. Communication alone increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (integer-valued tokens between 0 and endowment).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is determined by the quadratic production function; at Nash equilibrium, group yield is 39% of maximum.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment (fining) costs to punisher ranged from 5¢ to 40¢; fee-to-fine ratios of 0.25 or 0.5. Lower cost led to more frequent punishment, but overuse reduced efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per cost) varied; higher fines led to more punishment, but not necessarily higher efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects did not know the exact number of rounds, but knew the game was finite and bounded.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects saw their own profits, total group investment in the CPR, and cumulative profits. In some treatments, individual investment data by subject number was also shown.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: punished subjects did not know who punished them, only that they were punished.

## Important Limitations
- The CPR game is not a standard linear public goods game, but a quadratic common-pool resource game with subtractive returns and continuous investment.
- Punishment is always costly and implemented as a fine with a fee-to-fine ratio; no positive reward mechanism is studied.
- All experiments use groups of 8 players; generalizability to other group sizes is not tested.
- Communication is face-to-face and may not generalize to other forms of chat or messaging.
- Subjects are experienced with the CPR environment, which may affect behavior.
- The number of rounds is finite but not known exactly to subjects, which may affect endgame behavior.
- The study does not examine external enforcement or third-party punishment.
- The effect of punishment is highly dependent on the presence of communication and endogenous institution-building; results may not generalize to settings where these are not possible.
