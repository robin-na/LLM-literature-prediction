Journal: Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft
Year: 2016

empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group profit
- average group earnings
- efficiency (implied by profit relative to maximum possible)
- average individual profit
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment frequency
- reward frequency
- institutional choice/voting
- sanctioning behavior
- gender effects on voting and behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average group profit (payoff), average contributions, and institutional choice. Efficiency is directly discussed as average profit relative to the maximum possible. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution rates and sanctioning behavior are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that, although punishment increases contributions more than reward or the standard VCM, it reduces group efficiency because the costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. The reward institution, despite a lower maximum leverage, is both the most preferred and the most efficient, yielding the highest average profits. The standard VCM yields intermediate efficiency. The effectiveness (leverage) of both punishment and reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency. There is no significant difference in efficiency or institutional choice between partner and stranger matching. Gender differences exist in preferences for institutions, but not in efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency (average group profit) compared to both the control (standard VCM) and the reward institution. The efficiency loss is due to the cost of punishment actions. The reward institution, even with lower maximum leverage, is more efficient than both punishment and the standard VCM. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency, unless the punishment technology is extremely effective and/or punishment is used sparingly. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across partner and stranger matching, and is not moderated by self-selection or communication (which is absent). The paper also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment or reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds in the main (endogenous-choice) part, after 10 rounds of standard VCM.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed at any stage.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0 ≤ c_i,t ≤ E (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (γ) = 0.4; endowment E = 20; group size n = 4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment: 1 token cost to punisher per point; impact on target is |L| tokens (L < 0).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (leverage L) is chosen by group each round from -5 to -1 (punishment). Higher |L| increases contributions, but not efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward institution is available as a voting option in every round.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward: 1 token cost to rewarder per point; impact on target is L tokens (L > 0).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness (leverage L) is chosen by group each round from 1 to 2 (reward). Higher L increases both contributions and efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds in advance (10 rounds per part).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see all group members' contributions and the number of punishment/reward points assigned and received, as well as period payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is anonymous: subjects cannot identify who punished or rewarded them; group member IDs change every period.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment and reward mechanisms are binary (one point per target per round), not continuous or multi-level as in some other studies.
- Maximum leverage for reward (L=2) is much lower than for punishment (L=-5), potentially biasing against reward, yet reward is still more efficient.
- No communication is allowed, which may limit generalizability to settings with communication.
- The experiment uses student subjects in a lab setting, which may not generalize to field or real-world environments.
- Efficiency is measured as average profit, but the paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum directly.
- Punishment and reward are informal and anonymous; results may differ with formal or identifiable sanctions.
