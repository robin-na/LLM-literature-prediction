## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Peer punishment increases cooperation and group earnings (efficiency) in public good structures only when punishment is costless; costly punishment does not improve efficiency. In generalized exchange, peer punishment (both costly and costless) does not increase cooperation or group earnings. The effectiveness of punishment is thus highly contingent on the network structure: dense sanctioning networks (public good) allow punishment to deter defection and improve efficiency, while sparse networks (generalized exchange) do not. The cost-impact ratio of punishment is important, but its effect is moderated by the structural environment. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from peer punishment in public goods do not transfer to generalized exchange settings.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the network structure. In standard public good games, costless punishment can substantially increase efficiency, but costly punishment may not. In generalized exchange (circular, sparse punishment network), enabling punishment—even if costless—does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, it is crucial to account for the density of the punishment network and whether the game is a standard public good or a generalized exchange. Control efficiency is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency when moving from public good to generalized exchange structures.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 16 periods per session; participants were not told the exact number to avoid endgame effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; interactions were computerized and anonymized.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (send 10 MU) or defect (keep 3 MU).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In public good: each 10 MU contribution is split among 3 others, so MPCR = 10/3 ≈ 0.33 per recipient; in generalized exchange, each cooperation gives 10 MU to one recipient (MPCR = 1 for that recipient, but only one recipient).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Two punishment regimes: costly (cost = 1 MU per 2 MU penalty) and costless (cost = 0 MU per 2 MU penalty). Costless punishment increases efficiency in public good, but not in generalized exchange.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is manipulated: (2;1) and (2;0) regimes. Higher impact (costless) increases efficiency in public good, but not in generalized exchange.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not informed about the exact number of rounds to avoid endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In public good, participants observed the (randomly reshuffled) identifiers and decisions of all group members. In generalized exchange, they only learned about the decision of their sender.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Identifiers were randomized each period; in public good, players could see who punished them (by identifier), but these changed each round. In generalized exchange, only direct sender/recipient information was available.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses only 4-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- The generalized exchange structure is implemented as a circular network with random reassignment each round, which may not capture all real-world forms of generalized exchange.
- Punishment regimes are limited to (2;1) and (2;0); more severe or different forms of punishment may yield different results.
- No reward mechanisms are tested, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
- Participants are undergraduates in a laboratory setting, which may not reflect behavior in field or real-world contexts.
- The experiment does not inform participants of the number of rounds, which may affect strategies compared to games with known endpoints.
