Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics

empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

- average contributions
- total payoffs in points
Non-payoff outcomes:

- sanctioning strategy
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group-level payoffs (social welfare), average contributions, and the frequency and targeting of punishment. The paper also analyzes contributors' and sanctioners' behavioral responses, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a 4-player, 10-round public goods game with centralized punishment, the presence of a sanctioner (punishment enabled) increases both average contributions and total group payoffs (social welfare) compared to a control with no punishment. However, if the sanctioner's payoff is made contingent on group cooperation (pool punishment), both contributions and efficiency are lower than when the sanctioner receives a fixed endowment. The negative effect of the pool punishment scheme is not due to differences in punishment frequency or severity, but rather to contributors' aversion to the sanctioner's extrinsic incentives (perceived as indirect free-riding). The paper provides direct evidence that punishment increases efficiency, but the institutional details of the punishment mechanism (especially the sanctioner's payoff scheme) are crucial moderators.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the institutional design—specifically, whether the sanctioner is paid a fixed or variable (pool) payoff. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger when the punishment institution uses fixed payoffs for sanctioners. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the sanctioner's payoff scheme, and the marginal per-capita return (MPCR). Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the treatment effect of punishment is moderated by the sanctioner's incentive structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4 (3 contributors, 1 monitor/sanctioner).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 rounds (plus 10 trial rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; communication was explicitly forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions are continuous: each contributor chooses how many points (0-10) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 2/3 (0.67) for all players, including the monitor in pool treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 point to the monitor per sanctioned contributor.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment costs 1 point to the monitor and reduces the punished contributor's payoff by 3 points (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told the game consists of 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all players observe the contributions of each group member (arranged in decreasing order, without identification).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented by the monitor, but contributors do not know which contributor(s) were punished (no individual identification); only the monitor knows their own actions.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a specific centralized punishment design (one monitor per group, no peer punishment), which may not generalize to all PGG environments.
- The negative effect of pool punishment (variable payoff to the monitor) may depend on contributors' perceptions of fairness or indirect free-riding, which could vary in other contexts.
- The study does not vary MPCR, punishment cost, or punishment effectiveness across treatments, so the generalizability to other parameterizations is limited.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- The study does not include treatments with both punishment and reward, so the interaction between these mechanisms is not addressed.
