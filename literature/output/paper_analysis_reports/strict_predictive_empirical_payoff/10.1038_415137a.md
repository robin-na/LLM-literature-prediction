# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Altruistic punishment in humans
Authors: Fehr, E., & Gächter, S.
Journal: Nature
Year: 2002

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual income
- group monetary pay-off
- average investment (as it determines pay-off structure)
- earnings per session
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/investment levels
- frequency and pattern of punishment
- emotional responses (anger, annoyance)
- expectations of others' emotions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution/investment, but the paper also analyzes how punishment and deviation from group average affect individual income. Group efficiency (total payoff relative to maximum) is not directly reported, but can be inferred from average investment and the payoff structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with random group assignment and no reputation or repeated interaction, the opportunity for costly punishment (1 MU cost to punisher, 3 MU cost to target per point) leads to a large and sustained increase in average contributions compared to a no-punishment baseline. Most punishment is directed at below-average contributors by above-average contributors. The threat and execution of punishment both increase cooperation. Individual income is maximized when contributions are close to the group average, and both positive and negative deviations reduce income. The emotional mechanism underlying punishment is negative affect toward free riders. The paper does not report group efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the data and payoff structure imply that efficiency is much higher with punishment enabled.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds, no communication, continuous contribution choice, and no reputation or repeated interaction. The control (no-punishment) condition shows declining contributions and low efficiency, while the punishment condition shows increasing contributions and much higher efficiency. The punishment mechanism is costly (1:3 cost:impact), and punishment is anonymous. The findings support the prediction that, in similar designs, enabling punishment will substantially increase group efficiency relative to the control. The paper does not manipulate other design dimensions, so its predictive value is strongest for games matching its structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players per round; fixed throughout.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment lasts 6 rounds; subjects play two 6-round games (one with, one without punishment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; all decisions are anonymous and made via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects can contribute any amount between 0 and 20 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each MU contributed returns 0.4 MU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 MU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 3 MUs (1:3 cost:impact ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told at the start that the experiment will last 6 periods; after 6, they are told there will be another 6-period game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed about the investments of the other group members and their own monetary payoff.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; members are not informed of the identity of punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report group efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum; efficiency must be inferred from average investment and payoff structure.
- All design dimensions except punishment are held constant; no variation in player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, or other features.
- The experiment uses student subjects from a single country, which may limit generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not speak to the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The time horizon is short (6 rounds per treatment), and the effect of longer or indefinite games is not tested.
- The emotional mechanism is inferred from post-experiment scenarios, not from direct measurement during the game.
