# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups
Authors: Chen, J.
Journal: Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- average per-period earnings
- final earnings (adjusted for welfare construction/destruction)
- social welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- individual contributions
- punishment/reward assigned and received
- contribution decay over periods
- next-period contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (efficiency), with extensive analysis of contributions and incentivizing behavior. Efficiency is explicitly measured and compared across conditions, including adjustments for the welfare-creating/destroying effects of reward and punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 20 periods (10 control, 10 treatment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants is not allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-20 tokens per period).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Heterogeneous: one member MPCR=0.9, two members MPCR=0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs 1 token to assigner, reduces target's earnings by 3 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point costs 1, reduces target by 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available in a separate treatment, not in the punishment condition.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward point costs 1 token to assigner, increases target's earnings by 3 tokens.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (each point costs 1, increases target by 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of periods (10 per block, 20 total).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, participants see each group member's contributions and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the total amount of points assigned by the two other group members combined, but not who assigned how much.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a specific form of heterogeneity (one high-benefit, two low-benefit members with fixed MPCRs); results may not generalize to other forms of heterogeneity.
- Punishment and reward are implemented in separate treatments; there is no condition with both available simultaneously.
- The sample size is moderate (87 in punishment, 81 in reward), and the analysis mixes within- and between-subject comparisons.
- Endowments are earned, not windfall, which may affect the relative effectiveness of punishment and reward.
- The punishment and reward mechanisms use a 1:3 cost-effectiveness ratio, which may not match all real-world settings.
- No communication is allowed; effects may differ with chat or communication.
