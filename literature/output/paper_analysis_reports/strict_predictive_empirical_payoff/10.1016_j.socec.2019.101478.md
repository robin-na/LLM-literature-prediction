Authors: De Geest, L. R., & Kingsley, D. C.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2019

## Study Type

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- individual earnings
- welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned and received
- institutional choice (voting for peer punishment vs central authority)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group payoff (average earnings), with detailed analysis of how this varies by treatment and institution. Contribution and punishment behaviors are also analyzed, but as mechanisms for payoff differences.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a linear public goods game with three treatments (equal endowments, heterogeneous observed endowments, and heterogeneous unobserved endowments), the introduction of peer punishment increases group payoffs only when endowments are either equal or observed. When endowments are unobserved, peer punishment leads to lower group payoffs than the no-punishment baseline, due to mis-targeted punishment (cooperators being punished, freeriders escaping punishment). Central authority enforcement, which perfectly enforces the social optimum but at a fixed cost, yields similar payoffs across all treatments and is preferred by groups with incomplete information, especially by low-endowment members. The study demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the ability to observe and target deviations from contribution norms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games depends on whether group members can observe the relevant information (here, endowments) needed to target freeriders. When such information is available, punishment increases efficiency; when it is not, punishment can reduce efficiency below the baseline. The control efficiency (no punishment) is similar across treatments, but the treatment efficiency (with punishment) diverges depending on information structure. For prediction, the presence of incomplete information about contribution capacity (endowments) is a key moderator: with incomplete information, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency; with complete information, it is likely to increase efficiency. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant in this study, so the main moderator identified is information about endowments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 18 periods per session, divided into 6 phases of 3 periods each.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is mentioned or allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (subjects choose how much to contribute from their endowment).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher per reduction point is 1 ED; maximum 10 points per period.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reduction point costs 1 ED to assign and reduces the target's payoff by 4 EDs (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of periods and phases in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see aggregate group contribution, individual contributions by random ID, individual period earnings, total earnings, and history of outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is assigned by random ID; subjects know which group member punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- Short phases (3 periods per institution) limit analysis to short-run dynamics; long-run effects and norm emergence are not studied.
- Central authority is modeled as a perfect enforcer with full information and no possibility of corruption or error, which may not reflect real-world institutions.
- No reward mechanism is included, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- All other game parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are held constant, so findings about those dimensions are limited.
- No communication or chat is allowed, so results may not generalize to settings with communication.
