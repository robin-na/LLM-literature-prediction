# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation
Authors: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N.
Journal: Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (total earnings minus fixed endowments, including authorities)
- group profit
- punishment costs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned/distributed
- punishment received
- reaction to punishment (change in contribution)
- perverse/anti-social punishment
Outcome notes:
The primary outcomes are efficiency, contributions, and punishment. Efficiency is explicitly defined and analyzed. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution and punishment behavior are also analyzed in detail.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 5 participants: 4 contributors and 1 authority.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session, fixed and known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between participants is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous in increments of 2 from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 unit and reduces the target's income by 3 units. In CEN, each punishment point also reduces the payoff of each other participant by 1/3.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point reduces target's income by 3 units per 1 unit cost to punisher).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of rounds (30) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed about their income from the public good, the amount of punishment received, and their final total payoff. Signals about others' contributions are provided, with varying accuracy (noise).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are not identifiable: C-participants are identified by numbers 1-4, which are randomly reassigned each period, making identification difficult. In DEC, recipients cannot identify individual punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The authority in the centralized punishment regime cannot contribute to the public good, which may limit generalizability to settings where all players can both contribute and punish.
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (5) and a specific MPCR (0.4), so results may not generalize to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- No communication is allowed, which may not reflect real-world environments where discussion is possible.
- The study does not include a pure control (no punishment) condition in the main analysis, so direct comparison to a no-punishment baseline is not possible within this paper.
- The findings are specific to the laboratory setting and student sample, which may limit external validity.
