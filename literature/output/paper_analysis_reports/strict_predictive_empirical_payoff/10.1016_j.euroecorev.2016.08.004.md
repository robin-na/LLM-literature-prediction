# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Judicial error and cooperation
Authors: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- efficiency (earnings relative to full cooperation)
- total payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- willingness to pay to avoid type I vs type II error
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings (efficiency) and contributions. Willingness to pay to avoid type I error is also elicited, but is not a direct group payoff outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that error-free formal sanctions increase both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public goods game. When judicial errors are introduced (either type I or type II), both cooperation and efficiency decline. The effect on efficiency is more negative for type I errors than for type II errors, due to the wasteful punishment of cooperators. The negative effect of errors is stronger when the error probability is higher and when errors convert a deterrent sanction regime into a non-deterrent one. The effects of type I and type II errors on contributions are symmetric, but type I errors reduce group earnings more. Subjects show a clear preference for avoiding type I errors, but this preference is fully explained by income maximization rather than additional psychological aversion.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the introduction of punishment with judicial error reduces efficiency in public goods games, and that the magnitude of the efficiency loss depends on the type and probability of error, as well as the deterrence strength of the sanction regime. For prediction, the findings imply that enabling punishment increases efficiency only if sanctions are deterrent and error-free; otherwise, efficiency may decrease, especially with type I errors or high error probability. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the probability and type of judicial error, and whether sanctions are deterrent. Control efficiency (without punishment) is also reported, allowing for direct comparison.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 5 players (n=5) throughout all phases.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Phase 1: 5 rounds; Phases 2-4: one-shot (single round each).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how much (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.3 (m=0.3, n=5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fine imposed by the experimenter; cost is the fine paid by the punished, not by a punisher (centralized punishment). No explicit cost to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fine of s=0.6 or s=0.8 per unit kept; effectiveness is direct (each unit kept is fined at the specified rate).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in advance for each phase.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each phase, subjects are informed about the sum of contributions to the public good in their group and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized and administered by the experimenter; no identification of individual punishers (since there are none). Players are informed about their own exposure to error and fines, but not about others'.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot design for the main error treatments (phases 2-4), so long-term or repeated-game effects are not observed.
- Punishment is centralized and administered by the experimenter, not by peers, which may limit generalizability to decentralized punishment settings.
- The error regime (type I or type II) is exogenously assigned in most treatments; only in phase 4 do subjects choose between error types.
- The subject pool consists of freshman economics students from a single university, which may limit external validity.
- The experiment does not include a reward mechanism, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation effects.
- The study does not explore the effects of repeated exposure to errors over multiple rounds.
