The main dependent variables are group profit (payoff) and contribution. Profit is directly reported and analyzed as the main efficiency-related outcome. Contribution and number of sanctions are also reported but are not the primary focus for efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study experimentally compares two centralized punishment institutions in a linear public goods game: absolute punishment (all violators punished) and relative punishment (only the worst violators punished). With deterrent sanctions (where the penalty is strong enough), both institutions achieve similar, high efficiency (group profit). With non-deterrent sanctions (penalty not strong enough to make compliance a dominant strategy), the relative punishment institution achieves significantly higher efficiency and contributions than the absolute institution. The theoretically optimal threshold for punishment does not always yield the highest observed profit, suggesting behavioral factors affect outcomes. Overall, relative punishment is at least as effective, and often more so, than absolute punishment for sustaining group efficiency, especially when sanctions are weak.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution matters: relative punishment (punishing only the worst violators) can yield higher efficiency than absolute punishment (punishing all violators) when sanctions are not strong enough to be fully deterrent. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and the punishment is non-deterrent, enabling relative punishment is likely to increase efficiency more than enabling absolute punishment. When sanctions are deterrent, both institutions perform similarly well. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 15 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, and fixed punishment parameters as in the experiment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experimental groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group played 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous from 0 to 24 (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (beta) was 0.35 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was a fixed fine of 12 points per sanction; cost to punisher is not applicable as punishment is centralized (not peer).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact is 12 points per sanction; effectiveness is fixed and does not vary by degree of violation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the game would last 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw each member's contribution, group total, each member's earnings, and who was punished.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw who received punishment, but not who assigned it (since punishment was centralized).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4), fixed MPCR (0.35), and fixed punishment (12 points); results may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- Only centralized punishment (not peer punishment) is studied; findings may not apply to decentralized punishment settings.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not address interactions between punishment and reward.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory with Japanese university students, which may limit external validity.
- The theoretically optimal threshold for punishment did not always yield the highest observed profit, indicating that behavioral factors (e.g., envy, learning, error) can affect efficiency in ways not captured by the model.
- No communication or reputation effects are present; real-world settings may differ.
