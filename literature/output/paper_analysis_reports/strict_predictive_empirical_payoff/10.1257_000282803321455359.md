# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Monetary and nonmonetary punishment in the voluntary contributions mechanism
Authors: Masclet, D., Noussair, C., Tucker, S., & Villeval, M. C.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- group earnings
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned
- change in contribution after punishment
- sanctioning behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average earnings (payoff) and contribution rates. The paper also analyzes how punishment (monetary and nonmonetary) affects subsequent contributions and the determinants of sanctioning behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing either monetary or nonmonetary punishment in a standard voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no punishment. Nonmonetary (costless, expressive) punishment increases contributions and earnings immediately, while monetary punishment initially reduces earnings due to punishment costs but eventually leads to higher contributions and similar earnings as nonmonetary punishment. The effect of nonmonetary punishment is stronger when groups are stable (Partner matching) than when they are rematched (Stranger matching). Both types of punishment are most effective at increasing contributions among those who contributed less than the group average. The study provides direct evidence that both formal (monetary) and informal (nonmonetary) sanctions can increase efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (either monetary or nonmonetary) in a standard PGG/VCM increases group efficiency (average earnings) compared to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across two forms of punishment, but monetary punishment is more effective at sustaining high contributions over time, though it incurs costs that initially reduce efficiency. The design dimensions—such as group size (4), number of rounds (10 per segment), no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR (0.4), and anonymous punishment—are all specified, making the findings directly applicable to prediction tasks involving similar game structures. The results also highlight that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost structure and the matching protocol (Partner vs Stranger), with stable groups supporting more effective informal sanctions. For prediction, the presence of punishment (especially monetary) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude and time profile may depend on punishment cost and group stability.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players in every session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 30 periods, divided into three 10-period segments. Punishment was enabled in periods 11-20.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (subjects chose any amount from 0 to 20 ECUs using a scroll bar).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each ECU contributed to the group account yielded 0.4 ECU to each of the four group members (MPCR = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the monetary punishment (MP) treatment, assigning punishment points had a nonlinear cost schedule (e.g., 1 point = 1 ECU, 2 points = 2 ECU, up to 10 points = 30 ECU). In the nonmonetary punishment (NP) treatment, assigning points was costless.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In MP, each punishment point reduced the punished subject's first-stage earnings by 10% (up to 100%). In NP, points had no pecuniary effect.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the length of each 10-period segment but did not know whether the experiment would continue beyond the current segment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects saw each group member's contribution, the total group contribution, and their own earnings and points received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: subjects saw only the total number of points received, not who assigned them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only groups of size 4, so results may not generalize to larger or smaller groups.
- The punishment cost and effectiveness schedule is specific and nonlinear; results may differ with other punishment structures.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so findings do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The time horizon is segmented and partially uncertain (subjects know only the current 10-period segment), which may affect behavior.
- All sessions were conducted in laboratory settings with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- Punishment was anonymous; effects may differ if punishment is identifiable.
