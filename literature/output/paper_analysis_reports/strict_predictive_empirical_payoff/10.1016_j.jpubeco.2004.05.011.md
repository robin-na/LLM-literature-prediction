# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation under the threat of expulsion in a public goods experiment
Authors: Cinyabuguma, M., Page, T., & Putterman, L.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2005

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- efficiency (linear in net earnings)
- group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- expulsion votes
- number of expulsions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average earnings (efficiency) and contribution rates. Expulsion votes and number of expulsions are also analyzed. Efficiency is directly measured as average earnings relative to the maximum possible.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compared a standard public goods game (VCM) with a treatment in which group members could vote to expel others. In the expulsion treatment, contributions rose to nearly 100% of endowments, and average earnings (efficiency) were significantly higher than in the baseline. Expulsion was used sparingly, but the threat alone was enough to sustain cooperation. Expelled members earned much less, but overall group efficiency was still higher with expulsion. When the expulsion threat was removed, cooperation collapsed. The results suggest that the threat of exclusion is a powerful mechanism for sustaining cooperation and improving efficiency in public goods settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a credible expulsion (ostracism) mechanism in a public goods game with known group size, fixed rounds, and full information about others' contributions can substantially increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust even when the punishment (expulsion) is rarely used, as the threat alone is sufficient. The design dimensions—large group size (16), 15 known rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward option, and full feedback—are all specified, making the findings directly applicable to prediction tasks involving similar settings. The expulsion mechanism is a form of binary, irreversible punishment with a small cost to punishers. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling expulsion or strong exclusionary sanctions will lead to much higher efficiency than control.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 16 subjects in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 periods (rounds) in all treatments, known in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous (0 to 10 experimental dollars), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.2 (each unit contributed returns 0.2 to each of 16 members).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost to punisher is 0.25 experimental dollars per expulsion vote, but only if expulsion is successful (i.e., if half or more vote to expel). No cost if expulsion does not occur.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is binary and severe: expulsion from the group, with a large drop in endowment (from 10 to 5 per period) and no further voting rights. The impact per cost is very high.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only expulsion (punishment) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the total number of periods (15) in advance; current period was displayed each round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects saw all others' contributions (current and previous two periods), average assignment so far, and earnings, with anonymous identifiers.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects saw the number of votes against each individual, but not who voted for whom. Subjects were identified by fixed random letters, but voting was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The expulsion mechanism is a strong, binary, and irreversible punishment, which may not generalize to milder or more incremental punishment mechanisms.
- The group size (16) is larger than in many public goods experiments, which may affect generalizability.
- No communication or chat was allowed, so results may not generalize to settings with communication.
- No reward mechanism was present, so the interaction of expulsion with positive incentives is not tested.
- The experiment was conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The expulsion threat is not costless, but the cost is small relative to the impact, which may not match real-world sanctioning environments.
