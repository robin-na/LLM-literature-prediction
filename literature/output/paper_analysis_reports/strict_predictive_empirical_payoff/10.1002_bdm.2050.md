# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups
Authors: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral Decision Making
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total income (group payoff)
- period payoff
- efficiency (ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution (cooperation) rate
- intended punishment
- executed punishment
- antisocial punishment
- punishment of uncooperative individuals
- fairness perception
- satisfaction
- interpersonal trust
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (total income, period payoff, efficiency) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, psychological measures).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each game version was played for 6 rounds (periods), for a total of 18 rounds. Efficiency trends suggest that longer games may favor democratic punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed; all interactions were anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: each player could choose how many of their 20 MUs to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU contributed was multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 MU to reduce target's payoff by 3 MUs (cost:impact = 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each MU spent reduces target by 3 MUs).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew in advance that each game version would last 6 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the contributions and payoffs of all other group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew how much they punished and were punished, but the game was anonymous and player identities were not revealed. Punishment was assigned to player numbers, which were shuffled each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used a relatively short time horizon (6 rounds per condition), which may underestimate the long-run efficiency benefits of democratic peer punishment.
- The punishment mechanism was costly (1:3 cost:impact), which may not generalize to less costly punishment settings.
- No reward mechanism was included, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The study used a within-subjects design with all participants experiencing all conditions, which may introduce order or learning effects despite randomization.
- The sample was limited to university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
- The democratic punishment system required majority voting for execution but did not allow democratic determination of punishment magnitude.
- No communication or chat was allowed, which may affect generalizability to real-world group settings.
