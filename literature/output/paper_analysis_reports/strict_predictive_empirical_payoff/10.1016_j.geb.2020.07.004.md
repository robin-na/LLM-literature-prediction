Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is public good provision (average tokens contributed), which is a direct input to efficiency but not efficiency per se. Net earnings and total earnings are reported and are closely related to efficiency. The paper does not explicitly report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but net earnings and group payoff are sufficient to estimate it. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution rate, willingness-to-pay for monitoring, and punishment assigned are also reported and analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment uses a 2x2 design to compare exogenous vs. endogenous monitoring and peer vs. group monitoring in a linear public goods game with punishment. When monitoring is exogenously imposed, both peer and group monitoring yield similar public good provision and net earnings. When monitoring is endogenously chosen, group monitoring collapses (due to free riders refusing to pay for monitoring), leading to a 44% drop in public good provision, but net earnings are not significantly lower because less is spent on punishment. Peer monitoring, however, is robust to endogenization: public good provision and net earnings remain high, and willingness-to-pay for monitoring is not strongly correlated with contribution level. The results are robust to changes in the punishment mechanism and the voting rule for group monitoring. The key implication is that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on the monitoring regime and whether monitoring is exogenous or endogenous.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the impact depends on the monitoring regime and whether monitoring is exogenously imposed or endogenously chosen. If monitoring is exogenous (automatic), punishment increases or maintains efficiency (via higher public good provision) regardless of whether monitoring is peer-based or group-based. If monitoring is endogenous and requires group consensus, punishment may fail to improve efficiency because monitoring is not implemented (especially in the presence of free riders), leading to low public good provision but not necessarily lower net earnings due to reduced punishment costs. Peer monitoring is robust to endogenization: punishment maintains efficiency even when monitoring is endogenous. Thus, when using game design dimensions to predict treatment efficiency, it is crucial to account for whether monitoring is exogenous or endogenous and the type of monitoring (peer vs. group). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper provides sufficient data to estimate the relative effect of punishment under different monitoring regimes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use fixed groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played for 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates 0-5 tokens between public and private goods.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token contributed to the public good yields $1 to each of 4 players (total $4), so MPCR = 1.0.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays $1 per unit of punishment assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each $1 spent on punishment reduces the punished player's payoff by $3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown their own contribution, initial payoff, costs of punishments assigned and received. They are not told group contributions, but can infer some information from what is revealed in the monitoring stage.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but net earnings and group payoff are sufficient to estimate it.
- The study does not allow for a mixture of peer and group monitoring within the same game.
- The results may not generalize to settings with different group sizes, MPCRs, or punishment/reward structures.
- The laboratory setting may not capture all real-world complexities of monitoring and punishment.
- Control efficiency (no punishment) is not directly reported, so the absolute effect of punishment relative to a no-punishment baseline must be inferred.
