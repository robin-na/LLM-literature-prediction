# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy
Authors: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y.
Journal: Economic Record
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per round
- efficiency (ratio of actual to maximum possible group earnings)
- group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- number of punishers per punishee
- types of punishment (cooperative, perverse, retaliation)
- change in individual contribution after punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables for the prediction task are average earnings and efficiency per round, reported for each treatment and group size. The paper also reports detailed behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment patterns) but analyzes efficiency directly.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (b) In larger groups (n=8, 12), PPID yields the highest efficiency (80.60% and 78.05%), followed by AP (76.10% and 61.35%), with VCM lowest (68.60% and 63.30%). (c) In large groups, AP can reduce efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on group size and punishment institution. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size is directly manipulated (4, 8, 12). The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on group size: AP is most effective in small groups, PPID in large groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 40 periods: 10 rounds of VCM and 30 rounds of VCM with punishment. The number of rounds is fixed and known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication is not allowed in any treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: any amount between 0 and 10 (with decimals) can be contributed.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 2/n (group account is doubled and split equally). For n=4: 0.5; n=8: 0.25; n=12: 0.1667.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 EMU to reduce target's payoff by 2 EMUs (cost per unit = 1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 EMU spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by 2 EMUs (punishment multiplier = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of rounds is finite and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In all treatments, participants see the contribution and group account payoff of each group member (ordered by contribution, not by ID in VCM and AP; with ID in PPID).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. This is the key manipulation: in AP, punisher ID is not shown to the punished; in PPID, punisher ID is shown to the punished. The effect of punishment on efficiency is more positive in PPID than AP in large groups, but less positive in small groups.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted with student subjects in a laboratory setting, which may limit generalizability to field or real-world environments.
- Only one punishment per subject per round is allowed, which may not capture all dynamics of real-world sanctioning.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The effect of punishment is only measured for the specific punishment cost and effectiveness parameters used (1 EMU cost, 2 EMU impact).
- The study does not vary the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) within group size, so interaction effects between MPCR and punishment are not tested.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation effects, which may be important in some real-world public goods settings.
