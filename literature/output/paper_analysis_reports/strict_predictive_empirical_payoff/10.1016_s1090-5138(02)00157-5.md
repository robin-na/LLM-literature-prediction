Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (e.g., net payoffs, group earnings, welfare) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, punishment/reward frequency, effort levels, rejection rates). However, the effect of punishment on group payoff and efficiency is a central focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Experimental public goods games consistently show that when participants can punish defectors at a personal cost, cooperation rates remain high or even increase over time, and group payoffs (efficiency) are substantially higher than when punishment is not allowed. In the Fehr & Gächter (2000) experiments, for example, allowing punishment led to a 40% increase in net payoffs to all subjects, even after accounting for the costs of punishment. The effect is observed even under strict anonymity and in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, indicating that the mechanism is not driven by expectations of future reciprocity. The paper also reviews cross-cultural evidence and evolutionary models, but the core experimental result is that punishment opportunities robustly improve group efficiency in public goods game environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control conditions without punishment. The effect is robust across standard lab PGG designs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR. The magnitude of the efficiency gain is substantial (e.g., 40% increase in net payoffs in Fehr & Gächter 2000). The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency given the control game design and efficiency, especially in standard PGG settings. The paper also discusses the importance of group structure (e.g., partner vs. stranger matching), but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent across these variations.

- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Contribution is continuous (subjects can allocate any fraction of their endowment).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is specified as 40% (each subject receives 40% of the total public account).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly; e.g., in Fehr & Gächter (2000), the punisher pays 1 to reduce the target's payoff by 3. In employer-employee game, cost is 1 to change payoff by 2.5.

- reward technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is specified in employer-employee game (1:2.5 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told how many points were contributed to the common account each round.

## Important Limitations
- The paper synthesizes results from multiple experiments, but the most direct evidence for efficiency effects comes from Fehr & Gächter (2000), which uses standard lab PGG designs; generalizability to other settings or parameterizations may be limited.
- Some experiments allow both punishment and reward, but the main efficiency results are for punishment; the effect of reward alone is not isolated.
- The paper does not systematically vary all game design dimensions (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost) within a single experiment, so moderator effects are inferred rather than directly tested.
- Cultural and cross-societal variation is discussed, but the efficiency results are primarily from Western student samples.
- Theoretical and evolutionary models are discussed, but the main prediction task is supported by lab experimental data.
