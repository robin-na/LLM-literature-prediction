The main dependent variables are contribution and profit (payoff). Efficiency is measured as net individual profit per period, and group profit is also reported. Non-payoff outcomes include contribution rates and punishment behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 players per session, fixed matching throughout the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds total: 10 rounds baseline VCM, 10 rounds punishment phase.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0 ≤ c ≤ 20 ECUs per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (group account is doubled and divided equally among 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Two regimes: main experiment 1:1 cost (1 point spent reduces 1 point), second experiment 3:1 cost (1 point spent reduces 3 points). No significant difference in efficiency effect between regimes.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness varied: 1:1 (main) vs. 3:1 (second experiment). No significant moderation of efficiency effect.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in each phase (10 rounds per phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see others' contributions and profits in random order each period; cannot identify individuals.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; group members know the number of punishment points used but not to whom they were directed or who the manager is (after selection).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted with student samples from Chinese universities, which may limit generalizability to other cultural or institutional contexts.
- The design focuses on centralized punishment; results may not generalize to decentralized punishment or peer-punishment settings.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not speak to the effect of positive incentives.
- The manager's identity is anonymous after selection, which may differ from real-world settings where leaders are known.
- The study does not test for long-term or repeated exposure to the same institutional arrangements beyond 10 rounds per phase.
