# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Empowering neighbors versus imposing regulations: An experimental analysis of pollution reduction schemes
Authors: Cason, T. N., & Gangadharan, L.
Journal: Journal of Environmental Economics and Management
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (ratio of realized surplus to maximum possible surplus)
- total group earnings
- environmental damages (group-level negative externality)
- median pollution/emissions per group
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual and group pollution/emissions choices
- punishment points assigned and received
- variance in emissions
- behavioral response to punishment
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is efficiency, defined as realized surplus over maximum possible surplus. Pollution/emissions, punishment behavior, and variance are also analyzed, but efficiency and group earnings are the main payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab experiment simulating a non-point source pollution problem with heterogeneous agents, four treatments were compared: baseline (no intervention), group tax only, punishment only, and group tax plus punishment. The group tax mechanism (formal regulation) led to large and statistically significant increases in efficiency (from ~55% in baseline to ~87% with tax, and ~90% with tax plus punishment). Peer punishment alone increased efficiency only slightly and not significantly. When combined, punishment reduced variance and helped keep pollution below the target more often, but the efficiency gain was offset by the deadweight loss from punishment costs. Punishment was used less frequently and less intensely than in standard public goods games, limiting its effectiveness. The results suggest that formal regulatory mechanisms are more effective than informal peer punishment in increasing efficiency, but peer punishment can play a complementary role.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public-goods-like environment with a negative externality (pollution), enabling peer punishment alone yields only modest and inconsistent efficiency gains, while a formal group penalty (tax) mechanism yields large and robust efficiency improvements. Adding punishment to the group tax further reduces damages and increases the likelihood of meeting the target, but does not significantly increase efficiency due to the costliness of punishment. For prediction, the presence of a formal group penalty is the key driver of efficiency, and the marginal effect of enabling peer punishment is small or negligible unless punishment is much more frequent or severe than observed here. The game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, punishment tech, group size, rounds, information) are well-documented, making this study highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 6 players (firms), with 3 'near' and 3 'far' from the receptor point.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods (rounds) per session, with all rounds known in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; only structured information is provided.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (emission) choices are continuous, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard MPCR, but the marginal per-capita return is determined by the transfer coefficients (T_n=0.36, T_f=0.24) and group size (6). The marginal benefit and cost are explicitly parameterized.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point costs the punisher 0.5 Experimental dollars.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the punished player's payoff by 1.5 Experimental dollars (punishment effectiveness ratio 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of periods (30) was common knowledge and announced in the instructions.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the emissions of their two neighbors at the same location, total emissions at both locations, ambient pollution, and their own payoff each period. In punishment treatments, they also see punishment assigned/received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are given fixed anonymous labels throughout the session, so punishment is assigned to identified (but anonymous) neighbors at the same location.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a non-point source pollution game with heterogeneity and nonlinear payoffs, which differs from standard linear public goods games; results may not generalize to all PGG settings.
- Punishment was only allowed within-location (not across all group members), which may limit the generalizability of findings to settings with broader punishment opportunities.
- Punishment was used less frequently and less intensely than in standard PGG experiments, possibly due to the game's structure or heterogeneity.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so the effects of positive incentives are not addressed.
- The formal group tax mechanism does not include real-world enforcement or litigation costs, which may overstate its practical efficiency.
- Subjects were university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
