The main dependent variables are group payoffs and contributions. Efficiency is directly analyzed as group payoff relative to the social optimum. Non-payoff outcomes such as agreement formation, compliance, and punishment are also reported and analyzed as mechanisms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Punishment is used less frequently and more effectively in these settings, with little anti-social punishment. Punishment alone (without agreement) or with second-best agreements increases contributions but not payoffs, as punishment costs offset the gains. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; agreements are made via computer interface, not face-to-face or verbal.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 25 tokens; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (a=0.4, n=4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 token per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 10 rounds (finite, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown individual contributions and their own payoff at the end of each round; after punishment, they are informed about the number of points received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know how many punishment points they received, but not from whom (punishment is anonymous).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4), fixed MPCR (0.4), and a finite, known number of rounds (10), so generalizability to other parameterizations is uncertain.
- Punishment is always costly and anonymous; effects may differ with different punishment structures or identification.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not address reward or combined reward-punishment regimes.
- All agreements are non-binding and require unanimity; partial or majority agreements are not studied.
- Subjects are university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
- The effect of communication (chat, face-to-face) is not tested; only written, computer-mediated agreements are used.
