# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Shadow of conflict: How past conflict influences group cooperation and the use of punishment
Authors: Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., & Reddmann, L.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings (public goods game earnings minus cost of punishment)
- individual earnings
- total group welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rates
- punishment frequency
- punishment targeting
- reaction to punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for efficiency is group earnings (payoff from the public goods game minus cost of punishment). Contribution rates and punishment behavior are also reported, but efficiency/payoff is a primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab experiment with groups of four, participants either experienced an asymmetric intergroup conflict (attacker-defender game) or not, and then played a repeated public goods game with peer punishment. The main findings are: (1) Prior conflict does not significantly reduce average cooperation rates, but does reduce group earnings (efficiency) due to less effective punishment. (2) When previous conflict roles are identifiable, punishment is less likely to be targeted at free-riders and is less effective at increasing cooperation, especially when administered by previous attackers. (3) Hiding previous conflict roles (resetting group tags) increases both cooperation and group earnings over time, and punishment regains its effectiveness. (4) The negative effect of conflict on efficiency is not due to lower cooperation per se, but to maladaptive punishment patterns that reduce the welfare gains from cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with peer punishment, the presence of a prior asymmetric conflict (with identifiable roles) reduces group efficiency (earnings) even if average cooperation rates are not significantly lower. The reduction in efficiency is due to less effective punishment: free-riders are punished less, and punishment by previous attackers is less effective. If prior conflict roles are hidden, efficiency increases and punishment regains its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is moderated by the social context—specifically, whether there is a 'shadow of conflict' and whether past roles are identifiable. In standard PGG designs without such conflict history, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, identifiability) are all well specified, and the control efficiency is directly comparable.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public goods game with punishment is played for 15 rounds in all treatments.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed during the game. Participants could contact the experimenter via chat, but not each other.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: each player decides how many of their 20 units to invest (0-20).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit assigned for punishment costs the punisher 1 unit.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit assigned for punishment reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 units (1:3 punishment device).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the total number of rounds and can see which round they are in.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see each group member's contribution, total investment, return from the public good, and individual earnings. After punishment, they see who punished whom and the breakdown of earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In the 'shadow' and 'control' treatments, punishment is fully identifiable (players know who punished whom, and prior conflict roles are visible). In the 'reset' treatment, prior roles are hidden, so punishment is not linked to past conflict roles.

## Important Limitations
- The study focuses on asymmetric conflict (attacker-defender) and may not generalize to symmetric conflict settings.
- The laboratory setting may not capture all psychological and social complexities of real-world post-conflict cooperation.
- The effect of prior conflict on efficiency is contingent on the identifiability of past roles; in real-world settings, identifiability may be more complex.
- No communication between players; effects of communication or reconciliation processes are not tested.
- Sample is from a university population and may not generalize to other populations.
