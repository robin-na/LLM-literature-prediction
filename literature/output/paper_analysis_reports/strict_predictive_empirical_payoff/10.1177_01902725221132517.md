# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes
Authors: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T.
Journal: Social Psychology Quarterly
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- profits
- final earnings per round
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to the public good
- punishment sent
- punishment received
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is contribution to the public good (a behavioral outcome), but the paper also analyzes group earnings/profits, especially in the context of punishment and network density. Efficiency per se is not directly reported, but group earnings and profits are closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Groups were either small (3-5 participants) or large (7-9 participants), with mean sizes of 3.81 and 7.83, respectively. Group size was a key manipulated variable, and its interaction with punishment and network density was central to the findings.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each phase consisted of 7 rounds, with 4 phases per group (2 information-only, 2 information-and-punishment), totaling 28 rounds per participant. The exact number of rounds per phase was not revealed to participants to avoid end-game effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prevented in all conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions were continuous: participants could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 points per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point contributed to the group fund was doubled and then divided equally among all group members. For small groups (e.g., 4), MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5; for large groups (e.g., 8), MPCR = 2/8 = 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 point spent by punisher deducts 3 points from the punished. Each participant could spend up to 10 points per alter per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point spent removes 3 points from the target (3:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told in advance how many rounds each phase would last, to avoid end-game effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw the contributions and personal fund amounts of their network ties (with unique letter identifiers) after each round. In fully dense networks, this meant seeing all others' contributions; in sparse networks, only those to whom they were tied.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could see which of their connections punished them (via letter identifiers), but could not see the individual deduction decisions of their connections. Letter identifiers were reassigned between phases to prevent tracking across phases.

## Important Limitations
- The 'large' groups in the experiment (7-9 participants) are much smaller than many real-world collective-action groups, limiting generalizability to very large groups.
- The network structures were limited to fully dense and random sparse (Erdős-Rényi) networks; real-world networks may have more complex structures (e.g., clustering, small-world, scale-free).
- Only monetary punishment was studied; non-monetary sanctions and communication were not allowed, which may affect the external validity of findings.
- Efficiency was measured via group earnings/profits, not explicitly as a ratio to the fully cooperative benchmark (efficiency), though the outcomes are closely related.
- Participants could not communicate, which may differ from many real-world settings where informal sanctions and communication are possible.
