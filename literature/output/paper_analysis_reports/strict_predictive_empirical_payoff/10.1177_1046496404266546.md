# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts
Authors: Webb, J., & Foddy, M.
Journal: Small Group Research
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total group profit
- profit by individual
- profit by access level
- number of resources left in the shared resource
Non-payoff outcomes:
- harvesting behavior
- amount of fine paid
- voting for structural change (preference for sanctioning system)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group profit, individual profit, and resource preservation (number of trees left). Behavioral outcomes (harvesting, voting) are also analyzed, but efficiency/payoff is a primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule. Voting for structural change (switching sanctioning systems) is predicted by individual profit and fines, not by group-level resource preservation. The study highlights that the impact of sanctioning systems on efficiency and payoff is heterogeneous and depends on both the structure of the sanction and the distribution of resource access.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 25 trials (5 blocks of 5 trials), with some groups allowed more than 25 to avoid endgame effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; participants were isolated and told they could not communicate.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvesting) was continuous within a range (0-2, 0-3, or 0-6 trees per trial depending on access), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG; each tree harvested is worth 9 points to the harvester, but harvesting reduces the shared resource. There is a 5% regrowth per round. No explicit MPCR, but the structure is a common-pool resource dilemma.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Fine is $1.40 per block of 5 trials. In the punishment rule, only the highest harvester is fined; in the equality rule, all harvesters share the fine. The cost is imposed by the system, not by player choice.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated: targeted (punishment rule) vs. distributed (equality rule). Targeted punishment is more effective at resource preservation, but less effective for group profit.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told how many harvesting trials there would be, to avoid endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the total number of trees left and their own harvest/points after each trial. At the end of every fifth trial, they were told the group average harvest and points. In the equality-rule condition, they were told how many others had harvested.

## Important Limitations
- Exploratory study with low effect sizes; findings require replication.
- Laboratory setting with student volunteers; generalizability to real-world or applied settings is uncertain.
- Did not systematically control for individual differences such as social value orientation.
- Low overall harvesting rates may have been influenced by experimental framing or participant desire for equality.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) as in standard PGGs; structure is a common-pool resource dilemma.
