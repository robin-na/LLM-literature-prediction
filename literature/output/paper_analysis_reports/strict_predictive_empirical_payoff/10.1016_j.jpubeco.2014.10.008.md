# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Emotion venting and punishment in public good experiments
Authors: Dickinson, D. L., & Masclet, D.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- final payoffs (net of punishment costs)
- group welfare
- average earnings
- contributions (as a determinant of efficiency)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned
- frequency of punishment
- self-reported emotions (anger, joy, surprise)
- contribution rates
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is final payoffs (efficiency/welfare), but the paper also reports on punishment and contribution behavior, as well as emotional states.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a standard 4-player, 10-round public goods game with punishment (costly to punisher, more costly to target), the authors introduce several treatments allowing for different levels of emotion venting before punishment decisions: (1) Baseline (no venting), (2) Waiting (5-min cooling-off), (3) Waiting & Emotion (cooling-off plus self-reported emotions and unsent messages), and (4) Virtual Punishment (all previous plus non-binding virtual punishment). All treatments have punishment enabled. The main findings are: (a) All venting treatments reduce the amount and frequency of costly punishment compared to baseline; (b) The simple cooling-off period (Waiting) increases final payoffs (efficiency) relative to baseline, as reduced excessive punishment outweighs any loss in contributions; (c) More extensive venting (Virtual Punishment) reduces both punishment and contributions, and only increases efficiency in the early rounds, not in the long run; (d) The relationship between emotion venting and efficiency is non-linear (inverted-U): moderate venting is optimal for efficiency, while too little or too much venting is suboptimal. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so all results are relative to punishment-enabled baselines.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard lab PGG with punishment, the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the emotional context of punishment decisions. If punishment is enabled without any venting, excessive punishment can reduce efficiency. Introducing a simple cooling-off period (moderate venting) reduces excessive punishment and increases efficiency. However, if venting is too extensive (e.g., virtual punishment), punishment is reduced too much, contributions fall, and efficiency gains are not sustained. For prediction, this suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is maximized when the game design includes a moderate opportunity for emotional cooling-off before punishment, but not when venting is so extensive that it undermines the deterrence function of punishment. The results are most relevant for standard 4-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGGs with punishment enabled and no communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions have 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Waiting & Emotion and Virtual Punishment treatments, subjects can type anonymous messages to others, but these are never sent (no actual communication). No chat in Baseline or Waiting.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates any amount (0-20 ECU) to the group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4 (each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs 1 ECU to punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces target's payoff by 3 ECU (fine:punishment ratio 1:3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 10 periods (fixed, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, players see their own payoff, the total group contribution, and the individual contributions of each other group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- No no-punishment control: all treatments have punishment enabled, so the effect of punishment versus no punishment is not directly estimated.
- Punishment cost and effectiveness (price and fine ratio) are fixed; the study does not vary these parameters, so results may not generalize to other punishment cost structures.
- The emotional venting manipulations are specific (cooling-off, self-report, virtual punishment) and may not generalize to other forms of emotion regulation.
- The study uses a fixed group size (4) and number of rounds (10); results may differ in larger groups or longer/shorter games.
- No real communication is allowed; the 'chat' in some treatments is only for unsent messages, so the effect of real communication is not tested.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
