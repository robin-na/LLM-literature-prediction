The primary dependent variables include group earnings (payoff/efficiency) and group/individual contributions. Punishment behavior is also analyzed in detail. Efficiency is operationalized as group earnings relative to the social optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session: 10 without punishment, 20 with/without punishment depending on treatment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 to 20 tokens (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (a = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token per unit assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token spent on punishment reduces the target's earnings by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available in any treatment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were publicly informed of the number of rounds in each part.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the number of tokens allocated to (or taken from) the group account by each individual in descending order, as well as their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were shown the total punishment received but not who punished them or how many punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4) and a specific MPCR (0.5), so generalizability to other group sizes or MPCRs is not directly tested.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- No communication or reputation effects are allowed, which may differ from some real-world settings.
- The punishment mechanism is strictly monetary and anonymous; effects may differ with non-monetary or identifiable punishment.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The findings are specific to the Give vs. Take framing; other forms of social dilemmas may yield different results.
