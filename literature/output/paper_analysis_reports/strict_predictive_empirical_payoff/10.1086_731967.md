The main dependent variable for the prediction task is group outcome/payoff, which is directly reported and analyzed. Punishment behavior and contribution rates are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compared three public good game variants: (1) standard, (2) threshold (group must reach a minimum contribution or all is lost), and (3) monitor (one randomly chosen, anonymous group member can punish others at a cost). Both the threshold and monitor games increased individual contributions compared to the standard game, with the threshold game increasing contributions the most. However, because about one-quarter of groups failed to meet the threshold and lost all contributions, the monitor game produced the highest average group payoff (efficiency). The punishment mechanism was effective: monitors punished in about 73% of groups, and punishment was targeted at low contributors. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to order effects and individual characteristics. The study provides direct evidence that introducing costly punishment in a one-shot, anonymous, five-player public good game increases group efficiency relative to the control.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a five-player, one-shot public good game with continuous contributions, no communication, and a known MPCR of 0.4. The monitor (punishment) treatment increased group efficiency/payoff compared to the control. The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 5 participants, matching the typical work crew size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game variant was played once per participant (one-shot); no repeated rounds within a game variant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants during the games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0-10 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (contributions are doubled and split among 5 players: 2/5 = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 token to punisher, 3 tokens deducted from punished. This mechanism increased group efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 token spent by punisher reduces punished player's payoff by 3 tokens.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number and order of games in advance; each variant was played once.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No feedback on group contributions or payoffs was given until the end of all games; no interim summaries.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: the identity of the monitor was not revealed to the group, and the monitor did not know their role until after all games were played.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is one-shot for each game variant; no repeated interaction or learning dynamics are studied.
- The setting is a lab-in-the-field experiment with public sector employees, which may limit generalizability to other populations or real workplace settings.
- Punishment is implemented via an anonymous monitor, which may not be feasible or realistic in actual organizations.
- No communication or feedback during the games; real-world teams may have richer information and interaction.
- The threshold (common goal) mechanism, while effective at increasing contributions, led to lost contributions in about one-quarter of groups, reducing group efficiency.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism was tested.
- The experiment does not analyze the long-term or dynamic effects of punishment or threshold mechanisms.
