Journal: Journal of Public Economics

Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for efficiency is average net earnings (total payoff after both contribution and punishment stages). Contribution and punishment behavior are also analyzed, but efficiency/payoff is a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study uses a one-shot voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) public goods game with and without punishment, manipulating both the announcement of punishment and participants' emotional state (anger vs. happiness). The main findings are: (1) Announcing the punishment stage increases contributions and average net earnings, confirming that punishment can improve efficiency in the short run. (2) Induced anger reduces contributions and increases the severity of punishment, leading to lower average net earnings (i.e., lower efficiency) compared to the happy condition. (3) The efficiency benefit of punishment is present but is diminished when subjects are angry, as harsher punishment increases costs and reduces net group payoffs. (4) The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured directly via average net earnings, and the design allows for clean causal inference due to the one-shot structure and between-subjects manipulation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a one-shot, three-player VCM with punishment, the introduction of a punishment stage (when announced) increases group efficiency (average net earnings) compared to the same game without punishment. The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends on the emotional state of participants: it is larger when subjects are happy and smaller (but still positive) when subjects are angry, due to increased punishment costs. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, controlling for baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the effect can be moderated by factors (such as mood) that affect the severity and frequency of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per game, fixed across all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 0.5 (each token contributed increases each group member's payoff by 0.5 ECUs).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the recipient's payoff by 3 ECUs (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism does not exist; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, subjects see the profile of contributions of the other two group members before assigning punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is one-shot; results may not generalize to repeated or long-run settings.
- The study is conducted in a lab with university students, which may limit external validity.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured only in the short run; long-term effects are not addressed.
- Emotional states are induced via video clips, which may not fully capture real-world emotional dynamics.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about the interaction of punishment and reward.
