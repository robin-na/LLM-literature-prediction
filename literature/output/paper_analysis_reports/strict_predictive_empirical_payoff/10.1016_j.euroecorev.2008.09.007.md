# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Who to punish? Individual decisions and majority rule in mitigating the free rider problem
Authors: Ertan, A., Page, T., & Putterman, L.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (group earnings as a fraction of maximum possible)
- group earnings
- frequency of free riding (defined by earnings and contributions)
- net gain from contribution changes
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- voting behavior on punishment rules
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (group earnings relative to maximum), group earnings, and frequency of free riding (defined by both contribution and earnings). Contribution rates and punishment behavior are also analyzed, but efficiency and earnings are primary.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when groups can vote on punishment rules, they overwhelmingly prohibit punishment of high contributors and converge toward allowing punishment of low contributors only. This rule structure leads to higher contributions and, crucially, higher efficiency (group earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to both no punishment and unrestricted punishment. The cost of punishment under unrestricted rules reduces efficiency, while targeted punishment of low contributors effectively deters free riding and increases group welfare. The findings are robust across two experimental designs (3-vote and 5-vote), and the efficiency gains are statistically significant. The study provides direct evidence that the structure and targeting of punishment, not just its presence, are critical for achieving efficiency gains in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency only if punishment is restricted to low contributors (i.e., free riders) and not allowed against high contributors. Unrestricted punishment can reduce efficiency due to costly 'perverse punishment.' The experimental design and results allow direct mapping to prediction tasks: for a 4-player, 30-round, no-chat, continuous-contribution VCM with MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 0.25 per 1 unit impact, and anonymous punishment, efficiency is highest when punishment is allowed only for low contributors. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is known, enabling targeted punishment (low-only) is likely to increase efficiency substantially; enabling unrestricted punishment may not. The findings are directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the targeting and scope of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session has 30 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject allocates 0-10 units in integer amounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each unit contributed returns 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 0.25 per 1 unit reduction in target's earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 0.25 spent reduces target's earnings by 1.00 (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of periods (30) is finite and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see the contribution choices of others (under anonymized labels) and the total punishment received. Before votes, groups are shown other groups' average contributions and earnings under each rule.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: players know how much punishment they received, but not who assigned it.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with fixed groups of 4 and may not generalize to larger or more heterogeneous groups.
- Punishment is implemented via computer and is perfectly enforced; real-world implementation may be less precise.
- No communication is allowed, which may differ from real-world group dynamics.
- The study does not examine the effects of reward mechanisms, only punishment.
- The information structure (showing other groups' outcomes before votes) may accelerate convergence and is not always present in real-world settings.
- The experiment only tests a specific MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (0.25), and group size (4); results may differ with other parameters.
