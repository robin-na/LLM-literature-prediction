# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The choice of institutions to solve cooperation problems: a survey of experimental research
Authors: Dannenberg, A., & Gallier, C.
Journal: Experimental Economics
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff
- group payoff
- efficiency rate
- welfare
- total earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- cooperation rate
- punishment frequency
- institutional choice/voting
- migration between institutions
- beliefs about others
- support for institution
Outcome notes:
The review systematically reports both payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, average payoff, welfare) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation, punishment, voting, beliefs). Many studies report both types, and the review distinguishes between them.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review finds that (1) punishment institutions, when adopted, reliably increase both cooperation and group efficiency/payoff in public goods and related games; (2) the effect is strongest when the institution eliminates free-riding incentives and is not too costly; (3) the popularity and effectiveness of punishment institutions increase with repeated choice and learning; (4) endogenously chosen punishment institutions often yield higher efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, especially for weaker institutions, but the aggregate benefit of endogenous choice is ambiguous because some groups fail to implement the institution; (5) institutional cost and incomplete elimination of free-riding are key barriers to adoption and effectiveness; (6) most studies report both behavioral and payoff outcomes, with efficiency and group payoff being central dependent variables.

## Prediction Guidance
This review provides strong evidence that, in public goods game-like environments, enabling punishment mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and payoffs, especially when the punishment is effective (eliminates free-riding) and not too costly. The effect is robust across a variety of game designs, player counts, and institutional choice mechanisms. For prediction, the presence of a punishment institution should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, with the magnitude moderated by punishment cost, effectiveness, and whether the institution is adopted. The review also highlights that repeated exposure, information, and learning opportunities increase the likelihood of institution adoption and thus efficiency gains. However, if the institution is costly or does not fully deter free-riding, efficiency gains may be limited or absent. The review's synthesis of many studies with detailed design features makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Player count varies across studies (e.g., 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, etc.), but the review does not systematically analyze its effect on efficiency or punishment impact. Some studies report results for different group sizes.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Many studies use repeated rounds (e.g., 10, 30, 32, 100 rounds). Repeated choice and more rounds allow for learning, which increases institution adoption and efficiency.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Only one study (Ostrom et al. 1992) allows communication before institution choice; the review notes this as a gap and does not analyze its effect.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some studies use all-or-nothing contribution (e.g., forced full contribution under institution), others use continuous contribution. The review does not systematically compare these, but notes that forced full contribution is common in punishment institutions.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is varied in some studies (e.g., Kosfeld et al. 2009), but the review does not systematically analyze its moderating effect on punishment efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is a key moderator: higher cost reduces adoption and efficiency gains; low-cost punishment is more popular and effective.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (leverage, deterrence) is manipulated in several studies; higher effectiveness (deterrent punishment) leads to higher efficiency and cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Some studies allow for reward as an alternative to punishment. Reward institutions are often popular but less effective than punishment for increasing efficiency.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward cost/leverage is manipulated in some studies (e.g., Sutter et al. 2010); higher leverage (lower cost per impact) increases adoption and efficiency.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness (impact per cost) is varied; higher effectiveness increases efficiency, but reward is generally less effective than punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Most studies specify the number of rounds in advance, but the review does not analyze this as a moderator.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Providing information about group performance in different institutions increases learning, adoption, and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- As a review, the paper synthesizes many studies but does not provide new experimental data.
- Some dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, chat, all-or-nothing) are not systematically analyzed as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- Most studies use student samples; generalizability to other populations is limited.
- Communication between players is rarely allowed; only one study includes chat.
- Heterogeneity in institutional design, group size, and context may limit the precision of predictions for specific game designs.
- The review notes that the aggregate benefit of endogenous institution choice is ambiguous, as some groups fail to implement the institution and perform poorly.
- Some payoff outcomes are reported as group averages or efficiency rates, which may not always be directly comparable across studies.
