# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do the right thing: The effects of moral suasion on cooperation
Authors: Dal Bó, E., & Dal Bó, P.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group payoff
- earnings
- payoff increases associated with higher contributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- punishment assigned
- expectations about others
- preference changes
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good, but the paper also reports on average payoffs and earnings, especially in relation to changes in contribution. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated, but group payoffs and their changes are discussed as a result of treatment effects.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a series of lab experiments using a two-player linear public goods game, the authors show that (1) moral suasion (exposure to a moral message) increases contributions and group payoffs, but the effect is short-lived when punishment is not available; (2) when a punishment stage is added (where players can pay to reduce their partner's payoff), the effect of moral suasion becomes persistent, with higher and sustained contributions and higher group payoffs; (3) punishment alone increases contributions compared to no-punishment, but does not prevent a decline over time; (4) the combination of punishment and moral suasion produces the highest and most persistent cooperation and payoffs; (5) the mechanisms include both changes in expectations about others and changes in preferences. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, but does report group payoffs and their changes, which are closely related.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player linear public goods game with random rematching, adding a punishment stage (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases group payoffs and sustains higher cooperation, especially when combined with moral suasion. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, and the effect is amplified when moral suasion is present. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are well specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with and without punishment. The paper is less informative about settings with more players, field contexts, or different punishment technologies, but is highly informative for standard lab PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round is a two-player game, with subjects randomly rematched within groups of eight.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds in main experiments (10 pre-message, 10 post-message); some variants use only one post-message round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between subjects; all interaction is via computer terminals.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects can contribute any amount between 0 and 10 (with two decimal points).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.7 (joint account is multiplied by 1.4 and split between two players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 0.25 EP per 1 EP deducted from the other player (i.e., 1:4 cost-to-impact ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each 0.25 EP spent reduces the other player's payoff by 1 EP; punishment is direct and costly.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed about the number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see their own and their partner's contribution and payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments are conducted in a lab setting with UC Berkeley students, limiting generalizability to other populations or field settings.
- The public goods game is two-player with random rematching, which may not capture dynamics in larger groups.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio, but as group payoffs and earnings; direct efficiency calculations are not provided.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is studied, so results do not inform about the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The effect of punishment is only studied with a specific cost-to-impact ratio (0.25:1), so results may not generalize to other punishment technologies.
- The effect of moral suasion is only tested with specific, strongly worded messages; effects may differ with other forms of moral communication.
- No communication or chat is allowed, so results do not address the effect of endogenous communication among players.
