# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games
Authors: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean profit (group and individual)
- mean size of the public account
- welfare
- efficiency (implied by group profit relative to maximum possible)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- return rates from allocator
- excess contributions (coordination inefficiency)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual profits (payoff), as well as contributions. Efficiency is directly analyzed via group profit and excess contributions. Behavioral outcomes (contribution, return rates) are also reported but are not the primary focus for efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played for 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication among participants was prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 to 20 points (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public good multiplier is 1.6; with 4 players, MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (via allocation) is costless to the allocator; no explicit cost for reducing others' payoffs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocator can reduce any team member's share to zero; punishment is fully effective (1:1 impact).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocator can reward by allocating more of the public good to contributors.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is costless to the allocator except for opportunity cost (giving up own share).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocator can allocate up to the full public good to any member; reward is fully effective (1:1 impact).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, all team members are informed about the vector of contributions, public account, allocation, and final profits.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. The allocator is a known, fixed team member; all allocations are observable to all team members.

## Important Limitations
- The 'punishment' and 'reward' mechanisms are implemented via discretionary allocation by a team member, not via explicit costly punishment or reward as in standard PGG punishment treatments.
- The effect of the allocator is confounded with the ability to both punish and reward at zero explicit cost; results may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment costs.
- The study only examines groups of size 4 and 10 rounds; effects may differ with other group sizes or time horizons.
- The team allocator is randomly assigned, not elected; endogenous leadership may yield different effects.
- No communication is allowed; effects may differ with communication.
- The findings are specific to the three production technologies tested (linear, best-shot, weakest-link) and may not generalize to other forms.
