## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when participants can choose between groups with or without costly punishment, and with or without an indirect reciprocity (reputation) mechanism, most eventually prefer the group with both punishment and reputation. The combination (PUN&IR) leads to higher contributions, more efficient use of punishment (less frequent, more targeted), and significantly higher efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) than punishment alone (PUN). The destructive effect of punishment on efficiency is largely mitigated by the presence of reputation, as punishment is used less but more effectively. Full contributors in the combined treatment achieve payoffs close to the social optimum, and even the least cooperative subjects are more cooperative in the combined treatment. The results suggest that punishment and reputation are complementary, not substitutes, and their interaction provides a low-cost, high-efficiency solution to social dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, adding a reputation/indirect reciprocity mechanism increases efficiency beyond what is achieved by punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the presence of both punishment and reputation should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, especially when punishment is costly and group members can observe each other's contributions and reputational history. The efficiency boost is due to reduced punishment frequency, more targeted punishment, and increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when both punishment and reputation mechanisms are present.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experimental group consisted of 8 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 20 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is mentioned; all decisions are made on computer screens.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are all-or-nothing: each subject may contribute either 0 or 10 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contributions are multiplied by 1.6 and divided equally among group members; MPCR = 1.6 / n = 0.2 for n=8.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs the punisher 1 MU and reduces the punished subject's income by 3 MUs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 MUs lost per 1 MU spent (3:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Indirect reciprocity game allows for rewarding: sending 3 MUs (tripled to 9 MUs) to another player based on their reputation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sending 3 MUs in the indirect reciprocity game costs the sender 3 MUs.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 3 MUs sent are tripled, so the receiver gets 9 MUs per 3 MUs spent (3:9 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of periods (20) is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. At the end of each period, participants receive anonymous summary information about both groups. In the indirect reciprocity game, senders are informed about the receiver's current and past contributions and helping behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=8) and all-or-nothing contribution choices, which may limit generalizability to other group sizes or continuous contribution settings.
- The reputation mechanism is implemented via an indirect reciprocity game, which may not capture all real-world forms of reputation.
- Punishment and reward are both available in some treatments, but the design does not isolate the effect of reputation alone versus punishment alone in a fully crossed design.
- The laboratory setting and use of undergraduate students may limit external validity.
- The study does not systematically vary punishment cost, effectiveness, or MPCR, so findings may not generalize to other parameterizations.
