# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How transparency may corrupt - experimental evidence from asymmetric public goods games
Authors: Khadjavi, M., Lange, A., & Nicklisch, A.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean income
- mean income of citizens
- mean income of officials
- public good provision level
- aggregate contributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution decisions
- embezzlement by officials
- punishment points given/received
- stigmatization
- distribution of punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-related outcomes (mean income, public good provision) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contributions, embezzlement, punishment behavior). Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but mean income and public good provision are closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in an asymmetric public goods game (one official can embezzle, others can only contribute), transparency alone (without punishment) increases embezzlement by the official and reduces public good provision. Introducing a peer punishment stage (where group members can spend resources to reduce others' payoffs) increases public good provision and curbs embezzlement, but only if the official's identity is known. When both identity and actions are transparent (high transparency), punishment is more accurately targeted, reducing stigmatization and further increasing cooperation. Mean income is higher in punishment treatments, and income inequality between officials and citizens is reduced when punishment is possible and the official's identity is known.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling peer punishment in a public goods game with asymmetric roles (embezzlement possible for one player) increases group payoff and public good provision, especially when the official's identity is known. The effect is robust across both low and high transparency, but high transparency reduces erroneous punishment (stigmatization) and allows more targeted sanctions. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio), group size of 4, 10 rounds, and no communication, is associated with a substantial increase in efficiency-related outcomes compared to control. The effect is strongest when both identity and actions are transparent, but even low transparency with punishment is effective. Control efficiency (without punishment) is lower under high transparency due to increased embezzlement. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in asymmetric PGG-like environments with similar design features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players: 1 official and 3 citizens.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 periods (rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: citizens choose from 0 to 12, officials from -32 to 12.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return h = 0.4 (each Taler in the public good is multiplied by 0.4 and distributed equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 Taler per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 3 Taler (1:3 cost-to-impact ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the experiment lasts 10 periods; fixed and known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments provide information on the total public good account and payoffs. In high transparency, individual actions are also shown.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In low and high transparency, the official's identity is known; in high transparency, all actions are linked to identities.

## Important Limitations
- The game is an asymmetric public goods game with one official who can embezzle, which is not a standard symmetric PGG; results may not generalize to symmetric settings.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but as mean income and public good provision.
- The punishment mechanism is peer-to-peer and may not reflect institutional or centralized punishment.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit external validity for real-world settings.
- The sample consists of university students in a lab environment, which may limit generalizability.
- The effect of punishment is only tested for a specific cost-to-impact ratio (1:3); other ratios are not explored.
