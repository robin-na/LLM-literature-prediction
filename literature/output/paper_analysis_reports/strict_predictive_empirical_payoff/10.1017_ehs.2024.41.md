# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment
Authors: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Evolutionary Human Sciences
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- profits
- mean group contributions (as a proxy for efficiency)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned
- disapproval assigned
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment/disapproval assigned) and payoff-based outcomes (group earnings/profits). Efficiency is not reported as a normalized ratio, but group earnings and mean contributions are reported and interpreted as proxies for efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used 4-player groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games lasted 40 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players was allowed; sessions were conducted in silence.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could contribute any integer amount from their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total contribution multiplied by 1.6, divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each L$1 spent on punishment reduces target's earnings by 10%; cost is deducted from punisher's earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: L$1 spent reduces target's earnings by 10%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were not told how many rounds the game would last (unknown horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were informed of the contributions and earnings of all group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/disapproval was anonymous: players learned only the aggregate amount received from others, not the identity of the punisher/disapprover.

## Important Limitations
- Participants were all undergraduate students at a Canadian university, limiting generalizability to other populations.
- Only one type of costly punishment (monetary fines) and one type of costless disapproval (numerical ratings) were tested; results may differ with other forms.
- The study used a laboratory public goods game, which may not capture all real-world social dynamics or incentive structures.
- Efficiency was not reported as a normalized ratio, but as group earnings and mean contributions.
- Anomalous high cooperation in the Private Disapproval condition was likely a statistical fluke and did not replicate in later groups.
