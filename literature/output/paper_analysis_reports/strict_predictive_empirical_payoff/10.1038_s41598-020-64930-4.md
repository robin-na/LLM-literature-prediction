# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society
Authors: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average profit (group and individual)
- welfare (sum of payoffs at global/local/individual levels)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- PGG contribution
- reward and punishment assigned
- linkage between cooperation and reward/punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average profit (payoff) and PGG contribution. The paper explicitly analyzes group and individual profit as a function of the experimental conditions, and also reports on behavioral outcomes such as contribution and reward/punishment behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Player count is manipulated: BASE (4 players), Local0.1/Local0.4/Global0.1 (16 players). Group size interacts with localization and scope of punishment/reward.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All conditions use 15 repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; participants made decisions in isolated booths.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose any amount from 0 to 20 tokens in increments of 1.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. MPCR is manipulated at both individual and group levels: 0.4 or 0.1 for individuals; local group MPCR >1 (locally efficient) or <1 (locally inefficient). Effects on efficiency are directly analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token per unit; impact-to-cost ratio is 3:1 (punisher pays 1, target loses 3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each token spent causes 3-token loss to target).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Peer reward is available in all conditions; reward is the main mechanism facilitating cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1 token per unit; impact-to-cost ratio is 3:1 (rewarder pays 1, target gains 3).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (each token spent gives 3-token gain to target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told in advance that there are 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, players receive feedback on other players' contributions and on who rewarded/punished them and by how much.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about which other participants rewarded or punished them and the amounts.

## Important Limitations
- Sample size is relatively small at the group level (10-18 groups per condition), limiting generalizability.
- All participants are Japanese university students; cultural and demographic diversity is limited.
- Only two group sizes (4 and 16) and a single hierarchy structure (global/local) are tested; results may not generalize to other group sizes or more complex social structures.
- Only linear payoff structures are tested; real-world public goods may have nonlinear payoffs.
- Control efficiency (efficiency without punishment/reward) is not directly reported, making it difficult to isolate the marginal effect of enabling punishment alone.
