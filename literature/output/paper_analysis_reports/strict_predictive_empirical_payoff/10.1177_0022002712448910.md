# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Mediation and Conflict Prevention
Authors: Eisenkopf, G., & Bächtiger, A.
Journal: Journal of Conflict Resolution
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- overall performance (payoff) of A and B after punishment
- combined payoff of A and B
- inequality of outcomes (Gini coefficient, conflict asymmetry, payoff of 'loser')
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of conflicts
- punishment assigned
- communication content (qualitative)
- incidents of complete exploitation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the combined payoff of the two resource users (A and B), which is a direct measure of efficiency. Inequality and conflict frequency are also reported. Non-payoff outcomes include punishment frequency and qualitative communication analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a three-round common-pool resource game with two players, the addition of a third-party mediator who can both communicate and credibly threaten punishment leads to a substantial increase in group efficiency (combined payoff), with most groups achieving the efficient outcome. Communication alone (either direct or via mediator) increases efficiency relative to no mediation, but can also result in greater inequality if one player exploits the other's cooperation. Punishment alone, when costly and not paired with communication, does not improve efficiency. The effectiveness of the punishment-plus-communication treatment is robust to changes in the mediator's interests (impartial, biased, or no-stakes). The study provides direct evidence that communication and punishment are complementary tools for conflict prevention and efficiency in common-pool settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated common-pool resource game with two players, enabling both communication (even indirect, via a mediator) and credible punishment substantially increases efficiency compared to control conditions (no mediation or only one tool enabled). The effect is robust to mediator bias and to the mediator's economic stake. For prediction tasks, the presence of both punishment and communication should be expected to yield much higher efficiency than control, while punishment alone (especially if costly) may not improve efficiency. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close variant with continuous extraction and a well-defined efficient outcome, making the findings highly relevant for similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use two resource users (A and B), with a possible third-party mediator (C) in some treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments have three rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) is allowed in some treatments (direct between A and B, or indirect via mediator C). Communication alone increases efficiency, but the highest efficiency is achieved when combined with punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (extraction) is continuous: each player can choose any number of balls to extract in each round, up to the pool size.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is not constant but increases over rounds (1, 2, 3 points per ball in rounds 1-3; remaining balls after round 3 are worth 4 points each, split equally). The efficient solution is to leave the pool untouched.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: for the impartial mediator, each point of punishment costs 0.5 points (since the mediator's payoff is the average of A and B). For biased mediators, the cost per point varies (0.667 or 0.333). High punishment cost is cited as a reason why punishment alone is not effective.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a direct deduction from the target's payoff, up to the amount they have earned. The impact per unit cost is 2:1 for impartial mediators (1 point deducted from target, 0.5 point cost to mediator).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards are discussed as a possible mechanism but are not implemented in any treatment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the three-round structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all players receive full information about takeouts, current income, and punishment of each player.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always administered by the mediator (player C), whose role is known, but the identity of the person (A or B) is not revealed to the other player. Players know who the mediator is, but not the real identity of any participant.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a common-pool resource extraction game, not a standard linear public goods game, though it is a close variant.
- All experiments are conducted with only two resource users (A and B), which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- Punishment is costly for the mediator, and the cost structure may differ from other PGG or CPR experiments.
- The laboratory setting uses student subjects and low monetary stakes, which may affect external validity.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so results do not speak to the effect of rewards.
- Communication is either direct (between A and B) or indirect (mediator to A and B separately); no treatment allows both direct and indirect communication simultaneously.
- The study does not vary the number of rounds or group size, so effects of these dimensions are not tested.
