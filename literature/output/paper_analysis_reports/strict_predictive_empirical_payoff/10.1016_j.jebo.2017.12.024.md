# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Embezzlement, whistleblowing, and organizational architecture: An experimental investigation
Authors: Makowsky, M. D., & Wang, S. Y.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average income
- fraction of resources embezzled
- group payoff
- probability of zero payoffs (due to whistleblowing)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of whistleblowing
- embezzlement rate by player position
- round-by-round embezzlement
- whistleblowing by player level
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual payoffs (income), fraction of resources embezzled, and the probability that a group receives zero payoff due to whistleblowing. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures such as embezzlement and whistleblowing rates.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a laboratory experiment with 6-player groups, participants played a common pool resource game with a whistleblowing option: after each withdrawal, any non-first-mover could choose to 'blow the whistle', setting all group payoffs to zero. The game was played under six different organizational structures, varying the number of sequential levels (from 1 to 6) and the arrangement of players (pyramid, inverted pyramid, flat, etc.). The main findings are: (1) Embezzlement rates are high in all structures, but increase with the number of sequential levels; (2) The probability of group-level punishment (whistleblowing) increases with the number of levels; (3) Average group income (efficiency) decreases as the number of levels increases; (4) Flatter (simultaneous) structures have higher efficiency than taller (sequential) ones; (5) Pyramid structures have lower embezzlement than inverted pyramids, but this does not translate into significant efficiency differences due to similar rates of group-level punishment. The punishment mechanism is highly costly and does not improve efficiency; rather, it reduces it further when triggered.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-goods-like environments with a costly, group-level punishment mechanism (whistleblowing that sets all payoffs to zero), enabling punishment does not improve efficiency and may reduce it, especially as the game becomes more sequential (taller). The efficiency loss is driven by both high embezzlement and the destructive nature of the punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the control (no-punishment) game already has low efficiency due to high embezzlement, adding a highly destructive punishment option (like whistleblowing) is likely to further reduce efficiency, particularly in games with more sequential moves or more levels. The design dimensions most relevant are the number of players, number of rounds, the structure of play (sequential vs. simultaneous), and the nature/cost of punishment. The results are most applicable to settings where punishment is costly and group-level (not targeted), and less so to standard peer-to-peer punishment in classic PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use 6 players per group in all structures.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 10 rounds in their assigned structure.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (embezzlement) choices are continuous; players can withdraw any amount up to their share.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.3 (remaining group resource is multiplied by 1.8 and divided among 6 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (whistleblowing) is extremely costly: the punisher and all group members receive zero payoff if triggered.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: one whistleblower sets all group payoffs to zero (group-level, not targeted).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, players are informed of their own tokens, sum left in the pool, number of whistleblowers, and their own income. No detailed info about others' individual actions.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is group-level and maximally destructive (all payoffs set to zero), not targeted as in standard peer-to-peer punishment in PGGs.
- No positive reward or sanction mechanism is present; only costly, destructive punishment is studied.
- The experiment uses only 6-player groups and 10 rounds per subject; generalizability to other group sizes or longer games is untested.
- No communication or reputation effects are allowed; real-world settings may differ.
- Whistleblowing is modeled as purely destructive, with no positive externalities or future benefits, which may not reflect real-world whistleblowing.
- The findings may not generalize to standard PGGs with peer-to-peer punishment or to settings with less costly or more targeted punishment.
