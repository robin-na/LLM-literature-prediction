# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social sanctions and informal accountability: Evidence from a laboratory experiment
Authors: Lierl, M.
Journal: Journal of Theoretical Politics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average per capita payoff from the public good (as percent of endowment)
- group-level average payoff
- aggregate welfare
- provider and beneficiary payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contributions
- rent extraction (embezzlement) by provider
- punishment and reward behavior (frequency, magnitude)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the average per capita payoff from the public good, which is a direct efficiency/welfare measure. Secondary outcomes include contributions, rent extraction, and sanctioning behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment studies a public goods game with two beneficiaries and one provider, where the provider can embezzle contributions. The main manipulation is the structure of costly, mutual sanctioning (punishment and reward) relationships: (1) no sanctioning, (2) only among beneficiaries, (3) between provider and one or both beneficiaries, and (4) combinations thereof. The key finding is that mutual sanctioning among beneficiaries increases public goods provision and efficiency compared to no-sanctioning control, but this effect disappears or reverses when the provider is also part of the sanctioning network. The highest efficiency is observed when only beneficiaries can sanction each other. When both beneficiaries and the provider can mutually sanction, efficiency is not improved and can be lower than control. Actual punishment/reward is rare; the effect is mainly anticipatory. The results challenge the idea that denser or more encompassing sanctioning networks always improve efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends critically on the network structure of sanctioning relationships. If only beneficiaries can punish each other, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to control. If the provider can also punish or be punished, the effect is reduced or negative. For prediction, the presence and direction of punishment effects should be conditioned on who can punish whom. The game is a close variant of a standard PGG, with the addition of a third-party provider who can embezzle, so results are most directly applicable to co-production or agency settings. Control efficiency (no-sanction) is reported, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) varies by network structure. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment is not always efficiency-improving; the details of the punishment network matter.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 2 beneficiaries and 1 provider (total 3 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group plays 3 practice rounds and 10 paying rounds per treatment condition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; interactions are impersonal and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions are continuous (0-100 points), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public good investments are multiplied by 1.5 and split equally between 2 beneficiaries. MPCR = 0.75.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 point to reduce another's payoff by 3 points (cost ratio 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 point spent reduces target's payoff by 3 points).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is possible: 1 point spent increases another's payoff by 1 point.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1:1 (1 point spent increases other's payoff by 1 point).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated for 10 periods per treatment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All contributions, payoffs, and sanctioning decisions are observable to all players in the group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctioning decisions are observable to all group members; who punished/rewarded whom is known.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a close variant of a standard PGG, but includes a third-party provider who can embezzle, which may limit generalizability to standard PGGs without agency problems.
- Group size is fixed at 3 (2 beneficiaries, 1 provider); results may differ in larger groups.
- The experiment uses a laboratory student sample, which may not generalize to field or real-world settings.
- The structure of sanctioning relationships is manipulated, but only a limited set of network structures is tested.
- Actual punishment and reward behavior is rare; effects are mainly anticipatory.
- Sample size is relatively small, which may limit statistical power and generalizability.
- No communication is allowed, which may differ from real-world co-production or community settings.
