Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is net payoffs (efficiency). The paper also reports detailed behavioral outcomes (appropriation, punishment, beliefs), but efficiency is directly analyzed and compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a nonlinear CPR lab experiment with three punishment network structures (complete, undirected circle, directed circle), the authors find that average appropriation (resource extraction) is similar across all networks. However, the amount and type of punishment, as well as efficiency (net payoffs), differ. The complete network, with perfect monitoring and punishment, leads to the highest punishment expenditures and the lowest efficiency. Both imperfect networks (undirected and directed circles) have lower punishment expenditures and higher efficiency. The undirected circle network elicits more severe and more prosocial punishment than the other two, but overall, both imperfect networks are more efficient than the complete network. The results suggest that reducing the number of potential punishers (i.e., making monitoring and punishment less than perfect) can increase efficiency by reducing costly punishment, even if cooperation (appropriation) does not change.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in nonlinear CPR games with punishment enabled, the structure of the punishment network (i.e., the number of potential punishers and the network's density/directedness) can significantly affect efficiency. Specifically, reducing punishment opportunities (imperfect networks) increases efficiency compared to perfect monitoring and punishment, because less punishment is used and thus less is wasted on costly sanctions. Appropriation (cooperation) rates remain similar, so the efficiency gain is due to lower punishment costs. For prediction, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a perfect monitoring/punishment environment may reduce efficiency relative to imperfect punishment environments, and that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive—it depends on the punishment network's structure. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the structure of punishment opportunities.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All network groups have 4 players per group in every round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 rounds, common knowledge to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/appropriation is continuous: players allocate 0-25 tokens between private and common accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Nonlinear payoff function: group return is F(X) = aX - bX^2, with a=0.25, b=5, w=4. Nash equilibrium at 16 tokens, Pareto at 10 tokens. Not a constant MPCR, but the Nash/Pareto ratio is comparable to other studies.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is 1 token per deduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3), held constant across all treatments.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of rounds (15) is common knowledge to all participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After stage one, each subject is informed of their own earnings and total earnings from stage one. In stage two, subjects can observe the appropriation choices of those they are connected to (depending on network structure).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players cannot identify who punished them, only their node type. Stranger-matching and anonymization prevent identification.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not include a true no-punishment baseline; all treatments have punishment enabled, so the effect of punishment versus no punishment is inferred only by comparison to prior studies.
- The design confounds monitoring and punishment network structure; it is not possible to separate the effects of imperfect monitoring from imperfect punishment opportunities.
- The number of rounds (15) may be insufficient for long-run learning or for the full effects of punishment to emerge in nonlinear environments.
- The experiment uses a nonlinear CPR game, which may limit direct comparability to linear public goods games.
- The sample size is limited (7 sessions, 136 subjects), and randomization is at the session level, which may affect statistical power.
- No communication is allowed, so results may not generalize to settings where communication is possible.
- Punishment is always anonymous; the effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
