Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings, expected payoffs, and expected costs of noncompliance with contribution norms. Contribution rates and punishment behavior are also analyzed, but the paper explicitly models and reports payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with two high-endowment and two low-endowment players per group, and costly peer punishment, the ability to observe others' endowments (complete information) leads to the emergence and enforcement of efficient 'contribute-your-endowment' norms. This results in higher contributions and group earnings, especially from high-endowment players, and higher payoffs for low-endowment players. When endowments are private (incomplete information), groups adapt by enforcing a 'contribute-the-Low-endowment' norm, primarily enforced by low-endowment players. This norm prevents total unraveling of cooperation but enables high-endowment players to mimic low-endowment behavior and contribute less, reducing group efficiency. The expected costs and payoffs of noncompliance are explicitly estimated, showing that punishment is less targeted and less effective at sustaining high efficiency under incomplete information.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the information structure. When group members can observe both contributions and endowments, punishment supports high efficiency by enabling enforcement of optimal contribution norms. When endowments are unobservable, punishment still deters total free-riding but cannot sustain full efficiency, as high-endowment members can hide behind the low-endowment norm. For prediction, this means that the presence of punishment will increase efficiency relative to no punishment, but the magnitude of the effect is moderated by whether individual capacities (endowments) are observable. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the punishment technology and payoff structure, which can be used to calibrate predictive models.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 4 players (2 high-endowment, 2 low-endowment) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment lasted for 50 periods in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (any integer between 0 and endowment), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) was 0.4 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher paid 1 ED per reduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reduction point reduced the target's earnings by 3 EDs (punishment effectiveness 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the total number of rounds (50) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects saw aggregate group contributions, individual contributions (by random ID), their own period and total earnings, and a history of outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was assigned to group members identified by random ID, which was re-randomized each period to prevent tracking; subjects did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment only considers groups of four with a fixed 2-high/2-low endowment structure; results may not generalize to other group sizes or endowment distributions.
- Punishment is the only sanctioning mechanism; no reward or positive sanctioning is studied.
- The information manipulation is limited to endowment observability; other forms of incomplete or noisy information are not tested.
- The lab setting may not capture all relevant social or psychological factors present in real-world public goods dilemmas.
- The punishment technology (cost and effectiveness) is fixed and may not reflect other institutional or cultural contexts.
