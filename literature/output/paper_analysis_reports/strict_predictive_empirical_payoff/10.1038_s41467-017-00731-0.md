# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Peer punishment promotes enforcement of bad social norms
Authors: Abbink, K., Gangadharan, L., Handfield, T., & Thrasher, J.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- net earnings (after punishment costs)
- group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- normative attitudes (appropriateness ratings)
- reasons for punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group earnings (efficiency), especially in the negative-MPCR (destructive) condition. Contribution rates and punishment behavior are also reported, as well as survey-based measures of normative attitudes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a linear public goods game with 4 players and either zero (MPCR=0.25) or negative (MPCR=0.2) social returns to contribution, the availability of peer punishment increases contributions. However, in the negative-return condition, this leads to lower group earnings, i.e., punishment enforces a destructive norm. Survey and coordination game evidence show that subjects perceive non-contribution as socially inappropriate, even when it is efficient. Punishment stabilizes these inefficient norms. Thus, punishment does not always improve efficiency; its effect depends on the underlying norm and the payoff structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the MPCR and the prevailing social norm. In standard PGGs with MPCR > 1/n, punishment can increase efficiency by promoting cooperation. However, when MPCR < 1/n (as here, with MPCR=0.2 and n=4), punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency, as contributions are socially harmful. For prediction, this means that the sign of punishment's effect on efficiency is not always positive: it can be negative in games where cooperation is inefficient. The paper also provides clear parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, making it highly informative for prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session lasted 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: players could contribute any amount up to 20 MU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. MPCR was set to 0.25 (no group benefit) or 0.2 (group-destroying). With n=4, both are below the threshold for social efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost 1 MU to the punisher per deduction point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduced the target's earnings by 3 MU (punishment effectiveness 3:1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism did not exist; only punishment was available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the game would last 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were informed of the contributions of other group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were not told who punished them; player identifiers were randomly reassigned each round, making punishment anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability to real-world environments.
- The study only examines negative and zero MPCR conditions; it does not include standard (efficient) PGGs for direct comparison within the same experiment.
- The design does not allow for communication or endogenous norm formation beyond the imposed structure.
- The study cannot fully distinguish between social norms and moral commitments as drivers of punishment behavior.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is only tested for a specific punishment cost/tech ratio (1:3) and group size (n=4).
