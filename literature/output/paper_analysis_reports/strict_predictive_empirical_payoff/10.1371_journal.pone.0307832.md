# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Learning from regulatory failure: How Ostrom's restorative justice design principle helps naïve groups create wiser enforcement systems to overcome the tragedy of the commons
Authors: DeCaro, D. A., DeCaro, M. S., Janssen, M. A., Lee, A., Graci, A., & Flener, D.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- net tokens harvested (group and individual)
- group earnings
- resource sustainability
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation (resource conservation)
- creation and type of enforcement systems
- conceptual understanding of enforcement
- perceived legitimacy and restorative justice
- democratic decision-making index
- enforcement goals (e.g., desire to punish, prevent defection, gain control)
- frequency and type of sanctions
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is net tokens harvested (group payoff/efficiency). Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral and attitudinal measures, but the main analysis and treatment effects are on group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Facilitated groups (those guided to discuss enforcement using restorative justice principles) achieved higher group efficiency (net tokens harvested) than unfacilitated groups. The highest efficiency was observed in groups that first experienced regulatory abuse (punishment without communication) and then received facilitation (PFN treatment). Groups that experienced regulatory failure but did not receive facilitation often failed to develop effective enforcement systems and had lower efficiency. The presence of coordinated punishment systems (as opposed to independent or no punishment) was associated with the highest group efficiency. The study demonstrates that both the structure of punishment opportunities and the process of group learning and facilitation are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-like games depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the context in which it is introduced (e.g., prior experience with regulatory failure/abuse) and whether groups receive facilitation to discuss enforcement principles. In prediction tasks, the presence of facilitated discussion (restorative justice), the opportunity for communication, and the structure of the punishment system (cost, effectiveness, coordination) should be considered as moderators. The control efficiency (without punishment) is lower than the treatment efficiency (with punishment and facilitation), especially when groups experience regulatory abuse and are then guided to reflect and coordinate. The findings suggest that punishment can increase efficiency, but only under certain institutional and learning conditions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 9 rounds, divided into 3 phases of 3 rounds each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) was allowed in some phases/treatments and not others. Communication enabled higher efficiency, especially when combined with facilitation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Resource collection was continuous (players could collect any number of tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: $0.02 (1 token) to assign a penalty.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: $0.04 (2 tokens) deducted from target per $0.02 spent by punisher (2:1 impact/cost ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the number of rounds/phases in the experiment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players could see in-round total harvests for each player; sanctions were visible to all.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishments were assigned by player number (1-4), so identities were not personal but actions were attributable to player IDs.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used only undergraduate students from a single US university, limiting generalizability to other populations.
- The game is a spatial common-pool resource (CPR) foraging task, not a standard linear PGG, so some design dimensions (e.g., mpcr) do not map directly.
- The study did not include a reward mechanism, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to depend on facilitation and prior experience with regulatory failure, which may not be present in all real-world or experimental settings.
- Cultural and national context may affect learning and enforcement preferences, which were not varied in this study.
