## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: homogeneous groups, groups with one high-valuation member (valuation heterogeneity), and groups with one high-capability member (capability heterogeneity). Each group plays 20 rounds, with punishment enabled in rounds 11-20. Efficiency (group payoff as % of social optimum) is measured with and without punishment. In homogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly change efficiency. In valuation-heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase efficiency and increases inequality. In capability-heterogeneous groups, punishment significantly increases efficiency (from 86% to 93% of optimum) and reduces inequality. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus conditional on the type of heterogeneity, being most effective when group members differ in capability but not in valuation.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform but depends on the type of group heterogeneity. If the game is homogeneous or has only capability heterogeneity (where some players' contributions are more productive but all value the public good equally), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially in the capability-heterogeneous case. If the group has valuation heterogeneity (where some players value the public good more), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency and increases inequality. The control efficiency (without punishment) is also higher in capability-heterogeneous groups than in valuation-heterogeneous groups. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, the type of heterogeneity (capability vs. valuation) is a critical moderator. Other design features (e.g., punishment cost, group size, feedback) are held constant in this study.

- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses any amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR varies by player type and treatment: for l-types, 0.5; for h-types, 1.5 (either via valuation or capability).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds (20), but there is a surprise restart after 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects receive feedback about individual contributions and payoffs of each other group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the total number of punishment points received from each group member, so punishment is individually identified.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only three-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- Only two types of heterogeneity (capability and valuation) are studied; other forms of heterogeneity are not addressed.
- The lab setting may not capture all relevant real-world factors influencing cooperation and punishment.
- The surprise restart after 10 rounds may affect endgame behavior.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results pertain only to punishment.
