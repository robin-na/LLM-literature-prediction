# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Defending public goods and common-pool resources
Authors: De Geest, L. R., & Stranlund, J. K.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group profit
- average surplus
- average surplus net of theft
- average surplus loss to outsider theft
- final payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation index
- sanctioning behavior (extensive and intensive margin)
- variation in cooperation
- outsider theft behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group profit, surplus, and surplus net of theft (all efficiency/payoff outcomes). The paper also reports on cooperation/contribution rates and sanctioning behavior, but these are secondary to the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a 2x2 lab experiment (PG/CPR x Theft/No Theft), groups of four insiders played 15 rounds, with or without two outsiders who could steal surplus. Insiders could use costly sanctions (1:3 cost:impact) against outsiders. The main findings are: (a) In the CPR setting, the threat of theft increased cooperation and surplus creation, leading to higher group profits and surplus net of theft compared to the no-theft control. In the PG setting, the threat of theft did not significantly affect cooperation or surplus creation, and group profits decreased. (b) Sanctions were used more often in the PG setting, but were not effective at deterring outsider theft in either setting. (c) The presence of punishment did not restore efficiency to the fully cooperative benchmark, as insiders could not coordinate sanctions well enough to fully deter outsiders. (d) The introduction of theft increased variation in group outcomes, especially in the PG setting. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is mixed: it does not reliably increase efficiency, and its effectiveness depends on the game structure and the ability of insiders to coordinate.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment (sanctions) on efficiency in public-goods-like environments with an external threat (outsider theft). The design allows for direct comparison of efficiency (group profit, surplus net of theft) with and without punishment enabled, under known game parameters. The results show that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency: in the CPR setting, the threat of theft (with punishment available) increases surplus creation and efficiency, but in the PG setting, efficiency decreases. In both cases, punishment is not effective at fully deterring theft, and group variation increases. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is contingent on the type of social dilemma (PG vs CPR), the presence of an external threat, and the group's ability to coordinate sanctions. Control efficiency (no-theft, no-punishment) is higher than treatment efficiency (theft, punishment) in the PG setting, but lower in the CPR setting. The paper is highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in environments with external threats and endogenous punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 insiders (players) in all treatments; in theft treatments, there are also 2 outsiders, but the core public goods/CPR game is among 4 insiders.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; lack of explicit coordination mechanisms is noted as a limitation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/harvest decisions are continuous, not all-or-nothing; players choose how much to invest.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is determined by the nonlinear payoff function: V(G) = aG - bG^2, with a=6, b=0.025, n=4. The effective MPCR varies with group investment, but the design is based on standard PG/CPR parameters.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:3 (for every 1 unit spent, the target loses 3 units), following Gächter and Fehr (2000).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment technology is 1:3 (cost:impact). Insiders can reduce outsider payoffs by up to 3 times their own cost.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only costly punishment (sanctions) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the total number of rounds (15) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown group and individual payoffs, and outsiders are shown the value of the insiders' aggregate investment. Insiders are shown outsiders' decisions and payoffs before sanctioning.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Insiders can assign sanctions to specific outsiders; identification is by role/ID within the group, but not by real name.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit coordination mechanisms (e.g., communication, voting, or feedback on others' sanction decisions) were allowed, which may limit the effectiveness of punishment.
- The experiment uses a specific nonlinear payoff structure and a fixed group size, which may limit generalizability to other PG/CPR parameterizations.
- The punishment mechanism is limited to costly sanctions against outsiders; no positive rewards or endogenous institution formation.
- The external threat (outsider theft) is artificial and constrained (outsiders can take at most 50% of surplus), which may not fully capture real-world dynamics.
- Variation in group outcomes is high, especially in the presence of theft, suggesting that average effects may mask substantial heterogeneity.
