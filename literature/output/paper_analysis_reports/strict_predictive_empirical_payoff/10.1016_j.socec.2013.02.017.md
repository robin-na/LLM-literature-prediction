The main dependent variables are efficiency (average earnings as a percent of the Pareto optimal), average earnings, and group contributions. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution rates, expulsion, and punishment are also reported and analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a one-time punishment opportunity after a one-time expulsion vote in a 15-round public goods game increases contributions before the punishment phase, but does not improve overall efficiency or average earnings compared to expulsion alone. The threat of punishment raises the standard for avoiding expulsion, leading to more expulsions, including of moderate contributors. The costs associated with punishment (both direct and through increased expulsions) offset the gains from higher contributions, resulting in nearly identical efficiency between the two treatments. Thus, in this design, adding a costly punishment mechanism to a single expulsion opportunity does not yield net efficiency gains.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with a single expulsion opportunity, adding a one-time, costly punishment mechanism increases contributions but does not increase efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, under similar game parameters (large group, 15 rounds, MPCR 0.2, costly punishment, no communication), the efficiency with punishment enabled will be very similar to the efficiency with punishment disabled, as the costs of punishment offset the increased contributions. The findings are most applicable to settings with infrequent, costly punishment and expulsion opportunities, and may not generalize to games with frequent or low-cost punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 16 players; all treatments used this group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat was allowed; this is standard and referenced in the literature review.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: subjects could allocate any amount of their endowment to the group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.2 (each E$1 contributed returns E$0.2 to each of 16 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers pay E$0.25 per successful punishment vote; punished subjects lose E$40 if punished.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each successful punishment vote (majority) results in E$40 loss to the punished subject, at a cost of E$0.25 per punisher.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were informed of the total number of rounds (15) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects saw others' allocations, earnings, and average contributions for the relevant periods.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects saw the number of votes cast against them but not the identities of voters; punishment was not anonymous in terms of being targeted, but the identity of punishers was not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment and expulsion opportunities were one-time only, not repeated or frequent as in some other studies.
- Punishment was costly and only available after round 10, which may limit generalizability to settings with more frequent or less costly punishment.
- The subject pool was from a single regional university, which may differ from other populations.
- No communication or chat was allowed, which may affect external validity for real-world groups.
- No reward mechanism was present, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
