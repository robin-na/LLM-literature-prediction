The main dependent variables are net earnings (payoff), relative payoff loss/gain, and group performance, with detailed analysis of contribution and punishment behavior. Efficiency is measured as net earnings relative to the maximum possible (full cooperation, no punishment cost).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment compares three peer punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game: Baseline (standard, individual punishment), Sequential (punishment with information about others' punishment actions), and Consensual (punishment only enacted if at least two group members request it). All treatments use groups of 5, 20 rounds (10 without punishment, 10 with), no communication, and full information about group contributions and payoffs (but not identities). The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment costs 1 token per point and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) Punishment opportunities increase contributions in all treatments, but only the Consensual institution increases net earnings (efficiency) above the no-punishment baseline. In Baseline and Sequential, punishment reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. (2) The Consensual institution achieves higher cooperation and efficiency with less punishment, as it censors anti-social punishment (punishment of high contributors) and requires consensus to punish. (3) Providing more information about others' punishment actions (Sequential) does not improve coordination or efficiency. (4) The form of the punishment institution is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the institutional details of the punishment mechanism. In standard and sequential punishment settings (where any individual can punish unilaterally), punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. In contrast, a consensual punishment rule (requiring at least two agents to agree to punish) both increases contributions and improves efficiency, even surpassing the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be inferred from control efficiency and game structure alone; the specific punishment institution (especially whether it censors anti-social punishment and requires consensus) is a key moderator. The paper also shows that increasing information about others' punishment actions does not necessarily improve efficiency. Thus, for games with similar structure, expect punishment to reduce efficiency unless the institution filters out anti-social punishment and requires consensus.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 players; 20 participants per session, randomly assigned to groups of 5 each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per session: 10 without punishment, 10 with punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication among subjects is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject chooses any amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 token per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism does not exist; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told at the start that the experiment has two parts and are informed of the rules for each part as it begins; the number of rounds is known for each part.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed about the total contribution to the project as well as contributions and earnings of every member in their group (but not identities).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; subjects see the amount of punishment received but not who assigned it.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted with university students in a laboratory setting, which may limit generalizability to real-world groups.
- The study only examines three specific punishment institutions; other forms of punishment or hybrid institutions are not tested.
- The game uses random re-matching each round, which may reduce the impact of reputation or repeated interactions compared to fixed groups.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The analysis focuses on short-term (10 rounds with punishment) effects; longer-term dynamics are not explored.
