Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (group earnings, net profit, mean earnings, fraction of net earnings) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, punishment strategies, frequencies). Analyses of efficiency and group success are present, especially in the cross-society reanalysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in both treatments; this is standard across all reported experiments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each phase (punishment and no-punishment) consists of 6 rounds; total 12 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: any amount between 0 and 20 MUs can be contributed.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each MU contributed yields 0.4 MU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point assigned costs the punisher 1 MU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 MUs (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of periods (rounds) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see the contributions of all other group members and the total group contribution.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants are not informed who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which may be influenced by unobserved cultural or contextual factors.
- The experimental design uses relatively short games (6 rounds per phase), which may limit generalizability to longer or repeated interactions.
- The findings are based on lab experiments with student samples and may not generalize to field settings.
- No reward mechanism is included, so results may not extend to environments with both punishment and reward.
- The analysis of cross-society differences is correlational and cannot establish causality for cultural moderators.
