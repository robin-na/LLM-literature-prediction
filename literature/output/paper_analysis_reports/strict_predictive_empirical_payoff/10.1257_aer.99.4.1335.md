# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Institution Formation in Public Goods Games
Authors: Kosfeld, M., Okada, A., & Riedl, A.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group efficiency (ratio of observed group earnings to welfare maximum)
- average group earnings
- average contributions to the public good
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and size of implemented organizations
- participation rates in institution formation
- beliefs about others' participation
- implementation rates by organization size
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (as defined by group earnings relative to the welfare maximum) and average contributions. Non-payoff outcomes such as participation, beliefs, and organization size are also reported, but efficiency is the primary focus for the prediction task.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when groups can endogenously form a centralized sanctioning institution (which punishes non-contributors among its members), they do so in a large majority of cases, and most often as a 'grand organization' including all players. This leads to much higher and more stable contributions and efficiency compared to control groups without the institution option. In the lower MPCR treatment (a=0.4), efficiency rises from 25% (control) to 51% (institution formation); in the higher MPCR treatment (a=0.65), efficiency rises from 62% (control) to 70% (institution formation), though the latter difference is not statistically significant. The institution is only implemented if all players join, reflecting strong fairness concerns. When institution formation fails, efficiency is not lower than in the control. The results show that endogenous punishment institutions can solve the public goods dilemma and raise efficiency, but their success depends on fairness-driven willingness to include all group members.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling centralized punishment institutions in linear public goods games. When the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling institution formation can more than double efficiency, especially at lower MPCR. The effect is robust to group size (n=4), number of rounds (20), and the specific centralized punishment structure used here. The main moderator is the MPCR: the efficiency gain is larger when MPCR is lower. The institution is only effective if all players join, so settings that facilitate or require universal participation are more likely to see large efficiency gains. The cost of the institution (here, 2 points per round shared among members) is low relative to the efficiency gains. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment options.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experimental groups had 4 players (n=4).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; standard lab PGG protocol implies no chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous for nonmembers, but for organization members, contribution is forced to full endowment (all-or-nothing) if the institution is implemented.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Two MPCRs tested: a=0.4 (IF40/PG40) and a=0.65 (IF65/PG65). Efficiency gains from institution formation are larger at lower MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost of the organization is 2 points per round, shared equally among members. No per-punishment cost; punishment is automatic and centralized.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized and automatic: any organization member who does not contribute full endowment is punished by losing the amount not contributed (i.e., full deduction). Nonmembers are not punished.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 20 rounds (fixed and known).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the number of participants and the size of the implemented organization; likely also about group contributions, as is standard in PGGs, though not explicitly detailed.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use a fixed group size of 4; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Punishment is centralized and automatic, not peer-to-peer; findings may not apply to decentralized punishment settings.
- Institution formation requires unanimous agreement and is only effective if all players join; settings with partial or forced participation may yield different results.
- The cost of the institution is low and fixed; higher costs or different cost structures may affect willingness to form institutions and efficiency gains.
- No communication or chat is allowed; real-world settings with communication may differ.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects; external validity to field or policy settings is not established.
