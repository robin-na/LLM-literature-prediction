# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: PROMOTING COOPERATION IN A PUBLIC GOOD EXPERIMENT THROUGH OSTRACISM AS A PUNISHMENT MECHANISM
Authors: JARUNGRATTANAPONG, R.
Journal: Singapore Economic Review
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per participant
- group welfare (total payoffs)
- efficiency (implied by average earnings relative to maximum possible)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- voting/ostracism frequency
- punishment/anti-social punishment behavior
- cooperation rate
- types of contributors (conditional cooperator, free-rider, etc.)
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both contribution rates and average earnings/payoffs. Efficiency is discussed in terms of average earnings and welfare, with explicit comparison between ostracism (punishment) and control (no punishment) conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that allowing group members to ostracize (temporarily exclude) others via majority vote in a repeated public goods game increases average contributions by about 45 percentage points and increases average earnings (welfare) by about 23% compared to the control (no punishment) game. The effect is strongest for free-riders but is present for all contributor types. Ostracism is implemented with a small direct cost (1% of endowment per vote), but the main cost is the reduction in group size and thus potential public good returns. Anti-social punishment is observed: non-cooperators who are punished tend to retaliate by voting against others, but this is not seen among high contributors. The positive effect on efficiency is robust, but cooperation and efficiency decline when ostracism is removed (end-game effect).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling ostracism (a form of social punishment) in a repeated public goods game with 4 players, 6 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.625 increases both contribution rates and average group earnings (efficiency) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is large and robust across contributor types. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar lab PGGs. The main caveat is the presence of anti-social punishment and the collapse of cooperation when punishment is removed, suggesting that the positive effect is contingent on the continued availability of the punishment mechanism.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each repeated game (SPG and OPG) lasted 6 rounds; total number of rounds was known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication among participants was not allowed at any time.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: participants could contribute any integer from 0 to 10 baht.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR was 0.625 (each baht contributed yielded 0.625 baht per player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to vote to ostracize was 1% of endowment (0.10 baht) per vote.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ostracism required majority vote; ostracized members were excluded for 2 rounds, received no endowment or public good returns, and could not vote during exclusion.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment (ostracism) was implemented.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of periods in each experiment was known from the start to all participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants were informed about other group members' contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of votes against each member, but not the identity of voters; punishment was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is ostracism (temporary exclusion), not monetary punishment; results may not generalize to other punishment forms.
- Anti-social punishment (revenge voting) is observed, which may undermine long-term cooperation.
- Cooperation and efficiency gains collapse when ostracism is removed (end-game effect), indicating the effect is not persistent without ongoing punishment.
- The experiment uses Thai university students; results may not generalize to other populations or cultures.
- Group size is fixed at 4; effects may differ with larger or smaller groups.
- The exclusion period is temporary (2 rounds); permanent exclusion may yield different results.
