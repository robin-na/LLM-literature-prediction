## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study introduces a 'power transfer game'—a repeated public goods game with punishment, where group members can voluntarily transfer their punishment power to others. In groups of five, three conditions were compared: (1) endogenous (voluntary) power transfer, (2) exogenous (mirrored, but not voluntary) power transfer, and (3) fixed (no transfer, standard peer punishment). Only the endogenous condition, where group members could choose whom to empower, led to sustained or increasing cooperation and higher group earnings, approaching the social optimum. In the fixed and exogenous conditions, cooperation and group earnings declined over time. The mechanism works because non-punishing cooperators delegate power to those willing to punish, and centralisation of power in the hands of pro-social punishers deters free riding. The study provides direct evidence that voluntary centralisation of punishment power can efficiently sustain cooperation and group welfare in public goods dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with peer punishment, allowing voluntary centralisation of punishment power (i.e., enabling group members to transfer their punishment effectiveness to others) substantially increases group efficiency compared to standard decentralised peer punishment. The effect is not observed when centralisation is imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a voluntary power transfer mechanism is a key moderator: with it, efficiency is much higher than in standard peer punishment, even when the underlying MPCR, punishment cost, and other parameters are held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in games with and without voluntary centralisation of punishment power.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is described; only information about contributions, punishment, and power transfers is provided.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose how much of 20 MUs to contribute (0-20).
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. In each round, the previous round's power transfer decisions serve as the default, but contribution choices are not described as having a default; participants must actively choose their contribution each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.3 (total contribution multiplied by 1.5, divided by 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 MU per punishment point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per point is equal to the punisher's power (initially 1, can be increased by transfers). Voluntary centralisation increases punishment effectiveness for selected individuals, which is key to sustaining cooperation and efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the structure and number of rounds; instructions were given at the start of each new stage.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, participants are informed about each group member's contributions, power, and punishment assigned/received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not anonymous; participants see who punished whom. Each group member has a unique symbol for tracking behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world or field contexts.
- The power transfer mechanism is novel and may not map directly onto existing real-world institutions or informal power structures.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- Participants could not refuse power transfers, which may differ from real-world settings where individuals can reject responsibility.
- The study does not vary group size, MPCR, or punishment cost across conditions, so findings are specific to the parameter values used.
