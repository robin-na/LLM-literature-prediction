# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Endogenous and costly institutional deterrence in a public good experiment
Authors: Kingsley, D. C., & Brown, T. C.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- average group contributions (as a proxy for efficiency, since full contribution is the social optimum)
- marginal per capita return (MPCR) based payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- voting behavior for monitoring levels
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings and contributions. Contributions are interpreted in the context of efficiency, as the social optimum is full contribution. Earnings are directly reported and compared across treatments and conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a linear public goods game with a central authority that can monitor and punish free-riders, increasing the probability of monitoring (deterrence) increases contributions up to a point, but the benefit to group earnings depends on the cost of monitoring. When monitoring is cheap or moderate, higher deterrence increases both contributions and efficiency (group earnings as a share of the social optimum). When monitoring is expensive, the cost can outweigh the benefit, and efficiency does not improve or may decline. Allowing groups to endogenously select the level of monitoring does not increase contributions or efficiency at non-deterrent (low monitoring) levels, contrary to some previous literature. In fact, endogenous selection of non-deterrent institutions sometimes reduces contributions. The relationship between monitoring, punishment, and efficiency is thus strongly moderated by the costliness of institutional enforcement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-enabled institutions on efficiency in public goods games, especially when the punishment is implemented via a central authority with costly monitoring. The results show that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on both the probability of monitoring (deterrence) and the cost of monitoring. At low and moderate costs, enabling punishment increases efficiency, but at high costs, the efficiency gain is negated or reversed. The endogenous selection of non-deterrent institutions does not provide an efficiency boost. For prediction, the key moderators are the cost of monitoring (punishment_cost), the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment_tech), and whether the institution is deterrent (i.e., the expected cost of free-riding exceeds the benefit). Control efficiency (without punishment) is much lower than with effective, affordable punishment, but high punishment costs can erase the efficiency gain.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 subjects were used in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 25 periods per session, divided into 3 phases (3, 12, and 10 rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how much of their endowment to contribute (0-20 EDs).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Cost of monitoring (punishment_cost) is manipulated across treatments: m = 2, 5, or 8 EDs per monitored subject. Higher costs reduce the efficiency benefit of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented by a central authority: if monitored, the subject loses all EDs not contributed (s=1). Probability of monitoring (z/n) is varied, affecting deterrence. Higher probability increases contributions and, at low cost, efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of periods in each phase; the total number of periods is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects see aggregate group contribution, individual contributions by random ID, and their own and group earnings.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment only allows manipulation of the probability of monitoring, not the severity of the sanction.
- Monitoring is stochastic and costly, which may differ from real-world or other experimental settings where monitoring is certain or costless.
- The design does not include peer-to-peer punishment or reward mechanisms.
- Endogenous selection is limited to the level of monitoring; the sanction level is fixed.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with different group sizes, MPCRs, or institutional structures.
- The effect of endogenous selection may be confounded with learning or order effects, as acknowledged by the authors.
