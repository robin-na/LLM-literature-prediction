## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three treatments: (1) standard public good game (VCM, no punishment), (2) public good game with one-sided punishment (P), and (3) public good game with two-sided punishment/counter-punishment (PCP). In the P treatment, punishment increases contributions but not enough to offset the cost of punishment, so average earnings are lower than in the control. In the PCP treatment, the threat of counter-punishment reduces the willingness to punish free riders, leading to lower contributions and even lower earnings than in both the control and the P treatment. The presence of counter-punishment opportunities causes a breakdown of cooperation and group welfare. The results are robust to matching protocol (fixed vs random) and sequencing effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard linear public goods games with 4 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and standard punishment technology, enabling punishment reduces efficiency compared to the control (no punishment), and enabling counter-punishment reduces efficiency even further. The effect is robust across matching protocols. For prediction, if the control efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, and enabling counter-punishment will reduce it further. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the punishment cost and technology, which can inform predictions for similar designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per game, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment lasts 10 periods (rounds); each session has two treatments for a total of 20 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players; all decisions made simultaneously and without communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0 ≤ c_i ≤ 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each player gets 40% of the sum of group contributions).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is convex: 1 point costs 1 ECU, 2 points cost 2 ECU, 3 points cost 4 ECU, ..., up to 10 points costing 30 ECU (see Table 2).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's income by 10% of their first-stage income, up to a maximum of 100%.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment and counter-punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of periods (10 per treatment) in advance; game is finitely repeated.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed of their own and group total contributions, their income from the project, and their total income for the period. In punishment treatments, they are also informed of punishment points assigned/received and associated costs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the number of points each other group member assigned to them in the punishment and counter-punishment stages, but identities are anonymized by random assignment of 'names' (numbers 1-4) each period.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world environments.
- The punishment and counter-punishment mechanisms are highly structured and may not capture all forms of retaliation or norm enforcement found in natural settings.
- No communication or reward mechanisms are allowed, which may affect the external validity of the findings.
- The design restricts counter-punishment to only those who were punished in the previous stage and only towards their punishers, which may overestimate the negative effect of counter-punishment.
- The group size is fixed at 4, and the MPCR is fixed at 0.4, so results may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- The study does not allow for the punishment of non-punishers (second-order punishment), which could affect the sustainability of cooperation.
