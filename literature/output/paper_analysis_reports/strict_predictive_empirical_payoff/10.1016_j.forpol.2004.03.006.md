# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Norms from outside and from inside: an experimental analysis on the governance of local ecosystems
Authors: Cardenas, J. C.
Journal: Forest Policy and Economics
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate extraction (inverse of efficiency)
- group earnings
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual extraction choices
- frequency of extraction at social optimum
- distribution of extraction choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (payoff), and aggregate extraction (lower extraction = higher efficiency). Non-payoff outcomes such as extraction choices and their distribution are also reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing external regulation (punishment for over-extraction, with imperfect monitoring) or allowing face-to-face communication both lead to substantial increases in efficiency (lower extraction, higher group earnings) compared to a baseline with no intervention. The difference between high and low penalty treatments was much smaller than predicted by standard theory, with both leading to similar improvements in efficiency. A large fraction of participants complied with the social optimum extraction even when the expected cost of punishment was low, indicating that norm compliance and the salience of the rule were important. Communication (self-governance) was as effective as external punishment in increasing efficiency. The results suggest that both material and non-material incentives (norms, communication) can be effective in improving group outcomes in commons dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that both punishment (external regulation with fines and monitoring) and communication (self-governance) can substantially increase efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, even when the expected cost of punishment is low. The effect of increasing the penalty size (punishment_tech) was much smaller than predicted, suggesting diminishing returns to harsher punishment. The game design dimensions (player_count=5, num_rounds=20, no chat in baseline, chat in self-governance, continuous extraction, known payoff table, monitoring probability, and fine size) are all well specified, making this paper highly informative for predicting efficiency under different punishment regimes. Control efficiency (no punishment, no communication) is lower than in the treatments, and both punishment and communication increase efficiency to similar levels.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions used groups of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session, divided into two stages of 10 rounds each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Face-to-face communication allowed in the self-governance treatment (stage 2), not allowed in baseline or punishment treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Extraction choice is continuous: players choose any integer between 1 and 8 units.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is embedded in the payoff function; parameters a=60, b=5, alpha=20, n=5. The social optimum is at minimum extraction (1 unit), Nash at maximum (8 units).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is externally imposed: fine of 50 or 175 per unit above the social optimum, with 1/5 chance of being monitored. Cost is to the punished, not the punisher (no peer punishment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness varied by fine size (50 vs 175 per unit), but the increase in efficiency from higher fines was much smaller than predicted.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the number of rounds in each stage (10 rounds per stage), but were not told about stage 2 until after stage 1 ended.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, the total group extraction was publicly announced; individual choices remained private.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is externally imposed by the experimenter, not peer punishment; results may not generalize to peer-sanctioning settings.
- The monitoring probability is low (1/5), and only one player is checked per round; real-world monitoring may differ.
- The experiment was conducted with villagers in Colombia, which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- Communication treatment involved face-to-face discussion, which may not be feasible in all real-world settings.
- The study does not report detailed statistical tests of efficiency (payoff) differences between all treatments, though extraction and earnings are clearly reported.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so results do not inform about reward-based interventions.
