# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons
Authors: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S.
Journal: Nature Human Behaviour
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- effective size of the public good
- group earnings (money units, MU)
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/withdrawal decisions
- conditional cooperation (reciprocity)
- punishment assigned
- attitudes and beliefs about cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the effective size of the public good after contribution or withdrawal decisions, which is a direct measure of group efficiency. Individual and group earnings are also reported. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures (contributions, punishment) and psychological measures (attitudes, beliefs).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Repeated games use 27 rounds; one-shot games use a single round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/withdrawal choices are continuous from 0 to 20 tokens, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each token in the public good is worth 0.4 MU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 1 MU per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 3 MU (punishment impact per unit cost = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told how many rounds the experiment would last (unknown endpoint).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, participants received aggregate feedback on choices and outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments are conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world or field settings.
- The punishment mechanism is fixed (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU); results may differ with other punishment technologies.
- No communication or reward mechanisms are included, so findings may not generalize to environments with these features.
- The number of players (4) and rounds (27) are fixed; effects may differ with other group sizes or time horizons.
- Participants do not know the number of rounds in advance, which may affect strategic behavior compared to games with known endpoints.
- The study focuses on linear public goods games; results may not generalize to threshold or nonlinear public goods.
