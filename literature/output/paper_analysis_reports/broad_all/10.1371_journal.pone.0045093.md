# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation
Authors: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- comparison of payoffs between participants involved in vendettas and those not involved
- earnings of retaliators vs. non-retaliators
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rates
- frequency and intensity of punishment
- frequency and duration of vendettas
- retaliation rates
- timing of punishment (e.g., delayed revenge)
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, vendetta frequency) and payoff-related outcomes (earnings, losses from vendettas). Efficiency as a normalized group payoff is not directly reported, but group and individual earnings are compared, especially in relation to vendetta involvement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. PGG groups had 4 players; PD dyads had 2 players. The group environment (4 players) saw frequent punishment and vendettas, while dyads saw little punishment or vendetta activity.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each period had 1 contribution round followed by 5 punishment rounds. Multiple periods were played (PG: 3 periods; PD: 5 periods). Multiple punishment rounds enabled vendettas.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was all-or-nothing: participants decided whether or not to contribute 1.00€.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (sum of contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided by 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In PGG: Each punishment point cost 0.10€ to assign (e.g., 3 units = 0.30€), and imposed a 3x fine (0.30€ cost, 0.90€ fine). In PD: Each punishment cost 0.50€ and imposed a 1.50€ fine.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each unit of cost imposes 3 units of fine).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not informed about the number of PG/PD rounds nor the number of punishment rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution round, participants were informed about the investments of all subgroup members. After each punishment round, they knew who punished whom and by how much.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punisher identity was always revealed; participants knew exactly who punished whom and by how much.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not reported as a normalized group payoff; only individual and group earnings are compared.
- The study uses all-or-nothing contribution choices, which may limit generalizability to continuous-contribution PGGs.
- No control condition without punishment is included, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline is inferred rather than directly measured.
- The number of rounds and punishment opportunities is fixed and not announced to participants, which may affect strategic behavior.
- The sample consists of first-semester biology students from German and Austrian universities, which may limit external validity.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present, so findings are specific to punishment-only environments.
