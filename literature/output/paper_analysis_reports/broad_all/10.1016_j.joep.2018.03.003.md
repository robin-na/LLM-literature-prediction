# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Third-party punishment: Retribution or deterrence?
Authors: Tan, F. F., & Xiao, E.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment approval rate
- punishment magnitude
- cooperation rate
- punishment conditional on proposer/recipient actions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment approval rates and magnitudes (i.e., how often and how much third parties approve punishment proposals), and cooperation rates in the prisoner's dilemma game. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes. Some discussion of how punishment changes the incentive structure of the game is present, but no direct efficiency or group payoff analysis is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that group third parties are more likely to approve punishment for deterrence purposes (when their decisions are revealed before the PD game), while individual third parties' punishment decisions are not sensitive to whether punishment can deter. Groups are less likely than individuals to approve punishment overall, but the magnitude of punishment (when approved) is similar. Despite the presence of third-party punishment, cooperation rates in the PD game remain low across all treatments, and punishment is not sufficient to change the game into one where cooperation is the dominant strategy for most participants. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the behavioral results suggest that the punishment institution as implemented does not substantially increase cooperation or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about the behavioral effects of third-party punishment in a one-shot PD game, especially regarding the motives for punishment and the difference between group and individual third parties. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The findings suggest that punishment (even when costless to the third party) is not always used to maximize cooperation or group welfare, and that group decision-making may lead to more deterrence-driven but less frequent punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of third-party punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or cooperation, especially if punishment is not strong enough to change incentives. The design is adjacent to PGGs but not a direct variant.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PD game involves 2 players (A and B), with a third party (individual or group of 3) making punishment decisions. The main game is a 2-player PD, but the third party is either 1 or 3 people.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot; each participant plays a single PD game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group third parties (groups of 3) can communicate via electronic chat to reach a joint decision. No chat for individual third parties or for PD game players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. PD game is binary: each player chooses to cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing choice).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff table is provided: mutual cooperation yields 30 each, mutual defection 20 each, unilateral defection yields 40 for defector and 15 for cooperator. This is a PD, not a PGG, so MPCR is not directly defined, but the payoff structure is clear.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless for the third party; the cost (5 points) is paid by the punishment proposer (Person A or B), not the third party.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party can assign up to 40 tokens punishment (equal to the maximum possible earnings of a defector). The punishment does not benefit any player; it is a pure deduction.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot; all players know this.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe their partner's choice before deciding whether to propose punishment. Third parties see the choices of A and B before making punishment decisions.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot prisoner's dilemma game, not a repeated or standard public goods game, so results may not generalize to multi-round or PGG settings.
- The main outcomes are punishment approval rates, magnitudes, and cooperation rates, not efficiency or group payoff.
- Punishment is costless for the third party, which is not typical of most real-world or experimental punishment institutions.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, welfare, or total group earnings.
- The study does not manipulate or report on several PGG-relevant dimensions, such as marginal per-capita return (MPCR) in a public goods context, or the presence of reward mechanisms.
