# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The carrot or the stick: Rewards, punishments, and cooperation
Authors: Andreoni, J., Harbaugh, W., & Vesterlund, L.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average offer (amount shared by proposer)
- distribution of offers
- final payoff distributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and magnitude of punishment and reward actions
- proportion of responders who punish or reward
- conditional punishment/reward rates
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the proposer's offer (amount shared), which is a behavioral measure of cooperation, not direct group efficiency or total welfare. Final payoff distributions are reported, but efficiency (as a ratio to maximum possible group payoff) is not explicitly calculated. The game is a two-person proposer-responder game, not a standard public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a two-person proposer-responder game, the ability to punish (reduce the proposer's payoff at a cost) increases the amount proposers share with responders, compared to a baseline with no punishment or reward. The ability to reward (increase the proposer's payoff at a cost) alone has little effect on the modal offer, though it increases variance and leads a minority to be very generous. When both punishment and reward are available, proposers make the most generous offers, and the distribution of offers first-order stochastically dominates the other treatments. Punishment and reward act as complements: punishment moves people away from selfishness, and reward encourages further cooperation. The study does not use a standard public goods game, but the findings are relevant for understanding how punishment and reward options affect cooperative behavior and, by implication, group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-person proposer-responder environment, enabling punishment increases cooperative behavior (higher offers), and that combining punishment and reward is even more effective. While the game is not a standard public goods game, the findings suggest that, for similar environments, adding punishment (and especially both punishment and reward) will increase efficiency relative to a baseline with neither. The effect of reward alone is weaker. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, one-shot or randomly rematched games with costly punishment and reward options. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that punishment (especially when combined with reward) will increase efficiency or group payoff, even if the effect of reward alone is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (proposer and responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 10 rounds, with random rematching and no repeated pairs.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposer chooses any amount to share between 40 cents and 240 cents (continuous choice, not all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 cent to the responder to reduce the proposer's payoff by 5 cents.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 cent spent reduces proposer's payoff by 5 cents (5:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is available in Carrot and Carrot-Stick treatments; reward alone has little effect, but reward plus punishment has a strong positive effect.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward costs 1 cent to the responder to increase the proposer's payoff by 5 cents.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 cent spent increases proposer's payoff by 5 cents (5:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 10 rounds; no repeated pairs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Identities are never revealed; all interactions are anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a two-person proposer-responder game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-person or standard PGG settings.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as a ratio to maximum possible group payoff; main outcome is the proposer's offer (cooperation), not explicit group welfare.
- All participants are undergraduate business students, which may limit generalizability.
- The punishment and reward mechanisms are highly stylized (fixed cost and impact), which may differ from real-world or other experimental settings.
- No communication or reputation effects are present; all interactions are one-shot with random rematching.
