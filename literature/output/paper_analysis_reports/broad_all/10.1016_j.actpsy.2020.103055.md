# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How intergroup relation moderates group bias in Third-Party Punishment
Authors: Guo, R., Ding, J. S., & Wu, Z.
Journal: Acta Psychologica
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment size assigned by third-party observers to allocators
- group bias in punishment (difference in punishment toward ingroup vs. outgroup)
- effect of intergroup relation (competition vs. cooperation) on punishment
- effect of punishment cost on punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of punishment assigned by third-party observers to allocators in a dictator game context. No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. The study focuses on behavioral responses (punishment decisions) rather than payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that third-party observers punish selfish allocators more than fair ones, and punish outgroup members more harshly than ingroup members, demonstrating group bias in norm enforcement. Competition between groups increases punishment toward outgroup selfish allocators when the cost of punishment is low, but this effect is eliminated when the cost is high. The results support the 'mere-preference hypothesis' of group bias and show that cost-benefit analysis moderates the effect of intergroup relations on punishment behavior. However, the study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about how punishment behavior is moderated by intergroup relations and punishment cost in a third-party punishment context, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the findings suggest that punishment behavior (and thus potential effects on efficiency) may be more sensitive to intergroup competition and cost-benefit ratios, especially in settings where group identity and outgroup status are salient. However, since efficiency is not measured, the paper's value is mainly in informing behavioral mechanisms rather than direct efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 8-11 participants, but the TPP task is structured as a one-shot interaction between a third-party observer, an allocator, and a recipient (all roles except observer are simulated). The group size for the punishment decision is effectively 3 (observer, allocator, recipient), but the broader group context is larger.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant completed 16 trials (rounds) in the TPP task.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is manipulated: in Experiment 1, paying 1/3 unit deducts 1 unit from the allocator (low cost); in Experiment 2, paying 1 unit deducts 1 unit (high cost). Raising the cost eliminates the effect of competition on punishment toward outgroup selfish allocators.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is manipulated: 1/3 unit cost per 1 unit impact (Experiment 1) vs. 1 unit cost per 1 unit impact (Experiment 2). The cost-to-impact ratio moderates the effect of intergroup relation on punishment behavior.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of trials (16) in the TPP task.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; all dependent variables are behavioral (punishment assigned).
- The experimental design is based on a third-party punishment of a dictator game, not a public goods game or its direct variant.
- The findings on punishment behavior may not directly translate to efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods or other multi-player cooperation games.
- The manipulation of intergroup cooperation may have been weak, as acknowledged by the authors, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings regarding cooperation.
- All roles except the third-party observer are simulated by computer, which may affect ecological validity.
