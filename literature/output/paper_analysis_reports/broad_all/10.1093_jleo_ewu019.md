# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sustaining Group Reputation
Authors: Kimbrough, E. O., & Rubin, J.
Journal: Journal of Law Economics & Organization
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount sent (proxy for total welfare/gains from exchange)
- amount returned
- sender earnings
- receiver earnings
- total welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of sending full endowment
- probability of sending nothing
- probability of dispute filed
- probability of conviction
- message sent (pre-play communication)
- cheating frequency
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are amount sent (which directly determines total welfare in the trust game), amount returned, and earnings. The paper explicitly links amount sent to total welfare, as every ECU sent is tripled and increases group surplus. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures such as dispute rates and communication.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated trust game with random rematching and group reputation, the introduction of an in-group punishment mechanism (jury) leads to a significant increase in the amount sent by senders, which directly translates to higher total welfare/efficiency. Information sharing about group members' experiences also increases welfare, but mainly by reinforcing positive or negative group reputations (path dependence). The effects of punishment and information sharing are additive rather than superadditive or substitutive. The presence of punishment increases both the amount sent and the amount returned, with the largest gains in the treatment where both punishment and information sharing are present. The effect of punishment is primarily due to deterrence—the threat of being punished is enough to change behavior, not just actual punishment events.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in trust-game-like environments with group reputation and random rematching, enabling a punishment mechanism (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases efficiency (total welfare) relative to a control with punishment disabled. The effect is robust and additive with information sharing. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be higher than with punishment disabled, especially when punishment is feasible and not prohibitively costly. The results are most directly applicable to trust games but are relevant for adjacent public-goods-like settings where group reputation and in-group punishment are possible. The paper also provides evidence that the marginal per-capita return (here, the tripling of the amount sent) is important for welfare, and that communication (cheap talk) alone does not have a significant effect. The design dimensions most critical for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, information sharing, and the structure of the trust game.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 16 players, split into two groups of 8 (4 senders and 4 receivers per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 periods (rounds), but subjects were not told the number of periods in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Pre-play communication allowed: receivers could send a non-binding message (cheap talk) before each round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (amount sent) is continuous: sender chooses any integer amount between 0 and 10 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 3 (amount sent is tripled and given to receiver).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (jury trial) costs the sender 2 ECU to initiate. If convicted, the receiver pays the outstanding balance plus the 2 ECU cost to the sender.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective: if convicted, the receiver must pay the full amount owed plus the sender's cost. The threat of this punishment deters cheating.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were not told the number of periods in advance; the game ended before the scheduled session time to control for endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the Info treatments, subjects see detailed summaries of all other group members' transactions (amount sent, returned, messages, earnings, etc.).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is in-group: the jury consists of the three other receivers of the same color as the defendant. Group identity is known, but individual identity is not. Jury votes are anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game with group reputation, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Group identity is weakly induced (by color only), so effects of stronger group identity are not tested.
- The punishment mechanism is specific (jury of in-group peers, fixed cost, full restitution); other punishment forms may yield different results.
- Subjects were not told the number of rounds, which may affect strategies compared to games with known endpoints.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not address the effects of positive incentives.
- The sample is university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
