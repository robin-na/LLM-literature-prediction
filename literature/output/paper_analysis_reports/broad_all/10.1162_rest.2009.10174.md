Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (friend/foe choice)
- conditional cooperation by observable attributes
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the share of the jointly generated stakes that is actually paid out to players (i.e., not destroyed by mutual defection). The paper also analyzes the frequency of cooperative (friend) choices and how these vary by player and opponent attributes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study documents that, over two seasons of the game show, players shift from unconditional to conditional cooperation based on observable opponent characteristics (e.g., gender, race, age). In the first season, cooperation rates are unrelated to opponent attributes; in the second season, players are more likely to defect (choose 'foe') against opponents whose attributes predict less cooperation. This learning process leads to a substantial decline in overall efficiency: the average share of stakes converted to winnings drops from 39% in season 1 to 30% in season 2. The decline is especially pronounced for players with attributes associated with low cooperation (e.g., men, black players, younger players), whose average efficiency falls sharply. In contrast, players with more cooperative-associated attributes (e.g., women, white players) maintain their efficiency. The findings suggest that conditional cooperation, when based on imperfect signals, can reduce group efficiency in one-shot, high-stakes settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, simultaneous-move games structurally similar to the prisoner's dilemma, the emergence of conditional cooperation based on observable attributes can reduce overall efficiency, especially when players use imperfect signals to predict others' behavior. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling mechanisms that allow players to condition on opponent characteristics (e.g., through history, reputation, or observable traits) may decrease efficiency relative to baseline, particularly if the environment is not anonymous and players can stereotype. The findings are most relevant for games with binary (all-or-nothing) choices, no explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and high stakes. The results caution that learning and conditional strategies do not always improve efficiency and may exacerbate payoff inequality.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by two players (pairs).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a one-shot (single round) simultaneous-move game.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players interact in person and can talk during the show, but there is no structured chat or communication phase before the final decision.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The contribution/choice is binary: 'friend' (cooperate, split) or 'foe' (defect, take all).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is a one-shot, final decision.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players can observe each other's demographic characteristics and performance in the trivia phase, but do not see histories of past choices by others.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Choices are revealed publicly on television; no anonymity.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game but a one-shot, two-player prisoner's dilemma variant with binary choices.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are present; 'punishment' is only via mutual defection.
- Players are not randomly paired; partner selection is based on stated preferences and background information, which may bias results.
- The environment is highly non-anonymous (televised, face-to-face), which may increase fairness concerns and limit generalizability to anonymous settings.
- Conditional cooperation is based on observable demographic attributes, which may not generalize to settings where such signals are absent or less salient.
- The study is observational, not experimental; no random assignment to treatment conditions.
