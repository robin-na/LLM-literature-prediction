# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Deterrence through punishment can resolve collective risk dilemmas in carbon emission games
Authors: Jiang, L. L., Chen, Z., Perc, M., Wang, Z., Kurths, J., & Moreno, Y.
Journal: Chaos
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- failure probability (fraction of groups not reaching the target)
- average investment per group
- final earnings (implied, not directly tabulated)
- probability of achieving the collective target
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution behavior (free-rider, fair-share, altruist)
- risk perception of punishment
- deterrence (behavioral response to punishment risk)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the probability of group failure (not reaching the target), average investment, and the distribution of contribution types. Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the probability of reaching the target and average investment are closely related. Final earnings are discussed but not tabulated.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab experiment simulating a collective-risk public goods game (carbon emission game), costly punishment of free-riders (with fines imposed probabilistically and costs shared by all group members) increases the probability that groups reach the collective target, but only when the punishment risk is high (p > 0.8). At low punishment risk, deterrence is weak, and group failure rates remain high. Larger groups are less likely to succeed at any given punishment risk, requiring higher punishment risk for similar deterrence. The introduction of punishment shifts most players to fair-share contributions, reducing both free-riding and maximal altruism. The effect of punishment on group success (a proxy for efficiency) is robust to different loss probabilities. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the probability of reaching the target and average investment are closely related and show a positive effect of punishment when sufficiently strong.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in collective-risk public goods games with a threshold target and probabilistic costly punishment, enabling punishment increases group success rates (and thus efficiency) only when the punishment risk is high. The effect is moderated by group size (larger groups require higher punishment risk) and is robust to different loss probabilities. For prediction, the presence and strength of punishment (punishment risk, fine size, cost structure), group size, and the absence of communication are key moderators. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low group success rates, especially as group size increases. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is strong and credible, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or infrequent.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size (M) was varied between 2, 5, and 10. Larger groups had higher failure rates and required higher punishment risk for deterrence to be effective.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played for 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; groups were anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were discrete: 0, 1, or 2 MU per round (not strictly all-or-nothing, but not continuous).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is a threshold public goods game: group must reach a target (10 x group size over 10 rounds). No explicit MPCR, but the structure is similar to a threshold PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 MU per free-rider per round, shared equally among all group members. The fine is 3 MU per free-rider, imposed with probability p.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the fine (3 MU) and the probability of being punished (p). Only high p (p > 0.8) is effective for deterrence.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the game lasted 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw their own contribution, the group's total investment for the round, and the remaining gap to the target.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was automatic and anonymous; no identification of who punished whom. All group members shared the cost.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff; main outcome is probability of reaching the target and average investment.
- Punishment is implemented as probabilistic, automatic, and group-costly, not as peer or pool punishment with individual discretion.
- Contribution choices are discrete (0, 1, 2 MU), not continuous.
- No communication or negotiation is allowed, which may limit generalizability to real-world collective action problems.
- The experiment is short (10 rounds), which may affect the dynamics of cooperation and punishment compared to longer games.
- No explicit analysis of the effect of punishment cost or fine size beyond the fixed values used.
