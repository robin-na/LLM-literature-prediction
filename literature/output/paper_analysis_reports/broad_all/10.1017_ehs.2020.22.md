# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Generosity among the Ik of Uganda
Authors: Townsend, C., Aktipis, A., Balliet, D., & Cronk, L.
Journal: Evolutionary Human Sciences
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount donated in Dictator Game
- proportion of endowment given
- effect of cultural framing (need, supernatural punishment) on giving
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the proportion of the endowment given by the Dictator (i.e., generosity). No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported, as the Dictator Game is not a public goods game and does not have a group efficiency metric.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that Ik participants in unframed Dictator Games give on average 23.3% of their endowment, which is within the expected range for similar societies. Framing the game to invoke both need and supernatural punishment significantly increases giving (to 43.2%), while either frame alone does not have a significant effect. The results challenge the claim that the Ik are uniquely selfish and show that cultural norms and beliefs can increase generosity. However, the study does not use a public goods game, does not include punishment as a costly sanctioning option, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a Dictator Game, not a PGG or its variant, and the 'punishment' manipulation is a framing invoking supernatural punishment, not a real, costly, player-administered sanction. The outcome is individual giving, not group efficiency. The findings may be weakly informative for understanding how cultural beliefs about punishment can increase generosity, but they do not provide evidence about the efficiency effects of actual punishment mechanisms in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dictator Game is a two-player game: one dictator, one recipient (anonymous).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played a single one-shot Dictator Game (no repeated rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: dictator can allocate any number of 20 coins (0-20). Not all-or-nothing.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No information about others' choices or payoffs is shown; all decisions are anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses the Dictator Game, not a public goods game or its variants.
- No real punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only supernatural punishment is invoked as a belief-based framing.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or calculable from the design.
- The outcome is individual giving (generosity), not group-level cooperation or efficiency.
- Sample is convenience-based and limited to one community; external validity is limited.
- Findings about supernatural punishment are about beliefs and framing, not actual costly punishment choices.
