# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do humans really punish altruistically? A closer look
Authors: Pedersen, E. J., Kurzban, R., & McCullough, M. E.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned (costly to punisher, reduces target's payoff)
- amount of reward assigned
- self-reported anger and envy
- proportion of subjects punishing at or above a threshold
- behavioral forecasts in hypothetical scenarios
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount of punishment or reward assigned by subjects in a third-party punishment game, and self-reported emotional responses. No group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in a lab setting where third-party punishment is possible but demand characteristics and audience effects are minimized, witnesses of unfairness toward others rarely engage in costly punishment. When they do, it is not motivated by moralistic anger but by envy. Victims of unfairness (those personally harmed) do punish at nontrivial rates. In hypothetical scenarios, people forecast much more punishment and anger than is observed in real behavior. The results challenge the robustness of altruistic third-party punishment in humans and suggest that previous findings may be due to experimental artefacts. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in dictator/third-party punishment games where the punisher is not personally harmed, actual costly punishment is rare and mild, and not motivated by norm-enforcing anger. This suggests that enabling punishment in similar games is unlikely to increase efficiency or group payoff, since punishment is rarely used and not directed at norm enforcement. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The design is adjacent to public goods games, and the findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is only available to third parties who are not personally affected. The results caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in such designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three players: dictator, recipient, and third party (observer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Two rounds per subject; subjects not told the exact number of rounds to avoid end-game effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are via computer and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment/reward choices are continuous (any amount from $0 to $5 can be given, taken, or removed).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:4 (punisher pays $1 to reduce target's payoff by $4), which is less expensive than the typical 1:3 ratio.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4:1 (each $1 spent removes $4 from target).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects could also reward (give money to) the dictator; cost-to-reward ratio is 1:1.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1:1 (each $1 spent gives $1 to the target).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (each $1 spent gives $1 to the target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were not told the exact number of rounds to avoid end-game effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, a summary screen showed the amount of money each player earned.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were identified consistently throughout, so subjects knew which player was which, but the word 'punishment' was not used. The recipient of punishment could identify the punisher by role.

## Important Limitations
- The study is not a public goods game but a modified dictator/third-party punishment game; findings may not generalize to standard PGGs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment/reward behavior and emotional responses are measured.
- The design does not systematically vary or isolate the effects of individual methodological artefacts (e.g., strategy method, audience effects).
- The sample is limited to university undergraduates and online participants; external validity may be limited.
- The study focuses on third-party punishment by witnesses, not on punishment by directly affected parties or in repeated group contexts.
