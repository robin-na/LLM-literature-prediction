Authors: Riedl, A., Rohde, I. M. T., & Strobel, M.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2021

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- welfare gain (normalized group earnings relative to efficient and inefficient equilibria)

- total earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is a normalized welfare gain, which is a direct measure of efficiency. The paper also reports frequencies of efficient, inefficient, and miscoordinated outcomes, which are closely related to group payoff. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures such as inclusion/exclusion and action choice.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that free neighborhood choice in a repeated stag-hunt game (coordination problem) substantially increases the frequency of efficient coordination and group welfare compared to imposed (fixed) neighborhoods. In the fixed-neighborhood condition, groups mostly converge to the inefficient equilibrium, while in the free-choice condition, efficient outcomes become dominant over time. The ability to exclude inefficient players (a form of endogenous, costly social sanction) is the key mechanism: excluded players are much more likely to switch to efficient behavior, and overall exclusion is infrequent but sufficient to sustain high efficiency. Welfare gains in the free-choice condition are significantly higher, especially in later rounds, despite exclusion being costly. The results suggest that even limited, endogenous social sanctions (via exclusion) can robustly promote efficient group outcomes in coordination settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling endogenous, costly exclusion (a form of social sanction) in a repeated coordination game can dramatically increase group efficiency, even when exclusion is rare and costly. While the setting is a stag-hunt (coordination) game rather than a public goods game, the mechanism—costly exclusion of inefficient actors—parallels punishment in PGGs. The findings suggest that, in environments where players can choose their partners and exclusion is possible but costly, efficiency can be much higher than in fixed-group settings. For prediction tasks, this implies that the presence of endogenous exclusion or partner choice should be expected to have a large positive effect on efficiency, especially over repeated rounds, even if the cost of exclusion is nontrivial. However, the results may not transfer directly to standard PGGs with explicit punishment stages, as the underlying game is a coordination problem, not a social dilemma.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a stag-hunt (coordination) game, not a public goods (social dilemma) game, so direct transfer to PGGs may be limited.
- Punishment is implemented via endogenous, costly exclusion, not via explicit punishment stages as in standard PGG experiments.
- The group size is fixed at 6; effects in larger or smaller groups are not tested.
- The cost structure of exclusion is such that not playing is always worse than any played outcome; results may differ if outside options are more attractive.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit generalizability to settings with richer social interaction.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
