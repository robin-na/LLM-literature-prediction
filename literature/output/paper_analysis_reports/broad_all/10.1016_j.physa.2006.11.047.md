## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a theoretical framework for public goods games with endogenous network change and social control mechanisms. It predicts that clustered networks reduce collective action and that the ability to delete ties leads to segregation between contributors and defectors. Social control is operationalized as the ability to send happy or sad smileys, which are intended to induce conformity or selective incentives. The experimental design is described, but no data or results are presented. The main dependent variable in the proposed experiments is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical insights into how network structure and non-costly social control (smileys) might affect contribution behavior in public goods games. However, it does not provide empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it study costly punishment as typically defined in the literature. The model suggests that social control (even if non-costly) and network clustering can influence cooperation, but the lack of empirical results and the focus on non-payoff outcomes limit its direct utility for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions. The design dimensions are discussed in the context of the model and proposed experiment, but not tested empirically.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model and proposed experiment use n-person (specifically 6-person) public goods games.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The model is single-shot; the experiment is described as a series of games, but each is played as a one-shot game for payment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. All forms of communication are strictly prohibited except for sending smileys (happy/sad) as social control.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to contribute (1) or not (0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is defined as alpha (α), with c > α > 0; linear production function.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Social control (smileys) is costless; the model allows for both rewards and punishments, but in the experiment, smileys are non-costly.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as sending a sad smiley; the impact is assumed to be psychological/social, not monetary.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Happy smileys serve as non-costly rewards; both positive and negative social control are possible.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewards (happy smileys) are costless to send.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward effectiveness is assumed to be psychological/social, not monetary.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects know the structure of the game and that only one round is paid; the number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the information feedback condition, subjects are informed about the decisions of their contacts after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Smileys are sent to specific connected others; the recipient knows who sent the smiley.

## Important Limitations
- No empirical results are reported; only theoretical predictions and experimental design are presented.
- Social control is operationalized as non-costly (smileys), not as costly punishment or reward, limiting comparability to standard punishment treatments.
- Efficiency or group payoff outcomes are not measured or reported; the focus is on contribution rates and network structure.
- The model assumes costless social control and neglects second-order free rider problems.
- The experimental design is described but not implemented or analyzed in this paper.
