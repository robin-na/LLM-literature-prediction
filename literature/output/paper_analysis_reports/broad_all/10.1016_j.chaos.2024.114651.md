Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the evolution of cooperation (proportion of cooperators) in a continuous signed network version of the prisoner's dilemma. While payoffs are defined and used in the model, the main reported outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates, strategy distributions, network structure), not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that negative relationships, modeled as punitive ties that reduce payoffs for defectors, can promote the emergence and stability of cooperation in networked prisoner's dilemma games. The effect is stronger when the negative coefficient (punishment strength) or the proportion of negative ties is higher. Adaptive networks, where agents can adjust their ties, further facilitate cooperation. Structural balance (alignment of positive ties within groups and negative ties between groups) also supports higher cooperation. However, the paper does not report on group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff, focusing instead on the behavioral outcome of cooperation rates.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment-like mechanisms (here, negative relationships that reduce payoffs) can increase cooperation rates in networked social dilemmas. However, because the main outcome is the proportion of cooperators rather than efficiency or group payoff, and because the model is a prisoner's dilemma rather than a public goods game, its direct value for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments is limited. The findings suggest that increasing the cost or prevalence of punishment can promote cooperation, but do not quantify the effect on group efficiency or welfare.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use networks of 100 nodes (ER random network), 128 nodes (grid), and 100 nodes (BA scale-free).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 1,000 iterations (generations), with repeated runs for robustness.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Strategy is continuous in [-1,1], not all-or-nothing; both strategies and relationships are continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled via negative relationships that reduce payoffs; the 'negative coefficient' delta modulates the strength of this effect, but there is no explicit cost to the punisher as in standard PGG punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is controlled by the negative coefficient delta; higher delta increases the impact of negative ties (punishment).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run for a fixed, known number of iterations (1,000).

## Important Limitations
- The model is a prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- The main outcome is the proportion of cooperators, not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- Punishment is implemented via negative relationships that reduce payoffs, not via explicit costly punishment as in standard PGG experiments.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are from simulations.
- No explicit analysis of payoff-based efficiency outcomes.
- No human subjects or real-world behavioral data.
