## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average perceived payoff of cooperation (MeanVcp)
- average perceived payoff of conflict (MeanVcf)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of employees choosing cooperation
- proportion of employees choosing conflict
- frequency and reversals of behavior selection
- behavior state transitions (stable cooperation, stable conflict, turbulent state)
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation vs. conflict, reversals, stability/turbulence). Payoff-related outcomes are modeled as perceived payoffs (MeanVcp, MeanVcf), not actual monetary group efficiency or welfare. No direct reporting of group efficiency as a ratio to full cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the presence and magnitude of punishment (penalty for conflict) is a key determinant of stable cooperation in a group setting modeled as an evolutionary game. If the penalty for conflict exceeds half the cost of cooperation, cooperation becomes the evolutionary stable strategy, and the group achieves higher average perceived payoffs. If the penalty is lower, the group exhibits unstable, turbulent behavior with frequent reversals between cooperation and conflict, and the average perceived payoff from cooperation is often lower than from conflict. The model also shows that increasing the proportion of cooperators and the perceived benefit of cooperation stabilizes group behavior. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and simulation, not on real-world or laboratory data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that increasing the penalty for non-cooperation (punishment) above a critical threshold (half the cost of cooperation) can shift group behavior from unstable, low-payoff states to stable, high-cooperation, higher-payoff states. For prediction tasks, the presence and magnitude of punishment cost and effectiveness are critical moderators. However, the outcomes are modeled as perceived payoffs and behavioral states, not as direct group efficiency ratios. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is a salient, well-understood, and enforceable mechanism, and less so for empirical prediction of actual group efficiency in experimental PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulation models use a fixed group size (Numberofpeople = 100 in main simulation), but the model is general for any group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulation runs for 100 time stages (rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperation or conflict (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Penalty for conflict (delta) is a key parameter; higher penalty increases cooperation and perceived payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a direct penalty (delta) subtracted from payoff for conflict; effectiveness is direct and parameterized.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulation has a fixed, known number of time stages (rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Model assumes employees can observe neighbors' behavior (proportion of cooperators in neighborhood), but not explicit summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are used.
- Outcomes are modeled as perceived payoffs and behavioral states, not as direct group efficiency or welfare.
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a binary cooperation/conflict game with digital technology context.
- No marginal per-capita return (mpcr) or public goods multiplier is modeled.
- No reward mechanism is considered.
- The simulation does not account for complex social networks or longitudinal data.
- The findings may not generalize to empirical PGG experiments or real-world group efficiency outcomes.
