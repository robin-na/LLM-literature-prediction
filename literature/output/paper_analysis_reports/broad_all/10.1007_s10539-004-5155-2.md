## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment is a more general and empirically plausible mechanism for sustaining compliance with moral norms than reciprocity. Punishment can support cooperation in large groups and can enforce arbitrary rules, not just those with a cooperative payoff structure. The author reviews theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and ethnographic/experimental evidence to support the claim that punishment, including direct, exclusion-based, and reputation-based forms, is universal in human groups. The paper also discusses the problem of stabilizing costly punishment and the implications for understanding moral diversity. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental results on efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the importance of punishment mechanisms in sustaining cooperation and compliance in public-goods-like environments, especially in large groups. It suggests that, in theory, enabling punishment should increase compliance and cooperation, and thus could improve efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes. The arguments are useful for understanding why punishment may be effective, but the paper does not supply empirical data or parameterized results that can be directly used for predicting efficiency changes in experimental public goods games. Its value is mainly in supporting the general plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation and norm compliance, especially in large groups.
