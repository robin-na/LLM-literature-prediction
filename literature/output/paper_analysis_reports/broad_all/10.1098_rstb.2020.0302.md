# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Evaluating mechanisms that could support credible reputations and cooperation: cross-checking and social bonding
Authors: Samu, F., & Takács, K.
Journal: Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- gossip valence
- reputation scores
- frequency of gossip
- conditional cooperation on reputation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rate in the Prisoner's Dilemma, the valence and frequency of gossip, and reputation scores. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff is discussed in terms of individual incentives and competition, but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment found that allowing for cross-checking of gossip or opportunities for social bonding did not significantly improve cooperation rates compared to a control. High competition for reputation increased cooperation, but overall cooperation declined over time in all conditions. Gossip was generally honest but became more negative about rivals under high competition. Reputation scores and gossip valence influenced conditional cooperation, but the mechanisms tested did not create a reliable path to sustained high cooperation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff as an outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the effects of communication, reputation, and competition on cooperation in repeated dyadic games, but does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not include explicit costly punishment or group-level payoff analysis. Findings suggest that mechanisms to improve the credibility of social information (cross-checking, social bonding) may not be sufficient to sustain cooperation, and that competition for reputation can increase cooperation rates. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper offers only contextual or indirect guidance.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sessions had 16-20 participants, but the game is played in dyads (each round, each participant plays two simultaneous PDs with randomly assigned partners).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment had at least 12 rounds (5 in phase I, 7+ in phase II), but the exact total number was not known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Gossip (evaluative messages) is allowed between players, but not free-form chat; communication is limited to sending emoticons as gossip.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD game is a binary choice (cooperate/defect), i.e., all-or-nothing.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, players saw their own average reputation scores and those of their rivals, as well as their adjusted payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were identified by unique pseudonyms (planet moon names), and the sender/receiver of gossip was known in the interaction.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated dyadic Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to group settings.
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism is present; gossip is not implemented as a direct sanction with cost.
- No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes are reported.
- Communication is limited to sending emoticons as gossip, not free-form or content-rich communication.
- Social bonding is operationalized in a minimal way (reciprocity in communication), which may not capture real-world bonding effects.
- Participants did not know the total number of rounds, which may affect endgame behavior.
