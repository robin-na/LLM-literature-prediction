## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a voluntary deposit-commitment mechanism in public goods games can fully implement efficient contribution profiles as equilibrium outcomes, even in the absence of strong external enforcement or punishment institutions. The mechanism works by making non-contribution costly (loss of deposit) only if all players commit, thus aligning individual incentives with group welfare. Theoretical results show that, under this mechanism, the group can achieve payoffs and efficiency levels that strictly Pareto-dominate the standard Nash equilibrium of zero contribution. The mechanism is robust to various extensions, including repeated games and alternative uses of forfeited deposits.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that self-commitment mechanisms (such as refundable deposits) can substitute for traditional punishment in public goods games, leading to higher efficiency and group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in environments where such a deposit-commitment mechanism is available and properly designed, the efficiency with 'punishment' (here, self-sanctioning via deposit) can approach the social optimum, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the deposit mechanism is feasible and all players can observe and verify commitments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or parameterized predictions, but the qualitative direction is clear: enabling credible self-sanctioning or commitment mechanisms can dramatically improve efficiency in public goods games.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The mechanism assumes the existence of a neutral institution to hold and refund deposits, which may not be feasible in all real-world settings.
- The analysis assumes perfect monitoring of contributions and deposits.
- The model presumes rational, payoff-maximizing agents and common knowledge of preferences and mechanism structure.
- No analysis of partial commitment, noisy monitoring, or behavioral deviations from rationality is provided.
- No direct comparison to standard punishment institutions (e.g., peer punishment) in terms of cost-effectiveness or implementation challenges.
