## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that as collective action problems become larger in scale (more actors, greater spatial/temporal distance, more complexity), spontaneous cooperation becomes less likely due to increased stressors such as anonymity, lack of accountability, heterogeneity, and uncertainty. Third-party interventions (which may include sanctions, incentives, or other mechanisms) become increasingly necessary to generate and sustain collective action. The framework is intended to guide future empirical research and policy design, but does not itself provide new empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. However, it offers theoretical context: it suggests that in larger-scale settings, informal mechanisms (like peer punishment) are less effective, and formal third-party interventions (which may include sanctions or rewards) become more important for sustaining cooperation. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may diminish as group size, spatial/temporal distance, and complexity increase, unless punishment is institutionalized or externally enforced. The paper does not provide parameter-level guidance for the specific game design dimensions required for quantitative prediction.
