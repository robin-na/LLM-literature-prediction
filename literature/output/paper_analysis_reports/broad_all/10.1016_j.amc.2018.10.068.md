Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the total amount of contributions (interpreted as money available for public goods) and the standard deviation of contributions. These are not direct measures of efficiency (i.e., group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible), but are closely related. No direct analysis of group payoff, welfare, or efficiency ratio is provided. Behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rate and gossipmonger clustering are also discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Publishing the donation list partially (i.e., only for those who contribute above a threshold) increases total contributions to the public good compared to publishing all or none of the list. The optimal threshold for publication can be set dynamically as the mean of recent contributions, which is both effective and convenient. The model shows that this approach increases the sum of contributions and reduces the variance in contributions, indicating more uniform and higher cooperation. The effect is robust to changes in network connectivity and the window of historical data used. Gossip and self-assessment of face act as indirect social sanctions, motivating higher contributions. There is no explicit punishment or reward mechanism with costs and impact parameters as in standard PGG punishment treatments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that indirect social sanctions (via partial publication of contribution information and gossip) can increase total group contributions in a PGG-like environment. While it does not study explicit costly punishment, it suggests that reputational mechanisms can substitute for or complement formal punishment in promoting cooperation and group welfare. For prediction tasks, the findings imply that introducing a reputational/gossip-based sanction (by publicizing contributions above a threshold) can increase group payoff, especially if the threshold is set adaptively. However, the absence of explicit punishment cost/impact parameters means the results are not directly transferable to standard punishment treatments. The sum of contributions is a close but not exact proxy for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N=300 in all simulations; group size varies due to network structure.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10,001 time steps, with data averaged over 5,000 time steps after a long enough period.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous and heterogeneous; players can choose any amount (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor r=2; group size varies by network degree; MPCR is r/group_size, but group size is not fixed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The main intervention is the publication of the donation list (partial or full), which provides summary information about others' contributions above a threshold.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism; only indirect social sanctions via gossip and publication of contributions.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as group payoff relative to maximum possible, but inferred from total contributions.
- Model is theoretical/simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Network structure and group size are variable and may not map directly to standard PGG lab designs.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment or reward options.
