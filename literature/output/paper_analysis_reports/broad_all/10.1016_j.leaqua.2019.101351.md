# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The impact of leader communication on free-riding: An incentivized experiment with empowering and directive styles
Authors: Boulu-Reshef, B., Holt, C. A., Rodgers, M. S., & Thomas-Hunt, M. C.
Journal: Leadership Quarterly
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- follower earnings
- leader earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- free-riding (inverse of contribution)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is follower contribution (tokens contributed to the public good). Earnings for followers and leaders are reported, but the main analysis and statistical tests focus on contribution rates as the measure of free-riding. Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible) is not directly reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that empowering leader messages reduce free-riding (increase contributions) more than directive messages when there is no two-way leader-follower communication. When two-way communication is allowed, both leadership styles lead to higher contributions, and the difference between styles disappears. The presence of two-way communication is especially beneficial for directive leadership. The main dependent variable is contribution rate; earnings are reported but not the focus. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present, and efficiency is not directly analyzed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. However, it provides evidence that communication (especially two-way leader-follower communication) can increase contributions, which may be relevant for understanding the context in which punishment is or is not necessary. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, communication, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of punishment means the findings are only indirectly relevant for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 1 leader and 4 followers (5 players total per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 16 rounds per group, finite horizon, common knowledge.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Two-way leader-follower communication (private chat between leader and each follower) is a treatment variable. When present, contributions are higher.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: followers can contribute any amount from 0 to 10 tokens per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token contributed yields $0.04 to each of 4 followers (total $0.16 per token), so MPCR = 0.16.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Finite horizon (16 rounds) is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, followers see the total group contribution and their own contribution; leaders see the total group contribution.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present, so the findings are not directly relevant to the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximum) is not directly analyzed or reported; the main outcome is contribution rate.
- The experiment only studies leader communication style and two-way communication, not other game design dimensions such as punishment, reward, or institutional changes.
- The laboratory setting and finite horizon may limit generalizability to real-world or more complex environments.
- Only two leadership styles (empowering, directive) are studied; other styles (e.g., transactional, transformational) are not included.
