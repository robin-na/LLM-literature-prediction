# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: More 'altruistic' punishment in larger societies
Authors: Marlowe, F. W., Berbesque, J. C., Barr, A., Barrett, C., Bolyanatz, A., Cardenas, J. C., Ensminger, J., Gurven, M., Gwako, E., Henrich, J., Henrich, N., Lesorogol, C., McElreath, R., & Tracer, D.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- minimum acceptable offer (MAO) in third-party punishment game
- frequency/level of third-party punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the minimum acceptable offer (MAO) in a third-party punishment game, which reflects the threshold below which a third party will punish a dictator for an unfair offer. No direct group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the level of third-party punishment (as measured by MAO) is significantly higher in larger and more complex societies. Both local group size and ethnic group size are strong predictors of the willingness to punish unfair offers in a third-party punishment game. The results suggest that third-party punishment is more prevalent in larger societies, possibly as a response to increased anonymity and collective action problems. However, the study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the relationship between group size and the prevalence of third-party punishment in dictator-like games, which are adjacent to public goods games. It does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it should not be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it suggests that the willingness to punish norm violations increases with group size and social complexity, which may be relevant for understanding the conditions under which punishment is more likely to be used or effective. For the prediction task, this paper is mainly informative about the social and cultural moderators of punishment behavior, not about efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The third-party punishment game involves three players: a dictator (P1), a recipient (P2), and a third-party punisher (P3).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot (single round) and anonymous.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; games are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is continuous: P1 can give as much or as little as desired.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (P3) can pay 20% of their endowment to reduce P1's payoff by three times that amount.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (for every unit paid by P3, P1 loses three units).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is a one-shot (single round) game.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- The experimental design is a third-party punishment dictator game, not a public goods game; findings may not generalize to PGG environments.
- The main outcome (MAO) is a behavioral threshold, not a payoff-based measure.
- Cultural and ecological differences across societies may confound interpretation of punishment levels.
- The strategy method may influence punishment decisions compared to direct-response methods.
