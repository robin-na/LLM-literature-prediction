Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variables are the rate of cooperation (which is defined in terms of successful trust and reciprocation) and expected payoffs for individuals. The model also analyzes the conditions under which cooperation is sustained, and how institutional efficiency and population patience affect these outcomes. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution and reciprocation are analyzed as mechanisms, but the main focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that institutions, by leveraging contributions from individuals (motivated by reputation), can create strong enough incentives (via punishment, monitoring, or rewards) to sustain cooperation in settings where reputation alone would fail. The efficiency of the institution (how much incentive is generated per unit contribution) is a key moderator: high efficiency greatly expands the parameter space where cooperation and high payoffs are possible. The model also shows that when the population is patient (values future reputation), more individuals contribute, further increasing institutional effectiveness. However, when cooperation is easy, the institution can be wasteful, as its costs are not offset by additional gains in cooperation or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling efficient institutional punishment or monitoring in a public-goods-like environment will increase group efficiency and payoffs, especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low due to hard cooperation problems. The model clarifies that the effect of punishment depends critically on the efficiency of the institution (punishment technology), the cost of contributing to the institution, and the patience of the population. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that when institutional punishment is efficient and the population is sufficiently future-oriented, enabling punishment will lead to large increases in efficiency relative to control. However, if cooperation is already high in control, the marginal benefit of punishment may be negligible or even negative due to unnecessary costs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes a large number n >> 1 of actors and an infinite pool of choosers. The main results are derived in the large-n limit, but the number of actors per round is a parameter.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a repeated game with infinitely many rounds for actors (long-lived), while choosers are short-lived and participate in only one round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and reciprocation choices are binary: actors choose to reciprocate or cheat, and to contribute or free-ride.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The institution multiplies contributions by an efficiency parameter rho, analogous to MPCR. The model explicitly analyzes how varying rho affects outcomes.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost to contribute to the institution (c2) is specified and varied in the model. The institution uses these contributions to fund punishment, monitoring, or rewards. Lower cost (relative to incentive generated) increases effectiveness.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The efficiency parameter rho determines how much incentive (punishment, monitoring, or reward) is generated per unit contribution. Higher rho (greater punishment/reward per cost) leads to larger increases in cooperation and payoffs.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The model allows for institutions that use rewards, punishment, or monitoring, but the main analysis focuses on punishment and monitoring. Reward-only institutions are mentioned as a special case.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players (choosers) observe actors' reputations, which summarize their past actions (reciprocator, cheater, contributor, free-rider, or empty). The probability of observation is a key parameter.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes infinite or very large populations and infinite time horizons, which may not map directly to finite real-world or experimental settings.
- The model does not include communication, individual identification of punishers, or explicit group structure beyond the institution.
- Reward mechanisms are mentioned but not analyzed in detail; the main focus is on punishment and monitoring.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) choices for contribution and reciprocation.
- The model does not address dynamic or out-of-equilibrium behavior, only equilibrium outcomes.
- The effect of heterogeneity in endowments, power, or other individual differences (beyond time preference) is not analyzed.
