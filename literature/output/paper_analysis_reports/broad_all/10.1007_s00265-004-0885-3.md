# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputation is valuable within and outside one's own social group
Authors: Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M.
Journal: Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology
Year: 2005

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rate in public goods rounds
- probability of receiving help in indirect reciprocity rounds
- effect of reputation on receiving help
- awareness and use of group membership information
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on cooperation rates and the effect of reputation on receiving help, not on efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. No explicit analysis of efficiency or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that reputation built through cooperation in a public goods game increases the probability of being helped in subsequent indirect reciprocity rounds, and this effect is equally strong whether the helper is from the same or a different PGG group. Cooperation in the PGG is maintained at a high level while IR rounds are expected, but drops sharply when only PGG rounds remain. The experiment does not include punishment or sanctioning options, and does not report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. It does, however, show that reputation-based reward mechanisms (via indirect reciprocity) can sustain high cooperation rates in PGGs, but this is not equivalent to the effect of punishment. The findings are relevant for understanding how reputation and reward can substitute for or complement punishment, but do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The game design dimensions are well described, but the absence of punishment and lack of efficiency outcomes limit the paper's direct utility for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 12 players, split into two PGG groups of 6 for each public goods round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 16 rounds in total: alternating IR and PGG rounds for the first 12, then 4 PGG-only rounds. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; all decisions were made anonymously and in silence.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice in PGG was binary: contribute €1.25 or not (yes/no).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public pool was doubled and divided among 6 players: MPCR = 2/6 ≈ 0.333.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Rewarding is possible in the IR rounds: donors can give €1.25 to a recipient, who receives €2.00.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost in IR rounds: donor pays €1.25 to give €2.00 to recipient.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: €1.25 cost yields €2.00 benefit to recipient (multiplier = 1.6).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance; they were only informed at round 12 that only PGG rounds would follow.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All individual decisions and payoffs were displayed on a public screen with pseudonyms after each round.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design; only reward via indirect reciprocity is studied.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are not reported or analyzed; only cooperation rates and reward probabilities are measured.
- Findings on reputation and reward may not generalize to settings where punishment is the primary intervention.
- The experiment uses a binary (all-or-nothing) contribution structure, which may differ from continuous contribution PGGs.
- Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance, which may affect cooperation dynamics.
