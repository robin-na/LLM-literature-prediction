# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Can violence harm cooperation? Experimental evidence
Authors: De Luca, G., Sekeris, P. G., & Spengler, D. E.
Journal: Journal of Environmental Economics and Management
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperative ('low') extraction choices
- frequency of non-cooperative ('high') extraction choices
- frequency of 'chance' (violent appropriation/conflict) choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: the rates at which participants choose cooperative, non-cooperative, or conflict/appropriation actions. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes, though the theoretical model discusses payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares two treatments: a 'conflict' treatment where violent appropriation (chance) is a viable option when resources are low, and a 'control' treatment where the cost of appropriation is so high that it is never optimal. In the conflict treatment, participants are significantly less likely to choose the cooperative extraction rate ('low') and more likely to choose the non-cooperative rate ('high') in early rounds, anticipating future conflict. The expectation of future conflict thus undermines cooperation even before conflict occurs. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral shift from cooperation to non-cooperation implies lower efficiency in the conflict treatment. Additional analyses show that experiencing conflict in previous games further reduces cooperation in subsequent games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a credible, destructive appropriation/conflict option in a dynamic CPR game reduces cooperative behavior and likely reduces efficiency, even before conflict is actually chosen. For prediction tasks, the presence of a credible destructive sanction (even if not a standard punishment mechanism) should be expected to reduce efficiency relative to a control with no such option. However, the paper does not study standard punishment (costly peer punishment for norm violations) but rather a destructive appropriation/conflict option, so the results are most relevant for settings where punishment is destructive and ends the game, not for standard public goods games with ongoing punishment. The findings suggest that the threat of destructive sanctions can unravel cooperation and efficiency, especially when the sanction is credible and not prohibitively costly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games have a random number of rounds: first 6 rounds are certain, then each subsequent round continues with probability 0.7 (i.e., geometric distribution, average ~9.3 rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/extraction choices are discrete: 'low', 'high', or 'chance' (conflict). Not continuous, but not strictly all-or-nothing; rather, a menu of fixed options.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The 'cost' of conflict (chance) is manipulated: in the control, conflict destroys 60% of the resource (very costly, so never chosen); in the conflict treatment, the cost is lower and depends on resource stock, making conflict viable when resources are low.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/appropriation is highly destructive: it destroys a portion of the resource and ends the game for that pair, with remaining resource split and extraction fixed. The effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is not directly quantified, but the destructive nature is central.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the first 6 rounds are certain; after that, each round continues with probability 0.7. The random stopping rule is explained to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the amount of points that would be available in the next period for each possible choice, including those of the opponent, in each round.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as primary outcomes; all main results are in terms of behavioral choices (cooperation, non-cooperation, conflict).
- The 'punishment' studied is not standard peer punishment for norm violations, but a destructive appropriation/conflict option that ends the game for the pair.
- The game is a dynamic common pool resource extraction game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to standard PGGs with ongoing punishment.
- No direct analysis of marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or standard PGG parameters.
- No communication or chat between players, which may affect generalizability to settings with communication.
