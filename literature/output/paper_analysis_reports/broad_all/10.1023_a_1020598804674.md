Outcome notes:
The paper's main dependent variable is the proportion of altruists in the population over time, as determined by evolutionary replicator dynamics and simulation. Payoff is discussed as the mechanism driving evolutionary change, but the reported outcomes are not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, but rather the persistence or extinction of altruism.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment mechanisms, such as enforcement and ostracism, dramatically increase the robustness of altruism in evolutionary simulations of the prisoner's dilemma. While group selection and imitation can help sustain altruism under certain conditions (e.g., small group size, high imitation rates), punishment allows altruism to persist in much larger groups and under a wider range of conditions. The key mechanism is that punishment increases correlation among altruists, allowing them to interact more with each other and less with defectors. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes; it focuses on the evolutionary persistence of altruism.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment mechanisms can greatly increase the persistence of altruism (cooperation) in evolutionary environments similar to the prisoner's dilemma. However, because the main outcome is the proportion of altruists rather than group efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency in public goods games. The results suggest that, all else equal, adding punishment mechanisms is likely to increase cooperation and thus could plausibly increase efficiency, but the paper does not quantify this effect in payoff terms. The design dimensions most relevant here are the presence and effectiveness of punishment, group size, and the structure of interaction (e.g., group partitioning, imitation).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Simulations use population sizes of n=1000, but the effective 'group size' in group selection models is varied and shown to affect the persistence of altruism. Larger group sizes make it harder for altruism to persist unless punishment is present.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Simulations are run for a fixed number of rounds (usually ten), and the number of generations between group recombination is a key parameter affecting results.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The prisoner's dilemma is modeled as a binary choice: altruistic (cooperate) or selfish (defect).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The payoff matrix is specified for the prisoner's dilemma (e.g., mutual altruism (2,2), mutual selfishness (1,1), etc.), but not as a marginal per-capita return in the public goods sense.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment mechanisms are modeled with explicit costs to the punisher (e.g., enforcers have a 25% chance of being eliminated when punishing non-conformers; ostracism also has a cost). Even with substantial costs, punishment is highly effective at sustaining altruism.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is varied (e.g., enforcers have a 50% chance of eliminating non-conformers; ostracism has a 50-75% success rate). Higher effectiveness leads to greater persistence of altruism.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Simulations are run for a fixed number of rounds, but there is no discussion of whether agents know the number of rounds.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is the evolutionary persistence of altruism (proportion of altruists), not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The models are based on evolutionary simulations of the two-player prisoner's dilemma, not public goods games or multi-player settings directly.
- Payoff is used as the mechanism for evolutionary change, but is not reported as an outcome.
- The models are highly stylized and abstract, with strong assumptions about population structure, group formation, and agent behavior.
- No empirical or experimental data are reported; all results are theoretical or simulation-based.
