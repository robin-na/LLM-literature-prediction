The main dependent variable is the average payoff (efficiency) of the population under different action rules (cooperation, defection, punishment) and social norms. The paper also analyzes evolutionary stability of strategies, but the central outcome for comparison is efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper shows that in models of indirect reciprocity, strategies that use costly punishment (punishing defectors at a cost to oneself) are almost always less efficient than strategies that simply withhold cooperation from defectors. Costly punishment can only be the most efficient equilibrium in a very small region of parameter space, requiring fine-tuned values of benefit, cost, punishment cost, punishment impact, and reputation accuracy. In most cases, the highest group payoff (efficiency) is achieved by not using costly punishment. The results are robust to errors in reputation assignment and action execution, as long as these errors are not too frequent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in indirect reciprocity settings (which are adjacent to public goods games), enabling costly punishment will almost always reduce group efficiency compared to not enabling punishment, except in a very narrow set of parameter values. For prediction tasks, this suggests that unless the game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, punishment impact, benefit/cost ratio, reputation accuracy) are in the narrow region identified, enabling punishment should be expected to lower efficiency. The findings are most directly applicable to indirect reciprocity games with binary reputation and second-order norms, but the logic may transfer to PGG-like environments with similar structure.

- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes a population of players, with two randomly chosen for each interaction (donor and recipient). The exact number is not specified, but the model is for large, well-mixed populations.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is of repeated interactions, but does not specify a fixed number of rounds; it is a repeated game with ongoing random matching.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Gossip is discussed as a mechanism for establishing coherence in reputation, but not as strategic chat between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model uses binary choices: cooperate, defect, or punish. There is no continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The benefit/cost ratio (b/c) is a key parameter, analogous to MPCR in PGGs.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is explicitly modeled and varied. Higher punishment cost reduces the region where punishment is efficient.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment impact (beta) is explicitly modeled and varied. Higher punishment impact can shrink or eliminate the region where punishment is efficient.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players observe the actions and reputations of others; reputation is public in the main model.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not test predictions in actual public goods games or with human subjects.
- The setting is indirect reciprocity with binary reputation and second-order social norms, which is adjacent but not identical to standard PGGs.
- No reward mechanisms are considered; only costly punishment and withholding cooperation.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not address network or group structure.
- The model uses binary (all-or-nothing) actions rather than continuous contributions.
