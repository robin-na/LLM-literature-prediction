## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring (noisy signals about individual contributions, but perfect information about total group contribution), the authors experimentally compare five regimes: no punishment, peer-to-peer punishment, collective punishment, peer-to-peer punishment with commitment, and collective punishment with commitment. The key finding is that only collective punishment with commitment (where group members can opt in to a regime that automatically punishes the whole group if total contributions fall short) leads to significantly higher group payoffs and cooperation rates compared to both no punishment and peer-to-peer punishment. Peer-to-peer punishment does not improve payoffs or cooperation relative to no punishment in this noisy environment. The positive effect of collective punishment with commitment is especially pronounced in the later rounds, suggesting a learning or coordination effect. The study also shows that the ability to participate in the decision to implement collective punishment (i.e., voting/commitment) is crucial for its effectiveness.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling collective punishment with commitment in a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling collective punishment with commitment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff) substantially, especially over time, provided the punishment is collective, pre-committed, and participation in the institution is possible. The effect is not observed for peer-to-peer punishment or for collective punishment without commitment. The experimental design and reporting allow for direct mapping to prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, given the game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players; fixed across all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 50 rounds per group; all treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: each player can contribute either all (20 tokens) or nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 (alpha = 0.5, n = 4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 5 tokens per 15 punishment points assigned (beta = 1/3). Applies to both peer-to-peer and collective punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact is 15 tokens per punished player per 5 tokens cost to punisher (punishment effectiveness = 3). In collective punishment, all group members (including punisher) are punished.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is 50 rounds; fixed and common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are informed of the sum of group contributions (perfectly), and their own payoffs. No mention of more detailed summaries.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses all-or-nothing contribution choices, which may limit generalizability to continuous-contribution settings.
- Imperfect monitoring is implemented via noisy signals (60% accuracy) about individual contributions, but total group contribution is always perfectly observed; results may differ with different noise structures.
- The study does not separate the effects of commitment and information timing; commitment involves both pre-commitment and knowledge of the punishment regime before contribution decisions.
- No reward mechanisms are studied, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The lab setting uses fixed groups of 4 and 50 rounds, which may not generalize to larger groups or different time horizons.
- The effect of punishment cost and effectiveness is not systematically varied; only one parameterization is used.
- No communication or chat is allowed, so results may not generalize to settings with communication.
