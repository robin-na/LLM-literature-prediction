Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences

Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the fraction of cooperators (\rho_c) in the steady state. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. Cost to the third party is discussed, but not as a group payoff or efficiency measure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that third-party punishment and reward, when applied together, strongly promote cooperation in a spatial public goods game. The synergy between punishment and reward is more effective than either alone, especially at lower cost to the third party. Lower thresholds for intervention (greater tolerance for defection) and higher intensity of intervention both increase cooperation. More frequent intervention (shorter time intervals) also promotes cooperation. However, the main outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency effects of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the combination of punishment and reward can robustly increase cooperation rates in spatial public goods games, and that the effect is sensitive to the threshold, intensity, and frequency of intervention. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its findings are most useful for understanding how design dimensions like intervention intensity, threshold, and frequency affect cooperation rates, which may correlate with efficiency in some settings, but this is not demonstrated here.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are arranged on a square lattice (L x L), with simulations run for L=200 to L=800. Each player participates in G=5 games (with 4 neighbors). The effect of network size is tested and found not to affect qualitative results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is run for many Monte Carlo steps (MCS) until steady state is reached. The number of rounds is not fixed in the sense of a finite repeated game, but the system evolves over time.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: players are either cooperators (contribute c=1) or defectors (contribute 0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The enhancement factor R (1 to G) is varied; MPCR is R/G. R is a key parameter in the model and is systematically varied.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are implemented by a third party, with cost determined by the intensity factor (alpha) and the frequency (tau). The cost to the third party is reported in Figure 1b.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the intensity factor (alpha), which is systematically varied.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is present in some conditions and absent in others; the effect of reward alone, punishment alone, and both together is compared.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to the third party for reward is reported and compared to punishment (Figure 1b).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the intensity factor (alpha), same as punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare.
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or total group payoff, so findings are only indirectly relevant to efficiency prediction.
- Punishment and reward are administered by a third party, not by players themselves, which may limit generalizability to standard PGG lab experiments.
- The model assumes spatial structure (lattice), which may not generalize to well-mixed or other network structures.
