The model tracks the frequencies of behavioral strategies (cooperation, defection, punishment) and learning rules (conformism, payoff-dependent imitation) in simulated populations. Payoff functions are specified, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral frequencies, not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. The model is evolutionary and theoretical, not empirical.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the presence of both conformist learning and altruistic punishment allows for the stable maintenance of high levels of cooperation in large groups, even when migration is high and intergroup conflict is rare. Conformism preserves between-group variation and stabilizes punishment, while punishment protects against the spread of defection. The model demonstrates that cooperation can actually increase with group size when both mechanisms are present. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral frequencies (cooperation, punishment) rather than direct efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment (especially when combined with conformist learning) can sustain high cooperation in public-goods-like environments, particularly as group size increases. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency given control efficiency and game design dimensions. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of conformism and punishment as moderators of cooperation, especially in large groups and under high migration. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size (N) is a key parameter varied in the model. The model shows that the combination of punishment and conformism allows cooperation to be sustained at high levels even as group size increases.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost of punishing (k) is a parameter in the model (baseline k=0.2). The presence of punishment (with cost) is shown to increase cooperation compared to no-punishment scenarios.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is determined by the cost to the punished (p=0.8) relative to the cost to the punisher (k=0.2). The model shows that effective punishment supports cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and evolutionary, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are reported as behavioral frequencies (cooperation, punishment), not as group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The model is not a standard public goods game; payoffs are structured differently and do not include explicit marginal per-capita return.
- No direct quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment are provided.
- No analysis of communication, reward, or information feedback mechanisms.
