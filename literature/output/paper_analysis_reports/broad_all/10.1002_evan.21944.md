The paper does not report quantitative efficiency, payoff, or welfare outcomes. It focuses on qualitative and historical evidence of large-scale cooperation, the mechanisms that may sustain it, and the scale of collective action in foraging societies.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that large-scale cooperation (hundreds of individuals) in the production of public goods (e.g., communal hunts, construction of drivelines, warfare, habitat improvement) was common in Holocene and likely Pleistocene foraging societies, even at low population densities. Mechanisms such as direct sanctions (ostracism, denial of aid, physical punishment), social norms, and leadership roles were important for sustaining cooperation beyond the scale where mutualistic or reciprocal incentives suffice. The evidence challenges the view that ancestral human cooperation was limited to small groups. However, the paper does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes in a way that would directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides contextual and theoretical support for the idea that punishment and social sanctions can sustain large-scale cooperation in collective action problems, especially as group size increases and individual incentives to free-ride grow. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of specific game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, player count) on efficiency. Thus, while it supports the plausibility of punishment as a mechanism for sustaining cooperation in large groups, it does not provide data that can be directly used to predict efficiency changes in public-goods-game-like experiments. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of sanctions and institutions for large-scale cooperation and the likely need for additional mechanisms as group size increases.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The paper discusses group sizes in communal action, often citing numbers (e.g., 50, 100, 300 participants), but does not manipulate or analyze player count in a controlled way. It argues that as group size increases, sustaining cooperation requires additional mechanisms.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The paper discusses the costliness of imposing sanctions (e.g., ostracism, denial of aid, physical punishment) and how the incentive to punish declines as group size increases, but does not quantify punishment cost or analyze its effect on outcomes.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Discusses the effectiveness of sanctions (ostracism, denial of privileges, physical punishment) in motivating cooperation, especially in small groups, but does not quantify punishment impact per unit cost.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Mentions that rewards and privileges can be denied to non-cooperators, but does not analyze or focus on positive rewards as a mechanism.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The paper discusses that sanctions (ostracism, denial of aid, etc.) are often socially visible and that knowledge of who defected or punished is important for norm enforcement, but does not analyze this as a variable.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes in a quantitative or experimental way.
- No experimental or observational manipulation of game design dimensions; all evidence is historical, ethnographic, or archaeological.
- No direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments.
- Mechanisms such as punishment, reward, and group size are discussed theoretically and contextually, not as experimentally varied variables.
- Findings are based on qualitative synthesis and interpretation of historical and archaeological records, which may be incomplete or biased.
