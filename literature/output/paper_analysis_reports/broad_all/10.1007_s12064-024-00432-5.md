## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Only institutional (pool) punishment is modeled; peer punishment, rewards, and optional participation are not considered.
- No communication, reputation, or spatial structure is included; the population is well-mixed.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices.
- The results depend on initial conditions due to bistability; real populations may not meet the required initial thresholds.
- The model does not address the effects of repeated finite rounds, learning, or human behavioral biases.
- Findings may not generalize to small groups or settings with different punishment/reward mechanisms.
