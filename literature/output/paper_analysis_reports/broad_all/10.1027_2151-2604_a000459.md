# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Examining Consequentialist Punishment Motives in One-Shot Social Dilemmas
Authors: Funk, F., & Mischkowski, D.
Journal: Zeitschrift Fur Psychologie-journal of Psychology
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- final average payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment investments (absolute and relative to earnings)
- binary punishment decision
- contribution behavior
- personality measures (SVO, Honesty-Humility)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is punishment investments (amount spent to punish), both in absolute terms and as a percentage of earnings. Contribution behavior is also measured. Final average payoff is reported descriptively, but not analyzed as a main outcome or in relation to the experimental manipulation. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is conducted.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study found that providing participants with the prospect of receiving information about the effect of their punishment on transgressors did not increase punishment investments in a one-shot public goods game. There was no significant main effect of the information manipulation, nor any interaction with dispositional prosociality (SVO or Honesty-Humility). The only consistent predictor of punishment was the participant's relative contribution compared to group members. The study does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, and the main focus is on punishment motives and behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a one-shot public goods game with four players, punishment is infrequent and not significantly affected by the availability of consequentialist information. The main outcome is punishment behavior, not efficiency or group payoff, so the paper does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, the low punishment rates and high baseline cooperation suggest that, in similar one-shot, small-group, no-communication settings, introducing punishment may have limited behavioral or efficiency impact, especially when baseline cooperation is high. The paper does not provide direct evidence for the efficiency prediction task, but suggests that punishment may not always be effective in increasing cooperation or efficiency in one-shot games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 4 players in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is strictly one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: participants could contribute any amount between 0 and 400 cents.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contributed euro is doubled and divided equally among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 euro spent reduces target's payoff by 4 euros (cost-to-impact ratio 1:4).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 unit cost to punisher reduces target by 4 units.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw all group members' contributions (absolute and in pie chart) before punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players could punish any group member, but identities were not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is punishment behavior, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not analyzed as a function of punishment.
- High baseline cooperation and low punishment rates may limit generalizability to settings with lower cooperation or repeated interactions.
- Manipulation check was conducted after the dependent variable, raising concerns about whether participants understood the manipulation at the time of decision.
- The one-shot design may limit the relevance of findings for repeated or dynamic public goods games.
- The sample consisted mainly of university students, which may affect external validity.
