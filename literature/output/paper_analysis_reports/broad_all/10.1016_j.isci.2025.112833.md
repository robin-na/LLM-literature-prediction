# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Rewards and punishments help humans overcome biases against cooperation partners assumed to be machines
Authors: Makovi, K., Bonnefon, J. F., Oudah, M., Sargsyan, A., & Rahwan, T.
Journal: Iscience
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- machine penalty (gap in contributions between human- and machine-assumed partners)
- frequency of rewards and punishments
- change in contribution after receiving reward or punishment
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is contribution rate (tokens contributed to the public good). The 'machine penalty' is defined as the difference in average contributions between groups where partners are assumed to be humans versus machines. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both peer rewards and punishments independently increase contributions in a repeated public goods game, regardless of whether partners are assumed to be humans or machines. However, the gap in contributions ('machine penalty') between human- and machine-assumed partners persists unless both rewards and punishments are available together, in which case the gap is eliminated. The combination of rewards and punishments is uniquely effective for machine-assumed partners but inefficient for human-assumed partners. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency is not established.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, enabling peer punishment (and/or reward) increases contribution rates, but only the combination of both eliminates the machine penalty in contribution. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in contribution rates under different sanctioning regimes, especially in human-machine interaction contexts, but less so for direct efficiency prediction unless contribution is used as a proxy.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game lasted 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is mentioned or allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players decide what proportion of their resources to allocate to the public pool.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The sum of contributions is multiplied by 1.6 and divided evenly among 4 players, so MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment is at a cost to the punisher, but the exact cost per unit is not specified in the main text.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward mechanism is available in some conditions.
- reward cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is at a cost to the rewarder, but the exact cost per unit is not specified in the main text.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the feedback, reward, and punishment conditions, participants see what each player contributed at the end of each round.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; all main outcomes are based on contribution rates.
- Some analyses are exploratory and not pre-registered.
- The experiment uses deception (participants are told their partners are machines, but all are humans), which may limit generalizability.
- The incentive structure (e.g., cost and impact of punishment/reward) is not varied or deeply analyzed.
- The frequency of punishment is extremely low, limiting the ability to analyze its effects in detail.
