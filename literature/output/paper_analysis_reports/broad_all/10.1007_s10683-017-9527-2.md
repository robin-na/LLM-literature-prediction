# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Conducting interactive experiments online
Authors: Arechar, A. A., Gächter, S., & Molleman, L.
Journal: Experimental Economics
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per participant per period
- group efficiency (implied by average earnings relative to maximum possible)
- total group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- punishment severity
- prosocial vs antisocial punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment) and payoff-based outcomes (average earnings, group efficiency). Efficiency is not always labeled as such, but is directly calculable from reported payoffs and design.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that enabling peer punishment in a repeated four-person public goods game (20 rounds: 10 without, 10 with punishment) substantially increases average contributions and group efficiency in both laboratory and online (MTurk) samples. In the no-punishment condition, average contributions and earnings are lower and decay over time. When punishment is introduced, contributions jump and are sustained at higher levels, and average earnings per participant per period increase (lab: 22.64 to 26.91; online: 22.64 to 26.91, as reported). Punishment is mostly prosocial (cooperators punish defectors), and the effect is robust across both environments. The paper also finds that attrition (dropout) is not related to experimental condition or behavior, supporting the internal validity of the results.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with peer punishment (cost 1, impact 3), enabling punishment increases group efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust across both lab and online samples, and the design matches canonical PGG-with-punishment studies. The paper's detailed reporting of game parameters, outcome measures, and the direct comparison of control and punishment conditions make it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players (fixed).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds total: 10 without punishment, 10 with punishment (within-subjects).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players; experimenter took steps to prevent communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: each player chooses how many of 20 points to contribute (0-20). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4 (group project multiplier 1.6, 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point costs punisher 1 point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point assigned causes a 3-point loss to the target (impact per cost = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were aware there were two parts (10 rounds each), and knew when each part started and ended.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the average group contribution and the individual contributions of each group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is peer-to-peer, but the paper does not specify whether the identity of the punisher is revealed. Based on standard design and reference to Herrmann et al. (2008), punishment is likely anonymous (i.e., recipients know they were punished but not by whom).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a within-subjects design (no-punishment then punishment), so order effects cannot be ruled out.
- The subject pools differ between lab (university students) and online (MTurk), which may affect generalizability.
- The experiment only uses groups of 4 and a fixed MPCR; results may not generalize to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- Punishment is only peer-to-peer and only in the form of costly deduction; no reward or institutional punishment is tested.
- The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of maximum possible, but average earnings per period are provided and can be used to compute efficiency.
- The design does not include communication or chat, so results may not generalize to settings with communication.
