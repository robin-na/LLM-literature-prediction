## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total wealth (simulation output, not empirical)
- density of investment group (proxy for trust/cooperation)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy frequencies (investor, punishment-type investor, trustworthy trustee, untrustworthy trustee)
- evolution of trust level
- proportion of untrustworthy trustees
Outcome notes:
All outcomes are from agent-based Monte Carlo simulations of a networked trust game. The main dependent variables are the densities of different strategies and total wealth in the simulated population. No empirical or experimental data are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a graded punishment mechanism in a networked trust game simulation can effectively reduce untrustworthy behavior and increase the density of investment and trust. The effect of punishment depends on both the cost to punishers and the severity of punishment. Graded punishment outperforms traditional punishment in eliminating untrustworthy agents and increasing simulated group trust and total wealth. However, if punishment costs are too high, investment and trust collapse. The results are sensitive to network topology, with regular lattices supporting higher total wealth than scale-free networks. All findings are based on agent-based simulations, not real-world or laboratory experiments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, theory-based evidence that graded punishment mechanisms can increase simulated group trust and total wealth in a networked trust game, especially when punishment is not too costly. However, because the study is simulation-based and uses a trust game variant (not a standard PGG), its findings should be interpreted as suggestive rather than directly predictive for real-world or experimental PGGs. The results highlight the importance of the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and suggest that graded (rather than uniform) punishment may be more effective at sustaining cooperation and group payoff in networked environments. The paper does not provide empirical data for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, but its modeling results may inform hypotheses about the role of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and network structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use N=1024 agents on a 32x32 lattice; player count is fixed in the main results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 20,000 Monte Carlo steps, with results averaged over the last 5,000 steps.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is a key parameter; higher cost reduces the effectiveness of punishment and can lead to collapse of investment/trust.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment amount (beta) is varied; higher punishment effectiveness increases trust and investment density, up to a point.

## Important Limitations
- All results are from agent-based simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a networked trust game variant, not a standard public goods game.
- Outcomes are primarily behavioral (strategy frequencies, trust level) and only secondarily payoff-based (total wealth).
- No real-world or laboratory data are used; findings may not generalize to human behavior.
- No direct analysis of efficiency as defined by ratio of group payoff to full cooperation.
