Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average profits (payoff-based) and cooperation rates (behavioral). Attitudes towards punishment and group choice are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that a local, exogenous punishment institution can sustain high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency) when the matching structure (population viscosity) is high, i.e., when group members mostly interact with each other. As viscosity decreases (more random mixing), both cooperation and average payoffs decline. In the most separated case (x=0), average profits are 373 (out of a possible 400), while in the most mixed case (x=1), average profits drop to 230 (the mutual defection payoff is 200). A control with internalized punishment (punishment always applies) at low viscosity (x=2/3) yields higher average profits (243) than local punishment (230). Thus, punishment institutions can increase efficiency, but their effectiveness depends strongly on the matching structure and the scope of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on the matching structure (population viscosity) and the scope of punishment (local vs internalized). When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the presence of a punishment institution will increase efficiency only if the environment allows for sufficient group separation (high viscosity) or if punishment is internalized (applies in all matches). In low viscosity or random matching, local punishment is much less effective. The control efficiency (without punishment) is the mutual defection payoff (200), and the treatment efficiency with punishment can approach the cooperative payoff (400) under high viscosity, but remains low under random matching. The paper also shows that the technical details of punishment (local vs internalized) matter for efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experimental population consisted of 8 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 100 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG; in the prisoner's dilemma, mutual cooperation yields 400 per player, mutual defection 200, unilateral defection 550/50. The efficiency benchmark is 400.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous and automatic; defectors in group A lose 200 when matched with another group A member. No cost to other players; cost is a direct deduction from the defector's payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction of 200 from the defector's payoff per relevant interaction (local or internalized, depending on treatment).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed that the experiment consisted of 100 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects were informed of their partner's group, action, and their own payoff. They were also told the percentage of subjects in each group and their probability of meeting each type before each round.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is exogenous and automatic (not peer-administered), which differs from many PGG punishment designs.
- Group size is fixed at 8; effects of larger or smaller groups are not tested.
- Matching structure (viscosity) is manipulated, but real-world analogs may be more complex.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results are specific to punishment institutions.
- The experiment uses a binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation choice, not a continuous contribution.
