# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Hard Problem of Cooperation
Authors: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean group payoff
- individual payoff
- equilibrium payoff
- difference in payoffs between conditions and types
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- monitoring frequency
- voting on institutional parameters (punishment, threshold)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group/individual payoff, with detailed reporting of mean payoffs under different institutional conditions and group types. Contribution and monitoring are also reported, but as secondary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Unregulated PG: 4 rounds; Evolving institution: 16 rounds; Weak/strong institution: 4 rounds each; No institution: 4 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; participants were in separate cubicles and did not see each other.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: participants chose how many of 10 tokens to contribute (0–10).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 (each token contributed yields 0.5 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is automatic and collective: when a cheater is detected, a fine (F) is imposed; no direct cost to the punisher, as the punishment is not individually assigned but automatic. Monitoring costs 1 token; fine size (F) is manipulated (2 in weak, 10 in strong).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via fine size (F): weak (F=2), strong (F=10). Higher punishment tech (stronger fine) leads to higher efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward for monitoring: if a monitor detects a cheater, the monitor receives 3 tokens (R=3), paid from the common pot.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. No direct cost to the rewarder; reward is paid from the common pot, not from the monitor's own tokens.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward for successful monitoring is fixed at 3 tokens per detected cheater.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the number of rounds in each phase.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the total group contribution, own earnings, whether they were monitored/punished, number of cheaters and punishments, number of monitors, and average group payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses homogeneous groups (all high or all low cooperators), which may not generalize to mixed-type groups.
- Institutional strength is manipulated as exogenous (fixed) or endogenous (voting), but real-world institutions may evolve differently.
- No communication is allowed, which may limit generalizability to real-world settings where discussion is possible.
- Punishment is automatic and collective, not peer-to-peer, which differs from many other PGG punishment designs.
- Sample is Swedish university students, which may limit cross-cultural generalizability.
