## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both Vietnamese and Chinese participants do not show negative outgroup bias toward the low-status Khmer in games involving unidirectional prosociality (dictator, envy, and third-party punishment games). In the third-party punishment game, participants are as likely to punish unfairness against Khmer as against their own group, indicating a lack of typical ingroup bias in norm enforcement. However, in games requiring mutual cooperation (trust and coalition formation), the Khmer are excluded and earn less, reflecting negative outgroup effects. The main focus is on behavioral outcomes and trait perceptions, not on efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about punishment in public-goods-like settings, specifically via the third-party punishment game. It shows that punishment can be applied without strong ingroup bias, especially toward low-status, warm outgroups. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff as outcomes, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that social context and group perceptions can moderate punishment behavior, but the lack of efficiency data limits its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes in public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are played with 2 or 3 players depending on the game. The third-party punishment game involves 3 players (dictator, recipient, punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; each subject plays each game once (with some within-subject variation for group matching).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; all choices are made privately and without communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete but not strictly all-or-nothing: in the dictator and envy games, senders choose from a set of possible amounts; in the trust game, the choice is binary (invest or not).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the third-party punishment game, the punisher pays 1,000 dong to reduce the dictator's payoff by 3,000 dong.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1,000 dong cost to punisher reduces target's payoff by 3,000 dong).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; subjects know the structure and that each game is played once.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed of the group identity (via color tags) of their partners; no mention of summary statistics about others' behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes; main outcomes are behavioral (giving, punishment, trust, coalition choice).
- The third-party punishment game is not a repeated public goods game; it is a one-shot allocation game with punishment.
- Sample sizes for some group comparisons are small, especially in three-group villages.
- Individual-level stereotype measures do not robustly explain behavioral variation.
- Findings may not generalize to standard public goods games or to repeated interactions.
