Outcome notes:
The paper is a survey of experimental studies comparing the strategy method and direct-response method. The main outcomes reported are behavioral (e.g., punishment, cooperation, contribution, rejection, etc.), not efficiency or group payoff. Some studies report mean payoffs or earnings, but these are not the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that, across a wide range of experimental games (including some with punishment and cooperation), the method of elicitation (strategy vs. direct response) usually does not significantly affect behavior. However, in games involving punishment, direct response tends to result in higher punishment rates than the strategy method. The paper notes that this difference is robust across several studies, but the effect on efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed. The survey also finds that differences between methods are more likely when there are fewer contingent choices or in one-shot games, but these effects are not strong. Overall, the strategy method is generally a valid tool for studying behavior, but may understate punishment rates compared to direct response.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting efficiency in public-goods-like games with punishment. It suggests that the elicitation method can affect observed punishment rates, with direct response yielding higher punishment. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the paper does not provide quantitative guidance for predicting efficiency outcomes. For prediction tasks, the main implication is that studies using the strategy method may understate the impact of punishment on cooperation and, by extension, on efficiency. When synthesizing evidence, one should be cautious about comparing results across studies using different elicitation methods, especially for punishment-related outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Player count varies across surveyed studies (e.g., 2, 3, 4, 10), but is not systematically analyzed as a moderator of punishment or efficiency effects.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both one-shot and repeated/multi-round games are included in the survey. The paper notes that differences between elicitation methods are more likely in one-shot games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Some surveyed games use binary (all-or-nothing) choices, others use continuous or multi-level choices. The number of contingent choices is discussed as a moderator.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Some studies vary punishment cost (e.g., Brosig et al. 2003), but the survey does not systematically analyze its effect on efficiency or punishment's impact.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is discussed in some studies, but not systematically analyzed across the survey.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Some surveyed games include reward mechanisms (e.g., Brandts and Charness 2003), but reward is not the main focus.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward cost is mentioned in some studies (e.g., Brosig et al. 2003), but not systematically analyzed.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward effectiveness is mentioned in some studies, but not systematically analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a survey and does not provide new experimental data or direct analysis of efficiency or group payoff.
- Most outcomes reported are behavioral (punishment, cooperation, etc.), not efficiency or welfare.
- Game design dimensions are not systematically analyzed as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- The survey covers a wide range of games, not just public-goods games, so findings may not generalize directly to PGG-like environments.
- Potential publication bias is noted but not addressed.
- Differences in elicitation method may interact with other design features in ways not captured by the survey.
