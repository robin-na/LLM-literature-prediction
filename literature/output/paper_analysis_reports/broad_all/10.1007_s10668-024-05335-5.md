Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the dynamic evolution of strategies in a tripartite evolutionary game (village enterprises, government, villagers) under different reward and punishment mechanisms. Outcomes are probabilities of choosing certain strategies, not direct payoffs or efficiency. There is no explicit calculation or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that under a static reward and punishment mechanism (SRPM), the system does not reach a stable equilibrium; strategies of all parties fluctuate periodically. Under a dynamic reward and punishment mechanism (DRPM), the system converges to a stable mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE), interpreted as an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Increasing the upper limit of punishments for illegal emissions by village enterprises significantly increases the probability of legal emissions and reduces the need for strict government regulation and active supervision by villagers. Increasing the upper limit of rewards for villagers' active supervision increases their supervision probability but may reduce legal compliance by enterprises and government regulatory effort. The DRPM is recommended for more effective and stable governance.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that dynamic, behavior-dependent punishment mechanisms (analogous to more flexible or responsive punishment rules in public goods games) can stabilize cooperative behavior (legal emissions) and reduce the need for costly monitoring and enforcement. However, the model is not a standard public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The main outcomes are strategy probabilities, not payoffs. The findings suggest that increasing punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech) and using dynamic punishment rules may improve stability and compliance, but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The results are most relevant for understanding how punishment design (cost, tech, dynamic vs static) may affect behavioral outcomes in multi-agent governance games.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a tripartite evolutionary game with specific roles and payoff structures.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus; outcomes are strategy probabilities, not payoffs.
- Findings are based on theoretical modeling and simulation, not empirical or experimental data.
- Parameter values are illustrative and not empirically calibrated.
- The model assumes infinite or continuous time and does not address finite-round effects.
- No consideration of communication, information feedback, or identification of punishers/rewarders.
- Applicability to standard PGG environments is indirect and may require careful mapping.
