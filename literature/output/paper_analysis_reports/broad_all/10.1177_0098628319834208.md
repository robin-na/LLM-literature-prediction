# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Altruistic Punishment in the Classroom: An Update on the Tragedy of the Commons Extra Credit Question
Authors: Selterman, D.
Journal: Teaching of Psychology
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of students choosing each option (0, 2, or 6 points)
- proportion of classes staying under the 10% threshold (group success/failure)
- self-reported attitudes (understanding, positive feelings, prosocial motivation)
- qualitative reactions to the exercise
- learning outcome (exam performance on related concepts)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral choices (which option students select) and whether the group as a whole succeeds (stays under the 10% threshold). No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. The closest outcome is the group success rate (whether the class receives any points), but this is not analyzed as a continuous efficiency or payoff variable.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that adding an altruistic punishment option (students can sacrifice their own points to remove points from a random over-consumer) increases the rate at which groups successfully avoid the 'tragedy of the commons' (i.e., more classes stay under the 10% threshold and receive points) compared to prior years without punishment. However, the study does not directly measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The main findings are that the punishment option increases cooperation (fewer students choose the selfish option), and that students report positive attitudes and increased understanding of social dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods dilemma can increase group cooperation and the likelihood of group success (i.e., receiving the reward). However, because the outcome is binary (group gets points or not) and not a continuous efficiency or payoff measure, the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task. The evidence suggests that punishment can improve group outcomes in similar all-or-nothing, threshold-based social dilemmas, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency change. The design is also classroom-based and not a standard repeated PGG, so caution is warranted in generalizing to other settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Class sizes ranged from 29 to 244. The paper notes that smaller classes were more likely to succeed (stay under the threshold), consistent with prior research, but does not systematically analyze the effect of player count on the impact of punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round). Students make a single choice; there are no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed before choices are made. There is discussion after the exercise, but not before or during decision-making.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: students choose 0, 2, or 6 points; the group outcome is all-or-nothing (everyone gets their chosen points if the threshold is not exceeded, otherwise no one gets any points).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is the full opportunity cost: students choosing the punishment option receive 0 points (forgo all possible points) to remove the reward from a 6-point chooser.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 0-point chooser removes all points from one randomly selected 6-point chooser. Punishment is one-to-one and fully effective for the targeted individual.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Students know the game is a single round and the rules are explained in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. After the exercise, students are told the overall results (number of each choice), but not before or during decision-making.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; only binary group success/failure is reported.
- The design is a one-shot, threshold public goods dilemma, not a standard repeated or linear PGG.
- No experimental control group in the same semester; comparison to prior years is observational and not randomized.
- Punishment is implemented as a pre-committed, anonymous, one-shot option, not as a dynamic or repeated sanctioning mechanism.
- Sample is limited to psychology students in classroom settings; generalizability to other populations or real-world settings is uncertain.
- Small number of students choose the punishment option, limiting statistical power for subgroup analysis.
