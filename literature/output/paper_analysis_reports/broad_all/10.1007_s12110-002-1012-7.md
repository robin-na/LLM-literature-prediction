Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (aggregate monetary payoff, group earnings, total pie) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, punishment/reward behavior, expectations, norm compliance). The main public goods game experiments explicitly report the effect of punishment on aggregate monetary payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the introduction of a costly punishment option leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation and a 40% increase in aggregate monetary payoff, even after subtracting the costs of punishment. Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency unravel over time, with most subjects defecting by the end. With punishment, cooperation is sustained or even increases, and group earnings approach the social optimum. The effect is observed both in stable groups (partner design) and in randomly re-matched groups (stranger design), though the effect is stronger in stable groups. The paper also shows that punishment is targeted at defectors and is proportional to the degree of free-riding. The findings are robust across cultures and with high monetary stakes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard public goods game with known MPCR, group size, and round structure leads to a large, positive effect on group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust to group composition (fixed or random), and the paper provides enough detail on the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) to inform predictions for similar designs. The findings support the prediction that, controlling for control-game efficiency, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently effective and observable.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The main public goods game uses groups of 4 players. The effect of punishment is directly measured in this setting.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The public goods game is played for 10 periods (rounds) in both punishment and no-punishment conditions.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat is allowed between subjects; all interactions are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment decisions are continuous (subjects choose how many tokens to contribute, from 0 to 20). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token contributed yields 0.4 tokens to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: assigning 10 punishment points costs the punisher 30 tokens; cost is convex and increasing in points assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished subject's income by 10% (2-3 tokens on average). Punishment is effective and salient.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game lasts 10 periods; backward induction arguments are discussed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects are informed about the contributions of the other three group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; group members cannot trace the history of individual punishments or investments. No identification of punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The main public goods game experiments use groups of 4 and 10 rounds; generalizability to other group sizes or longer time horizons is not directly tested.
- Punishment is implemented with a specific cost and effectiveness structure; effects may differ with other punishment parameters.
- No communication or reputation is allowed; results may differ in settings with communication or repeated interactions with known partners.
- Reward is not available in the main public goods game; the interaction of reward and punishment is not tested in this context.
- The experiments are conducted in laboratory settings with student subjects; external validity to field or real-world settings may be limited.
