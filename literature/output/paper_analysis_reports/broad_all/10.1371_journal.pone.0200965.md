# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The emergence of inequality in social groups: Network structure and institutions affect the distribution of earnings in cooperation games
Authors: Tsvetkova, M., Wagner, C., & Mao, A.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group average wealth
- individual wealth
- Gini coefficient (inequality in wealth)
- Theil index (inequality in wealth)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation/contribution rates
- punishment behavior
- network assortativity by cooperativeness
- network assortativity by wealth
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is inequality in final wealth (Gini coefficient), not efficiency or total group payoff. Group average wealth is reported as a secondary analysis, but the main focus is on the distribution of wealth, not its total. Contribution, cooperation, and punishment behaviors are analyzed as mechanisms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that enabling punishment in networked cooperation games (including PGG-like games) increases inequality in the distribution of final wealth, as measured by the Gini coefficient. This is because the cost of punishment is disproportionately borne by poorer players, who become even poorer as a result. However, the paper does not find a systematic relationship between the presence of punishment and group average wealth (i.e., efficiency or total payoff). Thus, while punishment increases cooperation and inequality, it does not reliably increase or decrease group efficiency. The main focus is on the distributional consequences (inequality), not on efficiency or total group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGG-like networked cooperation games increases inequality in final wealth, but does not provide evidence that punishment systematically increases or decreases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation). For the downstream prediction task, this means that the presence of punishment should not be assumed to improve or worsen efficiency based on this evidence alone. The main effect is on the distribution of payoffs, not their sum. If the prediction task is about efficiency, this paper suggests that punishment's effect is likely neutral or context-dependent, and that other design dimensions (e.g., network structure, fluidity) may matter more for efficiency than for inequality.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Group sizes in the analyzed experiments range from 8 to 625. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not systematically analyzed by player count.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Number of rounds varies from 6 to more than 90 across experiments. No systematic analysis of num_rounds as a moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some games use binary (cooperate/defect) choices (e.g., PD), others use continuous contributions (e.g., PGG). The effect of this dimension is not systematically analyzed.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. MPCR (marginal per-capita return) varies across experiments (e.g., 0.4, 0.5, 0.65, 0.85). No systematic analysis of MPCR as a moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment is described as costly, but the cost per unit varies across experiments. The effect of punishment cost on efficiency is not systematically analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is not systematically reported or analyzed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some experiments include reputation tracking (information about others' past behavior), but summary information about contributions or payoffs is not systematically described.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is inequality (Gini coefficient), not efficiency or total group payoff.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is not systematically analyzed; group average wealth is only reported as a secondary analysis.
- The experiments included are heterogeneous in design (game type, group size, rounds, network structure), making it difficult to isolate the effect of individual dimensions.
- No systematic analysis of how game design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- No information on chat, default contribution framing, or whether players know the number of rounds.
- Reward mechanisms are not present in the analyzed experiments.
- The data are drawn from previously published experiments not originally designed to study efficiency or the effect of punishment on efficiency.
