# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Money and flexible generosity
Authors: Wang, X. J., Chen, Z. S., Krumhuber, E. G., & Chen, H.
Journal: British Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- generosity rate (amount allocated to other in DG/UG)
- flexible generosity (difference in generosity across game types or conditions)
- motivation to maximize self-gain
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is generosity rate (amount given to the other player), not group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. No explicit analysis of efficiency, welfare, or group payoff is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three studies, the authors find that using money as the currency in DG/UG games reduces generosity compared to candy or food, but only when there are no sanctions (DG) or reputation concerns (private condition). When punishment is possible (UG) or when allocations are public (reputation at stake), the negative effect of money on generosity is reduced or eliminated. Imbuing money with prosocial meaning (charity) also attenuates the effect. The main outcome is generosity rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the presence of sanctions or reputation mechanisms can offset the self-serving effects of money on prosocial behavior, but do not provide direct evidence about efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of punishment or reputation mechanisms can mitigate the negative effect of monetary incentives on prosocial behavior, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it use a public goods game structure. The results are most relevant for predicting how punishment or reputation might moderate the effect of monetary incentives on generosity or cooperation, but less directly informative for predicting efficiency changes in public goods games with or without punishment. The findings suggest that punishment/reputation mechanisms may help maintain prosocial behavior even in monetary contexts, but the lack of efficiency or group payoff data limits direct applicability to the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are two-player (allocator/recipient) in DG and UG; each round is with a different player.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 8 rounds per game type; Study 2: 6 rounds per condition; Study 3: 6 rounds per condition.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is described; interactions are anonymous or via facial images/IDs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (allocation) is continuous: participants can allocate any amount from 0 to 100 in increments of 10.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told they will play a fixed number of rounds (e.g., 8 rounds per game in Study 1, 6 rounds per condition in Studies 2 and 3).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses Dictator and Ultimatum Games, not public goods games; results may not generalize to PGG environments.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only generosity rates (amount given) are analyzed.
- Punishment is only present as the possibility of rejection in the UG, not as a costly punishment mechanism as in PGG.
- No analysis of how design dimensions like punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, or MPCR affect efficiency.
- Findings are based on non-payoff behavioral outcomes (generosity), limiting direct applicability to efficiency prediction tasks.
