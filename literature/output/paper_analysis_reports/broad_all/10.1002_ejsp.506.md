# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sanctions and moral judgments: The moderating effect of sanction severity and trust in authorities
Authors: Mulder, L. B., Verboon, P., & De Cremer, D.
Journal: European Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- moral judgment of rule-breaking
- social disapproval of rule-breakers
- trust in authorities as moderator
Outcome notes:
The dependent variables across all three studies are moral judgment (perceived immorality of rule-breaking) and social disapproval (willingness to disadvantage a rule-breaker), not efficiency, group payoff, or any direct payoff-based outcome. No group earnings, welfare, or surplus are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that severe sanctions (relative to mild or no sanctions) increase the extent to which people judge rule-breaking as morally wrong, and this effect is moderated by trust in authorities: it is present only when trust is high. In a behavioral allocation task, severe sanctions also increase social disapproval of rule-breakers (willingness to forgo personal gain to disadvantage a rule-breaker), again only when trust in authorities is high. The paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or any direct economic outcome, and does not use a public goods game or a close variant. The results are relevant for understanding how punishment can shape moral norms and social enforcement, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar economic games. Its findings are relevant for understanding psychological mechanisms (moral judgment, social disapproval) that may underlie compliance and norm enforcement, especially the moderating role of trust in authorities and sanction severity. However, because the outcomes are not payoff-based and the game structure is not a PGG or close variant, the paper should not be used as primary evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments. It may be useful for contextual or mechanistic discussion, especially regarding how punishment can affect perceived legitimacy and social norms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Study 1: individual decision; Study 3: participants observe allocation between two others (allocator/recipient), but not a multi-person group. No group interaction as in PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. All studies are one-shot or single-decision tasks; no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited in lab studies.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution or compliance is a discrete choice (e.g., give at least two tickets or not; allocate requested number of chips or not). Not continuous as in standard PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanction severity (fine amount) is manipulated: e.g., 0.20 EUR vs. 2.50 EUR (Study 1); 0.50 EUR vs. 5 EUR (Study 3). Cost is to the punished, not the punisher (authority-administered).
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is a monetary fine; effectiveness is not directly measured, but severity is manipulated.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Single-decision tasks; participants know the structure and that there is only one decision.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In Study 3, participants are told about the decisions of two allocators (one compliant, one non-compliant). No summary statistics, but explicit information about others' choices.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not use a public goods game or a close economic social dilemma; the tasks are adjacent but not equivalent.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; all dependent variables are moral judgment or social disapproval.
- Sanctions are administered by an authority, not by peers; no endogenous punishment.
- All studies are one-shot or vignette-based; no repeated interaction or dynamic effects.
- Artificiality of lab tasks may limit generalizability to real-world or economic game settings.
