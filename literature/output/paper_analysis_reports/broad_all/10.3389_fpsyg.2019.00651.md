# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Gossip in the Dictator and Ultimatum Games: Its Immediate and Downstream Consequences for Cooperation
Authors: Wu, J. H., Balliet, D., Kou, Y., & Van Lange, P. A. M.
Journal: Frontiers in Psychology
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- generosity (amount allocated in dictator/ultimatum game)
- trust (amount sent in trust game)
- reciprocity (proportion returned in trust game)
- emotional responses
- partner evaluations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (generosity, trust, reciprocity) and emotional/evaluative responses. No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus are reported. The games are dyadic and sequential, not group-based public goods games.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the possibility of gossip (reputation transmission) increases generosity in both dictator and ultimatum games. However, strategic motivation (fear of rejection in the ultimatum game) does not significantly increase generosity compared to the dictator game. In subsequent trust games, those who previously played the ultimatum game are less likely to reciprocate trust, especially when gossip is present. The effects are measured in terms of individual behavioral outcomes (generosity, trust, reciprocity), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that reputation systems (gossip) can promote cooperation, but strategic motivations may undermine future cooperation, particularly when reputation is at stake.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it offers adjacent evidence that reputation mechanisms (gossip) can increase cooperative behavior in dyadic allocation games, and that strategic motivations (as in the ultimatum game) may not robustly increase cooperation and can even reduce reciprocity in subsequent interactions. Since the design lacks group-level interaction, explicit punishment, and efficiency outcomes, its direct predictive value for the target task is limited. It may inform the prediction task by analogy: reputation-based incentives can substitute for punishment in promoting cooperation, but strategic extrinsic incentives may have negative downstream effects on intrinsic cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are dyadic (2 players per game: A-B in DG/UG, A-C in TG). Sessions had 6 or 12 participants, but each game is between two individuals.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played two sequential one-shot games (DG/UG, then TG). No repeated rounds within a game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No direct chat or communication between players; only structured 'gossip' (evaluation) transmission in the gossip condition.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/offer choices are continuous (0-100 MUs), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not applicable in DG/UG (allocation is direct transfer). In the trust game, the amount sent is tripled (multiplier = 3, 2 players, so effective mpcr for trustor's transfer is 1.5 for the trustee, but not a standard PGG).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the two sequential games and their structure; no uncertainty about number of games.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the gossip condition, Person C receives Person B's evaluation of Person A. No other summary information is provided.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses dyadic, sequential games (dictator/ultimatum and trust games), not group-based public goods games.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only reputation (gossip) is manipulated.
- Outcomes are behavioral (generosity, trust, reciprocity), not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- Sample size per condition is modest, potentially limiting statistical power.
- The design is asymmetric and may not generalize to symmetric, group-based cooperation contexts.
- No direct measure of efficiency or surplus is reported, limiting direct applicability to the prediction task.
