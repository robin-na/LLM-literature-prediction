The paper is entirely theoretical and analyzes efficiency, expected output, and intertemporal utility as the main outcomes. Behavioral outcomes like cooperation are discussed as equilibrium strategies, not as measured variables.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that organizations can sustain higher efficiency and cooperation by conditioning punishments (and rewards) not only on collective output but also on observable outside activities, even if those activities are not directly productive. Strategic distortion of privately beneficial effort (either increasing or decreasing it, depending on whether it is a complement or substitute to collective effort) reduces the short-run gain from defection, thus lowering the required severity and frequency of punishment. This mechanism increases the expected output and intertemporal utility of members compared to settings where only output is monitored. The result is robust to extensions with more generations and noisy monitoring of outside activities.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated public-goods-like environments with imperfect monitoring, the ability to condition punishment on additional observable actions (beyond just output) increases efficiency by making cooperation easier to sustain. If a game design allows punishment to be based on richer or more informative signals (e.g., monitoring of side activities), the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be higher than in designs where only output is observable. The model also predicts that the efficiency gain from punishment is larger when monitoring is more informative and when organizations can strategically distort observable but privately beneficial actions. However, the model is not an empirical test and does not provide quantitative effect sizes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The baseline model has two overlapping generations (one young, one old member at a time), with extensions to three or more generations. Each generation is a single member, but the model notes that this is for simplicity and could be generalized.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is an infinite-horizon repeated game (discrete time, t=0,1,...).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution to collective output is binary: a_t^i in {underline{a}, bar{a}} (i.e., shirk or cooperate). Privately beneficial effort is continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is captured by the parameter lambda in the utility function, which weights the value of organizational output. The output function G(a^i, a^{-i}) is increasing in individual effort.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a reduction in continuation utility, triggered probabilistically based on public signals. The model analyzes how the informativeness of monitoring (parameter L) affects the severity and frequency of punishment needed to sustain cooperation. More informative monitoring (higher L) reduces efficiency loss from punishment.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Rewards are mentioned as being linked to observable actions, but the model focuses on punishment as the main enforcement mechanism. Rewards are not explicitly modeled.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinite horizon (no known end), so the number of rounds is not shown but is common knowledge to be infinite.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players observe public signals of output (g_t) and, in the main model, also observe the privately beneficial effort (b_t) of others. This public observability is key to the main results.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- The model assumes overlapping generations with one member per generation, which may not capture all features of real organizations or standard public goods games with multiple simultaneous players.
- Punishment is modeled as a reduction in continuation utility, not as a costly action by another player, so the results may not transfer directly to settings where punishment is costly or requires active assignment.
- The model assumes infinite horizon and perfect rationality, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- The model's predictions depend on the ability to observe and condition on outside (privately beneficial) activities, which may not always be feasible in practice.
