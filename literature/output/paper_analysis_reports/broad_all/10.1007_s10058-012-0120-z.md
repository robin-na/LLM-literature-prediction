## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally studies public goods games with costly punishment under eight different network architectures, varying completeness, connectedness, and directedness. All games have four players, 15 rounds, and fixed MPCR and punishment cost. The main finding is that network structure crucially moderates the effect of punishment on both cooperation and efficiency. Complete and some connected networks achieve high efficiency because they elicit high contributions with relatively low punishment expenditures. In contrast, directed and disconnected networks often see more punishment and lower efficiency, despite sometimes higher contributions. The marginal benefit of adding monitoring edges is non-linear: adding edges to sparse networks increases efficiency, but in denser networks, more edges can actually reduce efficiency due to over-punishment. The paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment is not uniform but depends on the monitoring/punishment network.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that network architecture (who can punish whom) is a critical moderator. In games with complete or well-connected networks, punishment increases contributions and can improve efficiency, especially when punishment expenditures are low. In directed or disconnected networks, punishment is more frequent and severe, often reducing efficiency due to high punishment costs. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and other game parameters are held constant, so the findings isolate the effect of network structure. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one must account for the network's connectedness, completeness, and directedness, as these determine whether punishment will be efficiency-enhancing or not. The results suggest that simply enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the network context is decisive.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use four players per group in all network architectures.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 independent decision rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose any number of tokens (0 to 25) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (alpha) is fixed at 0.4 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher per unit of punishment is 0.5 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token spent on punishment reduces the target's payoff by 1 token (punishment effectiveness = 2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game consists of 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see the sum of all group contributions and the contributions of those they are connected to.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use only four-player groups; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- MPCR, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are held constant; effects of varying these parameters are not tested.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment is available.
- No communication is allowed; results may differ with communication.
- Network structures are limited to eight specific architectures; not all possible network types are explored.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
