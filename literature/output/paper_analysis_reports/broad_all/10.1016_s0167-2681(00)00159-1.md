## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in three-person repeated joint production games with exit, conditional cooperation and the ability to exit dysfunctional teams allow for higher average payoffs (efficiency) than unconditional defection. However, in short-lived enterprises, targeted punishment of defectors is necessary to sustain cooperation and high payoffs. When targeted punishment is strong enough, even in short-lived games, cooperation and efficiency are restored. The results are robust in both static and evolutionary (replicator dynamic) simulations. The study provides direct evidence that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong simulation-based evidence that enabling targeted punishment in small-group repeated public goods games with exit increases efficiency, especially when the number of rounds is small. The effect of punishment is more positive when the punishment is strong (high impact per cost) and can be targeted at defectors. The results suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of targeted punishment, the number of rounds, and the ability to exit are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher when exit and conditional cooperation are possible, but enabling punishment further increases efficiency, especially in short games. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All simulations are for three-person (n=3) teams.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds (production sessions) is systematically varied from 1 to 20. Longer games increase the positive effect of conditional cooperation and exit; punishment is especially important in short games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (contribute 1 unit) or free-ride (contribute 0 units).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 2/3 in the main setting (each unit of input increases group output by 2, split among 3 players). Alternative setting with lower fixed cost is also analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Targeted Retribution imposes a cost of 0.2 (or 0.3 in a variant) to punish a defector by 2 (or 3) units. Lower cost/higher impact punishment increases efficiency, especially in short games.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is high (e.g., 2 or 3 units impact for 0.2 or 0.3 cost). Higher punishment effectiveness stabilizes cooperation and increases efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (maximum production sessions) is fixed and known in each simulation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players (programs) can condition on their own payoffs, and in the case of Targeted Retribution, on the identity of defectors. No explicit summary information is modeled, but the ability to target defectors implies some information about others' actions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Targeted Retribution can identify and punish specific defectors, implying that punishment is not anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The study is based on computer simulations of stylized behavioral programs, not human subjects or field data.
- The game is a close but not exact variant of the standard public goods game: it is a three-person joint production game with exit and binary contribution choices.
- No communication, reputation, or partner choice beyond exit is modeled.
- Only punishment (not reward) mechanisms are considered.
- Parameter choices (e.g., punishment cost/impact, fixed costs) are stylized and may not map directly to experimental or real-world settings.
- Results may not generalize to larger groups, continuous contribution choices, or settings with more complex social structure.
