# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Leadership in social dilemmas: The effects of group identification on collective actions to provide public goods
Authors: Van Vugt, M., & De Cremer, D.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 1999

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- preference for leader structure
- self-esteem
- perceived legitimacy of leader
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good. No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. Self-esteem and perceived legitimacy are also measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that: (1) Instrumental (punishing) leaders increase contributions to the public good, especially when group identification is low. (2) Relational (encouraging) leaders only increase contributions when group identification is high. (3) In low group identification groups, relational leaders are ineffective and may even be counterproductive. (4) The legitimacy of the leader mediates the effect on contributions. (5) There is a general preference for democratic, elected, and internal leaders, especially when group identification is high. (6) No direct group payoff or efficiency outcomes are reported, but the pattern of contribution changes is clear.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment (via an instrumental leader) in increasing contributions depends on group identification. In low group identification settings, punishment is much more effective than encouragement. In high group identification settings, both punishment and encouragement can be effective. However, the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are partially specified and can inform dimension coding, but the main outcome is contribution, not efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 6 players in both studies.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 2 rounds (one before, one after leader selection); Study 2: 8 rounds (4 before, 4 after leader introduction).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players; all information was via computer and leader messages.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: all-or-nothing (contribute full endowment or nothing); Study 2: continuous contribution (0-300 pence).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: Step-level game, group bonus £30 if threshold met, 6 players, each gets £5 if successful. Study 2: If group contributes £12, group gets £30 bonus, split among 6 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Instrumental leader imposes a fine of 220 pence on the lowest contributor per round. The cost to the punisher (leader) is not specified, but the fine is subtracted from the punished player's earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective: 220 pence fine per round to lowest contributor. This is a strong punishment relative to the 300 pence endowment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the number of rounds in advance (2 in Study 1, 8 in Study 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants received feedback about group success/failure and, in the leader phase, the leader could see all contributions. It is not specified if individuals saw others' specific contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The leader (an external person) imposed punishment, and the punished individual was identified to themselves, but it is not specified if others saw who was punished.

## Important Limitations
- No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported; only contribution rates are measured.
- No leaderless control group in Study 2, so the effect of leader introduction versus no leader is not fully isolated.
- Leader types are extreme (purely punishing or purely relational), which may not reflect real-world leader styles.
- Continuous negative feedback about group failure may not be realistic and could affect generalizability.
- Punishment cost to the punisher (leader) is not specified; only the fine to the punished player is described.
