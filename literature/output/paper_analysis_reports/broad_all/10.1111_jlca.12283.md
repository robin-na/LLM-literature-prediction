# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Volver a Nuestras Raices: The Reemergence and Adaptation of Traditional Forms of Andean Reciprocity
Authors: Lyle, H. F.
Journal: Journal of Latin American and Caribbean Anthropology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- household investment in collective action (hours contributed)
- variation in participation (free riding vs. contribution)
- size and structure of social support networks
- reciprocity and exchange relationships (ayni, minga)
- correlation between collective action and network centrality
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes such as participation in collective action, free riding, and the structure of reciprocal support networks. There is no direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare in the sense of public goods game literature. The closest quantitative outcome is the correlation between collective action investment and social network centrality.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in the absence of formal or institutionalized punishment, collective action in the community is maintained through a combination of symbolic capital (reputation for generosity, skill, and reliability) and indirect social exclusion (withholding support from free riders). Households that invest more in collective action have larger and more robust support networks, suggesting positive assortment among prosocial individuals. Free riding is tolerated to some extent, and there is no evidence of direct punishment or sanctions. The system is sustained by the interplay of modified traditional reciprocity practices (ayni and minga), which have adapted to local needs. There is no direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff, but the system appears stable and resilient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as there is no experimental manipulation of punishment, no formal sanctions, and no measurement of efficiency or group payoff. However, it does suggest that in real-world settings, cooperation can be maintained without formal punishment through social mechanisms like reputation and indirect exclusion. For prediction tasks, this paper is most useful as contextual evidence that alternative mechanisms (symbolic capital, social networks) can substitute for formal punishment, but it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment interventions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The community consists of 24 households, which function as the units of participation in collective action. This is not a controlled variable, but the group size is clearly described.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Collective action events occur several times a month, with data collected over two years and 22 events. There is no fixed number of rounds as in a lab experiment, but repeated interactions are central.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Communication is present and important; discussions about participation and reputation occur before, during, and after collective action events.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Rewards exist in the form of social capital, reputation, and access to support networks, not as explicit material rewards.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Information about others' participation is shared informally through discussion and record-keeping by community leaders.

## Important Limitations
- No experimental manipulation or control group; all evidence is observational and ethnographic.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- No formal or institutionalized punishment mechanism is present, so the study cannot inform the effect of punishment interventions.
- Findings are context-specific to a small Andean community and may not generalize to laboratory or other public goods game settings.
- Social rewards and indirect exclusion are not parameterized or quantified in a way that maps directly to game-theoretic models.
