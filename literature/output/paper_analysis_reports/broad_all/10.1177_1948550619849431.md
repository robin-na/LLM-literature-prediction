# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Community Violence and Prosociality: Experiencing and Committing Violence Predicts Norm-Enforcing Punishment but Not Cooperation
Authors: Littman, R., Estrada, S., Stagnaro, M. N., Dunham, Y., Rand, D., & Baskin-Sommers, A.
Journal: Social Psychological and Personality Science
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation (contribution in Dictator Game, Trust Game, Public Goods Game)
- norm-enforcing punishment (Second-Party Punishment, Third-Party Punishment, Ultimatum Game MAO)
- antisocial punishment
Outcome notes:
The study's dependent variables are factor scores for cooperation and norm-enforcing punishment, derived from economic games. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in a high-violence urban US sample, exposure to and commission of violence are not associated with increased cooperation in economic games (including a Public Goods Game), but are positively associated with norm-enforcing punishment (i.e., willingness to pay to punish selfish others in economic games). The association is specific to norm-enforcing punishment and not to antisocial punishment or general willingness to punish. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. However, it does provide indirect evidence that, in a US urban context, individuals with higher exposure to or commission of violence are more likely to engage in norm-enforcing punishment, but not more likely to cooperate. This suggests that the presence of punishment may not increase cooperation or efficiency in similar populations, but the study does not test the causal effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The design and results are most relevant for understanding individual differences in punishment and cooperation, not for predicting treatment effects of punishment interventions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Public Goods Game used 4 players; other games used 2 or 3 players. The study does not analyze the effect of player count on outcomes.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single interaction); no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some games use binary (all-or-nothing) choices (e.g., Trust Game Player 1), others allow continuous or categorical choices (e.g., Dictator Game, Public Goods Game).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Public Goods Game, each player's contribution is doubled and divided equally among 4 players (MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is specified: e.g., in Second-Party Punishment, $1 spent removes $4 from the other; in Third-Party Punishment, $1 spent removes $5 from the target.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is specified: $1 spent removes $4 (Second-Party) or $5 (Third-Party) from the punished player.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are explicitly one-shot; participants know there is only one round per game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the other player's actions as required for the game (e.g., in punishment games, participants know if the other defected or cooperated). No summary statistics or history are shown, as games are one-shot.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; no identification of who punished whom. Participants are told they are matched with 'someone from your community like you,' but not the identity.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and does not manipulate the presence or absence of punishment; it cannot estimate the causal effect of enabling punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation and punishment) are analyzed.
- The sample is from a single US city (New Haven, CT) and is not representative of broader populations.
- All games are one-shot; results may not generalize to repeated or dynamic public goods games.
- The study focuses on individual differences (exposure to violence) rather than game design features as treatment variables.
