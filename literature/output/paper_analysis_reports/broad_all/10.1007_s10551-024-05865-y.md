# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Calculated Punishment
Authors: Chen, F. D., Nave, G., & Wang, L.
Journal: Journal of Business Ethics
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency (prosocial and antisocial)
- response times (RTs) for punishment and withholding punishment
- logistic regression on punishment decisions
- drift-diffusion model parameters (drift rate, starting point, etc.)
- relationship between SVO and punishment
- contribution rates (mean contributions per round)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variables are punishment frequency (prosocial/antisocial), response times, and computational model parameters. Group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff are not directly analyzed. Mean contribution is reported, but not as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that both prosocial and antisocial punishment rates are strongly affected by the cost and impact of punishment. When punishment is free, antisocial punishment increases substantially. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but the findings imply that the design of punishment (especially cost) can influence the prevalence of antisocial punishment, which is typically detrimental to group welfare. The main contribution is a process-level account of punishment decisions, showing that people are not intuitively predisposed to punish, and that punishment is a calculated response sensitive to incentives.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the cost and impact (effectiveness) of punishment are critical moderators of punishment behavior in public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the finding that antisocial punishment increases when punishment is free suggests that low-cost punishment can undermine group welfare. For prediction tasks, the results support the importance of including punishment cost and effectiveness as key dimensions, and caution that enabling low-cost punishment may not always improve efficiency due to increased antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so its value is mainly in highlighting moderators and mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per PGG session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; groups are anonymous and decisions are made independently.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (in multiples of 2), but punishment is binary (punish or not).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total group contribution is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is randomized: 0 or 2 points per punishment decision. Lower cost increases both prosocial and antisocial punishment, with antisocial punishment increasing dramatically when cost is zero.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact (effectiveness) is randomized: 2 or 8 points deducted from target per punishment. Higher impact increases punishment rates.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 20 periods (rounds) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, participants are informed about the contributions of all group members and their payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; group members are anonymous and cannot identify who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as an outcome; only behavioral outcomes (punishment rates, RTs, contributions) are analyzed.
- The experiment is conducted in a German university sample, which may limit generalizability to other populations or cultures.
- Antisocial punishment is shown to increase when punishment is free, but the direct impact on group efficiency is not measured.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so findings are limited to punishment-only settings.
- The study focuses on process-level (cognitive) mechanisms rather than aggregate welfare or efficiency outcomes.
