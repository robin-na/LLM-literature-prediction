## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of altruists (cooperators/punishers) vs defectors
- mean altruistic investment (I)
- population composition by type
- invasion capacity of cooperators
- distribution of altruistic investment
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the frequencies of different strategy types (altruists, defectors, punishers) and the mean level of altruistic investment. While payoffs are defined in the model, the reported simulation results focus on population composition and mean investment, not on group payoff, efficiency, or total welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The simulation results show that altruistic punishment alone does not sustain high levels of cooperation; defectors tend to dominate unless sexual attractiveness (mating preference for altruists) is present. When both punishment and sexual attractiveness are enabled, altruists (cooperators and punishers) become dominant, and the mean altruistic investment approaches its maximum. Variable population size and individual variation in investment further amplify the prevalence of altruism. The model is a close variant of a public goods game, but the main outcomes are population composition and mean investment, not efficiency or group payoff. Thus, the findings are most relevant for predicting the conditions under which punishment can sustain high cooperation, but less directly informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment, when combined with additional incentives (such as sexual attractiveness or mating preference for altruists), can lead to high levels of cooperation in public-goods-like environments. However, in the absence of such additional incentives, punishment alone may not sustain cooperation. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes from punishment. Instead, it suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may depend on the presence of complementary mechanisms that reward altruists. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment's positive effect on efficiency is conditional and may be weak or absent without additional incentives.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size is set at N=400 in the baseline, with equal numbers of each type; variable population size is also explored. The model is population-based, not small-group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 5,000 periods (rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Baseline model uses fixed investment (I=1); extension allows continuous investment from 0 to 1.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Benefit from altruistic investment is shared equally; payoff function includes a benefit term with parameters beta and kappa, but no explicit MPCR is given. The structure is analogous to a PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is gamma (e.g., gamma=0.4 in main simulations).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is delta (e.g., delta=0.6 in main simulations).
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Sexual attractiveness (mate preference) acts as a reward for altruists; modeled as parameter s. Not a standard monetary reward, but a fitness benefit.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost of sexual attractiveness to the rewarder (female cooperator) is set to zero.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact per unit cost is parameter s (e.g., s=1 in main simulations).

## Important Limitations
- The model is a simulation, not an empirical or experimental study.
- Outcomes are reported as population composition and mean altruistic investment, not as efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare.
- The public goods game is modeled in a stylized, evolutionary way, not as a repeated game with explicit payoffs per round.
- Sexual attractiveness as a reward is not directly comparable to monetary or material rewards in standard PGG experiments.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes; findings are only indirectly relevant for efficiency prediction.
- Population size is large and variable, unlike typical small-group PGG experiments.
