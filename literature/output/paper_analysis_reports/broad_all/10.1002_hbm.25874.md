# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Neural mechanisms of the mood effects on third-party responses to injustice after unfair experiences
Authors: Xie, E. H., Liu, M. D., Liu, J. Q., Gao, X. X., & Li, X. C.
Journal: Human Brain Mapping
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- endorsement rate of third-party punishment
- endorsement rate of third-party compensation
- endorsement rate of acceptance
- mood (positive/negative emotion)
- sense of unfairness
- brain activation (fNIRS, DLPFC activity)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the rates at which participants choose to punish, compensate, or accept as third parties after experiencing unfairness. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. The study focuses on behavioral and neural responses, not on efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that individuals who experience disadvantageous unfairness (receiving less than others) are more likely to choose third-party punishment in a subsequent justice game, while those who experience advantageous unfairness (receiving more than others) are more likely to choose third-party compensation. These effects are mediated by mood (negative emotion increases punishment, positive emotion increases compensation) and sense of unfairness. Neural data show that increased DLPFC activity is associated with compensation after advantageous unfairness, while decreased DLPFC activity is associated with punishment after disadvantageous unfairness. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, and the design is not a standard public goods game but a third-party justice game with costly punishment and compensation options.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. The design is adjacent to PGGs, focusing on third-party responses to unfairness rather than first-party cooperation or group provision. However, it provides informative evidence about the psychological and neural mechanisms that may underlie punishment and compensation choices, and how prior experiences and mood can shift the likelihood of punishment versus compensation. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address how punishment affects group payoff or efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each justice game round involves three roles: allocator, receiver, and third-party observer. The main experimental manipulation is on the observer, but the game is always a three-person interaction.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session (before and after unfair experience) consists of 40 trials per participant.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: accept, compensate, or punish. No continuous contribution; each is a categorical choice.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (and compensation, and accept) each cost the third party 3 yuan per action.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment reduces the advantageous player's outcome; the exact impact per unit cost is not numerically specified, but the mechanism is described.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation is available as a costly option, functionally a reward to the disadvantaged party.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation (reward) costs the third party 3 yuan per action.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation increases the disadvantaged player's outcome by taking from the advantaged player to equalize outcomes; the exact impact per unit cost is not numerically specified.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 40 rounds per session.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the monetary allocation between the two other players on each trial, but do not see further summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or any aggregate payoff-based outcome.
- The design is a third-party justice game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant.
- Outcomes are behavioral (punishment/compensation rates) and neural (DLPFC activity), not economic efficiency.
- The effect of punishment on group efficiency or welfare cannot be inferred from the reported results.
- The impact of punishment and compensation is only measured in terms of individual choice rates, not group-level outcomes.
- The neural findings are limited to cortical surface activity due to fNIRS constraints.
