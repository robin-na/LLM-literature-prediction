## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that human cooperation evolved in two major transitions: first, among Pleistocene foragers, where cooperation was individually advantageous and stabilized by mutualism, reciprocation, and punishment of freeriders (both shirkers and bullies); and second, in the Holocene, where larger, more hierarchical societies required new mechanisms (including group selection and intergroup conflict) to maintain cooperation. Punishment is discussed as a key mechanism for controlling freeriding and maintaining cooperation, but the analysis is conceptual and evolutionary rather than experimental or quantitative. The paper references experimental public goods games and their findings (e.g., punishment stabilizes cooperation), but only as background for theoretical arguments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and norm enforcement may affect cooperation and, by extension, efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence that can be directly used to predict efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions. However, it supports the idea that punishment is more likely to stabilize cooperation (and thus efficiency) in small, transparent, repeated-interaction groups, and that the effectiveness of punishment may depend on social structure, group size, and transparency. For prediction tasks, this paper is best used as background for mechanism hypotheses rather than as a source of direct predictive evidence.
