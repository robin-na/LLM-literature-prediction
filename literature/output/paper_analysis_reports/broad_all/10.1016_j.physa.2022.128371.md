Outcome notes:
The paper studies the evolutionary dynamics of cooperation, trust, and corruption in a model with central sanctioning institutions, but does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare. Outcomes are mainly the prevalence of cooperation and trust under different parameter regimes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that cooperation can persist in the presence of corrupt central sanctioning institutions, provided the effectiveness of sanctions (exclusion probability) is high and the corruption rate (embezzlement) is not too large. The model shows that players are more willing to tolerate exploitation by corrupt third parties than by free-riders, and that the evolutionary stability of cooperation depends on the balance between the benefits of cooperation, the costs of joining/leaving the institution, the intensity of exclusion, and the corruption rate. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as outcomes, focusing instead on the prevalence of cooperation and trust strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment (exclusion) and the cost/benefit structure are critical for sustaining cooperation, even when institutions are corrupt. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The results imply that punishment can support cooperation under a range of conditions, but the translation to efficiency is not explicit. The model is adjacent to public goods games but differs in structure (prisoner's dilemma with exclusion and embezzlement).

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Simulations use N=5000 players and M=500 third parties; population size is varied in robustness checks, but no direct effect on efficiency is reported.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Simulations run for 10,000 time steps; evolutionary dynamics are the focus, not discrete rounds as in standard PGG.

- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Equivalent to b/N in the two-player prisoner's dilemma; b and c are varied in the model, but not in standard PGG form.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Players pay a fixed membership fee t to the third party for sanctioning; t is varied in the model.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as exclusion probability rho; higher rho supports cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are proportions of strategies (cooperation, trust, corruption), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The game is a prisoner's dilemma with exclusion and embezzlement, not a standard public goods game.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes; results are not directly transferable to efficiency prediction.
- Assumes independence of exclusion effectiveness and corruption rate, which may not hold in real institutions.
- No consideration of peer punishment or decentralized sanctioning institutions.
