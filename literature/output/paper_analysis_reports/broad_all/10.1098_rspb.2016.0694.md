# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The price of being seen to be just: an intention signalling strategy for indirect reciprocity
Authors: Tanaka, H., Ohtsuki, H., & Ohtsubo, Y.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of use of costly signalling option after different types of defection
- giving rates to recipients with different signalling/reputation histories
- use of second-order information in cooperation decisions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: use of the costly signal (abandoning a resource), and rates of giving to others based on their previous behavior. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of treatment is reported. Participants' cumulative earnings are mentioned as feedback, but not as an outcome variable.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that participants in a dyadic donation game use a costly signalling option (abandoning a resource) to manage their reputation, especially after justified defection or implementation error. Others interpret this signal as a sign of cooperative intent and are more likely to cooperate with signallers. The signalling option allows players to maintain a good reputation without requiring second-order information. The study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that such signalling can stabilize cooperation in indirect reciprocity settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. The experimental design is a dyadic donation game, not a multi-player PGG, and the 'punishment' is a costly self-imposed signal (abandoning a resource) rather than a punishment directed at others. The main outcomes are behavioral (signalling and cooperation rates), not efficiency or payoff. However, the findings suggest that providing players with a costly signalling option can help maintain cooperation in the absence of second-order information, which may be relevant for understanding mechanisms that stabilize cooperation in related environments. The results do not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants were told they were playing with five others, but the game is dyadic with random pairing each round. No direct manipulation of group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Practice session: 50 rounds; main session: 100 rounds per participant.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: 'give' or 'not give' the resource.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the donation game, donor gives 5 JPY, recipient receives 10 JPY. MPCR is 2.0 for the recipient, but the game is dyadic, not a standard PGG.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the number of rounds in each session (50 practice, 100 main).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In experiment 1, recipients were informed of their own cumulative earnings every five rounds, but not of donor choices. In experiment 2, recipients were informed of donor choices every round and cumulative earnings.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is a dyadic donation game, not a multi-player public goods game.
- No direct punishment or sanctioning of others; only costly self-signalling is studied.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of treatment.
- All interactions were with computer-programmed partners, not real human groups.
- Findings may not generalize to standard PGGs or to environments with explicit punishment mechanisms.
