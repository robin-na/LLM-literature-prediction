# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Fairness is What You Can Get Away With: Proposer and Responder Behaviour in a Collective Action Ultimatum Game
Authors: Gordon, D., & Puurtinen, M.
Journal: Social Psychological Bulletin
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total earnings (Proposer and Responder)
- equality of earnings between roles
- mean losses per round
- Proposer offers
- Responder earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Responder rejection attempts
- Responder contributions to collective pot
- perceptions of power and fairness
- perceived fairness of allocations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based (earnings, equality of earnings, offers kept/given), but the study also reports on behavioral outcomes (rejection attempts, contributions) and post-experiment perceptions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The ease of collective action (operationalized as the probability of successful group punishment for a given investment) strongly affects both proposer and responder behavior in a multiplayer ultimatum game. When punishment is easy and effective, proposers immediately offer more equal splits, and group earnings are more equalized, with no significant difference between proposer and responder earnings. When punishment is hard or impossible, proposers keep more, and earnings are more unequal. Responders contribute more to punishment attempts when punishment is harder, but are also more likely to accept unfair offers as the game progresses. The main efficiency-related finding is that easier, more effective punishment leads to more equal and efficient group outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness and cost of punishment (here, the ease of collective action) is a key moderator of the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public-goods-like environments. When punishment is easy and effective, efficiency (measured as equality of earnings and group-level payoffs) increases substantially compared to control (no punishment). When punishment is hard or impossible, efficiency gains are minimal or absent. For prediction, this suggests that the punishment cost and effectiveness dimensions (punishment_cost, punishment_tech) are critical: low cost/high effectiveness punishment is likely to yield large efficiency gains, while high cost/low effectiveness punishment may not. The study also shows that group size (player_count = 4) and repeated rounds (num_rounds = 20) can support these effects, but the main moderator is the punishment mechanism.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4: 1 Proposer and 3 Responders per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session; participants were not told the number of rounds in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; participants were in isolated cubicles and only interacted via the computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices (Proposer offers and Responder contributions to the collective pot) are continuous, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is the number of points each Responder must contribute to the collective pot to attempt a rejection. Cost varies by condition: in 'Easy', low cost for high probability; in 'Hard', high cost for same probability. All contributions are lost regardless of success.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (probability of successful rejection per unit contributed) is manipulated: 'Easy' (convex, high effectiveness), 'Medium' (linear), 'Hard' (concave, low effectiveness), 'Impossible' (no punishment possible). Higher effectiveness leads to more equal and efficient outcomes.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told how many rounds would be played (unknown game length).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players only saw their own earnings and whether a rejection was successful; they did not see others' contributions or payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (rejection) is anonymous; neither Proposer nor Responders know which Responder contributed or how much.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a modified ultimatum game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG environments.
- Punishment is implemented as probabilistic group rejection, not as direct costly punishment of individuals.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment (rejection) is studied.
- Responder behavior is contingent on Proposer offers, making it difficult to fully disentangle responder sensitivity from proposer behavior.
- The design allows a single Responder to trigger rejection in the Easy condition, which may inflate rejection rates compared to real-world collective action.
- Participants did not know the number of rounds, which may affect end-game behavior.
- No communication or chat was allowed, which may limit coordination compared to real-world collective action.
- Sample size may be insufficient to detect small or medium effects in some analyses (e.g., fairness perceptions).
