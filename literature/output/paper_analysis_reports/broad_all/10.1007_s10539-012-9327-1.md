## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Most referenced PGG studies use 4-player groups; group size is reported in Table 2 but not systematically analyzed for its effect on efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Longer games (more rounds) allow punishment to become more efficient over time; short games do not. This is a central moderator in the paper.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is reported for each study (e.g., 1:3, 1:4), but not systematically analyzed for its moderating effect.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is discussed as a key factor; high costs can make punishment inefficient, but declining costs over time can reverse this.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is varied in some studies; higher effectiveness leads to higher efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Standard PGGs with known, fixed number of rounds are analyzed; the paper discusses the effect of time horizon and the 'shadow of the future'.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Standard PGGs typically provide feedback on others' contributions and payoffs, but this is not a focus of analysis.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily a theoretical and synthetic review, not a new empirical study.
- Findings are based on reanalysis and synthesis of existing studies, which differ in design and may not be directly comparable.
- Most laboratory PGGs analyzed use short time horizons and artificial settings, limiting ecological validity.
- The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is most evident in longer games and stable groups, which are less common in laboratory studies.
- The paper does not systematically analyze all game design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR) as moderators.
- Real-world evidence is discussed qualitatively and may not map directly onto laboratory PGG parameters.
