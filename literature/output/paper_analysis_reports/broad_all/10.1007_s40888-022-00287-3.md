# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On the propensity to settle or litigate in laboratory disputes
Authors: Migheli, M., & Saraceno, M.
Journal: Economia Politica
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of offender restoring (settling) after learning victim's resource origin
- probability of offender restoring to avoid litigation
- probability of victim initiating litigation
- effects of gender, religiosity, and litigation fee on above behaviors
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: restoration (settlement) by offenders, and litigation initiation by victims. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The design is a two-player dispute/litigation game, not a public goods game or direct social dilemma.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that the threat of costly, uncertain punishment (litigation) is a strong motivator for offenders to restore what they took, more so than fairness concerns about the victim's effort. Offenders are more likely to restore when they learn the victim worked for their endowment, but this effect is mainly present among female offenders. Higher litigation fees increase the probability that offenders restore to avoid litigation and decrease the probability that victims initiate litigation, with gender moderating these effects. The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral responses to punishment threats and fairness cues.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the behavioral effects of costly, uncertain punishment threats (litigation) in a two-player dispute context, not a public goods game. It shows that punishment threats (here, litigation) increase the likelihood of restoration (settlement), especially when the cost of punishment is high. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it involves second-party costly punishment with the possibility of redress, but lacks the multi-player, repeated, or contribution structure of PGGs. The main relevance is for understanding how punishment cost and fairness cues affect compliance and dispute resolution, not efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (offender and victim) interactions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair plays a single-shot (one-round) game; no repeated interactions.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all interactions are via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The offender can take a fixed amount (€2) or not; restoration is also all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Litigation fee (punishment cost) is manipulated: €1 (low) or €3 (high). Higher cost increases restoration to avoid litigation and decreases litigation initiation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (litigation) is probabilistic: 50% chance of full restoration if litigation is initiated. This is fixed and known to participants.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is a single-shot (one round) interaction.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the origin of the victim's resources and the outcomes of restoration/litigation decisions, but not about other pairs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the identity of their counterpart in the sense of role (A or B), but all interactions are anonymous; no identification of who punished beyond the dyad.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or direct social dilemma; it is a two-player dispute/litigation game, so findings may not generalize to multi-player or repeated PGG contexts.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (restoration, litigation) are analyzed.
- The sample consists of undergraduate students, which may limit external validity.
- Risk aversion was not directly measured, though random assignment is argued to mitigate this.
- The design does not include communication, repeated interaction, or contribution choices typical of PGGs.
