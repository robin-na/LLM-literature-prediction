# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: (Please Don't) Say It to My Face! The Interaction of Feedback and Distance: Experiments with Vulgar Language
Authors: Johnson, D. B.
Journal: Kyklos
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- proposer offer size (in ultimatum game)
- responder minimum acceptable offer
- proposer and responder earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and content of feedback (positive/negative/vulgar)
- acceptance/rejection rates
- variance in offers
- correlation between offer size and feedback valence
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the size of the offer made by the proposer in the ultimatum game, which is interpreted as a measure of generosity or fairness. While this is not group efficiency in the public goods sense, it is a closely related payoff-based outcome in the context of bargaining. The paper does not use the term 'efficiency' in the PGG sense, but does discuss monetary outcomes and the effect of feedback (punishment) on these outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The possibility of non-monetary punishment via unrestricted feedback increases proposer offers in the lab (where social distance is low), but not in online settings (where social distance is high). In the lab, known possible feedback leads to significantly higher offers, suggesting that the threat of negative feedback (punishment) can increase pro-social behavior and potentially improve monetary outcomes. However, in online/anonymous settings, the same feedback mechanism does not affect offers. The effect of punishment via feedback is thus moderated by social distance/proximity. The paper does not directly measure group efficiency as in public goods games, but the findings are relevant for understanding when non-monetary punishment can improve monetary outcomes in bargaining environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (here, non-monetary feedback) in increasing efficiency or pro-social monetary outcomes depends strongly on social distance and context. In environments with low social distance (e.g., lab, face-to-face), the threat of punishment can increase offers and thus improve monetary outcomes. In high social distance or anonymous environments (e.g., online), punishment has little or no effect on offers. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive only when social distance is low and the punishment is salient to the decision-maker. The findings are adjacent to public goods games but provide informative indirect evidence about the context-dependence of punishment effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player ultimatum games (one proposer, one responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) ultimatum games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Responder can send free-form (unrestricted) feedback to proposer after the offer; this is one-way and not strategic. The presence of this feedback is the main treatment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (feedback) is non-monetary and costless to the responder in terms of experimental currency, but may have non-monetary (psychological) costs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as unrestricted, potentially vulgar, free-form feedback. Its effectiveness depends on social distance: more effective in lab (low distance), ineffective online (high distance).
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Feedback can be positive or negative, so non-monetary rewards (praise) are possible, but there is no monetary reward mechanism.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Non-monetary reward (positive feedback) is costless in experimental currency.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward is implemented as free-form positive feedback; its impact is not directly analyzed but is present.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is a one-shot ultimatum game.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the lab, there is imperfect anonymity (subjects see each other before the experiment, but decisions are anonymous); online, there is perfect anonymity. The effectiveness of punishment depends on this identification/social distance.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, not a public goods game, so findings are only adjacent to PGG environments.
- Efficiency is not measured in the PGG sense; the main outcome is proposer offer size, not group payoff relative to a cooperative benchmark.
- Punishment is non-monetary (feedback), not costly in experimental currency, and may not generalize to monetary punishment mechanisms.
- The effect of punishment is highly context-dependent (social distance/proximity), limiting generalizability to other environments.
- Sample sizes are moderate and the study is limited to US/Canadian university students and AMT workers under 30.
- The study does not analyze repeated interactions or dynamic effects, only one-shot games.
