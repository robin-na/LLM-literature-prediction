The main dependent variable is the long-run population payoff (efficiency), with explicit analysis of steady-state payoffs under different reputation mechanisms. The paper also analyzes the evolution of cooperation and reputation distributions, but these are secondary to the payoff analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in the SRPD, only a small subset of reputation mechanisms can sustain cooperation and achieve efficient outcomes. Specifically, mechanisms that distinguish between justified punishment and defection, and allow for penitence, can support equilibria where the long-run group payoff matches the fully cooperative outcome. The analysis shows that, with these mechanisms and high enough continuation probability, the population evolves toward full cooperation and efficiency. In contrast, most reputation mechanisms result in universal defection and zero efficiency. The paper also extends the analysis to clan-based (group) settings, showing that the efficiency result generalizes when within-group matching is sufficiently likely.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that the structure of the punishment/reputation mechanism is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated social dilemmas. If the punishment mechanism is designed to avoid penalizing justified punishers and allows for penitence, and if the continuation probability is high, then enabling punishment can move efficiency from zero (universal defection) to the full-cooperation benchmark. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the details of the punishment/reputation update rule and the effective 'delta' (continuation probability) are crucial for forecasting the efficiency impact of punishment. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, anonymous-matching games with public reputations, but the logic may transfer to PGG-like settings with similar reputation mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The core model is a two-player (dyadic) repeated game (Prisoner's Dilemma). Clan-based extensions consider groups, but the main analysis is for N=2.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is indefinitely repeated with random stopping (infinite horizon, geometric discounting).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Action space is binary: Cooperate (C) or Defect (D).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff structure is parameterized by b (benefit to defector) and c (cost to cooperator), with full cooperation normalized to 1 per player. Not a standard PGG MPCR, but analogous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: playing D against a Bad reputation is not penalized. There is no explicit cost to punishing, but the mechanism is designed so that justified punishment does not harm the punisher's reputation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via reputation downgrades; the effectiveness is determined by the RM's update rule. Efficient RMs ensure that justified punishment is effective and not self-damaging.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is indefinitely repeated with random stopping; players do not know the exact number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reputations are public; players know the reputation of their match partner before acting.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model is limited to two-player (dyadic) repeated games, with only brief discussion of group (clan) extensions.
- The results depend critically on the structure of the reputation mechanism; most real-world or experimental settings may not implement the efficient RM exactly.
- The model assumes perfect public reputations and error-free updating, which may not hold in practice.
- No explicit cost to punishment is modeled; punishment is implemented via reputation downgrades, not material payoffs.
- The setting is not a standard public goods game, but a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with reputation.
