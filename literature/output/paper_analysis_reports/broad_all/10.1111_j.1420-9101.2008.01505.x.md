## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the threat of terminating an interaction (partner control) can suppress cheating and maintain cooperation, but its effectiveness depends on the relative abundance of exploiters and victims (market effects). When victims are rare, they can enforce cooperation and achieve higher payoffs; when they are common, exploiters can cheat more, reducing the efficiency of the interaction. If exploiters gain more control over the duration of interaction, they can extract more benefit, potentially driving the system into parasitism (negative payoffs for the victim). The model provides explicit conditions for when mutualism (positive payoffs) or parasitism (negative payoffs) will occur, and shows that efficiency is maximized when partner control is strong and the cost of finding new partners is high for exploiters.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment-like partner control on efficiency in public-goods-like environments is highly sensitive to the structure of partner choice and market conditions. If the 'punishment' (exit threat) is credible and the cost of finding new partners is high for exploiters, efficiency is increased. If exploiters can easily find new victims, or if they gain control over the interaction, efficiency can decrease or even become negative for the exploited party. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will depend on parameters analogous to partner abundance, search costs, and control over interaction duration. The model does not map directly onto standard PGGs, but its logic is relevant for settings where partner choice or exit is possible.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested within this paper.
- It does not map directly onto standard public goods games; it models dyadic, asymmetric mutualisms with partner choice and exit, not n-player PGGs.
- No explicit group size, marginal per-capita return, or round structure as in PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as exit/termination, not as costly assignment of punishment points or fines.
- Assumes random matching and does not consider reputation or indirect reciprocity.
- Assumes benefit functions and search costs that may not generalize to all real-world or experimental settings.
