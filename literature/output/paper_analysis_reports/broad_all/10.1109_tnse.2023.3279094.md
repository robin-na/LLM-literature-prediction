## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in structured populations (regular networks), the optimal allocation of a fixed institutional incentive budget between reward and punishment depends critically on the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment. When punishment is more efficient, pure punishment is optimal for maximizing cooperation and minimizing the payoff advantage of defectors. When reward is more efficient, pure reward is optimal. For intermediate efficiency ratios, an adaptive hybrid protocol—rewarding when cooperation is low, punishing when it is high—is optimal. These results are derived analytically and verified numerically. The findings differ from well-mixed populations, where the adaptive hybrid protocol is always optimal regardless of efficiency ratio. The main outcome is the expected payoff difference between defectors and cooperators, which is minimized under the optimal protocol, leading to higher group efficiency and cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games with institutional incentives on regular networks. The key moderator is the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment: if punishment is more efficient, enabling punishment (with a fixed incentive budget) increases efficiency and cooperation more than reward; if reward is more efficient, the reverse holds. For intermediate ratios, a state-dependent switch is optimal. The results are most applicable to games with institutional (not peer) incentives, fixed budgets, and structured (not well-mixed) populations. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (vs. reward or hybrid) given knowledge of the incentive budget, the efficiency of each incentive, and the network structure.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and based on evolutionary dynamics in infinite structured populations (regular networks); no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes institutional (not peer) incentives, with a fixed budget allocated between reward and punishment; results may not generalize to peer punishment or variable budgets.
- The population is assumed to be infinitely large and the network static; dynamic or finite networks are not analyzed.
- The model assumes perfect implementation of incentives; real-world imperfections in punishment or reward are not considered.
- The results are specific to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices and may not generalize to continuous contribution settings.
