# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Potential influence of decision time on punishment behavior and its evaluation
Authors: Maeda, K., Kumai, Y., & Hashimoto, H.
Journal: Frontiers in Psychology
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned
- impression evaluation of punishers and non-punishers
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount of punishment assigned (how much participants pay to punish a non-cooperator/selfish actor) and the impression evaluation of punishers and non-punishers (on a -4 to +4 scale). No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that participants assign more punishment under time pressure (intuition) than after deliberation, especially in one-shot PGG and second-party punishment games. However, punishers are evaluated more positively when their punishment is deliberative rather than intuitive, and when they are third-party (not direct victims). The study does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so it does not directly inform how punishment affects efficiency in PGG-like games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in PGG or related games. It does, however, provide informative context about how punishment behavior and its social evaluation depend on decision time and the role of the punisher. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that the psychological and reputational consequences of punishment may depend on whether punishment is seen as deliberative or intuitive, and whether it is administered by a direct victim or a third party. However, since efficiency or payoff outcomes are not measured, the paper cannot be used to calibrate the effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG groups had 4 players; SPPG had 2 players; TPPG had 3 players. The group size is fixed and known for each game type.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1 manipulates one-round (single-shot) vs. repeated PGG (number of rounds not specified, but repeated with same group). Study 2 is one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment choices are discrete but not all-or-nothing: participants choose from several options (e.g., JPY 0, 100, 200, 300, 400 for punishment).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In PGG, total contributions are doubled and divided equally among 4 players (MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:3; for each JPY 100 spent, the target loses JPY 300.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (target loses 3x the cost paid by punisher).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are explicitly told whether the game is one-shot or repeated (with same group).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown summary information about group contributions and payoffs (via feedback sheet), though the information is faked to ensure a non-cooperator is always present.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not know who punished whom. Evaluations are hypothetical/impression-based, not based on actual identification.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- All participants are young Japanese female undergraduates, limiting generalizability.
- The use of deception (fake feedback about group contributions) may affect external validity.
- The deliberation condition always follows the intuition condition, which may introduce order effects.
- Impression evaluation is measured with a single Likert scale, not with behavioral or incentivized choices.
- No between-subjects or counterbalanced design for decision time manipulation.
