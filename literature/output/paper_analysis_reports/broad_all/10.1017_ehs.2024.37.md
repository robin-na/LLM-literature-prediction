## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model shows that cooperation can evolve in collective action dilemmas when injunctive norms strongly encourage punishment, but not when they only encourage cooperation. Conformity (descriptive norm psychology) alone can support intermediate cooperation, but is unstable and easily undermined. When conformity is exapted (pre-existing), it can expand the conditions under which punishment-based cooperation evolves, acting as a scaffold for the emergence of stable, high-cooperation societies. The model identifies two stable types of cooperative societies: those based on conformity (unstable, lower cooperation) and those based on punishment (stable, higher cooperation). However, all outcomes are reported in terms of cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the importance of punishment norms (and the cost/benefit structure of punishment) in enabling high cooperation in public-goods-like environments, especially when conformity is present. However, because the model does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment is more effective than conformity at sustaining high cooperation, and that the presence of conformity can expand the parameter space where punishment-based cooperation emerges. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment mechanisms (especially when normatively encouraged) are likely to increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size is fixed at 16 in all simulations (n = 16).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each simulation run involves 40 rounds of collective action per generation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both cooperation and punishment are binary (0 or 1) choices.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The payoff structure is a general social dilemma; the exact MPCR is not specified, but the model is designed to capture the conflict between individual and group interest.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is costly to the punisher; exact cost per unit is not specified in the main text, but the cost is sufficient to make punishment a non-trivial choice.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment harms all defectors in the group; the impact per unit cost is not numerically specified in the main text.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (40) is fixed and known in the simulation structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents observe the frequency of cooperation and punishment among other group members in the previous round, which informs descriptive norms.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; all main outcomes are cooperation and punishment rates.
- The payoff structure is not specified in detail (e.g., exact MPCR, punishment cost/impact), limiting quantitative mapping to empirical PGGs.
- No reward mechanism is modeled; only punishment and conformity are considered.
- Assumes binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation and punishment choices, which may differ from continuous-contribution PGGs.
- Assumes no communication or identification of punishers.
- Assumes random strategy acquisition at the start of each generation; vertical transmission is not the default.
- Injunctive and descriptive norms are modeled as independent and exogenous (for injunctive norms), which may not reflect real-world endogeneity.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with endogenous or evolving injunctive norms, or with different transmission mechanisms.
