# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Juvenile cleaner fish can socially learn the consequences of cheating
Authors: Truskanov, N., Emery, Y., & Bshary, R.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of cooperative (against-preference) feeding choices
- preference for tolerant vs. responsive (punishing/fleeing) plates
- copying of arbitrary demonstrator preferences
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: how often juveniles feed against their preference (cooperatively), and their choice of partners (plates) based on observed consequences. No direct measurement of group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings is reported. The design is a dyadic animal model, not a multi-player public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Juvenile cleaner fish, after observing adults interacting with model clients (plates) that respond negatively to cheating (either by fleeing or punishing), subsequently behave more cooperatively (feed more against their preference) and prefer partners that are tolerant to cheating. Social learning about the consequences of cheating shapes cooperative strategies, but juveniles do not simply copy arbitrary preferences of adults. The study provides evidence that social learning about sanctions can promote cooperation in a non-human animal, but does not directly measure efficiency or group payoff as in economic games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that observation of punishment or negative partner responses can increase cooperative behavior in a dyadic animal model. While not a public goods game, the findings suggest that the presence of observable sanctions can bias individuals toward more cooperative choices, which in analogous human or multi-player games could translate to higher efficiency if similar mechanisms operate. However, the lack of direct payoff or efficiency measurement, and the dyadic (not group) structure, limit direct transfer to the prediction of efficiency in public goods games with punishment. The main value is mechanistic: social learning about sanctions can promote cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments are dyadic: one juvenile observer, one adult demonstrator, and a model client (plate). Not a multi-player game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of trials per phase is specified (e.g., 14 demonstrations, 15 test trials in Experiment 1; 10 test trials in Experiment 2).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (feeding) is discrete: choice between prawn (cheating) and flake (cooperation) items; each feeding event is a binary choice.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as the plate chasing the cleaner for ~3s or fleeing; the effect is to end or disrupt feeding opportunity.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of trials is fixed and known to the experimenters; not clear if fish can anticipate the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Juvenile observers can see the demonstrator's interaction with the plate and the plate's response, but not summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Observers can directly see which plate (client) delivers punishment or negative response.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental paradigm is dyadic (one observer, one demonstrator, one model client) and does not model group interactions or public goods games.
- No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; outcomes are behavioral (cooperation/cheating choices, partner preference).
- Punishment is simulated by experimenter-controlled plate behavior, not by actual agents incurring costs.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player or human public goods games due to species, context, and structural differences.
- No manipulation or measurement of marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, or reward mechanisms.
