# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social image concerns promote cooperation more than altruistic punishment
Authors: Grimalda, G., Pondorfer, A., & Tracer, D. P.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per treatment
- payoff net of punishment costs
- group welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- punishment frequency
- anti-social punishment
- altruistic punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is average payoff (efficiency), but the paper also reports cooperation rates and punishment behaviors.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a one-shot, two-player prisoner's dilemma, the presence of a local authority (Big Man) as an observer increases cooperation and leads to the highest average payoffs, exceeding baseline. Introducing a costly punishment stage (where players can spend to reduce the other's payoff) does not increase cooperation above the social image condition and results in lower average payoffs than baseline, due to the costs incurred by both punisher and punished. When both social image and punishment are present, efficiency is still lower than with social image alone and remains below baseline. The presence of an external (outgroup) authority does not increase cooperation or payoffs. Thus, punishment is not an efficient mechanism for promoting cooperation in this setting, and social image concerns are more effective.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a one-shot, two-player PD with costly punishment, enabling punishment reduces efficiency compared to baseline (no punishment). The effect is robust: both punishment alone and punishment combined with social image concerns yield lower payoffs than baseline. The design is not a standard public goods game but is closely related (two-player PD with all-or-nothing contribution). The findings suggest that, for similar game designs (one-shot, small group, all-or-nothing, high punishment cost), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, even if it increases cooperation rates. The control (baseline) efficiency is higher than the punishment treatment efficiency. This evidence should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment in similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) prisoner's dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players; decisions made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: keep K10 or give K10.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If a player gives K10, the other receives K20 (researcher adds K10). Thus, MPCR for recipient is 2.0, but for the group, each K10 given yields K20 to the other, so total group benefit per K10 given is K20 (K10 cost to giver, K20 benefit to receiver).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punisher can spend K0, K2, or K4 to reduce the other's payoff by K0, K10, or K20, respectively. Thus, cost to punisher per K10 punishment is K2.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: K2 spent reduces other’s payoff by K10 (ratio 5:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot (single round).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a two-player, one-shot prisoner's dilemma, not a standard multi-player public goods game; generalizability to larger groups or repeated games is limited.
- The setting is a small-scale, non-Western society; results may differ in other cultural or institutional contexts.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so findings do not address the effect of positive incentives.
- All-or-nothing contribution structure may not generalize to continuous contribution games.
- Punishment is costly and relatively severe (K2 to reduce K10), which may not match all PGG-like environments.
