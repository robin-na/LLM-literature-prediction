# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Decentralized management of common property resources: experiments with a centuries-old institution
Authors: Casari, M., & Plott, C. R.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group efficiency (percent of maximum rent)
- net efficiency (after inspection costs)
- group use (aggregate resource use)
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of inspections requested
- individual use levels
- inspection aggressiveness by type (spiteful/altruistic)
- heterogeneity in agent types
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group efficiency (ratio of group earnings to maximum possible), group use, and net efficiency (after deducting inspection costs). Non-payoff outcomes include inspection behavior and agent heterogeneity.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a common-pool resource environment with 8 players and continuous contribution/appropriation choices, the addition of a decentralized punishment mechanism (where agents can pay to inspect and fine others for overuse, with fines paid to the inspector) leads to large increases in group efficiency. Without sanctions, efficiency is low (28.4%), below the Nash equilibrium prediction. With weak sanctions, efficiency rises to 57.2% gross (48.3% net after inspection costs). With strong sanctions, efficiency reaches 94% gross (76.9% net). The classical model cannot explain the effect of weak sanctions, but a model with heterogeneous, linear other-regarding preferences (spiteful and altruistic agents) fits the data well. The mechanism channels spiteful behavior into socially useful monitoring. The effect is robust across subject experience and is not due to repeated game effects or communication.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling decentralized punishment in a common-pool resource game (close variant of a public goods game) can substantially increase efficiency, even when the punishment is relatively weak and should not be effective under standard selfish Nash equilibrium predictions. The effect is robust to subject experience and is not driven by communication or repeated game effects. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are: player_count=8, num_rounds~27-33, no chat, continuous contribution, no default, non-linear MPCR (parabolic then exponential decay), punishment cost and tech specified, no reward, number of rounds not known in advance, summary info provided, and punishment identity not revealed. The control (no-sanction) efficiency is low (28.4%), and the treatment (punishment enabled) efficiency is much higher (48-76% net, 57-94% gross depending on punishment strength). This paper should be weighted heavily in predicting efficiency gains from punishment in similar decentralized, continuous-action, no-communication environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. All experiments used groups of 8 players. The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured directly in this setting.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds per session was 27-33 (excluding 2 practice rounds). Subjects were not told the number of rounds in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between subjects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/appropriation choices were continuous (any real number between 0 and 50). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is non-linear: group revenue is a quadratic (parabolic) function of total group use up to a point, then exponential decay. Social optimum and Nash equilibrium are calculated for the given parameters.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (inspection) cost per inspection is k=7 tokens in both weak and strong sanction treatments.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost: in weak sanctions, fine is h=1 per unit overuse; in strong sanctions, h=4. Fine is proportional to overuse above threshold.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. The fine is paid to the inspector, so there is a reward for monitoring, but there is no separate positive reward mechanism for cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 'reward' is the fine paid to the inspector; the cost is the inspection fee (k=7). No separate reward system.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is the amount of fine transferred to the inspector per inspection. No separate reward for positive behavior.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were not told the number of rounds in advance; only notified two rounds before the end.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, total group use and gross group return were displayed. In sanction treatments, individual use levels of inspected agents were revealed to the inspector and became public if inspected.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Identity of the punished agent was publicly known after a fine; identity of the inspector was not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a common-pool resource (CPR) game, which is a close but not exact variant of the standard public goods game; results may not transfer perfectly to standard PGGs.
- The punishment mechanism is decentralized and involves inspection plus fine paid to the inspector, which differs from some PGG punishment designs where fines are paid to the experimenter or are a deadweight loss.
- The MPCR is non-linear and the action space is continuous, which may limit comparability to linear, discrete PGGs.
- No communication or chat was allowed; results may differ in settings with communication.
- The number of rounds was not known in advance, which may affect endgame behavior.
- The subject pool was from a single university (Caltech), which may limit generalizability.
- No explicit analysis of group size effects beyond N=8.
- No separate positive reward mechanism for cooperation; only punishment/inspection.
