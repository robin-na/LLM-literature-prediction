# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Criminal Defectors Lead to the Emergence of Cooperation in an Experimental, Adversarial Game
Authors: D'Orsogna, M. R., Kendall, R., McBride, M., & Short, M. B.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy frequencies (Paladin, Apathetic, Informant, Villain)
- transition rates between strategies
- reporting rates
- conviction rates
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the frequencies of different behavioral strategies and transitions between them. While payoffs are described in the game structure, the results focus on the prevalence of cooperation (Paladins), defection (Villains), and the role of Informants, not on aggregate group payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that the availability of the Informant strategy (criminals who also report/punish other criminals) is necessary for the emergence and stability of high cooperation in an adversarial game. When Informants are allowed, the system reliably transitions to and maintains a high-cooperation state (Utopia), regardless of initial conditions or parameter profile. When Informants are not allowed, the system converges to high defection (Dystopia). The results are robust across different initializations, parameter profiles (costs of reporting), and levels of behavioral inertia. However, the study does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare as outcome variables.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of a punishment mechanism (specifically, the Informant strategy) is crucial for the emergence and maintenance of cooperation in adversarial games. However, because the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategies rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The game is adjacent to PGGs, and the behavioral results suggest that enabling punishment (especially by defectors) can transform a high-defection environment into a high-cooperation one, which would likely increase efficiency if measured. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of this inference.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round, subjects are randomly paired into groups of two (dyads).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session lasts a minimum of 25 periods, with up to 35 periods possible (uncertainty about the final round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; anonymity is enforced.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (steal or not steal; report or not report) is all-or-nothing, not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to the criminal (if convicted) is θ = 45 points in both parameter profiles.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is effective: if convicted, the criminal pays back the stolen amount plus θ; conviction probability r depends on the fraction of reporters.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the minimum and maximum number of rounds, but each round after 25 is probabilistic (75% chance of continuation), so the exact end is uncertain.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see their own results and the outcome of their dyadic interaction, but there is no evidence that they see summary statistics about the whole group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Pairings and actions are anonymous; players do not know the identity of the other player or who punished/reported.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game but an adversarial dyadic game, so findings may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategies (cooperation/defection/punishment) rather than group efficiency or total payoff.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, welfare, or group payoff; only indirect inference is possible.
- The punishment mechanism is implemented via reporting and conviction, not peer-to-peer punishment as in many PGG experiments.
- The study does not analyze the effect of varying punishment cost or effectiveness beyond the two parameter profiles for retaliation cost.
