# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Negative reciprocity and its relation to anger-like emotions in identity-homogeneous and -heterogeneous groups
Authors: Bicskei, M., Lankau, M., & Bizer, K.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment points assigned (negative reciprocity)
- frequency of punishment
- anger-like emotions (ALEs)
- conditional and unconditional contributions
- cooperation type classification
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment behavior (negative reciprocity), frequency and intensity of punishment, and emotional responses. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes, though it discusses implications for social welfare in the discussion.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when peer punishment is available in a one-shot PGG, the likelihood and intensity of punishment for low contributions is significantly lower in identity-homogeneous (in-group) groups than in identity-heterogeneous (out-group) or random groups. Anger-like emotions (anger, contempt, irritation) are a strong driver of punishment, but their effect is moderated by group identity: they lead to more punishment in out-groups than in-groups. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but argues that higher punishment in out-groups, being costly, likely reduces social welfare relative to in-groups, where punishment is less frequent and less intense.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on behavior in PGGs is moderated by group identity: punishment is less frequent and less intense in in-groups, and anger-like emotions drive punishment more in out-groups. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in settings with identity-heterogeneous groups, punishment is more likely to be used, which (given the costliness of punishment) may reduce efficiency relative to in-groups. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms and moderators of punishment, but not about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 3.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each subject plays one PGG with punishment opportunity.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Chat is allowed only during the social identity induction task (Stage B), not during the PGG or punishment stages.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how many points (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each point contributed returns 0.4 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is non-linear: e.g., 1 point costs 1, 2 points cost 2, 3 points cost 4, up to 10 points costing 30 (see Table 2).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's income by 10%; 10 or more points reduce income by 100%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributions, subjects are shown how much each group member contributed (anonymously as player x and y).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment; only punishment behavior and emotional responses are analyzed.
- The experiment is one-shot, so findings may not generalize to repeated or long-term public goods games.
- The social identity manipulation is artificial (color groups), which may differ from natural identities in real-world settings.
- The cost and impact of punishment are non-linear and may not match standard linear punishment schemes.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
- The sample is from a university laboratory, which may limit external validity.
