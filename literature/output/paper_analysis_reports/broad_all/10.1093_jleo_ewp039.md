## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

Punishment is modeled as a sanction g imposed on defectors; its effectiveness is direct (defectors pay g). - reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards/subsidies are mentioned as a possible extension, but not analyzed in the main model.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot; all players know the structure and timing.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes a one-shot game with a large (infinite) population, which may not capture dynamics in repeated or small-group settings.
- Sanctions are set by a central authority with perfect information about the population distribution, which may not reflect real-world information constraints.
- The model does not analyze peer punishment, only centralized (government) punishment.
- Reward mechanisms are only mentioned as an extension and not analyzed in detail.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices, not continuous contributions.
