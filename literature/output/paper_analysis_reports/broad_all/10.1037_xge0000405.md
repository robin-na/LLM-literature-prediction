# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Learning Moral Values: Another's Desire to Punish Enhances One's Own Punitive Behavior
Authors: FeldmanHall, O., Otto, A. R., & Phelps, E. A.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Psychology-general
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- endorsement rate of punitive (Reverse/Reject) option
- endorsement rate of compensatory (Compensate) option
- change in punitive behavior after exposure to another's preferences
- learning model fit (reinforcement learning parameters)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate at which participants choose the punitive option (Reverse in the Justice Game, Reject in the Ultimatum Game) before and after exposure to another's punitive preferences. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The task is a dyadic justice game and ultimatum game, not a standard public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across four experiments, participants who learned about another's strong preference for punishment increased their own endorsement of punitive options when responding to unfair offers. This effect was observed both when participants actively implemented another's preferences and when they passively observed them. The effect generalized across online and lab settings, and from a justice game (with compensatory and punitive options) to the ultimatum game (where only costly punishment is available). The learning process is best described by a reinforcement learning model sensitive to the magnitude of unfairness. However, the paper does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare; the focus is on individual behavioral change in punitive choices.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that exposure to others' punitive preferences increases individuals' own willingness to punish in response to unfairness, even when punishment is costly. However, it does not report on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public goods or similar games. The design is dyadic and focused on justice restoration, not multi-player public goods provision. The findings are most relevant for predicting changes in punitive behavior, not efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players: Player A and Player B, with a third-party Player C in the learning phase).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Baseline phase: 4 trials; Learning phase: 80 trials; Transfer phase: 20 trials (Experiment 1). Other experiments have similar multi-trial structures.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/punishment choices are discrete: Accept, Compensate, or Reverse (Justice Game); Accept or Reject (Ultimatum Game). Not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Justice Game, punishment (Reverse) is 'free' for the punisher (Player B's payout is the same for Compensate and Reverse). In the Ultimatum Game, punishment (Reject) is costly (both players get nothing).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Justice Game, Reverse option swaps payoffs, maximally punishing Player A. In the Ultimatum Game, Reject results in zero payoff for both.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials/phases in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants receive explicit feedback about the Receiver's preferences in the Learning phase (trial-by-trial). No summary statistics about other players' payoffs or contributions.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is behavioral endorsement of punishment, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The game is dyadic (two players, sometimes with a third-party learning phase), not a multi-player public goods game.
- No analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes is reported.
- Findings may not generalize to standard public goods games or to settings where group efficiency is the primary concern.
- Punishment is sometimes costless (Justice Game) and sometimes costly (Ultimatum Game), but the effect on group outcomes is not analyzed.
