# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Worker Empowerment and Subjective Evaluation: On Building an Effective Conflict Culture
Authors: Macleod, W. B., Lara, V. V., & Zehnder, C.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total surplus
- group efficiency
- average surplus
- manager and worker payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- effort rate
- bonus payment frequency
- conflict (punishment) frequency
- norm compliance
- message/communication choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is total surplus (sum of manager and worker payoffs), which is directly analogous to efficiency. The paper also reports effort rates, bonus payment rates, and conflict rates, but these are secondary to the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the effect of enabling punishment (conflict) in a principal-agent game with subjective evaluation is highly context-dependent. In the baseline, communication, and agreement (code of conduct) experiments, allowing workers to punish managers for not paying bonuses does not increase efficiency: the costs of conflict outweigh any small increases in effort, and total surplus is lower or unchanged compared to the no-punishment condition. Workers and managers fail to coordinate on an efficiency-enhancing norm, and punishment is often misapplied. However, when conflicts are institutionalized through a formal grievance process (grievance experiment), workers and managers coordinate more on a pay-for-output norm, effort increases significantly, and total surplus is higher in the punishment-enabled condition. Thus, punishment can enhance efficiency, but only when supported by strong institutional framing and norm coordination.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform and is highly sensitive to the institutional environment, especially the presence of mechanisms that coordinate norms (such as a grievance procedure). In environments lacking such coordination, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency due to the direct costs of conflict. In contrast, when a formal grievance process is present, punishment can increase both effort and efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of institutionalized conflict resolution and norm-coordination mechanisms should be considered a key moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment) unless the institutional context is specified.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 16-24 participants, but the game is played in manager-worker pairs (2 players per group) with random rematching each round. The relevant 'group' for efficiency is the pair.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experiment lasted 15 periods (rounds) with random rematching each period.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Only the communication experiment allowed structured preplay communication via predefined messages; other experiments had no chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort choice is binary (high or low). In the first two experiments, punishment was continuous; in the last two, punishment was binary.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the conflict treatment, the cost to the punisher (worker) is 10 per unit of punishment (K=0.1, F=100, so cost=10 per full punishment); in the no-conflict treatment, cost is infinite (punishment disabled).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment reduces the manager's payoff by 100 per full punishment (F=100) at a cost of 10 to the worker (K=0.1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the experiment lasts 15 periods.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental environment is a principal-agent game with subjective evaluation, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to classic PGG settings.
- Punishment is only available to workers against managers, not peer-to-peer or group-based as in PGGs.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the presence of institutionalized grievance procedures; results may not apply to environments lacking such mechanisms.
- The experiments use binary effort and punishment choices (except for continuous punishment in the first two experiments), which may limit generalizability to settings with continuous contribution or punishment.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not speak to the interaction of punishment and reward.
- Participants are rematched each round, so repeated-game effects within fixed groups are not studied.
- The game is dyadic (manager-worker pairs), not multi-player as in standard PGGs.
- The study does not address real-world implementation challenges of grievance procedures or informal punishment channels.
