## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in indirect reciprocity games with binary reputations, only eight specific social norms (the 'leading eight') can maintain both evolutionary stability and a high level of cooperation/efficiency, even when errors in action and assessment occur. These norms require players to cooperate with good-reputation individuals, punish defectors, justify punishment, and allow for apology and forgiveness. Under these norms, the average payoff per interaction is close to the theoretical maximum (b-c), and the fraction of cooperative (good) players remains high, with only a small reduction due to errors. The paper provides analytical conditions for when these norms are stable and efficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in indirect reciprocity games with binary reputations and one-shot interactions, the structure of the social norm (reputation dynamics) is critical for achieving high efficiency. Specifically, norms that combine justified punishment (refusing to help bad actors and not being penalized for it) and forgiveness (allowing recovery from bad reputation) can maintain efficiency close to the maximum. While the game is not a standard public goods game, the findings are highly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms in repeated or reputation-based social dilemmas. The results suggest that, when punishment is structured as justified refusal to cooperate (and not as costly peer punishment), and when forgiveness is possible, efficiency losses due to punishment can be minimized or avoided. The paper does not address group size, communication, or other PGG-specific dimensions directly, but it does show that the design of sanctioning and reputation mechanisms is a key moderator of efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model assumes a large (effectively infinite) population with random pairwise interactions; group size is not a manipulated variable.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each player plays multiple one-shot games per generation, with the number of rounds per player following a geometric distribution (expected number determined by parameter ω).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution is binary: cooperate (pay cost c, give benefit b) or defect (pay nothing, give nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as refusal to cooperate with bad-reputation players, which is not costly to the punisher in the same way as peer punishment in PGGs. The cost is opportunity cost (foregone benefit of mutual cooperation), not a direct cost.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: defectors are immediately labeled as bad and refused cooperation, which deters defection and maintains high efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds; the number of rounds is stochastic (geometric distribution), and future rounds are discounted by ω.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reputation is public and shared among all players; everyone knows each other's reputation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a binary prisoner's dilemma with indirect reciprocity, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs with group interactions or continuous contributions.
- Punishment is modeled as justified refusal to cooperate, not as costly peer punishment; efficiency effects of costly punishment are not addressed.
- The model assumes public, shared reputation and does not consider private or noisy reputation systems.
- No analysis of group size, communication, or other PGG-specific design features.
- Assumes infinite or very large populations and random matching; does not address network or repeated group structure.
