# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Collusion as public monitoring becomes noisy: Experimental evidence
Authors: Aoyagi, M., & Fréchette, G.
Journal: Journal of Economic Theory
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- punishment received
- punishment decision
- punishment severity
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates and punishment behavior. The paper does not directly report group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings as a primary outcome, though it does mention efficiency loss in passing and provides some information on payoffs in the text.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that, in both homogeneous and heterogeneous groups, contributions decline over time in the absence of punishment, but increase over time when punishment is available. The opportunity to punish leads to higher contributions, with no significant difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. The origin of endowment (effort vs. windfall) does not affect contribution levels or punishment patterns. Punishment is used to enforce the equal-contribution rule, and both effort and windfall subjects contribute similarly. There is no evidence of a normative conflict arising from endowment heterogeneity. The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in contributions with punishment is consistent with higher efficiency, subject to the cost of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public good game with 4 players, 12 rounds, and a punishment stage, enabling punishment increases contributions regardless of whether endowments are earned or windfall. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions is a strong proxy for increased efficiency, though the cost of punishment may offset some gains. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward, anonymity of punishment, feedback on others' contributions) are all specified and match standard PGG lab designs. For prediction, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency (proxied by contributions) in similar game designs, and that endowment origin does not moderate this effect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consist of 4 players in the public good game phase (after the contest task in HET treatments).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public good game lasts for 12 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject chooses how much of 20 tokens to invest.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token invested returns 0.4 to each group member (MPCR = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly and convex: e.g., 1 point costs 1, 2 points cost 2, 3 points cost 4, up to 10 points costing 30.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished subject's payoff by 10%.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of periods (12) is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are informed of the total group contribution, return from the public project, return from tokens kept, and their overall earnings. In the punishment stage, they see individual contributions of group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; subjects cannot identify who punished whom. Types are also anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as a main outcome; all main results are in terms of contribution and punishment behavior.
- The cost of punishment is not subtracted from group earnings to report net efficiency, so the net effect of punishment on efficiency is not quantified.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability.
- The design does not allow for identification of types (effort vs. windfall) by group members, which may affect the emergence of property rights or equity norms.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results are limited to punishment-only environments.
