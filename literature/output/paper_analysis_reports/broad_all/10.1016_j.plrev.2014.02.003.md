Outcome notes:
The main reported outcome is 'group achievement' (fraction of groups that reach the public good threshold), which is closely related to efficiency but not exactly the same as total group payoff or welfare. Average payoff is present in the model but not the main focus of reported results. Most results are about cooperation rates and group achievement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing local (group-level) sanctioning institutions (punishment) in threshold public goods games with risk of collective loss significantly increases the fraction of groups that successfully achieve the public good, especially under low risk perception and high contribution thresholds. Global (population-wide) sanctioning institutions provide only marginal improvement. Smaller group sizes and higher risk perception also promote higher group achievement. Threshold uncertainty (uncertainty about the required contribution level) can sharply reduce group achievement. The model suggests that decentralized, polycentric punishment is more effective than centralized punishment for promoting cooperation and group success.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment (specifically, local/group-level sanctioning institutions) in threshold public goods games with risk of collective loss will increase group achievement (a close proxy for efficiency), especially when group size is small, risk perception is high, and contribution thresholds are stringent. The effect of punishment is much stronger when implemented locally rather than globally. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of local punishment should be expected to yield a more positive effect on efficiency-related outcomes than global punishment or no punishment, particularly in small groups and under high risk. However, the main outcome is group achievement, not exact efficiency, so predictions should be interpreted accordingly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Population size (Z) and group size (N) are both varied in the model. Smaller group sizes (lower N) lead to higher group achievement and more effective punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and assumes repeated updating, but does not specify a fixed number of rounds. Agreements are described as short-term and strategies are revised frequently.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is modeled as binary: Cooperators contribute a fixed fraction of their endowment, Defectors contribute nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by the payoff structure: if the threshold is met, all keep their endowment; if not, all lose it with probability r. The model varies c (contribution fraction), b (endowment), and n_pg (threshold).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishers pay a punishment tax (pi_tax = 0.03) to fund the institution; defectors are fined (pi_fine = 0.3) if the institution is established. The cost structure is explicit in the model.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the fine per unit tax (pi_fine/pi_tax). The model explores the effect of punishment magnitude and scale (local vs global).

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main outcome is 'group achievement' (fraction of groups reaching the public good), which is closely related to but not identical with efficiency or total group payoff.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contributions and does not allow for continuous or graded contribution levels.
- No modeling of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback.
- Threshold uncertainty is modeled, but other real-world complexities (e.g., wealth inequality, conditional cooperation, reactive strategies) are not included.
- The model does not specify a fixed number of rounds; results are based on evolutionary dynamics and stationary distributions.
