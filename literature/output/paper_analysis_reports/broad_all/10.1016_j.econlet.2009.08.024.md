# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The pleasure of being nasty
Authors: Abbink, K., & Sadrieh, A.
Journal: Economics Letters
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of destruction choices
- amount of destruction inflicted
- destruction as a function of endowment
- destruction as a function of observed destruction
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the frequency and amount of anti-social destruction (money burning) in a two-player game. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The main dependent variables are behavioral: how often and how much subjects destroy the other player's endowment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that a substantial fraction of subjects are willing to destroy the other player's endowment when their actions are hidden, but not when they are observable. The presence of anonymity increases anti-social behavior. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and is not a public goods or punishment game. The findings are relevant for understanding anti-social punishment and spite, but do not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The game studied is a two-player, anti-social destruction game with no cooperation or group payoff structure. The main insight is that anonymity increases anti-social behavior, but this is not directly transferable to the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. The findings may be contextually relevant for understanding anti-social punishment or spiteful behavior, but do not inform the prediction task in a quantitative or directly comparable way.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always played with two players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds in open treatment, 8 rounds in hidden treatment due to time constraints.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; subjects interacted anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Destruction choices are continuous in multiples of 10c between 0 and 80c.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Destruction is costless to the destroyer; no cost to punish.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit destroyed reduces the other player's endowment by the same amount; 1:1 impact.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told the number of rounds (aimed for 10, 8 in hidden treatment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the open treatment, destruction is perfectly observable; in the hidden treatment, only total destruction is observed (including random component).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the open treatment, the source of destruction is perfectly identifiable; in the hidden treatment, it is not.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a punishment game; it is a two-player anti-social destruction game.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (destruction rates) are analyzed.
- Findings about anti-social behavior under anonymity may not generalize to the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
- Small sample size (40 subjects) and only undergraduate students from one university.
- No analysis of cooperation, contribution, or norm compliance.
