# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Fair and unfair punishers coexist in the Ultimatum Game
Authors: Brañas-Garza, P., Espín, A. M., Exadaktylos, F., & Herrmann, B.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Rejection rates of unequal offers in the Ultimatum Game
- Dictator Game offer (fair/unfair/remaining)
- Classification of punishers as prosocial or antisocial
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the likelihood of rejecting unequal offers in the Ultimatum Game, analyzed in relation to behavior in the Dictator Game. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both prosocial and antisocial individuals engage in costly punishment in the Ultimatum Game, as measured by rejection of unequal offers. Prosocial punishers are those who also behave fairly in the Dictator Game, while antisocial punishers are unfair as dictators but still reject unequal offers in the Ultimatum Game. The coexistence of these types is robust across two large-scale experiments (one with a representative city sample, one with university students). The paper highlights that antisocial punishment may undermine the efficiency and stability of social systems, but does not provide direct experimental evidence on efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games, as it focuses on the motivations behind punishment in the Ultimatum Game. However, it offers important contextual evidence that not all punishment is prosocial or norm-enforcing; antisocial punishment can exist and may have negative implications for efficiency in broader social dilemmas. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment does not guarantee increased efficiency, especially if antisocial motives are prevalent. The findings are most relevant for understanding heterogeneity in punishment motives, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game and Dictator Game are both two-player games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject made one set of decisions per game; one-shot games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; decisions made anonymously.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (rejection) is costly: responder forgoes positive payoff to punish proposer.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is maximal: rejection leads to zero payoff for both players.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot; no repeated interaction.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, not a public goods game or direct variant, so findings may not transfer directly to multi-player cooperation dilemmas.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is provided.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of offers, which is a binary, maximal punishment, not a graded or targeted sanction as in many public goods experiments.
- The study focuses on individual motives for punishment, not on aggregate outcomes or efficiency effects.
- Population differences are discussed but not systematically analyzed for their impact on efficiency or cooperation in broader settings.
