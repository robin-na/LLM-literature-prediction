## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, both strong altruistic punishment and reward are unable to sustain cooperation when implemented individually and funded only by voluntary contributions, due to their high cost. However, when a tax is imposed on all participants and the proceeds are used to fund either punishment or reward institutions, cooperation and group efficiency are greatly enhanced. The model shows that with sufficient tax compensation, strong altruistic punishers or rewarders can coexist with cooperators, eliminating defectors and increasing group welfare. Furthermore, tax-based strong altruistic reward leads to higher levels of cooperation and efficiency than tax-based strong altruistic punishment under otherwise identical conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that institutional (tax-based) punishment and reward mechanisms can substantially increase group efficiency in public goods games, compared to traditional voluntary punishment/reward or no-sanction conditions. The results suggest that, for a given set of game design parameters (e.g., player count, MPCR, cost/effectiveness of punishment/reward), introducing a tax-funded punishment or reward institution will increase the average efficiency of the group, with reward institutions being even more effective than punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to settings where punishment/reward is implemented institutionally and funded by mandatory contributions (taxes), and less so to peer-to-peer or voluntary sanctioning. The results are based on evolutionary game theory and numerical simulations, not empirical experiments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model uses a group size parameter M, with numerical examples at M=15. The effect of player count is embedded in the replicator dynamics and payoff equations.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each individual either contributes a fixed cost c or nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/M, with r=3 and M=15 in numerical examples. The effect of MPCR is explicit in the payoff and stability equations.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers pay a cost H_SP to the punishment pool; the cost is a key parameter in the model.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized by ω (for defectors) and ε (for cooperators); these are varied in the model.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model includes both punishment and reward institutions, analyzed separately.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarders pay a cost H_SR to the reward pool; the cost is a key parameter in the model.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is parameterized by γ (for cooperators) and η (for rewarders); these are varied in the model.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and based on evolutionary game theory and numerical simulations; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model assumes infinite, well-mixed populations and does not account for finite group effects, learning, or bounded rationality.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as institutional (tax-based) mechanisms, not as peer-to-peer or voluntary actions; results may not generalize to other forms of punishment/reward.
- The model uses binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- No analysis of the effects of communication, information feedback, or repeated finite rounds is provided.
