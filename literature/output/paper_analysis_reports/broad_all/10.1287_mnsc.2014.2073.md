# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inducing Social Norms in Laboratory Allocation Choices
Authors: Schram, A., & Charness, G.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocation choice (selfishness vs. unselfishness)
- response to advice
- effect of public observability
- gender differences in norm adherence
- shared understanding of norms
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the allocation choice made by the 'dictator' (red player) in a one-shot allocation game. The study focuses on how advice (shared understanding) and public observability affect the degree of selfishness in choices. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare is conducted. Outcomes are behavioral, not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that providing impartial advice about what one 'ought to do' (shared understanding) only affects behavior when choices are made public, i.e., when social (not just moral) norms are salient. In the advice + public condition, dictators are significantly less selfish than in other conditions. Advice alone (private) does not change behavior, nor does public observability alone without advice. The effect is interpreted as the creation of a social norm, enforced by the threat of social disapproval (shame), not by material punishment. There is no direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus. The findings are relevant for understanding norm induction and the role of observability, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. It does not implement punishment, rewards, or repeated interaction, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. However, it does provide informative context about how social norms (via advice and observability) can shift behavior toward less selfish choices in allocation games. For prediction tasks, the main takeaway is that nonmaterial social sanctions (public observability) combined with shared understanding can induce prosocial behavior, but the absence of payoff-based outcomes and the lack of a PGG or punishment mechanism limit its direct applicability.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: one red (dictator), one green, one blue. Only the red player makes a decision.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment is strictly one-shot; each red player makes a single allocation decision.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chat is allowed only among advice group members (who do not make allocation decisions); no chat or communication is allowed among choice group members.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The red player chooses one of six discrete allocation options; not a continuous contribution.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are explicitly told the experiment is one-shot (single round).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or a repeated social dilemma; it is a one-shot allocation (dictator) game.
- No punishment or reward mechanism is implemented; social sanctions are nonmaterial (shame via public exposure), not monetary.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or surplus outcomes are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (allocation choice).
- Findings may not generalize to environments with repeated interaction, strategic punishment, or explicit payoff-based sanctions.
- Advice is provided by impartial peers, not by affected parties, and the advice mechanism may not map onto real-world norm enforcement.
