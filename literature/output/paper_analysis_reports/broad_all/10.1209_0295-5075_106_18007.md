## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a stress-based cost for defectors in a public goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable if the stress intensity exceeds a calculable threshold. This threshold is lower for larger populations and higher public goods multipliers, and higher for larger group sizes. The model provides explicit formulas for the fixation probability of cooperation and the conditions under which cooperation can invade and dominate a population of defectors. The findings are directly relevant to efficiency, as they show under what conditions the group can achieve higher payoffs through sustained cooperation, even in the absence of explicit external punishment mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that internalized costs for defection (modeled as stress or moral emotions) can substitute for explicit punishment in promoting cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games. While it does not study explicit punishment, it shows that any mechanism that imposes a cost on defection (whether internal or external) can shift the evolutionary dynamics toward cooperation, provided the cost is above a threshold determined by game parameters (group size, population size, and MPCR). For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of any effective cost for defection—whether via punishment or internalized emotion—can increase efficiency, and that the required cost decreases with larger populations and higher MPCR. However, the model does not address explicit punishment technology, cost, or identification, so its direct applicability to those dimensions is limited.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The 'punishment' mechanism is internalized (stress/emotion) rather than explicit peer or institutional punishment, so results may not transfer directly to explicit punishment settings.
- No explicit modeling of punishment cost, punishment technology, or identification of punishers.
- No analysis of communication, round structure, or feedback mechanisms.
- Assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contributions and does not address continuous contribution choices.
