Non-payoff outcomes such as strategy frequencies and reputation states are also tracked, but payoff is central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that in a repeated two-player deterrence game (with roles of challenger and defender), the possibility of costly punishment (retaliation) can lead to higher defender payoffs and social welfare, but only when reputation information is minimal (e.g., only the last action is observable and costly to obtain). When reputation information is rich (e.g., challengers can observe a defender's empirical fighting frequency over many rounds), deterrence breaks down, punishment loses its deterrent effect, and defender payoffs collapse. Agent-based simulations confirm that defender payoffs decline as the amount of reputation information increases, with a sharp drop when information becomes too rich. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is context-dependent: positive under minimal information, negative or null under rich information.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments depends critically on the information structure: punishment increases efficiency (defender payoffs) when reputation information is minimal and costly, but not when information is rich and easily available. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the design dimension 'information about others' past actions' (analogous to 'show_other_summaries' or 'show_punishment_id') is a key moderator: enabling rich, low-cost information can undermine the efficiency benefits of punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player deterrence games, but the mechanisms may generalize to PGG variants where punishment and reputation interact.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Interactions are repeated indefinitely (infinite horizon) with random rematching; agent-based simulations use many rounds (e.g., 10,000).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (attack/defend) choices are binary: take or respect (challenger), fight or yield (defender).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment (retaliation/fighting) is costly for the defender; cost parameter c is varied in analysis and simulations. Higher punishment cost reduces the likelihood of deterrence evolving.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the probability of keeping the resource (50%) minus cost c; not varied independently from cost.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The core manipulation is the amount of reputation information available to challengers: minimal (last action only) vs. rich (many past actions/fighting frequency). - seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Challengers can observe the reputation (tough/weak) of the specific defender they are matched with, but not the identity of punishers in a group context. The model is two-player and reputation is individual.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game but a two-player deterrence game; generalization to multi-player PGGs is not directly tested.
- All results are theoretical or simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes random matching and large populations; effects in small groups or with fixed partners may differ.
- The main manipulation is the amount of reputation information, which may not map directly onto all PGG experimental designs.
- No reward mechanisms or positive incentives are considered.
- The model does not include communication or chat between players.
