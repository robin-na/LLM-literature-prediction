## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The central finding is that coalitional enforcement—cooperative, low-cost punishment of defectors—was necessary and sufficient for the evolution of large-scale, stable cooperation among unrelated individuals in humans. The theory predicts that as the efficiency and scale of coalitional enforcement increased (via technological and social monitoring advances), so did the extent and complexity of human cooperation. The paper discusses how this mechanism underlies the emergence of language, culture, large political units, and rapid technological progress. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative findings about efficiency or payoffs in public goods game-like environments; rather, it offers a theoretical framework for understanding the evolutionary roots of cooperation and punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that punishment (specifically, coalitional or group-based punishment) can enable and stabilize high levels of cooperation among non-kin, by reducing the individual cost of enforcement and making defection unprofitable. For the downstream prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence and efficiency of punishment mechanisms are critical moderators of group cooperation and, by extension, group efficiency. However, because the paper does not report empirical or experimental data on efficiency or payoffs in public goods games, it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions. Its main value is in supporting the general principle that more efficient, lower-cost, and more widely distributed punishment mechanisms should be associated with higher group cooperation and efficiency, all else equal.
