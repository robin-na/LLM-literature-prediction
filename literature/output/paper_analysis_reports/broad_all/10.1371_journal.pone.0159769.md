# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Exploring the Motivations for Punishment: Framing and Country-Level Effects
Authors: Bone, J. E., McAuliffe, K., & Raihani, N. J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of punishment assigned by P1 to P2
- antisocial punishment (punishment of non-stealing partners)
- punishment in response to disadvantageous inequality
- punishment in response to losses
- country-level differences in punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is whether P1 punished P2, under various conditions (stealing, disadvantageous inequality, etc). No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported or analyzed. The study focuses on motivations for punishment, not on efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment behavior in a two-player stealing game is strongly influenced by country of origin. US-based participants are more likely to punish in response to both losses and disadvantageous inequality, while India-based participants are more punitive overall and engage in more antisocial punishment, but only increase punishment when experiencing disadvantageous inequality. Methodological factors such as stake size and punishment cost (within the tested range) do not significantly affect punishment rates. The study does not analyze efficiency or group payoff, and all outcome measures are behavioral (punishment frequency) rather than payoff-based.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report or analyze efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. However, it provides informative evidence about how punishment behavior varies with country of origin and with the presence of disadvantageous inequality, which may be relevant for understanding when punishment is likely to be used. The design is adjacent to PGGs (two-player stealing game with punishment), but not a PGG or threshold public goods game. The findings suggest that punishment rates and motives can vary substantially by context and population, which may moderate any efficiency effects in related games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is strictly two-player (P1 and P2) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot (single round) for each participant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; anonymity is maintained.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. P2's choice is binary: steal a fixed amount or do nothing. P1's punishment choice is also binary: punish or not.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to P1 is $0.10 to reduce P2's bonus by $0.30 (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3). This is held constant across treatments, but the relative cost (as a proportion of endowment) is varied in one treatment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed at 1:3 (pay $0.10$ to reduce $0.30$ from P2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly one-shot; participants know there is only one round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown their own and their partner's payoffs at each stage, including after stealing and after punishment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are anonymous; MTurk IDs are not revealed to partners. No identification of who punished.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or threshold public goods game, but a two-player stealing game; findings may not generalize to multi-player or public goods contexts.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported or analyzed; all outcomes are behavioral (punishment frequency).
- The study is one-shot; repeated interaction and dynamic effects are not addressed.
- Punishment is only possible in one direction (P1 to P2), and only after a stealing decision.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present.
- Country-level differences are observed, but the underlying causes (e.g., norms, economic factors) are not experimentally identified.
