# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effect of rewards and sanctions in provision of public goods
Authors: Sefton, M., Shupp, R., & Walker, J. M.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group efficiency (earnings as % of maximum possible earnings)
- group earnings
- individual earnings
- variance and range of earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to group account
- use of sanctions and rewards
- patterns of sanctioning and rewarding
- deviation from group average contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group efficiency and earnings, with detailed analysis of how sanctions and rewards affect these. Contribution rates and sanction/reward behavior are also analyzed, but efficiency is a primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and ten rounds per sequence, the introduction of costly sanctioning (punishment) opportunities after an initial baseline phase leads to sustained higher contributions and, after an initial efficiency loss due to sanctioning costs, to higher group efficiency compared to the baseline. Reward opportunities alone do not maintain high contributions or efficiency, as the use of rewards declines quickly. The combination of sanctions and rewards produces the highest average contributions and earnings, but the effect is primarily due to the sanctioning mechanism. The cost structure is linear: each token used for punishment costs the punisher 10 cents and reduces the target's earnings by 10 cents (total group loss: 20 cents per token). The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5. Players cannot identify who punished or rewarded them, and all information is anonymous. No communication is allowed. The number of rounds is known in advance. The findings are robust across groups, though there is heterogeneity in group-level outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and known round structure. The results show that enabling costly punishment (with a 1:1 cost-impact ratio) increases efficiency over the baseline, but only after an initial period where the direct costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. Over time, as the threat of punishment sustains cooperation and the use of punishment declines, efficiency improves. The effect is robust to the absence of communication and anonymity of punishment. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs (4 players, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.5, linear punishment, no chat, anonymous), enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, especially in later rounds. The cost structure of punishment is critical: with a 1:1 cost-impact, the efficiency gain is positive but moderate; more effective punishment (higher impact per cost) would likely yield larger efficiency gains. The paper also shows that reward mechanisms alone are insufficient to sustain efficiency. These results should be directly used for prediction in similar lab PGG settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players, fixed throughout the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session has 20 rounds (two sequences of 10 rounds each).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses 0-6 tokens to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each token in group account gives 5 cents to each of 4 players; endowment is 6 tokens).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each token used for punishment costs the punisher 10 cents and reduces the target's earnings by 10 cents (1:1 cost-impact).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology is linear: 1 token = 10 cents cost to punisher, 10 cents loss to target (1:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward mechanism is present in some treatments; rewards are costly to giver and benefit receiver (pure transfer).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each token used for reward costs the giver 10 cents and gives the receiver 10 cents (pure transfer, no net group gain).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward technology is linear: 1 token = 10 cents cost to giver, 10 cents gain to receiver (no net group gain).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of rounds (20) is known in advance to all participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see the group account allocations of all group members (in random order, anonymous), and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are anonymous: players know how much they were punished/rewarded, but not by whom.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment cost-impact ratio is fixed at 1:1; results may differ with more effective or less costly punishment technologies.
- All sessions are conducted in the lab with student subjects; external validity to field or larger groups is not tested.
- No communication is allowed; effects may differ if chat or discussion is permitted.
- The number of rounds is relatively short (10 per phase); longer games may show different dynamics.
- Punishment and reward are anonymous; effects may differ if identities are revealed.
- The study does not vary MPCR or group size, so generalizability to other parameterizations is limited.
