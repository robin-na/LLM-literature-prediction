## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews evidence that punishment in public goods games can maintain high levels of cooperation, but often at a net cost due to the expenses of punishing and being punished. Reputation mechanisms, such as indirect reciprocity, can also sustain cooperation and do so more efficiently by avoiding these costs. When both punishment and reputation mechanisms are available, punishment is used less frequently and more selectively, and overall group efficiency is increased. The review also notes that reward mechanisms are generally less effective than punishment in sustaining cooperation. The findings are based on a synthesis of multiple experimental studies, with particular emphasis on the interaction between punishment and reputation in public goods environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games generally increases cooperation and can improve group efficiency, especially when combined with reputation mechanisms. However, punishment alone may not always increase efficiency due to its costs. The review provides indirect evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the presence of reputation systems and the ability to observe or communicate about others' actions. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency gain from punishment is context-dependent and is maximized when reputation mechanisms are also present. The review does not provide new quantitative estimates but synthesizes qualitative and comparative findings from the literature.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review describes typical public goods games with 4, 6, or 12 players, but does not systematically analyze the effect of player count on efficiency or punishment effects.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes repeated games with varying numbers of rounds (e.g., 6, 16, 20), but does not analyze round number as a moderator.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes public goods games where players can invest either €1 or nothing (binary choice), but does not analyze this as a moderator.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes doubling of the public account and redistribution, implying an MPCR of 0.5 for 4 players, but does not analyze MPCR as a moderator.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is described (e.g., punisher pays €1, punished loses €3). The costliness of punishment is cited as a reason why efficiency gains are limited when only punishment is available.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is implied (e.g., €1 cost yields €3 penalty), but not systematically analyzed.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward mechanisms are discussed and found to be less effective than punishment in sustaining cooperation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players can observe all previous actions and reputation/image scores, which is central to the reputation mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- This is a review article and does not present new experimental data; findings are synthesized from prior studies.
- Efficiency and payoff outcomes are often discussed qualitatively or via referenced studies, not with new quantitative results.
- The review does not systematically analyze the moderating effects of all game design dimensions; most evidence is indirect.
- Some dimensions (e.g., chat, default contribution, reward cost/tech, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id) are not addressed.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and may not generalize to all public goods game variants.
