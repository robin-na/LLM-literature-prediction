## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review synthesizes evidence that mechanisms such as reputation, reciprocity, and social preferences (including egalitarianism and parochialism) are central to sustaining cooperation in social dilemmas. It highlights that in repeated public goods games, cooperation can be stabilized by making contributions observable and by enabling accountability through punishment or gossip. The review notes that punishment can promote cooperation, but also that gossip may be as effective as punishment and more efficient due to lower costs. The paper discusses the challenges of scaling cooperation to large groups and complex real-world dilemmas, emphasizing the importance of observability, local interactions, and psychological proximity. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions or direct evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment, reputation, and observability can promote cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It provides conceptual support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation, but also cautions that gossip and reputation mechanisms may be equally or more efficient due to lower costs. The review does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency effects, so it should be used for contextual and mechanistic understanding rather than for parameterizing predictive models of efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the inclusion of observability, reputation, and punishment as important moderators, but does not allow for direct estimation of their effects on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- This is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or meta-analytic effect sizes.
- Efficiency and payoff-based outcomes are discussed conceptually but not directly measured or reported.
- Punishment is discussed as a mechanism for promoting cooperation, but the review does not provide quantitative estimates of its effect on efficiency or group payoff.
- Many claims are based on synthesis of prior literature, and the review does not systematically compare the efficiency of punishment versus other mechanisms across game design dimensions.
