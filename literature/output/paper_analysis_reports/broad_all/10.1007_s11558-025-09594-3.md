# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?
Authors: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N.
Journal: Review of International Organizations
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings of public good players
- group earnings
- mean earnings by treatment
- payoff equations for A-players and B-players
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- punishment frequency and severity
- number of players punished
- voting behavior
- third/fourth party approval
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, voting) and payoff-based outcomes (earnings, group welfare). Efficiency is not reported as a normalized ratio, but group earnings and mean earnings are directly comparable across treatments and serve as a proxy for efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 public good players (A-players) per round. Treatments add 1 (Baseline), 2 (Competition), or 1 (Fourth Party) additional non-PGG players, but the core PGG is always 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for 20 periods in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete but not all-or-nothing: players choose from {0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20} out of 20 points.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (each point contributed returns 0.5 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher (B-player) 1 point and reduces the punished player's payoff by 2 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2:1 (1 point cost to punisher, 2 points loss to target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated for 20 periods; number of rounds is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, A-players see their own contribution, total group contribution, punishment received, and own payoff. In Competition, they also see all punishment proposals before voting.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Baseline and Fourth Party, the punisher is a designated B-player (third party), and the role is known. In Competition, A-players see which candidate proposed which punishment before voting.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is measured as average earnings, not as a normalized efficiency ratio; however, the design allows for direct comparison across treatments.
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (3 PGG players) and a specific punishment technology (2:1), so generalizability to other group sizes or punishment ratios may be limited.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not address interactions between punishment and reward.
- The higher-order punishment institutions (Fourth Party, Competition) are stylized and may not capture all complexities of real-world governance or reputational effects.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The effect of more than two candidates in Competition, or endogenous selection of institutions, is not explored.
