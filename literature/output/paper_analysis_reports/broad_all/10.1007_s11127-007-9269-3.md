## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that cooperation can be sustained in populations where some individuals are willing to punish cheaters, even if those punishers are themselves not cooperative. The evolutionary stability of cooperation depends on the relative costs of punishing, being punished, and being cheated. When the cost of punishing is less than the cost of being cheated, punishment can support a stable mix of cooperators and punishers, increasing overall efficiency compared to a world with only cheaters. If punishment is too costly, cheaters dominate and cooperation collapses. The model demonstrates that the existence of punishment mechanisms—regardless of the punishers' motives—can improve group welfare and efficiency in collective action settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in collective action games (including public goods game variants) can increase efficiency, as measured by average group payoff, provided the cost of punishing is not too high. The findings suggest that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment. While the model is not an exact public goods game, its structure and payoff logic are closely related, and its results are directly relevant for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency. The paper does not address empirical game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, or communication, but it does provide strong evidence that the cost and effectiveness of punishment are critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (phi) is a key parameter; the model shows that lower punishment cost relative to the cost of being cheated (beta) supports cooperation and higher efficiency, while high punishment cost undermines the effectiveness of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is discussed in terms of its impact on cheaters' payoffs; more effective punishment (higher cost to cheater per unit cost to punisher) supports cooperation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Model assumes cheaters are identifiable and that punishment can be targeted; this is necessary for punishment to be effective in the model.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model is adjacent to, but not an exact, public goods game; it uses a general collective action framework.
- Game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, communication, and contribution granularity are not addressed.
- The model assumes perfect identification of cheaters and does not consider errors in punishment or misidentification.
- No consideration of reward or positive incentives; only punishment is modeled.
