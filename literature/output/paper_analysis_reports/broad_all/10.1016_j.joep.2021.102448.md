# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation between newcomers and incumbents: The role of normative disagreements
Authors: Otten, K., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned/received
- group identification
- social norm emergence
- normative views and expectations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution rate (proportion of endowment contributed). No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. Punishment behavior and group identification are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Normative disagreement between newcomers and incumbents in a public goods game with peer punishment does not reduce average contributions to the public good. While group identification and the emergence of a shared social norm are negatively affected by normative disagreement, cooperation (as measured by contribution rate) is maintained. Punishment is used as a norm enforcement tool, especially targeting low-contributing newcomers, who subsequently increase their contributions to match incumbent standards. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a 3-player repeated public goods game with peer punishment, normative disagreement does not reduce average contributions, and punishment is effective at norm enforcement. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and findings are relevant for understanding behavioral mechanisms, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper can inform the mapping from design to expected contribution rates, but not to efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds total: 10 rounds before and 10 rounds after membership change.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0-20 MU in integer steps.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Two members have m_i=0.5, one has m_i=0.75; group multiplier M=1.75; MPCR for low-return = 0.5, for high-return = 0.75.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs 1 MU to assign.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's payoff by 3 MU.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there will be two parts of 10 rounds each; total number of rounds is known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, contributions and payoffs of each member are communicated to all group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not see who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as outcome variables; only contribution rates and behavioral measures are analyzed.
- The experiment uses a small group size (N=3) and a specific heterogeneity in returns, which may limit generalizability.
- Punishment is always enabled; there is no control condition without punishment, so the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- Findings are based on lab experiments with student participants and minimal group identities, which may not generalize to natural groups or field settings.
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency; thus, the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment.
