# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishing the weakest link - Voluntary sanctions and efficient coordination in the minimum effort game
Authors: Lec, F. L., Matthey, A., & Rydval, O.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group payoff (as fraction of maximum possible)
- net efficiency (payoff minus cost of punishment)
- total group profit
- average individual profit
Non-payoff outcomes:
- effort level
- minimum group effort
- punishment frequency
- disapproval frequency
- distribution of punishment/disapproval points
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual payoffs (including net of punishment costs), as well as behavioral outcomes like effort and punishment assignment. Efficiency is explicitly analyzed as the ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that voluntary costly punishment opportunities in a repeated minimum effort game (with 8 players per group, 16 rounds, and a two-stage design) significantly increase both effort levels and group efficiency compared to a baseline with no punishment and a treatment with costless disapproval (communication). In the punishment treatment, after an initial drop, average group payoff (net of punishment costs) rises and eventually exceeds the control, with about a third of groups reaching or approaching the Pareto-optimal equilibrium. Punishment is mainly directed at minimum-effort players, and its use is frequent. Disapproval/communication alone produces only a temporary or weak improvement. The results show that decentralized sanctions can serve as an effective coordination device in Pareto-ranked coordination games, with a net positive effect on efficiency in the longer run.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated weakest-link (minimum effort) games with large groups and a history of inefficient coordination, enabling voluntary costly punishment leads to a robust and lasting increase in group efficiency, even after accounting for the direct costs of punishment. The effect is stronger and more persistent than that of costless communication. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, holding control efficiency and other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment (with a 2:1 fine-to-cost ratio and anonymous assignment) will increase average efficiency, especially in settings with multiple rounds, large groups, and no prior communication. The initial rounds after introducing punishment may see a drop in net efficiency, but this reverses as groups coordinate on higher efforts. The results are most directly applicable to coordination games with similar structure, but provide adjacent evidence for public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 8 players, chosen to make coordination difficult and efficiency low in baseline.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 16 rounds total, split into two stages of 8 rounds each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. No free-form chat; in the Disapproval treatment, costless disapproval points serve as a structured, non-monetary communication device. This communication alone produces only a temporary or mild improvement in efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort choice is discrete but not all-or-nothing: players choose integer effort levels from 1 to 7.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.4 (from the payoff function: 0.4 × minimum effort).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 0.10 euro and reduces the punished's payoff by 0.20 euro (2:1 fine-to-cost ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology is 2:1 (each point costs 0.10 euro to punisher, reduces punished by 0.20 euro). This is effective in increasing efficiency over time.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are two stages of 8 rounds each, but only learn about the second stage after the first is complete. Within each stage, the number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see a table with all group members' effort choices and payoffs, ordered from lowest to highest effort.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: players see the sum of points assigned to them, but not the identity of punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a minimum effort (weakest-link) game, which is a coordination game, not a public goods game; results may not fully generalize to standard PGGs.
- Punishment is only enabled in the second stage, after a history of inefficient coordination, which may affect the observed effect size.
- The punishment technology (2:1 fine-to-cost ratio) and group size (8) are fixed; effects may differ with other parameters.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so the comparative effect of positive incentives is unknown.
- Communication is limited to structured disapproval points; no free-form chat or richer communication is allowed.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
