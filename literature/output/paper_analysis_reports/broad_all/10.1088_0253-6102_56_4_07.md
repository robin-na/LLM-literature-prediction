## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates, through theoretical modeling and numerical simulation, that adding punishment to voluntary public goods games in finite populations with bounded rationality increases both the frequency of cooperation and the average payoff of the group. The effect of punishment is especially strong when the loner's payoff is not too high, and the model shows that punishment can suppress defectors and promote higher payoffs for cooperators and punishers. The results are compared to both compulsory PGG and VPGG without punishment, showing that the addition of punishment leads to higher cooperation and average payoffs. The findings are robust to variations in group size, rationality parameter, and loner's payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public goods games with voluntary participation, enabling punishment mechanisms increases average group payoff (i.e., efficiency) compared to the same game without punishment, especially when the loner's payoff is not too high. The model explicitly includes key design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds (implicitly infinite or repeated), punishment cost and effectiveness, and the presence of loners. The results suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, with the effect moderated by the value of the loner's payoff and the rationality parameter. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with voluntary participation, finite groups, and bounded rationality.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and simulation-based; it does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but as average payoff and its relation to loner's payoff.
- The model assumes evolutionary dynamics and does not specify a fixed number of rounds, which may limit direct comparability to finite-round experiments.
- No analysis of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback is included.
- Findings are most applicable to settings with voluntary participation and may not generalize to compulsory PGG or other institutional contexts.
