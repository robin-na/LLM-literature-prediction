# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Coevolution of Behavior and Normative Expectations: An Experiment
Authors: Engel, C., & Kurschilgen, M.
Journal: American Law and Economics Review
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per participant in each treatment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- frequency of full contribution
- frequency of full defection
- statements of norm existence
- expected minimum contribution
- change in contribution after receiving punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels and norm-related beliefs. Average earnings are reported for each treatment, but efficiency (as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome) is not directly calculated or analyzed. Group payoff is not the main focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that (1) both norm elicitation and legal framing increase cooperation in a repeated public goods game, but do not differ significantly in the absence of punishment; (2) introducing a costly peer punishment option (1:1 cost-to-impact) increases cooperation and reduces free-riding, but the effect is strongest when the norm is embedded in a legal frame; (3) in the presence of punishment, the legal frame prevents the crowding out of intrinsic motivation and leads to higher contributions and more stable norm perceptions; (4) average earnings are higher in the Law-S (legal frame with sanctions) treatment than in other sanctioning treatments, indicating that punishment can improve group welfare when combined with legal framing. However, the paper does not directly analyze efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings per treatment are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 4 players, 30 rounds, and a 1:1 punishment cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment increases group earnings and cooperation, especially when the norm is legally framed. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive and most pronounced when combined with a legal frame. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is lower than in the punishment-enabled treatments, particularly Law-S. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on the presence of legal/normative framing. The results are most directly applicable to games with continuous contributions, no chat, full information about others' contributions, and a 1:1 punishment cost-to-impact ratio.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use fixed groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games have 30 announced periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication between participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous from 0 to 20 (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:1; to reduce another's income by 1, the punisher pays 1.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is 1:1 (each point costs 1 and reduces target's income by 1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment (income reduction) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed in advance that the game lasts 30 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants see how much each group member contributed.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome; only average earnings are reported.
- The experiment uses student participants in a lab setting, which may limit generalizability to real-world or field contexts.
- The punishment cost-to-impact ratio is 1:1, which is lower leverage than in many public goods experiments; results may differ with higher leverage.
- No analysis of heterogeneity by player characteristics or group composition.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
