# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Unravelling the many facets of human cooperation in an experimental study
Authors: Rostovtseva, V. V., Puurtinen, M., Salinas, E. M., Cox, R. F. A., Groothuis, A. G. G., Butovskaya, M. L., & Weissing, F. J.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prosocial decision rates (generosity, trust, trustworthiness, fairness, risk-taking, freeriding, work share, coordination, demand, punishment)
- correlations between prosocial tendencies across games
- effect of partner's facial appearance on prosocial behavior
- consistency of individual decisions across games
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral choices in a suite of economic games, including a punishment game, but does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus. Outcomes are primarily rates of prosocial or punishment decisions, not payoff-based measures.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that individuals are consistent in their prosocial and punishment-related decisions across a variety of economic games, but that punishment is not correlated with other prosocial tendencies. The appearance of interaction partners (as judged by facial attributes) systematically affects prosocial behavior in most games, but not in the punishment game. There is no evidence that facial appearance predicts actual prosocial behavior. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and thus does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a suite of economic games including a punishment game, the tendency to punish is not associated with other prosocial behaviors and is not influenced by partner appearance. However, it does not report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, so it cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its main value is in showing that punishment behavior is psychologically and behaviorally distinct from other forms of cooperation, and that its drivers may differ from those of prosociality. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the presence of punishment may not always align with higher prosociality, but provides no direct evidence about efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) interactions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each decision situation is a one-shot game; each participant makes three decisions per situation (anonymous and two personalised), but there is no repeated interaction with the same partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some games have binary (all-or-nothing) choices (e.g., cooperate/defect), others have continuous choices (e.g., 0-100 points to invest or donate).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Punishment Game, by spending 30 points, a player can take away 100 points from the other player.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 100 points deducted per 30 points spent (3.33x impact).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure: each game is a one-shot decision, repeated three times (anonymous and two personalised), not a repeated game with the same partner.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include a public goods game or any group game with more than two players; all games are dyadic.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; only behavioral choices are analyzed.
- Punishment is studied as a binary or conditional choice, not as a continuous or strategic variable.
- The sample consists of young, mostly university students from WEIRD backgrounds, limiting generalizability.
- No communication or feedback between participants; all decisions are made in isolation.
- Order effects may be present, as anonymous decisions always precede personalised ones, though the authors report minimal evidence for this.
