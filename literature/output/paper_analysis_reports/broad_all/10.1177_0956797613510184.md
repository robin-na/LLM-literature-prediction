# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Gossip and Ostracism Promote Cooperation in Groups
Authors: Feinberg, M., Willer, R., & Schultz, M.
Journal: Psychological Science
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- ostracism frequency
- gossip frequency
- reputation strength
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contributions, gossip, ostracism) and payoff-based outcomes (group and individual earnings). Efficiency is directly addressed through group and individual earnings, especially in the context of ostracism and gossip treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with random rematching, the ability to gossip about others' contributions and to ostracize (exclude) group members based on this information leads to a substantial increase in cooperation and, after several rounds, to the highest group and individual earnings. Gossip alone also increases cooperation and earnings compared to a basic game with no reputation or exclusion, but the effect is less pronounced and decays over time. Ostracism is used primarily against low contributors, and those who are ostracized tend to increase their contributions upon returning. The efficiency gains from gossip and ostracism are not immediate, as exclusion temporarily reduces group size and the public good multiplier, but by the final rounds, these groups outperform the others in earnings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a punishment-like mechanism (ostracism) in addition to reputation/gossip in a public goods game increases efficiency (group and individual earnings) compared to both a basic game and a gossip-only game. The effect is robust despite a temporary efficiency loss due to exclusion. The design dimensions—such as group size (4), number of rounds (6 per game), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR of 0.5 (or 0.375 when group size drops to 3), and anonymous exclusion—are all specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (ostracism) in similar lab PGGs. The results suggest that, even when punishment is implemented as exclusion rather than direct costly punishment, and even when exclusion temporarily reduces group size and multiplier, the long-run effect on efficiency is positive if reputation information is available.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 participants per round; total of 24 participants per session, randomly rematched.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 6 rounds per game (basic, gossip, gossip-with-ostracism); 18 rounds total per participant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No verbal communication allowed; only structured gossip notes in relevant conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each participant chooses how many of 10 points to contribute (0-10).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (group fund doubled and split among 4); when group size drops to 3 (after ostracism), multiplier is 1.5, so MPCR is also 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Ostracism is costless to the ostracizer (anonymous vote); cost is imposed on the ostracized (cannot participate, earns nothing that round).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: ostracized participant is excluded from the round and earns zero; group size and multiplier are adjusted.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds per game (6 rounds per game, 3 games).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see how much each group member contributed and earned.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ostracism votes are anonymous; participants are identified only by code letters that change each game, so punishment is not directly attributable to a specific individual.

## Important Limitations
- Ostracism is not a standard costly punishment mechanism; it is exclusion from the round, which also reduces group size and the public good multiplier, complicating direct comparison to standard punishment treatments.
- The study uses a lab setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to field or real-world settings.
- The number of rounds per game is relatively small (6), and the long-term effects beyond this time frame are unknown.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so the interaction of reward and punishment is not addressed.
- Ostracism is anonymous and based on group voting, which may differ from individual punishment or more transparent sanctioning mechanisms.
