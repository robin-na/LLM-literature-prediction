## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when multilateral punishment is ineffective due to group size and heterogeneity, agents can still achieve efficient, self-enforcing exchange by using costly, observable signals that reduce social distance (e.g., adopting local customs, gift giving, religious conversion). These signals are more costly for cheaters (impatient agents) than for cooperators (patient agents), allowing communities to screen out cheaters and admit only those likely to cooperate. In equilibrium, this mechanism exhausts the gains from trade and achieves social efficiency, even without formal enforcement or effective punishment mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that, in environments where punishment is not feasible or effective (e.g., large, heterogeneous groups), alternative mechanisms such as costly signaling can substitute for punishment to achieve efficient outcomes. For the prediction task, this suggests that the absence of punishment does not necessarily preclude high efficiency if credible signaling mechanisms are available and used. However, the model is not a direct public goods game and does not provide quantitative estimates or experimental evidence, so its predictive value is mainly conceptual and applies to adjacent settings where punishment is infeasible.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide experimental or quantitative empirical evidence.
- The model is not a public goods game or direct variant; it is a signaling model of bilateral or intergroup trade.
- Punishment is modeled as multilateral refusal to trade, not as costly punishment actions as in PGG experiments.
- Historical evidence is qualitative and illustrative, not systematic or causal.
- The model assumes the availability and observability of costly signals, which may not generalize to all environments.
