The main outcomes are the stationary distributions of strategies in evolutionary dynamics, which directly reflect average payoffs and thus group efficiency. The paper explicitly discusses which sanctioning strategies yield higher payoffs and dominate in the population.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in finite-population public goods games, exclusion strategies (both peer and pool) consistently outperform punishment strategies (peer and pool) in terms of evolutionary advantage, which is measured by stationary frequencies and average payoffs. Peer exclusion dominates in the absence of second-order sanctioning, while pool exclusion dominates when second-order sanctioning is present. The results hold for both optional and compulsory PGGs and are robust to variations in group size and punishment fine (unless the fine is extremely high). The findings imply that, for a given control efficiency, enabling exclusion (especially peer exclusion) will yield higher group efficiency than enabling punishment, and that the presence of second-order sanctioning shifts the advantage to pool exclusion.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in standard PGGs, enabling exclusion (especially peer exclusion) will increase group efficiency more than enabling punishment, across a wide range of parameter settings. The effect is robust to group size, number of rounds (since the model is evolutionary and not round-based), and the relative costs of punishment and exclusion (as long as they are not extremely imbalanced). The presence of second-order sanctioning is a key moderator: without it, peer exclusion is optimal; with it, pool exclusion is optimal. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment (vs. exclusion), one should expect a positive but smaller efficiency gain than with exclusion, and that the details of sanctioning (peer vs pool, second-order) are critical.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and based on evolutionary game dynamics; no empirical or experimental data are used.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations; results may differ in structured populations.
- No explicit time horizon or number of rounds; results are about stationary distributions, not finite repeated games.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are considered.
- Second-order sanctioning is modeled as a binary parameter, but real-world implementation may be more complex.
- The model assumes perfect detection of defectors and perfect implementation of punishment/exclusion.
