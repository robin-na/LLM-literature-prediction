## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing social punishment (where cooperators punish defectors with a small probability) in a mobile PD population can transform a system that would otherwise end in universal defection into one of universal cooperation, thereby maximizing group payoff. Even a 10% probability of social punishment is sufficient in many parameter regimes. The effect is robust to some degree of nonsocial punishment, but as nonsocial punishment increases, cooperation and mean payoff decline. The paper provides explicit analytical predictions and simulation results for mean payoff in the final state, showing that social punishment increases efficiency (mean payoff), while nonsocial punishment reduces it. The effect of punishment is moderated by mobility and temptation to defect: higher values of these parameters require higher rates of social punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in pairwise PD games with mobile agents, enabling social punishment (even at low rates) can dramatically increase group efficiency (mean payoff), often shifting the system from all-defection (zero payoff) to all-cooperation (maximum payoff). The effect is robust to moderate levels of nonsocial punishment, but high nonsocial punishment or harsh environments (high mobility, high temptation to defect) can negate the benefits. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs (pairwise PD, mobile or spatially structured populations, punishment with cost:impact ratio of 1:3), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. However, the results are for pairwise PD, not n-person public goods games, so direct quantitative transfer to PGGs should be cautious, though qualitative patterns are likely similar.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use N=1000 players; population size is fixed in all main results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for thousands of rounds until a stable state is reached; time evolution is shown in figures.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: players choose to cooperate or defect in each interaction.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Pairwise PD: R=1, S=0, P=0, T=b>1. MPCR is not defined as in PGG, but the payoff structure is fully specified.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 1 unit to punisher per act; impact is 3 units lost by punished. This cost:impact ratio is fixed in all main results.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed: 1 unit cost to punisher, 3 units penalty to target. The effect of this ratio is discussed in context of previous experiments.

## Important Limitations
- The model studies pairwise prisoner's dilemma, not n-person public goods games; results may not transfer quantitatively to PGGs.
- All results are from theoretical modeling and simulations, not laboratory or field experiments.
- Punishment is implemented as a probabilistic action after each interaction, not as a strategy type; this may differ from some experimental or real-world settings.
- No communication, reputation, or identification of punishers is modeled.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is considered.
- Population size, density, and other parameters are fixed in most simulations; generalizability to other settings is not directly tested.
