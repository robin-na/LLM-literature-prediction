## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a formal model of social preferences that incorporates both other-regarding preferences and social image concerns, showing that these together create incentives for individuals to credibly signal their social preferences through both actions (such as contributing to a public good, punishing, or rewarding) and communication (cheap talk). The model predicts that individuals will use both costly and costless signals to communicate their type (altruistic or spiteful), and that the availability of communication can affect the use of costly signals like punishment or reward. The paper discusses, but does not empirically test, the implications for public goods games, including the idea that punishment may serve as a signal of ill will and that communication may substitute for punishment. No direct findings on efficiency or payoff outcomes are provided.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions relevant to forecasting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its main contribution is a theoretical framework for understanding how social preferences and signaling motives may influence the use of punishment, reward, and communication. While it suggests that communication opportunities may reduce the use of costly punishment (and vice versa), and that punishment can serve as a signal of ill will, it does not provide data or direct analysis of efficiency outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly by highlighting possible mechanisms and moderators (such as the availability of communication or the signaling value of punishment/reward), but not by providing parameter estimates or outcome data.
