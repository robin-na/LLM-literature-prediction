The review focuses on cooperation and its determinants as the main outcome, not on efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. Some included studies mention payoff-related outcomes, but the review does not systematically report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review finds that both individual (e.g., social identification, trust, values) and organizational (e.g., incentives, communication, social norms, anonymity) factors significantly influence cooperation in commons and social dilemma settings. Regarding punishment, meta-analyses and experimental studies included in the review show that punishment systems reliably increase cooperation rates, but may reduce group earnings or efficiency due to the cost of punishment, especially in early rounds. Reward systems also increase cooperation and tend to result in higher group payoffs than punishment. The review notes that the effectiveness of punishment depends on factors such as cost, group homogeneity, information accuracy, and cultural context. However, the review does not provide quantitative estimates or systematic synthesis of efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on cooperation as the main dependent variable.

## Prediction Guidance
This review is useful for identifying which game design dimensions are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation, such as the presence and cost of punishment, group size, communication, and information structure. It provides strong evidence that punishment increases cooperation, but only indirect and mixed evidence about its effect on efficiency or group payoff, with some included studies noting that punishment can reduce group earnings due to its cost. For the downstream prediction task, this review suggests that enabling punishment will likely increase cooperation, but the effect on efficiency will depend on the costliness of punishment, the possibility of antisocial punishment, and whether rewards are also available. The review does not provide direct quantitative evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (including chat and in-person) is discussed as a factor that increases cooperation. The review summarizes that allowing communication increases cooperation, but does not link this to efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The review notes that the cost of implementing punishment moderates its effectiveness: costly punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation, but can reduce group earnings/efficiency due to the cost. No quantitative synthesis.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment (impact per unit cost) is discussed as a moderator, but not systematically analyzed. Graduated and targeted punishment are noted as more effective.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward systems are discussed as increasing cooperation and leading to higher group payoffs than punishment in some studies.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Providing feedback about others' contributions (i.e., showing summaries) increases cooperation by making social norms salient.

## Important Limitations
- The review does not systematically report or synthesize efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as dependent variables; its focus is on cooperation and related behavioral outcomes.
- Game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and contribution structure are not systematically coded or analyzed.
- Most included studies are laboratory experiments, with limited coverage of real-world commons or field experiments.
- There is a cultural/geographic bias toward studies conducted in Western countries.
- The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes or meta-analytic estimates for efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Potential publication bias and quality variation among included studies.
