# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social Expectations are Primarily Rooted in Reciprocity: An Investigation of Fairness, Cooperation, and Trustworthiness
Authors: Bogdan, P. C., Dolcos, F., Moore, M., Kuznietsov, I., Culpepper, S. A., & Dolcos, S.
Journal: Cognitive Science
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- participant earnings (Study 4, as manipulation check)
- UG payouts (Study 4, as manipulation check)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned (PGG, Study 3)
- rejection likelihood (UG, Studies 1 and 2)
- trust (Trust Game, Study 4)
- contribution behavior (PGG, Study 3)
- expectation-violation metrics (ExV[Proposed], ExV[Received])
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment assigned, rejection likelihood, and trust, all as functions of expectation-violation. Payoff/efficiency is not a primary outcome; group earnings are only reported as manipulation checks in Study 4.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across four studies, including a reanalysis of a large cross-cultural PGG with punishment, the paper finds that people punish others whose contributions deviate from their own prior behavior, rather than from the group norm. This applies to both prosocial and antisocial punishment. The effect is robust across cultures and game types. However, the paper does not report on how punishment affects group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. The only payoff-related results are manipulation checks (e.g., in Study 4, generous partners yield higher earnings). Thus, the findings are highly informative about the psychological mechanisms of punishment and norm enforcement, but do not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in PGGs with punishment, the main driver of punishment is the dissimilarity between a punisher's own prior contributions and the target's contribution, rather than deviation from the group norm. This insight is useful for predicting when and whom players will punish, including antisocial punishment. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings may be used to inform mechanistic models of punishment assignment, but not to calibrate the effect of punishment on group efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 3 (PGG) uses 4 players per group, as in Herrmann et al. (2008).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 3 (PGG) uses 10 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication is allowed between players in the PGG (Study 3).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG contributions are continuous (0-20 tokens per round). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token spent on punishment costs the punisher 1 token and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (target loses 3 tokens per 1 token spent).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is not available in the PGG design analyzed; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 rounds (standard in Herrmann et al. 2008).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see each other player's contribution before the punishment phase.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told if they are punished, but not by whom. Punishment is anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes in the PGG with punishment.
- Findings are about punishment assignment and norm enforcement, not about the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
- Some studies use deception (computer partners), which may affect ecological validity, though Study 3 uses real human data.
- Time-saving incentives (rather than monetary) are used in some studies, which may affect generalizability.
- The paper does not analyze the effect of game design dimensions on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
