## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a framed field experiment with Colombian fishing communities, groups of 5 played a 15-round linear public goods game with a marginal per capita return of 0.4. Treatments included a baseline, a 'frame' (reminder of social optimum), 'guilt' (private feedback), 'shame' (random public revelation of non-cooperation), and two punishment treatments (low and high penalty, both with 1/5 audit probability and per-unit fines). Both punishment treatments increased contributions and expected earnings relative to baseline, but the high-penalty treatment (designed to induce full compliance) resulted in the lowest average expected earnings due to fines. The shame treatment produced the highest contributions and expected earnings, significantly outperforming both punishment treatments. The paper concludes that social mechanisms (shame) can be more effective and efficient than regulatory punishment in promoting cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with 5 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known round count, introducing monetary punishment (with random audits and per-unit fines) increases efficiency relative to baseline, but the effect is limited: high-penalty punishment can reduce efficiency due to fines. The effect of punishment is smaller than that of a social mechanism (random public revelation/shame). For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment will not always increase efficiency above control, especially if fines are high and compliance is imperfect. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit probability, punishment cost/tech) are all specified, and the results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency under punishment in similar field or framed settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 15 rounds, known in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No verbal communication was permitted in any treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous from 0 to 25 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (a=2, n=5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token (low penalty) or 4 tokens (high penalty) per unit not contributed, with 1/5 audit probability. Cost is to the punished, not the punisher (external enforcement).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1 or 4 tokens per unit not contributed, with 1/5 audit probability. High penalty is theoretically sufficient for full compliance, but does not achieve it in practice.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all individual contributions were posted in random order (anonymous) in all treatments.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted in two specific fishing communities in Colombia; results may not generalize to other populations or contexts.
- Punishment was externally administered (not peer punishment), so findings may not transfer to peer-sanction settings.
- Audit probability was fixed at 1/5; effects of different audit probabilities are not tested.
- No reward treatments were included, so cannot compare punishment to positive incentives.
- The 'shame' treatment involved random public revelation, not systematic or certain disclosure, which may differ from other forms of social pressure.
- The field context and framing may have influenced results due to local norms and experiences with regulation.
