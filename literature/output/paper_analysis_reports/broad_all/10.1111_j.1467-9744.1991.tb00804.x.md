## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper theorizes that the universal presence of supernatural sanction systems (rewarding and punishing afterlives) in archaic civilizations functioned to curb individual selfishness and support group-level cooperation, overcoming the problem of genetic competition among cooperators. It suggests that such beliefs and associated costly rituals (e.g., wasteful royal funerals) reinforced the credibility of these sanction systems, thereby enhancing group cohesion and effectiveness. The argument is that these group-level cultural adaptations, including moral norms and invisible sanction systems, were selected because they increased the survival and success of social groups, even if they imposed costs on individuals. However, the paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that sanction systems (punishment and reward, especially supernatural or invisible ones) can enhance group-level cooperation and cohesion, potentially increasing group effectiveness or efficiency in a broad sense. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. Its relevance is mainly conceptual, supporting the plausibility of positive effects of sanction systems on group outcomes, but it does not inform specific game design dimensions or control efficiency values.
