# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Representational altruism: The wary cooperator as authoritative decision maker
Authors: Smith, K. B.
Journal: American Journal of Political Science
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean division of tokens/points between allocator and recipient
- mean payoff to allocator and recipient
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocation fairness (as perceived by others)
- representational altruism (incurring cost to benefit others)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the division of tokens/points (payoff) between allocator and recipient in dictator/ultimatum-like games. Outcomes are interpreted in terms of fairness and representational altruism, but the data are payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that individuals acting as allocators in one-shot, anonymous dictator/ultimatum-like games often divide resources evenly or even disadvantage themselves to appear fair, supporting the 'wary cooperator' hypothesis. The presence or absence of a veto (ultimatum vs. dictator) does not significantly affect allocations; rather, information asymmetry (who values the resource more) and the framing of the allocator's role (merit vs. trust) are key. When allocators are told they are entrusted with responsibility, they are more likely to allocate less to themselves than when told they earned the role by merit. The findings suggest that internalized social preferences, not external punishment or veto threats, drive representational altruism and fairness in allocation decisions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting efficiency in public-goods-like environments with and without punishment. While it does not use a standard PGG or explicit punishment mechanism, it shows that internalized social preferences can lead to fair or altruistic outcomes even in the absence of external sanctions. The findings suggest that, in some contexts, adding external punishment or veto power may have limited additional effect on efficiency if internalized fairness norms are strong. However, context (information asymmetry, framing of authority) can shift behavior toward more or less self-interested allocations. For prediction, this implies that game design features affecting perceived fairness and social expectations may be as important as formal punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are two-player (allocator and recipient) in all experiments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot (single round), no repeated interaction.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; decisions are made anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (allocation) is continuous: allocators can divide 15 tokens in any way.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is a one-shot (single round) allocation.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a public goods game or a direct PGG variant; it uses dictator/ultimatum-like allocation games.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; findings are about internalized social preferences, not external punishment.
- Games are one-shot and anonymous, which may not generalize to repeated or group settings typical of PGGs.
- Subjects are undergraduates, not actual policymakers; external validity to real-world authoritative decision makers is limited.
- The study does not address how group identification or outgroup/ingroup dynamics affect allocation decisions.
- No direct measurement of efficiency as defined in PGGs (group payoff relative to full cooperation); outcomes are individual allocations.
