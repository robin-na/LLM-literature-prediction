# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Resource Allocations and Disapproval Voting in Unequal Groups
Authors: D'Exelle, B., & Riedl, A.
Journal: Journal of Conflict Resolution
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- representative's share of resources
- frequency of exclusion of villagers
- group earnings (implied by allocations and disapproval costs)
- frequency of disapproval (which affects payoffs via costs and lost reimbursement)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- disapproval voting rates
- voting behavior by villager type
- allocation strategies (exclude-one, exclude-all, etc.)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are allocation strategies (who gets resources), disapproval voting rates, and the resulting payoffs to representatives and villagers. Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the social optimum, but group earnings and the frequency of costly disapproval events are reported and discussed. The study focuses on how institutional features (information and voting rules) affect both behavioral and payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Disapproval voting as a punishment mechanism in unequal groups does not consistently improve efficiency or prosocial allocations. Instead, its effectiveness is highly sensitive to institutional details. When villagers have only private information about their share and voting is public, disapproval rates are low and representatives often use a minimal coalition (exclude-one) strategy, which avoids disapproval and maintains higher group earnings, though at the cost of excluding the poorest villager. When allocations are common knowledge or voting is secret, disapproval rates are higher, leading representatives to frequently switch to the exclude-all strategy, which is inefficient as it maximizes the representative's payoff at the expense of the group. The punishment mechanism can thus backfire, reducing efficiency, especially when disapproval is frequent. The study highlights the importance of information structure and voting rules in moderating the effects of punishment on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment mechanisms (here, disapproval voting) on efficiency in resource allocation games is not universally positive and depends critically on the design dimensions, especially information disclosure and the publicity of voting. In settings where punishment is possible but disapproval is rare (private information, public voting), efficiency is higher, but this is achieved by excluding the least powerful group member. In settings where punishment is more likely (common information, secret voting), efficiency is lower due to frequent costly disapproval and representatives' defensive strategies. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effect is moderated by how information and punishment are structured. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported, but the treatment effects indicate that punishment can reduce efficiency if it triggers defensive, exclusionary, or retaliatory behavior by the allocator.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 1 representative and 3 villagers (total 4 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The allocation game is played for 10 consecutive rounds in the same group.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment (disapproval) costs each villager 20 francs if disapproval is implemented; the representative loses 200 francs (not reimbursed). High impact-to-cost ratio for punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented via majority disapproval voting; if disapproved, the representative loses the full 200 francs (not reimbursed), villagers each lose 20 francs. The punishment is severe for the representative relative to cost for villagers.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (10) is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Varied as a treatment: in 'common information' (CI) condition, all villagers see the full allocation; in 'private information' (PI), each villager only sees their own share.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Varied as a treatment: in 'public voting', all votes are revealed to the group (including the representative); in 'secret voting', votes are anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a resource allocation game with a single allocator and collective punishment via voting, so results may not generalize to standard PGGs.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as a ratio to the social optimum, but is inferred from group earnings and the frequency of costly disapproval events.
- The punishment mechanism is collective (majority voting) rather than individual, and the cost-to-impact ratio is much higher than in typical PGG punishment studies.
- The group structure is fixed (one representative, three villagers) and may not generalize to other group sizes or structures.
- No control condition without punishment is reported, so the baseline efficiency is not directly comparable.
- The exclusion of the poorest villager in the most efficient condition raises equity concerns not captured by efficiency alone.
- The study does not explore endogenous institution formation or alternative punishment/reward mechanisms.
