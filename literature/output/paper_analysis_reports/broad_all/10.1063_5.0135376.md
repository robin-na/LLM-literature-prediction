Outcome notes:
The model focuses on the intention to cooperate, the evolutionary stability of tolerant vs. intolerant strategies, and the conditions for the spread or containment of intolerance. Payoff is used as a technical device to determine evolutionary stability, but group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff are not the main reported outcomes. No explicit efficiency or group payoff ratios are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that economic inequality is a strong enhancer of intolerance in reputation-based populations. Intolerant strategies can invade tolerant groups more easily when inequality is high, even if overall economic stress is moderate. Redistribution of wealth can prevent or reverse the spread of intolerance, but only if implemented before intolerance becomes widespread. The model shows that tolerant minorities under economic stress may reduce their willingness to punish defectors or may even help wealthy discriminators. The main reported outcomes are the intention to cooperate and the evolutionary stability of strategies, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. It suggests that in reputation-based cooperation environments, inequality and group structure can strongly moderate the effectiveness of punishment-like mechanisms (here, reputation-based exclusion) in sustaining cooperation. However, because the model does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The findings are most useful for understanding how inequality and group structure might interact with punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only intention to cooperate and evolutionary stability are analyzed.
- Punishment is implemented via reputation-based exclusion, not explicit costly punishment as in standard PGG experiments.
- The model assumes infinite population size and infinite time horizon, limiting direct applicability to finite, repeated public goods games.
- No explicit reward mechanism is modeled.
- Redistribution of wealth is assumed to be costless, which may not be realistic.
- Findings are not directly transferable to quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments.
