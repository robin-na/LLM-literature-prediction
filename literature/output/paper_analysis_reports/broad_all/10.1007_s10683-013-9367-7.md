## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a voluntary Mutual Aid game (a variant of the public goods game), groups can choose between no punishment, peer punishment, pool punishment, or non-participation. Pool punishment only becomes dominant and sustains high cooperation if it includes second-order punishment (punishing those who do not contribute to the punishment pool). In this treatment, cooperation rates are very high (88.2%) and average payoffs for pool and peer punishment are similar and high. In the absence of second-order punishment, pool punishment is not adopted, cooperation rates are lower (48.9%), and peer punishment is preferred but less stable. Peer punishment is more efficient when cooperation is achieved, but pool punishment with second-order punishment is more stable and leads to higher sustained cooperation and payoffs. Players adapt their choices based on observed payoffs, indicating social learning. The results highlight the importance of sanctioning second-order free-riders for the success of institutional punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency (group payoff) in public-goods-like games depends critically on the design of the punishment institution, especially whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. When pool punishment includes second-order punishment, it leads to high and stable cooperation and payoffs, outperforming peer punishment in stability (though not always in efficiency if all cooperate). Peer punishment is initially attractive but declines over time. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the presence of second-order punishment, the type of punishment (peer vs. pool), and the ability to opt out are key moderators. Control efficiency (no punishment) is much lower than with effective (second-order) pool punishment. The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in games with similar design features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 12 to 14 players; each group is independent and fixed for 50 rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 50 rounds per group; number of rounds was not revealed to participants to avoid end-game effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are all-or-nothing (full contribution or none); punishment is also all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 1 MU contribution is multiplied by 3 and divided among all other players; MPCR = 3/(m-1) for group size m.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: punisher pays 0.5 MU per defector; Pool punishment: contributor to punishment pool pays 0.5 MU per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: each defector fined 1 MU per punisher (fine-to-fee ratio 2:1); Pool punishment: each non-contributor (or second-order free-rider) fined 1 MU per pool punisher.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewarding was not allowed in this experiment; only punishment mechanisms were available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the number of group members choosing each game and the payoffs for all strategies used in their group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know who punished whom; all actions were anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a Mutual Aid game, which is a close but not exact variant of the standard public goods game; results may not generalize to all PGG settings.
- Only all-or-nothing contribution and punishment choices were allowed; no gradation in contribution or punishment.
- No communication, reputation, or deliberation was allowed, which may limit external validity for real-world group interactions.
- Players could opt out of participation, which is not possible in all real-world public goods dilemmas.
- No reward mechanisms were included; only punishment was studied.
- The institutional design was minimalistic and did not allow for more complex or realistic institutional features (e.g., partial refund of unused punishment pool funds).
- The number of rounds was fixed but not known to participants, which may affect learning and end-game behavior.
- No possibility for counter-punishment or retaliation, which can be important in real-world peer punishment scenarios.
