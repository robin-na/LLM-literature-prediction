# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Motives for sharing in social networks
Authors: Ligon, E., & Schechter, L.
Journal: Journal of Development Economics
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount shared in dictator game variants
- decomposition of sharing into motives (baseline, directed altruism, sanctions, reciprocity)
- correlates of sharing motives with real-world network and gift-giving behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount shared in each of four dictator game variants. The paper decomposes sharing into preference-based and incentive-based motives, but does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare in a public goods context. There is no group production or public good; the game is a modified dictator game with doubled transfers, not a PGG.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds high baseline sharing in anonymous, random-recipient dictator games, with small but significant increases in sharing when the dictator can choose the recipient or when the dictator's identity is revealed. The decomposition shows that preference-based motives (baseline, directed altruism) account for most mean sharing, but incentive-based motives (sanctions, reciprocity) explain a large share of the variance in sharing and are strongly associated with real-world gift-giving and social network connectedness. The effect of revealing identity (potential for social sanctions or reciprocity) is modest on average but important for explaining individual differences. The study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a set of dictator game variants, not a PGG or linear public goods game, and there is no explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism—only the possibility of social sanctions or reciprocity inferred from information structure. The findings suggest that the potential for social sanctions or reciprocity can modestly increase sharing, but the context and outcome measures are not directly transferable to PGG efficiency predictions. The results may be useful for understanding how information and recipient choice affect sharing motives, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by a single dictator and a recipient (dyadic, not group). 371 dictators participated, each making decisions in four games. Not a group game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays four one-shot dictator game variants in random order. No repeated interaction within a game; each variant is a single decision.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication about the game is explicitly prohibited during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (amount sent) is continuous from 0 to 14 KGs; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 2 (amount sent is doubled for the recipient).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told they will play four games; order is randomized but number is known.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In two of the four games, the dictator's identity is revealed to the recipient; in the other two, it is not. This is the main manipulation for inferring 'sanctions' and 'reciprocity'.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a set of dictator game variants, not a public goods game or linear PGG; there is no group production or group efficiency.
- There is no explicit punishment or reward mechanism; 'sanctions' and 'reciprocity' are inferred from information structure, not implemented as costly actions.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual sharing decisions are measured.
- Findings on the effect of information and recipient choice on sharing may not generalize to PGG environments with explicit punishment.
- The context is rural Paraguay, which may limit generalizability to other populations or settings.
