# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Anger motivates costly punishment of unfair behavior
Authors: Seip, E. C., Van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M.
Journal: Motivation and Emotion
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of costly punishment assigned
- anger ratings
- appraised unfairness
- sadness ratings
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of costly punishment assigned to other players. No group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported. The studies focus on the psychological mechanisms (anger, unfairness) underlying punishment decisions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three laboratory experiments, the paper finds that (1) participants assign more costly punishment to less cooperative (unfair) players in a PGG, (2) anger mediates the relationship between perceived unfairness and punishment in both PGG and STG settings, and (3) experimentally induced anger increases costly punishment of unfair players in a subsequent STG. The studies do not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment is motivated by anger in PGG and STG settings, and that non-cooperation reliably triggers costly punishment. However, it does not provide any data or analysis on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, which are the primary outcomes for the downstream prediction task. Therefore, while the paper supports the behavioral mechanism by which punishment is enacted, it does not inform whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency under any game design dimension. The design details (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are reported and may be useful for mapping to other studies, but no efficiency outcomes are available.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 3 players per group in PGG; Study 2 and 3: 2 players per group in STG.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 5 rounds of PGG; Study 3: 7 rounds of STG; Study 2: one-shot hypothetical scenario.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: continuous contribution (0-20 VUs); Study 2 and 3: all-or-nothing transfer in STG.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: MPCR = 1.5/3 = 0.5; Study 2 and 3: STG multiplier is 4, but not a standard PGG MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 1 VU cost to punisher per punishment point; Study 2 and 3: 1 VU cost per punishment point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 3 VUs lost by punished per 1 VU spent; Study 2 and 3: 3 VUs lost by punished per 1 VU spent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed of the number of rounds in advance (e.g., 5 rounds in Study 1, 7 rounds in Study 3).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown the contributions and payoffs of other players after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined.
- All studies focus on the psychological mechanisms (anger, unfairness) underlying punishment, not on economic or group-level outcomes.
- The STG is not a standard PGG, so results from Studies 2 and 3 may not generalize directly to PGG settings.
- No analysis of how game design dimensions moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff.
- No reward mechanism is present or analyzed.
