The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes such as the timing and level of contribution and defection in response to the disappearance and reappearance of punishment (policing), not on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Some real-world examples mention economic and social activity, but these are not quantified as efficiency or payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in large groups, the disappearance of institutional punishment leads to a delayed decline in public goods contribution and a rapid increase in defection, while the reappearance of punishment leads to a delayed recovery of contribution and a rapid decline in defection. The length of these delays depends on factors such as trust in the punisher, transparency, media behavior, and regime support. The paper highlights the asymmetry between small and large groups, and between peer and institutional punishment, in terms of the speed and nature of behavioral responses. It also discusses the role of blame assignment and political manipulation in shaping these dynamics.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide quantitative or directly predictive evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. However, it offers theoretical and qualitative insights into how the effect of punishment on contribution (and by extension, efficiency) may be moderated by group size, institutional structure, trust, and information transparency. For prediction tasks, the main implication is that in large, real-world groups, the positive effect of punishment on contribution (and thus efficiency) may be delayed or attenuated compared to small, transparent, lab-like settings. The paper is not suitable for direct quantitative prediction but may inform qualitative expectations about moderators.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and qualitative; it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- Real-world examples are illustrative and lack systematic data or controls.
- The public goods framework is used conceptually, not as a direct mapping to experimental PGG designs.
- Findings may not generalize to controlled lab or field experiments with well-defined game parameters.
