# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When to leave carrots for sticks: On the evolution of sanctioning institutions in open communities
Authors: Chugunova, M., Luhan, W. J., & Nicklisch, A.
Journal: Economics Letters
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- profits (group and individual)
- income after public good game
- total payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- migration between communities
- use of punishment and reward points
- inequality within communities
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-related outcomes (profits, payoffs) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contributions, migration, use of sanctions). Efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome is not directly reported, but group profits and welfare losses are discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Participants initially choose reward-based public goods communities, which provide higher payoffs and are preferred by most. Over time, as rewards decline and inequality increases in the reward community, some participants migrate to the punishment community. The punishment community achieves higher contribution rates, but lower average payoffs compared to the reward community. Importantly, when punishment and reward communities compete, the punishment community requires less actual punishment to sustain cooperation, reducing welfare losses typically associated with punishment. The threat of punishment, rather than its frequent use, is sufficient to maintain high contributions. However, the reward community still retains a majority of participants and maintains higher average payoffs, though the difference with the punishment community narrows over time.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the institutional context. In isolation, punishment communities sustain high contributions but at a welfare cost due to punishment expenditures. When a reward community is available, migration dynamics mean that the punishment community can sustain cooperation with less actual punishment, reducing welfare losses. Thus, the negative efficiency impact of punishment is mitigated when there is institutional competition. For prediction, this implies that the efficiency of punishment-enabled games may be higher when players can choose between institutions, and that the mere threat of punishment can be effective if migration is possible. However, in direct comparison, reward communities yield higher payoffs than punishment communities, though the gap narrows over time.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 12 players were used in the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; interactions were anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions are continuous, not all-or-nothing; players choose how many tokens to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 1.6/12 ≈ 0.133; tokens contributed are multiplied by 1.6 and split among 12 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs 1 token to the punisher per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's income by 3 tokens per 1 token spent.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism is available in the reward community.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding costs 1 token to the rewarder per point assigned.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward point increases the target's income by 3 tokens per 1 token spent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated for 30 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players receive feedback on individual contributions of others, as well as summary information on contributions, income, points received/distributed, and total payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player IDs change each round, so punishment/reward is anonymous and not attributable to specific individuals.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome; only profits and payoffs are reported.
- The design allows migration between communities, which is not typical of standard PGG experiments; results may not generalize to fixed-group settings.
- Punishment and reward are implemented in separate communities, not simultaneously within the same group.
- Player IDs change each round, preventing reputation effects.
- The experiment uses a fixed MPCR and group size, so results may not generalize to other parameterizations.
