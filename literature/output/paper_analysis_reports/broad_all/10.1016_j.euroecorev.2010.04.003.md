# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The limits of self-governance when cooperators get punished: Experimental evidence from urban and rural Russia
Authors: Gächter, S., & Herrmann, B.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings
- relative loss in earnings
- group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned
- punishment of cooperators
- punishment of free riders
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are earnings (payoff), contribution rates, and punishment behavior. Efficiency is directly addressed via group earnings and relative losses due to punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, contrary to much of the previous literature, introducing a punishment option in one-shot public goods games in Russia did not increase cooperation or group efficiency. In fact, average earnings (a direct measure of efficiency) were substantially lower in the punishment condition than in the no-punishment condition across all subject pools. This is attributed to high levels of punishment directed at cooperators as well as free riders, leading to large payoff losses. The effect is robust across urban/rural and young/mature subject pools. The findings are supported by both within-subject and between-subject comparisons, and are further corroborated by similar results in repeated-game settings in Russia.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot public goods games with a punishment option, the effect of punishment on efficiency can be negative if misdirected punishment is prevalent. The game design here (n=3, one-shot, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=1, punishment impact=3, no chat, no reward, continuous contributions) is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar settings. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment in a similar Russian context is likely to reduce efficiency, not increase it. The findings highlight the importance of considering the prevalence of antisocial punishment and the social context when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 3 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment was a one-shot game (single round). In some referenced repeated games, 10 rounds were used, but the main experiment is one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: participants could contribute any number of tokens from 0 to 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) was 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher: 1 token per deduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduced the punished subject's payoff by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told each experiment was one-shot; in repeated games, the number of rounds was known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, participants were informed of the contributions of the other two group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted in Russia, and the findings may not generalize to other cultural or institutional contexts.
- The main design is one-shot; repeated-game effects may differ, though similar results were found in Russian repeated games.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The study does not directly manipulate or analyze the effect of group size beyond n=3.
- The prevalence of misdirected punishment may be context-dependent and could differ in other populations.
