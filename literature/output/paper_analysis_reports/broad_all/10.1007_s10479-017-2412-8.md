Journal: Annals of Operations Research

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment rate, sender action, and sender message. Ex-post payoffs (including combined payoff) are reported for each message/action combination, but efficiency (as a ratio to maximum possible group payoff) is not directly calculated. Combined payoff is the closest reported outcome to efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares two elicitation methods (strategy vs. direct-response) in a multi-round, two-player punishment game. The strategy method leads to significantly higher punishment rates (84% vs. 62%) and more frequent cooperative sender actions (S1: 68% vs. 44%). Combined payoffs (group welfare) are higher for S1 actions, and the higher frequency of S1 under the strategy method results in higher average group payoffs. Regression and learning model analyses show that the difference in punishment rates is due to mental accounting: the strategy method allows receivers to integrate the cost and expected benefit of punishment, increasing its perceived utility. Over repeated rounds, senders learn to avoid punishment by choosing S1 more often, further increasing group welfare. Reward rates are low and do not differ between methods. The findings suggest that, in repeated games, the strategic value of punishment (deterrence) is more important than the immediate emotional satisfaction of punishing, and that the design of the punishment mechanism (including decision timing) can significantly affect cooperation and group outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated two-player punishment games, higher punishment rates (enabled by the strategy method) lead to more cooperative sender behavior and higher group payoffs. While the game is not a standard public goods game, the structure is adjacent: one player can take a selfish or cooperative action, and the other can punish. The results suggest that, in repeated settings, enabling effective punishment (with high perceived strategic value) increases efficiency by deterring selfish behavior. For prediction tasks, this implies that, all else equal, punishment mechanisms that increase the perceived strategic value of punishment (e.g., via decision timing or repeated interaction) will lead to higher efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low due to frequent selfish actions. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, punishment-enabled games, but the mechanism may generalize to other repeated social dilemmas with punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by two players: one sender (S) and one receiver (R).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. 40 rounds per session; multi-round design is central to the findings. Higher rounds allow for learning and strategic value of punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sender chooses between two discrete actions (S1 or S2); not a continuous contribution.
- default contribution framing: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. No explicit default; however, in the punishment stage, 'no punishment' is described as the default option.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment reduces the receiver's own payoff from 3 to 2 (cost = 1 unit) and reduces the sender's payoff from 12 to 2 (impact = 10 units). The cost is fixed and known.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: 1 unit cost to receiver reduces sender's payoff by 10 units (from 12 to 2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is possible: receiver can change (6,9) to (8,7), i.e., reduce own payoff by 2 to increase sender's by 2.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward costs the receiver 2 units (from 9 to 7) and increases sender's payoff by 2 units (from 6 to 8).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 2 units cost to receiver yields 2 units benefit to sender (1:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are NOT informed of the total number of rounds in advance (unknown to avoid end-game effects).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, both players are informed of all decisions and payoffs for that round, but not of prior history or other players' histories.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not anonymous: both players know each other's decisions and payoffs at the end of each round.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a two-player sender-receiver punishment game, so findings may not generalize to larger groups or standard PGGs.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to maximum possible group payoff, but combined payoffs for each action are provided.
- The experiment only compares two elicitation methods (strategy vs. direct-response) and does not manipulate other punishment parameters (e.g., cost, effectiveness).
- All participants are university students in a lab setting; external validity to real-world or field settings is limited.
- The findings are most directly relevant to repeated, two-player, high-impact punishment games; generalization to other social dilemmas should be made with caution.
