# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Preferences and constraints: the value of economic games for studying human behaviour
Authors: Pisor, A. C., Gervais, M. M., Purzycki, B. G., & Ross, C. T.
Journal: Royal Society Open Science
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocation decisions (who gives to whom, who takes from whom, who punishes whom)
- patterns of generosity, exploitation, and costly punishment
- effects of recipient and decider characteristics (e.g., kinship, ethnicity, need, wealth, friendship) on transfer and punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes in allocation, taking, and costly reduction (punishment) games, as well as real-world resource transfer networks. It does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus. Outcomes are about who gives, takes, or punishes, and the predictors of these behaviors.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that economic games can be strategically designed to reveal either private-world or real-world preferences, depending on which constraints are relaxed (e.g., resource constraints, anonymity, framing). In the Colombian case study, allocation and punishment behaviors in the games map onto real-world social variables (e.g., kinship, ethnicity, need), but also reveal preferences that are masked in observational data due to real-world constraints. Costly punishment (costly reduction) is more likely to be directed at wealthy or high-status individuals, and less likely at marginalized individuals, but the paper does not analyze the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff. The main contribution is methodological: showing how game design choices affect what is measured and how results relate to real-world behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it discusses costly punishment (costly reduction) games, the outcomes are about who is punished and why, not about group payoff or efficiency. The findings are useful for understanding how punishment behavior is shaped by social context and game design, but do not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency. The paper is most useful for methodological considerations and for understanding the importance of matching game design to research questions and local context.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Games involve multiple players (deciders and recipients), but the exact group size varies by game and is not always specified. In the Colombia case, deciders allocate among community members, but the number per game is not fixed as in standard PGGs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Allocation and taking games involve discrete coin allocations to recipients; choices are not strictly all-or-nothing, but are not continuous in the sense of a divisible endowment.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Costly reduction game involves deciders paying to reduce a recipient's payoff, i.e., costly punishment. The exact cost per unit is not specified.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Allocation game allows for positive transfers (rewards/generosity), but not in the sense of a formal reward mechanism as in PGGs with reward options.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In some games, recipient identity is known (e.g., via photos, names), but it is not always clear if punished individuals know who punished them. The design varies between anonymous and non-anonymous games.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; all outcomes are behavioral (who gives, takes, or punishes whom).
- The games studied are not public goods games or direct variants; they are allocation, taking, and costly reduction (punishment) games, which are adjacent but not equivalent.
- Punishment is studied as costly reduction of others' payoffs, but the effect of punishment on group efficiency or cooperation is not analyzed.
- Game parameters (e.g., group size, number of rounds, punishment cost) are not always specified in detail, limiting comparability to standard PGGs.
- Findings are primarily methodological and contextual, not quantitative about efficiency effects.
