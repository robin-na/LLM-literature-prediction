# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Assumed similarity in personality traits has a short-lived positive effect on cooperation in absence of reciprocity
Authors: Alenaizi, R., Contu, D., Elshareif, E., Kaya, O., & Kaya, I.
Journal: Applied Economics Letters
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- change in contribution between rounds
- effect of personality framing on contribution
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good, and the difference in contribution between rounds under different personality framing treatments. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when participants are told they are matched with others who have similar personality traits, their contributions to the public good increase in the short term (notably in round 7 compared to round 4). When told they are matched with dissimilar personalities, contributions decrease. However, the effect of similarity framing is short-lived and diminishes quickly. The game includes a penalty for non-contribution, but this is a fixed, automatic penalty rather than a peer-administered punishment. The main outcome is contribution level, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The penalty mechanism is automatic and not peer-administered, so it does not map directly to the punishment dimension of interest. The findings are relevant for understanding how framing and perceived similarity affect contributions, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment mechanisms. The game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, and penalty structure are described, but the absence of a peer punishment treatment means this paper offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rounds 1-3 use 2 players; rounds 4-9 use 4 players. The effect of player count on contribution is observable in the data, but not directly analyzed for efficiency or punishment effects.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. There are 9 rounds in total.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous between 0 and 50 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The payoff structure is nonstandard: each player is penalized for non-contribution, and the penalty is shared. The marginal per-capita return is not directly stated, but the payoff equations are provided.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see the resulting payoff after each round, but it is not specified if they see others' contributions or only their own payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The penalty for non-contribution is automatic and not peer-administered, so the study does not test the effect of enabling or disabling punishment in the standard sense.
- No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is provided; only contribution levels are analyzed.
- The experiment is conducted as an online survey with hypothetical matching, not real-time interaction or reciprocity.
- The findings on personality framing are not directly transferable to the effect of punishment mechanisms on efficiency.
