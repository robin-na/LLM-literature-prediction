## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. If the expected loss equals the bribe, the system exhibits neutral cycles (limit cycles) with periodic oscillations in cooperation and efficiency. If the expected loss exceeds the bribe, the system can either converge to all-defection (low efficiency) or to a heteroclinic cycle with cyclic dominance, which may still allow for periods of cooperation. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings. The findings are directly informative for mapping game design parameters (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, bribe size, probability of corruption) to expected efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N is a parameter in the model; numerical examples use N=5. The model is for infinite populations with groups of size N sampled each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is evolutionary and continuous-time (replicator dynamics), not round-based, but numerical simulations show time evolution. No fixed number of rounds.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N, where r is the multiplication factor and N is group size. Used directly in payoff equations and stability conditions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers pay cost G to the punishment pool. The cost to defectors is B (fine), but G is the cost to punishers. Both are varied in analysis.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is B (fine per punisher per defector). The model analyzes how B, G, and bribe parameters interact to determine outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and based on infinite, well-mixed populations; results may not generalize to finite or structured populations.
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are from mathematical analysis and numerical simulation.
- The model only considers pool punishment and does not address peer punishment or reward mechanisms.
- Corruption is modeled as probabilistic bribery, but real-world corruption may involve more complex dynamics.
- No explicit anti-corruption strategies or institutional responses are modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or information feedback mechanisms are included.
