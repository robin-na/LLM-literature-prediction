# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Who Will Do It? Volunteering to Change Cooperation Rules in a Heterogeneous Population
Authors: Gërxhani, K., Volker, B., & Van Breemen, J.
Journal: European Sociological Review
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate individual earnings
- collective welfare
- inequality in earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- rule change attempt (call for vote)
- social value orientation
- risk attitude
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the attempt to change the cooperation rule (call for vote), but the paper also reports aggregate earnings and collective welfare before and after rule changes. Contribution rates are reported as behavioral outcomes. Inequality is also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Changing the minimum contribution rule from low to high in a public goods game increases both individual and collective earnings for all group members, regardless of their MPCR. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The likelihood of attempting a rule change depends on both social value orientation and benefit level: high-earning proselfs are most likely to initiate a change, while prosocials' behavior depends on whether they are high or low earners. The rule change also increases inequality between high and low earners. The experiment does not include peer punishment or reward mechanisms; the only 'cost' is the fee to initiate a rule change vote.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that increasing the minimum required contribution in a public goods game (via a formal rule change) increases group efficiency and total earnings, even in the presence of heterogeneity in MPCR. The findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of institutional changes that increase minimum cooperation requirements, but less directly relevant for peer punishment interventions. The absence of peer punishment or reward means the results are most applicable to settings where institutional rules, rather than decentralized sanctions, are the main intervention. The efficiency gain from raising the minimum contribution is clear and robust, suggesting that, in similar designs, enabling a mechanism to increase minimum contributions can substantially improve efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game consists of 10 rounds; two games per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous in integers from 0 to 10, subject to a minimum imposed by the rule (2 or 8).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is 0.3 for low earners and 0.5 for high earners; both types are present in each group. Higher MPCR leads to higher earnings and more willingness to initiate rule change.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed that each public goods game consists of 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, the total team contribution is made public to all members; individual contributions are not shown.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not include peer punishment or reward mechanisms; only institutional rule change is studied.
- Participants could only choose between two minimum contribution rules (low or high); no opportunity to design or negotiate other rules.
- The only cost for attempting a rule change is a fixed fee to call for a vote; real-world institutional change may involve more complex costs.
- Majority voting is the only decision-making structure used; other mechanisms (e.g., negotiation, consensus) are not explored.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with direct peer punishment, informal norm enforcement, or more complex institutional environments.
