# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social roles and performance of social-ecological systems: evidence from behavioral lab experiments
Authors: Perez, I., Yu, D. J., Janssen, M. A., & Anderies, J. M.
Journal: Ecology and Society
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total group water extractions
- group earnings
- infrastructure efficiency
Non-payoff outcomes:
- presence and combination of social roles (leader, connector, enforcer, etc.)
- distribution of roles by position
- communication patterns
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group performance, measured as total group water extraction, which is highly correlated with group earnings and infrastructure efficiency. The study focuses on the relationship between emergent social roles (coded from chat) and group performance, not on direct manipulation of punishment or sanctions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that group success in a lab irrigation game is best explained by the presence of certain combinations of emergent social roles, rather than any single role. All groups had leaders, knowledge generators, and followers, but successful groups also had connectors and either moralists or observers. The enforcer role (which involves punishing others' behavior, e.g., via threats to withhold investment) was not necessary for group success and was more common in unsuccessful groups. The presence of enforcers appears to be a symptom of conflict and lack of cooperation, not a cause of improved efficiency. The experiment does not include a formal punishment mechanism (e.g., costly punishment with explicit payoff consequences), so findings about 'enforcer' roles are based on communication and threats rather than actual sanctioning actions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling formal punishment mechanisms on efficiency in public goods or common-pool resource games. Instead, it provides evidence that informal enforcement roles (as expressed in chat) are not sufficient or necessary for group success, and that their presence may indicate underlying conflict. For prediction tasks about the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper suggests that the mere presence of enforcement or sanctioning behavior (in the absence of formal mechanisms) does not guarantee improved efficiency. The findings are most relevant for settings with communication and emergent informal roles, not for games with explicit, costly punishment options.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 5 players, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session, with analysis focused on the first 10 rounds (stable environment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chat is enabled for 60 seconds at the start of each round; communication is a central feature.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) and extraction choices are continuous, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is nonlinear and depends on group investment; below a threshold, no output, then increasing and then diminishing returns. Not a fixed MPCR as in standard PGG.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds; experiment is described as 20 rounds with 2 training rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see summary information about their neighbors' investments and actions, but not the whole group; observation is limited to immediate neighbors.

## Important Limitations
- No formal punishment or reward mechanism is present; 'enforcer' is a coded social role based on chat, not an actual costly action.
- Findings about enforcement are based on communication and threats, not on observed payoff consequences of punishment.
- The experiment uses a nonlinear, thresholded production function, not a standard linear PGG.
- Results may not generalize to settings without communication or with different network structures.
- Analysis is limited to the first 10 rounds (stable environment); later rounds with environmental uncertainty are not analyzed.
- The study does not manipulate or compare punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled conditions.
