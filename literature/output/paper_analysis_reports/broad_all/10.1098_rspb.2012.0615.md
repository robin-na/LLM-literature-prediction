## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that individuals who believe more strongly in a powerful, intervening God are less willing to engage in costly punishment of norm violators in a third-party punishment game (3PPG), and less supportive of state-sponsored punishment, but only when these beliefs are made salient. General religiosity has the opposite effect, increasing willingness to punish when salient. The mechanism is that believers in a powerful God attribute responsibility for punishment to God, reducing their own motivation to punish. The studies use both hypothetical and incentivized versions of the 3PPG, but do not report group or individual payoffs or efficiency. The findings are relevant for understanding psychological moderators of punishment behavior, but do not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. Instead, it provides evidence that individual willingness to punish in a 3PPG is reduced by salient belief in a powerful, intervening God, and increased by general religiosity. The findings suggest that psychological or cultural context (e.g., religious beliefs) can moderate the frequency of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency in public goods games, but the paper does not measure or report efficiency or payoff outcomes. For the prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding potential moderators of punishment behavior, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency given game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 3PPG involves three players (A, B, C). All studies using the 3PPG use this structure.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 3PPG is played as a one-shot game (single round) in all studies.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; participants are anonymous and do not interact.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment choices are continuous (not all-or-nothing): Player A can share in $2 increments; Player C can spend any amount of their $10 in $1 increments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs the punisher $1 per $3 reduction in the target's payoff (Player C spends $1 to reduce Player A's payout by $3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed: $1 spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by $3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a one-shot (single round) scenario.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player C is informed of Player A's allocation to Player B and can condition punishment on it. No summary statistics, but full information about the relevant action.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know each other's identities.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, support for state punishment), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The 3PPG is not a standard public goods game; it is a third-party punishment game with a dictator allocation and punishment stage.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus is reported.
- Samples are small and not globally representative (mainly undergraduates and US online participants).
- The effect of punishment on cooperation or efficiency is not studied; only willingness to punish is measured.
- Religious beliefs are manipulated via salience, which may not fully capture real-world effects.
- Potential demand characteristics, though authors argue these are unlikely to explain results.
