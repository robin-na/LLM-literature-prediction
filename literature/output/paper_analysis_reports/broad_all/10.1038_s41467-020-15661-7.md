# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effects of contemporaneous peer punishment on cooperation with the future
Authors: Lohse, J., & Waichman, I.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate earnings
- likelihood of reaching future generations (sustained generations)
- session-level group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- incidence and determinants of punishment
- proportion of cooperators
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the likelihood of sustaining future generations (which is directly tied to group earnings and efficiency), and aggregate earnings. Individual contribution and punishment behavior are also analyzed, but the primary focus is on group-level payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a multigenerational public goods game, voluntary cooperation is difficult to sustain across generations. When peer punishment is available within a generation, the likelihood that the group meets the contribution threshold and sustains future generations more than doubles compared to the baseline. However, the increase in group earnings from more sustained generations is offset by the costs incurred through punishment, resulting in no significant difference in average aggregate earnings (efficiency) between the punishment and no-punishment conditions. The effect of punishment on individual contribution levels is small, but it is sufficient to increase the rate at which groups meet the threshold. Punishment is mainly directed at those who contribute less than their peers, especially when the group fails to reach the threshold.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that enabling peer punishment in a threshold-based, multigenerational public goods game increases the probability of group success (i.e., reaching future generations and thus group-level payoff), but the efficiency gains are offset by the costs of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of peer punishment can increase the likelihood of group success, but unless punishment is low-cost or highly effective, the net effect on efficiency may be limited or null. The design dimensions—such as group size, threshold, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the absence of communication—are all well-specified and should be used to inform predictions. The results are most relevant for settings with threshold public goods, no communication, and contemporaneous peer punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players; each session has 4 groups (generations) of 3 players each.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes at most one contribution decision; the game proceeds sequentially through up to 4 generations (groups).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how much of their €10 endowment to contribute (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0 for the current generation (no benefit to self or peers), but 2.0 for the next generation (each €1 contributed by the group yields €2 to the next group if threshold is met).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs €1 per point inflicted; each punisher can assign up to 2 points per other group member.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each €1 spent on punishment reduces the target's earnings by €3 (fine-to-fee ratio 3:1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed about the total number of generations (4) and their position in the sequence.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, group members are shown the individual contributions of each member of their own group (but not of other groups).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is assigned to group members identified by a number; group members can identify who punished whom within their group.

## Important Limitations
- The public goods game is a threshold (all-or-none) multigenerational design, not a standard linear PGG, so results may not generalize to linear or non-threshold settings.
- Punishment is only possible within the same generation; no cross-generational punishment is allowed.
- The costliness of punishment in this design offsets the gains from increased cooperation, so efficiency does not increase with punishment.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit coordination compared to real-world settings.
- The sample consists of German university students, which may limit generalizability.
- The number of generations and group size are fixed and relatively small.
