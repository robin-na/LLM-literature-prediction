# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: What Are Punishment and Reputation for?
Authors: Krasnow, M. M., Cosmides, L., Pedersen, E. J., & Tooby, J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust decisions
- punishment decisions
- cooperation decisions
- use of first-party and third-party reputation
- association between punishment and subsequent cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: whether subjects trust, punish, or cooperate with partners, and how these are influenced by reputation information. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff is described in the game structure, but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that (1) subjects use third-party reputation to regulate trust only when first-party (personal) information is unavailable; (2) punishment is not more likely to be directed at partners with a reputation for norm violation; (3) subjects are more likely to cooperate with defectors whom they have punished, consistent with punishment as a bargaining tool for future cooperation; (4) when deciding to cooperate, subjects rely on the partner's most recent behavior toward themselves, not on past reputation. The results support social exchange theory over group norm maintenance theory in the context of dyadic trust games with punishment. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the psychological function of punishment in dyadic trust games, showing that punishment is used to bargain for better treatment rather than to enforce group norms. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it use a public goods game or a multi-player setting. The findings suggest that in small-scale, dyadic games, the presence of punishment may not increase group efficiency, but rather serves to regulate individual relationships. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on whether the game context is dyadic or group-based, and on the psychological mechanisms engaged. The design dimensions most relevant here are the dyadic structure, the use of reputation, and the role of punishment as a bargaining tool.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players: subject and partner).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trust game consists of 2 rounds per partner.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/trust decisions are binary: trust/distrust, cooperate/defect.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Study 1, trusting multiplies the endowment (e.g., $0.90$ to $1.50$ if partner cooperates); in Study 2, passing multiplies 100 ep to 200 ep. The marginal return is determined by the game's payoff structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Study 1, punishment costs $0.30$ to reduce partner's payoff by $1.20$; in Study 2, costs 15 ep to reduce partner's payoff by 165 ep.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: cost-to-impact ratio is 1:4 (Study 1) or 1:11 (Study 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the game structure and number of rounds (2 rounds per partner).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are shown summary information about partners' past behavior (reputation for cheating/cooperating with others and with self).

## Important Limitations
- The experiments use dyadic trust games, not public goods games or multi-player settings, so findings may not generalize to group contexts.
- No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (trust, punishment, cooperation).
- The use of deception (sham partners) may limit ecological validity.
- The sample consists of university undergraduates, which may limit generalizability.
- The findings are most relevant to the psychological function of punishment in dyadic exchange, not to the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
