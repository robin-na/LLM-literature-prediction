## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that mainstream cultural evolutionary models, which treat punishment as an individual-level, uncoordinated action, fail to capture the collective, power-based enforcement of prosocial norms observed in real-world societies, especially among hunter-gatherers. It contends that collective punishment and coordinated norm enforcement are central to the evolution of morality and prosociality, and that these processes are not well-modeled by standard public goods game frameworks. The paper reviews anthropological evidence for collective punishment and discusses how this shapes moral psychology, but does not report or synthesize efficiency or payoff outcomes from experimental games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides important theoretical context for interpreting the role of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, emphasizing that real-world punishment is often collective and coordinated, not merely the sum of individual actions. However, it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it analyze how game design dimensions (such as player count, rounds, or punishment cost) moderate these effects. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that models which ignore collective decision-making and power dynamics may misrepresent the real impact of punishment, but it does not provide data or direct evidence to calibrate predictions of efficiency outcomes.
