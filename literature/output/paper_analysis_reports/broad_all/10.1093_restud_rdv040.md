## Source
Title: Efficient Coordination in Weakest-Link Games
Authors: Riedl, A., Rohde, I. M. T., & Strobel, M.
Journal: Review of Economic Studies
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group welfare (sum of earnings)
- average group payoff

- proportion of optimal welfare achieved
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual and group effort levels
- frequency of exclusion
- interaction/neighbourhood size
- responses to exclusion
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group welfare (total earnings), directly compared to the optimal (fully efficient) benchmark. Effort levels and exclusion rates are also reported, but as mechanisms or secondary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated weakest-link games, groups with fixed interaction structure (all interact with all) rapidly converge to inefficient equilibria, especially as group size increases. In contrast, when players can choose their interaction neighbourhoods (i.e., whom to interact with), groups quickly achieve and sustain near-optimal efficiency and welfare, even in groups of 24. The key mechanism is that high-effort players exclude low-effort players, who then increase their effort to regain inclusion. This exclusion is often costly to both parties, resembling costly punishment, but the effect persists even when exclusion is not materially costly (i.e., in a 'self-protection' treatment). The results are robust to severe reductions in information, alternative payoff functions, and large group sizes. The findings suggest that enabling endogenous partner choice is a powerful mechanism for achieving efficient coordination in weakest-link environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling endogenous neighbourhood choice (partner selection) in weakest-link games leads to large, robust increases in group efficiency and welfare, compared to fixed-group baselines. The effect is observed across a range of group sizes (8 and 24), information conditions, and payoff structures. The mechanism is exclusion of low-effort members, which induces higher effort and thus higher group payoffs. Importantly, the effect does not depend on exclusion being materially costly (punishment per se is not required). For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of endogenous partner choice is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of exclusion/punishment-like mechanisms in weakest-link (and possibly related public goods) environments. However, the results are for weakest-link (minimum effort) games, which are adjacent but not identical to standard linear public goods games.

Main treatments use groups of 8 and 24; effect of neighbourhood choice is robust across both sizes. In fixed groups, efficiency collapses as size increases; with neighbourhood choice, high efficiency is sustained.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. All treatments use 30 repeated rounds. Efficiency with neighbourhood choice is achieved and sustained over rounds; in fixed groups, efficiency declines over time.

- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the game would last 30 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In most treatments, subjects had access to the complete history of their own and others' actions; in NT-IL, information was restricted to past interactions.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental environment is a weakest-link (minimum effort) game, not a standard linear public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented via exclusion (partner choice), not direct costly punishment as in standard PGGs.
- No reward mechanisms are studied.
- All experiments are conducted in the lab with student subjects; field generalizability is not tested.
- The effect of exclusion may include symbolic or signaling components not fully disentangled from material punishment.
- The study does not explore transaction costs or limits on the number of possible neighbours, which may affect real-world applicability.
