## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average total payoff of the population
- accumulated income of individuals
- income gap/variance/standard deviation of incomes
Non-payoff outcomes:
- fraction of cooperators
- fraction of defectors
- fraction of loners
- strategy distribution (C, D, L, I, U)
- spatial pattern formation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average total payoff (efficiency), accumulated income, and income distribution, as well as cooperation rates and strategy fractions. The model is simulation-based, not empirical.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This simulation study finds that a centralized intervention mechanism (distinct from peer or pool punishment) can increase the average total payoff of the group and reduce income inequality, provided the intervention is sufficiently strong (i.e., the product of intervention degree and intensity exceeds a threshold). Weak intervention can actually reduce cooperation and group payoff compared to no intervention. The model provides explicit parameter ranges for when full cooperation and maximum efficiency are achieved. The intervention cost is shared by all, and the mechanism is implemented by a central authority, not by individuals punishing each other.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that centralized, institutionally-enforced interventions (akin to pool punishment but implemented as forced contributions rather than direct payoff reduction) can increase efficiency in spatial voluntary PGGs, but only if the intervention is strong enough. The effect is non-monotonic: weak intervention can be counterproductive. The findings are most relevant for settings with voluntary participation, spatial structure, and centralized enforcement. The explicit mapping between intervention parameters and efficiency outcomes can inform predictions about the efficiency impact of institutional punishment-like mechanisms, especially when the control (no intervention) efficiency is low. However, the mechanism is not standard punishment, so direct transfer to peer punishment settings should be cautious.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use a 100x100 lattice; each PGG is played with 5 players (focal + 4 neighbors).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 60,000 Monte Carlo Steps (MCSs), with results averaged over the last 1,000 MCSs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Synergy factor r (and effective r') is varied; higher r' increases efficiency and cooperation, especially with intervention.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Intervention cost is subtracted from the common pool (reducing r to r'); cost is shared by all participants.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Intervention intensity (β) and degree (η) determine how much defectors are forced to contribute; higher values increase effectiveness.

## Important Limitations
- The model is simulation-based and not empirically tested with human subjects.
- The intervention mechanism is not standard peer or pool punishment, but a centralized forced contribution system.
- Findings may not generalize to non-spatial, non-voluntary, or non-centralized PGGs.
- No direct comparison to standard punishment treatments; results may not transfer to peer punishment settings.
- Players have no information about others' actions or payoffs, which differs from many experimental PGGs.
