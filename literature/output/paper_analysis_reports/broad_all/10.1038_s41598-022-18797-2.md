## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that conditional defector strategies—cheaters who sometimes cooperate (e.g., to share dispersal costs or pay to escape punishment)—can successfully invade and dominate populations even when mechanisms like punishment, kin selection, and spatial structure are present. In the models, when cheaters can share dispersal costs, they can migrate and outcompete cooperators, leading to cooperator extinction. Similarly, when cheaters reduce their own payoff to avoid detection and punishment, they can dominate despite the presence of punishment mechanisms. The main reported outcomes are the frequencies of agent types and extinction events, not direct efficiency or group payoff measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a cautionary theoretical perspective: the presence of punishment mechanisms does not guarantee the maintenance of cooperation or high efficiency if conditional defector strategies are possible. The models are adjacent to public goods games and do not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so direct quantitative prediction is not possible. However, the findings suggest that in environments where agents can adaptively evade punishment (e.g., by reducing their own payoff to avoid detection), the expected efficiency gain from enabling punishment may be much lower or even negative, as cheaters can still dominate. For prediction tasks, this paper highlights the importance of considering the sophistication of defector strategies and the limitations of punishment as a stabilizing mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- The models are agent-based simulations, not empirical laboratory or field experiments.
- Outcomes focus on population composition (trait frequencies, extinction) rather than direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
- The models are adjacent to, but not exact, public goods games; resource harvest and regrowth are modeled, but not in standard PGG terms.
- No direct quantitative analysis of efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
- The models assume agents can implement sophisticated conditional defector strategies, which may not always be possible in real-world or experimental settings.
