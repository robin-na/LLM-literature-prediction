# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Effects of Gender on Costly Punishment
Authors: Mieth, L., Buchner, A., & Bell, R.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral Decision Making
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount of money spent on punishment
- cooperation rate
- harshness of punishment (money spent per punishment event)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of cooperation
- probability of different types of punishment (moralistic, hypocritical, irrational, antisocial)
- gender effects on cooperation and punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: cooperation rates and punishment rates/types. The amount of money spent on punishment is reported, but group efficiency or total group payoff is not directly analyzed. The design is a two-player prisoner's dilemma with a costly punishment option, not a standard public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that costly punishment is used frequently in a two-player prisoner's dilemma with a punishment option, primarily as moralistic punishment (punishing defection after cooperating). Participants spend more money punishing defecting female partners than male partners, but this is due to higher cooperation rates with female partners, not a direct gender bias in punishment. Female participants are more likely to cooperate with and punish female partners, while male participants do not differentiate by partner gender. The presence of punishment by partners increases cooperation rates, but the paper does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, nor does it compare efficiency with and without punishment in a way that would allow direct prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that costly punishment is used in a two-player prisoner's dilemma, and that the opportunity to punish is strongly influenced by cooperation rates, which in turn are affected by gender dynamics. However, it does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, nor does it provide a control (no-punishment) condition for direct comparison. The findings suggest that punishment can increase cooperation when the threat of being punished exists, but the lack of efficiency or group payoff data limits its direct utility for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games or their variants. The design is adjacent to PGGs, and the behavioral findings may inform models of punishment and cooperation, but not efficiency prediction per se.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use a two-player (dyadic) prisoner's dilemma game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant interacts with 40 partners (20 female, 20 male), one-shot per partner; no repeated rounds with the same partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: cooperate (invest 30 cents) or defect (invest 0 cents).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player’s investment is summed, a bonus of 1/3 of total investments is added, and the total is split equally. For mutual cooperation: both invest 30c, total 60c, bonus 20c, total 80c, each gets 40c (gain of 10c each). For unilateral cooperation: cooperator invests 30c, defector 0c, total 30c, bonus 10c, total 40c, each gets 20c. MPCR for each cent invested is 0.5 (since each cent increases the total by 1.5, split equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 cent to reduce partner’s account by 10 cents (cost:impact ratio 1:10).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 cent spent reduces partner’s account by 10 cents.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials (40 partners, one-shot each).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a two-player prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game; findings may not generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment) and money spent on punishment are reported.
- No control condition without punishment for direct comparison of efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Partners are preprogrammed, not real human players, which may affect ecological validity.
- No communication or repeated interaction with the same partner; all interactions are one-shot.
- Gender effects are the main focus, not the overall effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
