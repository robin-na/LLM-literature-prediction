# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: An economic experiment reveals that humans prefer pool punishment to maintain the commons
Authors: Traulsen, A., Röhl, T., & Milinski, M.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (average payoff per individual per round)
- net average payoff in euros
- maximum average payoff
- loss in efficiency (relative to theoretical optimum)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- level of cooperation
- level of defection
- frequency of loners
- frequency of peer punishment
- frequency of pool punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, average payoff) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (cooperation, defection, punishment choices). Efficiency is a central dependent variable, especially in relation to the introduction of second-order punishment and the type of punishment mechanism.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated public goods games with five players per group, the introduction of pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) increases cooperation and reduces defection, but leads to a significant loss in efficiency (average payoff per round drops by about a third). Peer punishment alone does not significantly affect efficiency, as punishment is rarely used when cooperation is high. When both peer and pool punishment are available, pool punishment dominates in the presence of second-order punishment, again with a substantial efficiency loss. The results support the theoretical prediction that pool punishment can stabilize cooperation but at the cost of group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard public goods game with five players, 25 rounds, no communication, binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, and known punishment costs and effectiveness, enabling pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) reduces efficiency compared to the control (no or only peer punishment). The efficiency loss is substantial (about one third) when pool punishment is used with second-order punishment. Peer punishment alone does not significantly reduce efficiency. Thus, for prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment mechanism (peer vs pool), and whether second-order punishment is implemented, are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. The specific parameterization (punishment cost, punishment impact, MPCR) is also informative for quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game 3 (main analysis) had 25 rounds, with second-order punishment introduced after round 10.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was binary: contribute €0 or €0.50.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Money in the pool was multiplied by 3.1 and redistributed among all PG players. With 5 players, MPCR = 3.1/5 = 0.62.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punish was €0.50 per punished individual (peer) or €0.50 per pool punisher (pool).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact: punished player pays €1.00 per punisher (peer) or per pool punisher (pool).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the number of rounds (game 3 had 25 rounds, with a clear transition at round 10).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players received information on contributions before making punishment decisions (peer punishment), but not before pool punishment decisions.

## Important Limitations
- The pool punishment mechanism was implemented in a way that is less efficient than some real-world or more sophisticated institutional designs; the fixed cost of maintaining the punishment institution is high.
- The experiment used only five-player groups and binary contribution choices, which may limit generalizability to other group sizes or contribution structures.
- No communication or reputation mechanisms were allowed, which may affect the external validity for real-world commons dilemmas.
- Second-order punishment was implemented as punishing all cooperators who do not punish, which may not reflect all real-world institutional settings.
- No reward mechanisms were tested, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
