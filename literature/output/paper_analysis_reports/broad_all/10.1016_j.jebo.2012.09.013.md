# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputation in a public goods game: Taking the design of credit bureaus to the lab
Authors: McIntosh, C., Sadoulet, E., Buck, S., & Rosada, T.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group contribution
- individual contribution
- ejection rates
- probability of ejection
Non-payoff outcomes:
- conditional cooperation
- sensitivity of ejection to contribution
- demographic correlates of contribution
- correlation of game play with real-world default
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good, not efficiency or group payoff. Ejection rates and selection are also analyzed. There is no direct calculation or reporting of efficiency (i.e., group payoff as a fraction of the maximum possible), but average contributions are reported and can be mapped to efficiency in a standard PGG. No explicit analysis of total group earnings, welfare, or surplus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that both group-level and individual-level reputation mechanisms (used for reassigning ejected players) increase average contributions in a repeated public goods game with ejection, compared to a random reassignment baseline. The effect size is about a 7.5% increase in average contribution (from ~75% to ~82% of the endowment). There is no significant difference in average contribution between group and individual information treatments. Ejection is more sensitive to individual contribution in the group information treatment. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the contribution data can be mapped to efficiency. The ejection mechanism is not a standard punishment technology (no direct costly reduction of others' payoffs), but rather exclusion from play. The findings suggest that group-based reputation is at least as effective as individual-based reputation in sustaining cooperation, and that transitioning microfinance credit bureaus from group to individual reporting may not yield substantial gains.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with ejection (exclusion) as the sanction mechanism, both group-level and individual-level reputation mechanisms increase average contributions compared to random reassignment. The effect is similar for both information types. While the study does not report efficiency directly, the increase in average contribution implies a corresponding increase in efficiency. The ejection mechanism is not a standard punishment technology (no direct costly punishment), so the results are most relevant for environments where exclusion or ostracism is the main sanction. The findings suggest that, for games with endogenous group selection and ejection, introducing a reputation-based reassignment mechanism (either group or individual) can improve cooperation and implied efficiency, but the marginal benefit of individual-level over group-level information is minimal. The results are less directly informative for standard PGGs with costly punishment, but may be useful for predicting the effect of exclusionary sanctions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 50 players, assigned to groups of 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consisted of 2 preliminary rounds, followed by 4 sequences of 2 rounds each (total 10 rounds per game).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; likely not allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how many of 20 units to contribute each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit contributed is doubled and divided among 5 players: MPCR = 2/5 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ejection (exclusion) costs the leader 1 unit per ejection. No standard costly punishment stage.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exclusion (ejection), not direct payoff reduction. Ejected players are removed from play and may or may not be reassigned, depending on the treatment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told the rules and structure of each game before play; the number of rounds is fixed and known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told the average contribution in their group at the end of each round. Only leaders see individual contributions (anonymized) for their group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Leaders are identified anonymously to themselves; ejection decisions are made using anonymous code numbers. Players do not know who the leader is in each period.

## Important Limitations
- The sanction mechanism is exclusion (ejection), not standard costly punishment; results may not generalize to games with direct costly punishment.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not reported directly; only average contributions are analyzed.
- The experiment is conducted with micro-entrepreneurs in Guatemala, which may limit generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is present; only exclusionary sanctions are studied.
- The design does not include uncertainty or risk in payoffs, unlike some real-world microfinance contexts.
- The study does not manipulate or analyze all game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task (e.g., chat, default contribution, reward).
