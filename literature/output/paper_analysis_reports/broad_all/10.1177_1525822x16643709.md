# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: RICH Economic Games for Networked Relationships and Communities: Development and Preliminary Validation in Yasawa, Fiji
Authors: Gervais, M. M.
Journal: Field Methods
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate participant earnings
- amount kept/allocated/taken/reduced
- target earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- altruism
- spite
- decision rationales
- mapping to real-life experiences
Outcome notes:
The study reports both payoff-based outcomes (e.g., total earnings, amount reduced, amount kept) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., altruism, spite, rationales for decisions). Efficiency per se is not directly calculated as a ratio to a fully cooperative outcome, but group and individual payoffs are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a Fijian village, participants are willing to spend significant portions of their endowment to reduce the earnings of others in a costly reduction game (CRG), with 66% of deciders reducing at least one target. The mean amount spent on reductions was 45% of the available stake. Target earnings varied widely, and some participants ended with negative aggregate earnings (before post-hoc adjustment). The motives for reduction included both punitive and leveling (egalitarian) reasons. Compared to anonymous-target punishment games in neighboring villages, the rate of punitive behavior is higher in the CRG, likely due to the integration of recipient identities. However, the study does not directly measure the effect of punishment on group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, nor does it use a standard public goods game structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a context where punishment is possible and recipient identities are known, individuals are willing to pay substantial costs to reduce others' payoffs, often for leveling or punitive reasons. The cost-to-impact ratio is clearly specified (0.5 FJ$ cost to reduce 2 FJ$ from a target). However, because the game is not a standard public goods game and there is no control condition without punishment, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments. The high willingness to punish and the resulting wide variation in earnings suggest that punishment can lead to significant reductions in aggregate payoffs, especially when used for leveling rather than norm enforcement. The study is most informative for settings where punishment is possible, costly, and can be used for reasons beyond norm enforcement, particularly in small, close-knit communities.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involved 54 participants (all adult males in the village), with each acting as both decider and target. The CRG is a multi-recipient game, but not a simultaneous group interaction as in a standard PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a one-shot decision; there are no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants was allowed; decisions were made in isolation with only the experimenter and assistant present.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/reduction choices are continuous (participants can allocate, take, or reduce any number of coins within the constraints), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the CRG, each reduction costs the punisher FJ$0.50 and reduces the target's earnings by FJ$2.00.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4:1 (each FJ$0.50 spent reduces target by FJ$2.00).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know each game is a one-shot decision; the structure is explained in advance.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reduction decisions are confidential; targets do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The games are not standard public goods games; the CRG is a multi-recipient costly reduction game, not a group contribution game.
- There is no control condition without punishment, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly estimated.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to a fully cooperative baseline; only aggregate and individual earnings are reported.
- The sample is all-male and from a single Fijian village, limiting generalizability.
- Recipient identities are always known, which may not generalize to anonymous or impersonal settings.
- Some participants ended with negative earnings, which required post-hoc adjustment, complicating interpretation of payoff outcomes.
