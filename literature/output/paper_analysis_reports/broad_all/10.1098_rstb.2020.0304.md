# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Is it costly to deceive? People are adept at detecting gossipers' lies but may not reward honesty
Authors: Fonseca, M. A., & Peters, K.
Journal: Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- participant payoff in trust game (tokens/ECU earned)
- group payoff (implied by trust and return rates)
- payoff as a function of gossip honesty
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust decisions (amount sent to Agent or gossiper)
- accuracy ratings of gossip
- frequency and type of lies in gossip
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoffs (efficiency-related) and trust decisions. The paper directly analyzes how acting on honest vs dishonest gossip affects payoffs, and how participants treat gossipers based on perceived honesty.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Dishonest gossip reduces efficiency in trust-based interactions: participants who act on dishonest gossip (especially positive lies about untrustworthy agents or negative lies about trustworthy agents) earn lower payoffs than those who act on honest gossip. Participants can detect some dishonest gossip, especially after interacting with the target, and are more likely to trust gossipers they believe to be honest. However, the tendency to socially sanction dishonest gossipers (by reducing trust) is inconsistent and influenced by the valence of the gossip: gossipers who share negative information, even if honest, are trusted less. Thus, while social sanctions for dishonesty exist, they are imperfect and do not always align with actual honesty, limiting their effectiveness in promoting efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the presence of dishonest communication in trust-based games reduces efficiency, as measured by participant payoffs. While not a public goods game, the trust game with gossip is adjacent and the mechanism—misleading information undermining cooperation—transfers to PGG-like settings. The findings suggest that, in environments where social sanctions for dishonesty are weak or inconsistently applied, the efficiency gains from reputation mechanisms (like gossip) are limited by the prevalence and detectability of dishonesty. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effectiveness of punishment or sanction mechanisms in improving efficiency depends not only on their presence but also on their alignment with actual dishonest behavior and the social context (e.g., whether negative but honest information is socially penalized). The paper does not manipulate classic PGG punishment dimensions (cost, tech, etc.), but it does show that the ability to sanction dishonesty is necessary but not sufficient for efficiency gains.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 2 used 8-person Investor networks; each round is a one-shot trust game between an Investor and an Agent (Agent strategies pre-collected from 24 participants). The group size is fixed and known.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 12 rounds per participant; Study 2: 24 rounds per participant (part 1), plus 1 gossiper-trust game (part 2).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (trust) is continuous: participants choose how many tokens/ECU (0-10) to send.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust game: tokens sent are tripled (multiplier = 3), then Agent decides how much to return. Effective MPCR for Investor is determined by Agent's return rate, not fixed as in PGG.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: participants know there are 12 rounds. Study 2: number of rounds in part 1 is not revealed to avoid endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants receive structured gossip messages summarizing previous Agent behavior (amount sent and returned).

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is based on the trust game with gossip, not a public goods game; findings are adjacent but not directly transferable to PGG settings.
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism is implemented; social sanctions are operationalized as trust decisions, which may not map directly to PGG punishment.
- The ability to sanction dishonest gossipers is limited and inconsistently applied, especially in incentivized settings.
- Sample size for some analyses (e.g., gossiper-trust game in Study 2) is small, limiting statistical power.
- The study does not test whether social sanctions actually increase honesty in gossipers, only how participants respond to perceived dishonesty.
- Valence effects (preference for positive gossip) may confound the relationship between honesty and social sanctioning.
