# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games
Authors: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y.
Journal: Social Psychology Quarterly
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total earning (MUs)
- individual earning per round
- efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum possible)
- average contribution per round
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prosocial punishment (punishment of defectors)
- antisocial punishment (punishment of cooperators)
- perceived propriety/legitimacy
- compliance (change in contribution after punishment)
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (earnings, efficiency, contributions) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (punishment types, legitimacy perceptions, compliance).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 3 players in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 6 rounds (described as 'up to 10 rounds', but experiment ended after 6 periods).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; participants were isolated at computer terminals.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could contribute any amount (0-20 MUs) per round.
- default contribution framing: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. If a participant missed a turn, the server automatically made a contribution based on their previous contribution, but this was a fallback, not a default framing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) was 0.5 per MU contributed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost was manipulated: 1 MU per point (costly) or 0 MU per point (costless). Costly punishment increased efficiency, especially for designated punishers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness was fixed: each punishment point reduced the target's earnings by 3 MUs.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment (deduction points) was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told they would play 'up to 10 rounds', but the experiment ended after 6 rounds. The exact number was not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all players saw how much each person contributed, who was punished, by whom, and at what cost.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers were identifiable; all players knew who punished whom and at what cost.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used only three-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- The number of rounds was short (6 rounds), and participants did not know the exact number in advance.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism was included; only punishment was studied.
- The manipulation of punishment cost was binary (costly vs. costless), not continuous.
- The study was conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may not fully capture real-world dynamics.
- Efficiency was measured as total earnings, but the paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the maximum possible in all tables.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with communication, larger groups, or different MPCR values.
