## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that the evolution of moral judgment is rooted in emotional dispositions that support the enforcement of group-beneficial norms through punishment and meta-punishment. It draws on ethnographic and evolutionary accounts of hunter-gatherer societies, where collective punishment of cheaters and bullies (those who disrupt cooperation and resource sharing) is common and is enforced through both direct and reputational means (e.g., gossip, ostracism, disapproval). The author suggests that these mechanisms enabled stable cooperation in small groups, but the analysis is conceptual and evolutionary rather than empirical or quantitative. The paper does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment and meta-punishment mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in small, egalitarian groups, especially in contexts analogous to public goods dilemmas (e.g., meat sharing after cooperative hunting). However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff outcomes with or without punishment. Its relevance is mainly in supporting the plausibility of punishment as a stabilizer of cooperation, especially when punishment is collective, reputational, and embedded in group norms. For prediction tasks, this paper may inform the expected direction of punishment effects in small, highly interactive, reputation-sensitive groups, but it does not provide quantitative or design-specific guidance.
