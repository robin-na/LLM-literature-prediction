## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model shows that cooperation can be sustained or undermined depending on how agents interpret the motives behind gossip and how they update reputations. If gossip is interpreted as pro-social and agents use a single reputation system, cooperation can rise to high levels. If gossip is interpreted as selfish or negative, a double reputation system (where agents discount unreliable gossip but do not punish the gossiper by refusing to cooperate) helps maintain initial cooperation levels. The model highlights the importance of motive interpretation and the structure of reputation systems for sustaining cooperation, but does not analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games. However, it offers indirect insights into how information transmission, reputation updating, and the interpretation of motives can moderate the effects of social information on cooperation. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that mechanisms which allow agents to discount unreliable information without ostracizing the source may help sustain cooperation, but the model's outcomes are in terms of cooperation rates, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N is a model parameter, default 200, with robustness checks for 50 and 100.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10,000 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent chooses to cooperate or defect in each round (binary choice).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents receive structured gossip about others' recent actions (cooperation/defection) via gossip statements.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game and does not include explicit punishment or costly sanctions; it models repeated dyadic cooperation with gossip-based reputation updating.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of cooperation rates, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The model assumes fixed motives for gossip and a fixed probability of correct motive interpretation, which may not reflect real-world dynamics.
- No explicit costs or payoffs are modeled for gossiping, updating reputation, or for cooperation/defection choices.
- The model does not address how agents interpret gossip motives in detail; interpretation is exogenously set as a probability.
