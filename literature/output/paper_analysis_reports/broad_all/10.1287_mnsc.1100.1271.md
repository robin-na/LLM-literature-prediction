## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the effect of forgetfulness on efficiency in repeated games with punishment is fundamentally ambiguous: it can either reduce or increase the set of discount factors and game parameters under which full cooperation (and thus maximum efficiency) is sustainable. When the stage game is such that the cooperative action always yields nonnegative payoffs, forgetfulness weakens punishment and makes cooperation harder to sustain, reducing efficiency. However, when the stage game allows for the possibility that a forgetful player can be punished below the minimax, forgetfulness can make severe punishments credible and thus expand the range of parameters supporting cooperation, increasing efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas and graphical illustrations for these effects in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and a franchising effort game. The results are theoretical and depend on the structure of the stage game and the probability of forgetfulness.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games is not monotonic and can be moderated by cognitive or institutional features like forgetfulness. In environments where punishment is only as severe as the stage minimax, introducing forgetfulness will generally reduce the efficiency gains from punishment. However, in environments where forgetfulness allows for below-minimax punishments, punishment can become more effective at sustaining cooperation, potentially increasing efficiency beyond what is possible with perfect recall. For prediction tasks, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined solely from standard game design dimensions; one must also consider whether the environment allows for forgetfulness and whether the stage game payoffs permit below-minimax punishments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates but gives clear theoretical boundaries and mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main analysis is for two-player (duopoly) games, as in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and the franchising effort game. The model is symmetric and focuses on two players, but the framework could be extended.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The games are modeled as infinitely repeated (infinite horizon) with discounting. Some discussion of finite automata and memory, but main results are for infinite repetition.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main examples (prisoner's dilemma) are discrete action games (cooperate or defect). The franchising effort game allows for continuous effort, but the analysis focuses on collusive vs. non-collusive actions.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: it is implemented by reverting to Nash equilibrium or other strategies, not by explicit costly punishment. The cost is implicit in the payoffs from punishment phases.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is central: the paper analyzes how forgetfulness changes the severity of punishment (including below-minimax punishments), which in turn affects the ability to sustain cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is infinitely repeated (or with a known discount factor). There is no uncertainty about the horizon.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe all actions taken in the previous period (unless they are forgetful). The model assumes full observability of actions, subject to forgetfulness.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- The analysis is limited to two-player symmetric games (prisoner's dilemma and a specific effort game); results may not generalize to larger groups or public goods games.
- Punishment is implemented via equilibrium strategies (e.g., Nash reversion), not via explicit costly punishment mechanisms as in many experimental public goods games.
- The model assumes perfect monitoring of actions, except for forgetfulness; real-world environments may have additional noise or information frictions.
- The effect of forgetfulness depends critically on the structure of the stage game payoffs; the results may not apply to all games.
- No direct analysis of public goods games or marginal per-capita return; relevance to PGGs is indirect.
- No analysis of contribution rates, punishment frequency, or other non-payoff behavioral outcomes.
