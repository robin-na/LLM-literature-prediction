# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The dynamics of human behavior in the public goods game with institutional incentives
Authors: Dong, Y. L., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group average payoff
- expected payoff per round
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution
- behavioral response coefficients (b1, b2, b3, b4)
- conditional cooperation
- conforming behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average contribution (cooperation level). Group average payoff is analyzed in relation to behavioral coefficients, but efficiency (as a ratio to maximum possible) is not directly reported. Payoff is discussed as a function of group behavior, but not as the primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The group average payoff is strongly positively correlated with b1 (self-regarding preference) in punishment and combined conditions. The effect of varying the intensity of incentives (Up, Const, Down) is minimal. The presence of institutional punishment is the key driver of higher cooperation and payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with four players, 50 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, adding institutional punishment (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases group payoffs and average contributions compared to control. The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions are 50 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; subjects interact anonymously via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject decides how much (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total contributions multiplied by 1.6, split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Institutional punishment is not paid by players but administered by the institution; cost to punisher is not applicable.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is administered by the institution; one player per round is punished, amount varies by treatment (16-25.6 units).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is present in IR and IRP treatments; reward alone does not increase payoffs, but combined with punishment (IRP) it does.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Institutional reward is not paid by players but administered by the institution; cost to rewarder is not applicable.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is administered by the institution; one player per round is rewarded, amount varies by treatment (16-25.6 units).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is 50 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the average contribution of other group members each round.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to maximum possible; only group average payoff and average contribution are analyzed.
- All games are with 4 players and 50 rounds; generalizability to other group sizes or time horizons is untested.
- Institutional incentives are administered by the experimenter, not by peers; results may not generalize to peer punishment/reward.
- Only one player per round is punished or rewarded, which may limit the strength of the incentive compared to designs where all defectors/cooperators are targeted.
- No analysis of heterogeneity in response to punishment/reward beyond average effects.
