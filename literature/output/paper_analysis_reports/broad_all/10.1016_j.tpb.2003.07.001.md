The main dependent variables are group average fitness/payoff and the average shirking rate, which is a behavioral proxy for efficiency. The model is agent-based and theoretical, but all outcomes are defined in terms of fitness/payoff. Contribution and punishment rates are discussed as mechanisms, but the main focus is on group-level payoff and the maintenance of cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model shows that in groups where some members are willing to incur costs to punish norm violators (strong reciprocators), high levels of cooperation and group efficiency are sustained. Without punishment, groups devolve to all-Selfish agents and low efficiency. The presence of punishment leads to a stable mix of Reciprocators, Cooperators, and Selfish agents, with low average shirking rates and high group payoffs. The model is robust to changes in group size, cost of punishment, and other parameters. The findings support the idea that costly punishment is a key mechanism for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling costly punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases group efficiency and average payoffs, relative to control conditions without punishment. The model is not an empirical experiment but simulates a wide range of parameter values, showing that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in group size, cost of punishment, and other design features. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism (even with moderate cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, especially in settings with repeated group interaction and no central authority. The model's structure and outcomes are directly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and agent-based, not an empirical experiment; results are based on simulation, not observed human behavior.
- Punishment is modeled as ostracism, which may not map directly to monetary or point-based punishment in laboratory PGGs.
- No communication, reputation, or explicit feedback mechanisms are included.
- No reward mechanism is modeled; only punishment is considered.
- The model assumes repeated group interaction and evolutionary dynamics, which may differ from finite-round laboratory games.
- Parameter values are chosen to approximate plausible ancestral environments, but may not match all experimental or real-world settings.
