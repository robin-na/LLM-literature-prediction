# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Payback: Effects of relationship and cultural norms on reciprocity
Authors: Jung, Y. B., Hall, J., Hong, R. Y., Goh, T., Ong, N., & Tan, N.
Journal: Asian Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount given (positive reciprocity)
- amount destroyed (negative reciprocity)
- reciprocity as a function of relationship, treatment, and culture
Outcome notes:
The dependent variable is the amount of money given (positive reciprocity) or destroyed (negative reciprocity) by the participant in response to the partner's action. No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. Outcomes are individual-level behavioral responses, not group-level payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that reciprocity norms (reward and punishment) are shaped by relationship and culture. Collectivists are more generous to friends after positive treatment and less likely to punish after negative treatment (even to strangers), sometimes offering an 'olive branch' (positive or neutral response to negative treatment). Individualists are more likely to punish norm violators (destroy money at a cost to themselves) regardless of relationship. The main dependent variable is the amount given or destroyed, not group efficiency or total payoff. The study does not use a public goods game or a repeated multi-player setting, but rather a dyadic hybrid trust-dictator game with a single round (though participants believed there would be five rounds).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the behavioral drivers of punishment and reward in dyadic trust/dictator game settings, especially the influence of culture and relationship on willingness to punish at a cost. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it use a public goods game or multi-player repeated setting. The findings may inform expectations about the likelihood and intensity of costly punishment or reward in similar dyadic games, but cannot be directly used to predict efficiency changes from enabling punishment in public goods games. The evidence is most relevant for understanding the psychological and cultural moderators of punishment behavior, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is dyadic (2 players: participant and confederate).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants believed there would be 5 rounds, but only 1 round was actually played.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No direct communication allowed between players; experimenter mediated all communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/destruction choices are continuous between $1 and $10, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (destroy) costs participant $1 per unit, removes $2 from partner per unit.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each $1 destroyed by participant removes $2 from partner.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants can reward (give money) as well as punish (destroy money).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (give) costs participant $1 per unit, gives $2 to partner per unit.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: each $1 given by participant gives $2 to partner.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told there would be 5 rounds (though only 1 was played).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the identity of their partner (friend or stranger), but all actions were mediated by the experimenter; no explicit mention of anonymity regarding punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The game is dyadic (2 players), not a multi-player public goods game.
- Only one round was actually played, though participants believed there would be five rounds.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual-level behavioral responses (amount given or destroyed).
- The partner was always a confederate, not a real participant.
- The findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods game settings.
- Cultural and relationship effects are specific to the hybrid trust-dictator game format.
