Outcome notes:
The main reported outcomes are aggregate and average contribution levels, which are behavioral (contribution) rather than direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Utility functions are described in the model, but simulation results focus on contribution rates.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the structure of hierarchical organizations and the design of punishment mechanisms significantly affect collective action, as measured by aggregate contribution levels. Peer-based (collegial) punishment mechanisms lead to higher contributions than supervisor-based punishment. Organizations with more hierarchical levels achieve higher contributions when supervisor-based punishment is used. Smaller organizational units (lower span of control) also foster higher contributions, especially when only the most egoistic agent is punished. The presence of any punishment mechanism increases contributions compared to no punishment. However, all results are reported in terms of contribution rates, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the design of punishment mechanisms (peer vs. supervisor), the number of hierarchical levels, and the span of control can substantially affect contribution rates in repeated public goods games. However, because the outcomes are reported as contribution levels rather than efficiency or group payoff, the direct predictive value for efficiency is limited. The findings suggest that, in similar agent-based or organizational settings, enabling peer punishment and structuring organizations into smaller units with more levels may increase contributions, which could correlate with higher efficiency if punishment costs are not excessive. The paper does not provide direct evidence on the net efficiency impact, especially considering the costs of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Player count varies with organizational size, span of control, and number of levels; organizations up to 5000 agents were simulated, but the effect of absolute player count is not isolated.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All simulations use 20 rounds, matching common experimental designs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (agents choose a proportion of endowment to contribute), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Linear public goods game is used; utility function is described, but the exact MPCR value is not specified. Returns are based on average contributions within units.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs are modeled: punishers pay a cost (proportional to their endowment divided by number of subordinates/neighbors); punished agents pay a fine (40% of endowment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is specified: fine is 40% of endowment; cost to punisher is proportional to their endowment divided by group size.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The number of rounds is fixed and known in the simulation (20 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents can perceive the contributions of others in their unit, which informs their perception and strategy updates.

## Important Limitations
- The study is based on agent-based simulation, not empirical or experimental data with human subjects.
- Outcomes are reported as aggregate and average contribution levels, not as efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The model does not account for the explicit cost-benefit trade-off of punishment mechanisms on net efficiency.
- No reward mechanisms are modeled.
- Communication (chat) between agents is not included.
- The model assumes symmetric organizations and does not explore asymmetrical or very large organizations (>5000 agents).
- Findings may not generalize to real organizations or human behavior without further empirical validation.
