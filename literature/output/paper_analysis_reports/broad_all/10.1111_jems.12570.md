# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Free riding, democracy, and sacrifice in the workplace: Evidence from a real-effort experiment
Authors: Kamei, K., & Tabero, K.
Journal: Journal of Economics & Management Strategy
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- work productivity (number of correct answers per minute of teamwork)
- average number of correct answers
- average payoff in the main task-solving phase
Non-payoff outcomes:
- time spent shirking (playing Tetris)
- voting for/against reduction policy
- communication content/coded motives
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is work productivity (correct answers per minute of teamwork), which is a direct efficiency measure. Other outcomes include total correct answers (group output), average payoffs, and behavioral measures such as shirking time and voting behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a real-effort lab experiment with teams of three, groups could either democratically vote or have randomly imposed a policy to reduce the private return from shirking (playing Tetris). The reduction was small and nondeterrent. Teams in the democratic (ENDO) condition exhibited significantly higher work productivity (correct answers per minute of teamwork) than those in the random (EXO) condition, regardless of whether the reduction policy was enacted. This effect (the 'dividend of democracy') was about 20% and persisted over time. However, democracy also led to more shirking (less work time), so total group output and payoffs did not significantly increase. The reduction policy itself had little effect, as shirking remained privately optimal for almost all teams. Communication analysis showed that support for the reduction policy was driven by intentions to work and beliefs about deterring others' shirking, but the policy's weakness limited its impact.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a democratic process (even with a weak, nondeterrent sanction) can substantially increase work productivity (efficiency per unit work time) in team-based, real-effort social dilemmas. However, unless the sanction is strong enough to change private incentives, total group output and payoffs may not increase, as increased productivity is offset by increased shirking. For prediction, the presence of a democratic process is a strong positive moderator of efficiency per work time, but the effectiveness of punishment/sanction depends on its deterrence strength. The experiment's design is adjacent to PGGs: it features a real-effort team task with revenue sharing and a private alternative activity, but not a standard PGG or explicit punishment tokens.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Teams of 3; 3 teams per group (9 per group). All sessions use this structure.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main task-solving phase is a 35-minute continuous period, not discrete rounds. Practice phase is 3 minutes. Tasks are repeated as many times as possible within the time.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Free-form electronic chat is available within teams during the entire task-solving process and during the pre-vote discussion.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Work/shirk is a binary choice at each moment: participants can either work on the task or play Tetris (cannot do both).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 20 pence per correct answer (180 pence per correct answer, split among 9 group members).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 'punishment' is a reduction in the per-minute return from shirking (from 18p to 16p/min) if the policy is enacted. No cost is directly paid by punishers; the cost is a group-level reduction in potential private benefit.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is low: the reduction in shirking return is small (2p/min), and the policy is explicitly described as 'nondeterrent.'
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the duration of the main phase (35 minutes) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of the main phase, participants learn (a) the total number of correct and incorrect responses of their own team and (b) the total number of correct responses in their group. No feedback during the phase.

## Important Limitations
- The reduction policy (punishment) is weak and nondeterrent, so results may not generalize to settings with stronger or more targeted punishment.
- The experiment uses a real-effort task with a binary work/shirk choice, which is adjacent but not identical to standard public goods games.
- Efficiency gains are measured as productivity per work time, not as total group output or welfare, which did not significantly increase.
- The design does not include a reward mechanism, so no evidence on reward effects.
- The findings may not generalize to environments with different group sizes, marginal returns, or stronger/targeted sanctions.
