## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a small benefit to punishers (such as status or a payment from cooperators) destabilizes corrupt equilibria and enables the evolution and stability of 'righteousness'—a state where cooperative punishers dominate and both corruption and defection are suppressed. Righteousness is associated with higher collective payoffs (efficiency/wellbeing) than either corruption or defection. The model demonstrates that egalitarian and harsh punishment (i.e., similar and sufficiently high penalties for defectors and corrupt punishers) are key to achieving righteousness. Power inequalities that favor corrupt punishers stabilize corruption, but removing or reducing these inequalities (and/or providing small benefits to punishers) can shift the population to righteousness. Once established, righteousness is highly stable and resistant to invasion by defectors or corrupt punishers.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public-goods-like environments, enabling punishment (especially when punishers receive even small additional benefits) can increase group efficiency/wellbeing compared to control conditions without punishment or with only corrupt punishment. The model suggests that the design dimensions most relevant for maximizing efficiency gains from punishment are: (1) ensuring punishers are not disadvantaged relative to cooperators (e.g., via small payments or status), (2) making punishment of defectors and corrupt punishers sufficiently harsh, and (3) minimizing power inequalities that allow corrupt punishers to avoid punishment. While the model is not an exact PGG, its structure and outcomes are highly relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like games, especially regarding the role of corruption, punishment severity, and benefits to punishers.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (c) is a key parameter; higher costs make punishment harder to sustain, but small payments to punishers (e) can offset this. The model explores the effect of varying c and shows that even when c is much larger than e, righteousness can be stable if punishment is harsh and egalitarian.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled via parameters p (punishment to defectors) and q (punishment to corrupt punishers). Higher p and q (harsher punishment) increase the likelihood of righteousness and higher efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. A small payment (e) from cooperators to punishers functions as a reward. The presence of this reward is critical for destabilizing corruption and enabling righteousness.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost to the rewarder (cooperator) is parameter e, which is assumed to be very small (e << c). Even a tiny cost is sufficient to enable righteousness.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is high: even a very small payment (e) to punishers can have a large effect on equilibrium outcomes and efficiency.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model assumes that punishers are recognizable (e.g., by physical traits or cultural tags), which is necessary for cooperators to make payments to punishers. This is discussed as a plausible mechanism in both human and insect societies.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- It is based on infinite-population replicator dynamics, not finite groups or discrete rounds as in typical PGG experiments.
- The payoff structure is based on a Prisoner's Dilemma, not a standard public goods game with MPCR.
- Player count, group size, and round structure are not specified, limiting direct mapping to experimental PGGs.
- Assumes that punishers are always recognizable, which may not hold in all real-world or experimental settings.
- Does not model communication, reputation, or more complex social dynamics beyond punishment, corruption, and small rewards.
