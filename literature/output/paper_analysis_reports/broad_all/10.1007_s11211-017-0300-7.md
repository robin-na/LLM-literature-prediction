# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Justice Sensitivity and Cooperation Dynamics in Repeated Public Good Games
Authors: Schlösser, T., Berger, S., & Fetchenhauer, D.
Journal: Social Justice Research
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution per round
- contribution dynamics over rounds
- punishment frequency and amount
- relationship between justice sensitivity and contribution
- relationship between justice sensitivity and punishment
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is contribution to the public good. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare directly, but does report average contributions in both punishment and non-punishment conditions. Punishment behavior is also analyzed. Payoff-related outcomes (e.g., group earnings, efficiency) are not directly reported, but can be inferred from contribution rates and the described payoff structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public good game leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (from 9.18 to 18.34 tokens per round out of 20). This replicates the classic result that punishment options strongly promote cooperation. The effect of individual justice sensitivity on cooperation is strong in the absence of punishment but disappears when punishment is available, as cooperation becomes strategic to avoid sanctions. Punishment is used infrequently, but its mere availability is sufficient to sustain high cooperation. Although efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the high contribution rates in the punishment condition imply much higher group efficiency compared to the no-punishment condition.

## Prediction Guidance
For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a standard repeated public good game with four players, ten rounds, and a punishment technology similar to Fehr & Gächter (2002) leads to a large increase in group efficiency, as measured by average contributions. The control (no-punishment) condition yields about 46% of maximum possible contributions, while the punishment condition yields about 92%. This suggests that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will approximately double efficiency relative to the control. The paper's design and results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated linear PGGs with similar parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of four participants per game, fixed for all rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ten rounds per game, fixed and known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; standard anonymous lab protocol.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose any integer amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (total multiplier 1.6, group size 4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is nonlinear: e.g., 1 point costs 1 ECU, 2 points cost 2 ECU, up to 10 points costing 30 ECU. Each point reduces the target's income by 10%.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is effective: each point assigned reduces the target's income by 10% (up to 100%); maximum punishment is the entire profit of the round.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the period number and how many rounds remain; the game is fixed at 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see the total amount contributed by all group members and their own return. In the punishment condition, they also see each individual's contribution before assigning punishment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not know who punished them, only the total punishment received.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare; only average contributions are reported. Efficiency must be inferred from contribution rates and the known payoff structure.
- The main focus is on personality predictors (justice sensitivity), not on the effect of punishment per se, though the punishment effect is robustly replicated.
- Punishment is used infrequently, so the effect is mainly due to its deterrent presence rather than actual punishment events.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment is available as a sanctioning institution.
