# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Perceptions of Economic Fairness Positively Affect Altruistic Punishment
Authors: Yang, S. S., Chen, S. J., & Liu, Y. F.
Journal: European Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- altruistic punishment (tokens spent to punish unfair dictator in TPP game)
- direct and indirect punishment in real-world recall
- reporting social problems
- protesting misbehaviour of boss
- sending disapproval message (non-monetary punishment)
Outcome notes:
All main outcomes are measures of punishment behavior (e.g., tokens spent to punish, likelihood of punishing, reporting/protesting, sending messages of disapproval). No group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus is reported. Some studies use a third-party punishment (TPP) game with dictator allocation, but only the amount of punishment is measured, not group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across five studies (including lab experiments with TPP games), the paper finds that individuals with higher perceptions of economic fairness are more likely to engage in costly punishment of norm violators. This effect is causal and robust to different manipulations of fairness perceptions. However, the effect is moderated by outcome efficacy: the positive effect of fairness perceptions on punishment only appears when punishment is expected to be effective at changing behavior or upholding norms. The main dependent variable is the amount of punishment assigned (tokens spent or likelihood of sending a disapproval message), not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about psychological moderators of punishment behavior (specifically, perceptions of economic fairness and outcome efficacy), but does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes in public-goods-like games. The experimental paradigm is adjacent to PGGs (third-party punishment of unfair dictator allocations), but the findings cannot be directly used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs. The results may inform models of when punishment is more likely to be used, but not its effect on group efficiency. Game design dimensions such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost are not systematically manipulated or reported, and efficiency is not measured.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. TPP game involves three roles (dictator, recipient, third-party punisher), but only the punisher is a real participant; the other roles are simulated. No systematic variation or analysis of player count.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. All experimental games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between participants is allowed or mentioned.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment choice is continuous (number of tokens to deduct) in Studies 3 and 4; binary (send message or not) in Study 5. Not all-or-nothing for contribution or punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Studies 3 and 4, cost to punisher is 0.25 tokens per token deducted from dictator. In Study 5, cost is 10 tokens to send a disapproval message. Cost is fixed and not varied experimentally.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (one token deducted from dictator per 0.25 tokens spent by punisher in Studies 3/4). In Study 5, punishment is non-monetary (message), so effectiveness is not monetary. Not systematically varied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Participants know the game is a single round (one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Participants observe the dictator's allocation to the recipient before deciding on punishment. No summary statistics or history is shown.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The experimental paradigm is adjacent to, but not the same as, a public goods game; results may not generalize to PGG efficiency effects.
- Game design dimensions such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost are not systematically varied or analyzed for their effect on efficiency.
- All studies are conducted in China, limiting generalizability to other cultural contexts.
- The main findings concern psychological moderators of punishment, not the effect of punishment on cooperation or efficiency.
