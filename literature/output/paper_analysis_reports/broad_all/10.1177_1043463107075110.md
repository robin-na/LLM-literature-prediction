# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Institutions and trust - Implications for preferences, beliefs and behavior
Authors: Bohnet, I., & Baytelman, Y.
Journal: Rationality and Society
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount sent by trustor
- amount returned by trustee
- expected amount returned
- expected amount sent
Non-payoff outcomes:
- intrinsic motivation (social orientation)
- beliefs about others' behavior
- effect of institutional constraints on motivation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: willingness to trust (amount sent), willingness to be trustworthy (amount returned), and beliefs about others' actions. The paper discusses efficiency in the sense that institutional constraints (including punishment) increase the amounts sent and returned, but does not directly report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome. The focus is on trust/trustworthiness and the crowding out of intrinsic motivation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional constraints, including a post-play punishment option, increase trustors' willingness to send money and trustees' willingness to return money in a trust game scenario, compared to an anonymous one-shot game. This suggests that efficiency (total group payoff) is higher with punishment. However, the presence of punishment and other institutional constraints reduces the role of intrinsic motivation (social orientation) in driving trust, supporting the 'crowding out' hypothesis. For trustworthiness, the effect of punishment is indirect: trustees return more because they expect to receive more, not because their intrinsic motivation changes. The study uses a within-subject design with senior executives and hypothetical payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that adding a punishment option to a trust game increases the amounts sent and returned, which is a proxy for increased efficiency. While the design is not a public goods game, the trust game with punishment is adjacent and the direction of effect is likely to generalize to PGG-like settings: punishment increases efficiency-related outcomes. However, the paper also highlights that the mechanism is not an increase in intrinsic motivation, but rather a shift in beliefs and expectations. For prediction, this suggests that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low. The effect is robust across several institutional manipulations, but the crowding out of intrinsic motivation may have long-term implications not captured by short-run efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trust game scenario involves two players: a trustor and a trustee.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Most scenarios are one-shot; there is also a finitely repeated game for five rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Pre-play and post-play communication (face-to-face chat) are included as treatments and increase trust and trustworthiness.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The amount sent by the trustor is tripled for the trustee (multiplier = 3, player count = 2, so mpcr = 1.5 for the trustee, 0 for the trustor).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher: trustor can reduce trustee's payoff by 50% after learning the outcome, with no cost to self.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: a single action can reduce the trustee's payoff by 50%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the repeated game scenario, the number of rounds (five) is known in advance.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses hypothetical payoffs rather than real monetary incentives, which may affect external validity.
- The subject pool consists of senior executives, not representative of the general population or typical lab subjects.
- The game is a trust game, not a public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (amount sent/returned), not direct measures of efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
- Within-subject design may induce order or anchoring effects, though a control group was used to check for this.
