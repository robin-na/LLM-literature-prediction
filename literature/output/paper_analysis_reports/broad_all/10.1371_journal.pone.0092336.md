# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Justine Effect: Punishment of the Unduly Self-Sacrificing Cooperative Individuals
Authors: Kubena, A. A., Houdek, P., Lindová, J., Príplatová, L., & Flegr, J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment received
- punishment assigned
- correlation between contribution and punishment
- classification of punisher types
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes such as contribution rates, punishment received, and the relationship between contribution and punishment (the 'Justine Effect'). It does not report group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus, nor does it provide direct analysis of payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a 12-player PGG, the introduction of punishment leads to a U-shaped relationship between contribution and punishment received: both the lowest and highest contributors are punished, with the most severe punishment falling on the highest contributors (the 'Justine Effect'). Antisocial punishment is primarily carried out by free riders and low contributors. While the paper reports that average contributions nearly double when punishment is available, it does not analyze or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, nor does it compare these outcomes between the punishment and no-punishment conditions. The focus is on the behavioral dynamics of punishment, not on payoff-based outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the behavioral dynamics of punishment in large-group PGGs, especially regarding antisocial punishment and its targets. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, which are central to the downstream prediction task. The finding that punishment increases average contributions suggests a possible positive effect on efficiency, but the presence of antisocial punishment (which reduces the payoffs of high contributors) could offset these gains. Without explicit efficiency or payoff data, the paper's value for predicting treatment efficiency is limited to indirect inference based on contribution patterns and punishment behavior.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 12 players (with two groups of 11 plus virtual players). The large group size is a key design feature to study antisocial punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group played 6 rounds of standard PGG and 6 rounds of PGG with punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interaction was via web application and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: players could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 CZK.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 2/12 ≈ 0.167; the public fund doubled total contributions and was split equally among 12 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is superlinear: c(p) = 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 16, 20, 25, 30 CZK for 0-10 penalty points. Each penalty point costs more at higher levels.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each penalty point reduces the punished player's payoff by 10% of their round earnings (γ = 0.1). Punishment effectiveness (fine/cost) varies with level, average effectiveness reported as 1.54.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the number of rounds (6 per game type).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the contributions of all other players (anonymously, not linked to identities).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players could not identify who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report or analyze group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
- All outcome analysis is based on behavioral measures (contribution, punishment) rather than payoff-based outcomes.
- The study uses a large group size (12), which is less common in the PGG literature and may limit comparability to standard 4-player designs.
- Punishment is only possible after observing contributions, and is always anonymous; no analysis of the effect of punishment identification is possible.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
