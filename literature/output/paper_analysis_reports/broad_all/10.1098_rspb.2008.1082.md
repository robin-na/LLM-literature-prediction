The primary dependent variables are group and individual profits (payoff-based), as well as contributions and punishment behavior. The analysis of profits is central to the paper's claims about efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compared three conditions in a repeated public goods game: (1) no punishment (control), (2) all group members can punish (all-punishment), and (3) only one randomly selected group member can punish (one-punishment). Both punishment conditions led to significantly higher and more stable contributions than the control. Profits (efficiency) were higher in the one-punishment condition than in the all-punishment condition, and both were more stable than the control. The cost of punishment reduced profits relative to the control in the short run, but the control condition's profits declined over rounds due to decaying cooperation, while punishment conditions maintained stable profits. The one-punisher regime avoids redundant punishment costs, making it more efficient. The punishment mechanism used a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio. The study provides direct evidence that both diffuse and designated punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency, but a single punisher can do so more efficiently.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, six-round public goods game with a 1:3 punishment cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment increases efficiency (group profit) relative to a no-punishment control. Furthermore, a single designated punisher is as effective as allowing all members to punish in sustaining cooperation, but is more efficient due to lower aggregate punishment costs. For prediction, this suggests that, under similar game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the structure of the punishment regime (single vs. diffuse) can moderate the efficiency gains. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per round; fixed throughout the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each segment had 6 rounds; total of 12 rounds (6 control, 6 treatment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; all interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could invest any amount from their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU invested was doubled and split among 4 players: MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher paid 1 MU to reduce target's payoff by 3 MUs (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3). Max 10 MUs per punished member.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 MU spent reduces target by 3 MUs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told at the start that there were two segments, but within each segment, the number of rounds was fixed and known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the contributions of other group members. In punishment conditions, they also saw deductions made from their own account.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used undergraduate students from a single university, which may limit generalizability.
- The punishment cost-to-impact ratio (1:3) is relatively high; lower ratios may not sustain cooperation.
- Punishment was anonymous and retaliation was not possible, which may not reflect real-world dynamics.
- The single punisher was randomly assigned each round, not self-selected or leader-elected, which may differ from naturalistic leadership.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so findings are specific to punishment-based enforcement.
