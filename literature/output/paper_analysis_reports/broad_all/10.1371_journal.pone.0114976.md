# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Gratefully Received, Gratefully Repaid: The Role of Perceived Fairness in Cooperative Interactions
Authors: Ma, L. K., Tunney, R. J., & Ferguson, E.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- gratitude
- perceived helpfulness
- reciprocating tendency
- acceptance/rejection of offers
- defaulting on repayment (breach of contract)
- emotional responses (annoyance, indebtedness, obligation)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are psychological (gratitude, perceived helpfulness, willingness to reciprocate) and behavioral (acceptance, defaulting on repayment). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that recipients of unconditional (free) help perceive the donor as more genuinely helpful, feel more gratitude, and report a higher tendency to reciprocate in the future compared to recipients of conditional (repayment-required) help. Among those who received conditional offers and accepted, 38% defaulted (either partially or fully) on repayment, suggesting a behavioral response to perceived unfairness. The effect of offer conditionality on willingness to reciprocate is serially mediated by perceived helpfulness and gratitude. The study does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and the game is a one-shot, dyadic trust game rather than a repeated public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how conditionality (akin to punishment or sanctions) affects willingness to reciprocate and actual repayment behavior in a one-shot, dyadic trust game. It does not provide direct evidence about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The findings suggest that perceived fairness and the framing of help (conditional vs. unconditional) can influence cooperation and defaulting, which may be relevant for understanding psychological mechanisms in cooperation, but the results cannot be directly used to predict efficiency changes in public goods games with punishment enabled or disabled.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played in dyads (2 players per group: P1 and P2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. P1 can choose how much to offer and whether to require repayment; P2 can choose whether to accept and how much to repay (if conditional). Choices are not strictly all-or-nothing, but are discrete and allow for partial repayment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot (single round).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a one-shot, dyadic trust game, not a repeated public goods game or a standard public goods game with or without punishment.
- No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, total earnings, or welfare is reported.
- Punishment is not implemented as a formal, costly sanctioning mechanism; defaulting on repayment is not equivalent to punishment in public goods games.
- The main outcomes are psychological (gratitude, perceived helpfulness, willingness to reciprocate) and behavioral (acceptance, defaulting), not payoff-based.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods environments with explicit punishment or reward mechanisms.
