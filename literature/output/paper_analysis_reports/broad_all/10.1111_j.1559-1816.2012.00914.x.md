# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Procedural Justice and Sanctions in Social Dilemmas: The Moderating Effects of Group Feedback and Identification
Authors: De Cremer, D., Hoogervorst, N., & Desmet, P.
Journal: Journal of Applied Social Psychology
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- cooperation rate (implied by contribution)
- manipulation checks (perceived fairness, perceived monitoring, perceived group success/failure)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good in each session. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. The design and discussion focus on contribution behavior as the main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that accurate sanctioning systems (those that monitor all contribution sessions) increase contributions relative to inaccurate systems, but this effect depends on group identification and feedback. For high group identifiers, procedural justice (accuracy) increases contributions only after group failure; for low identifiers, it increases contributions only after group success. The experiment uses a step-level public good game with a threshold and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The findings highlight the importance of procedural justice in sanctioning systems for promoting cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence about effects on efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions depends on the perceived procedural justice (accuracy) of the sanctioning system, and that this effect is moderated by group identification and feedback about group success or failure. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in step-level public good games with punishment, the design of the punishment system (especially its perceived fairness and accuracy) and the social context (identification, feedback) can strongly moderate behavioral responses, which may in turn affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment will be more effective at increasing cooperation (and possibly efficiency) when the system is accurate and procedurally just, but the effect is context-dependent.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. There were two contribution sessions (rounds) per participant, with the first round before and the second after the sanctioning system was introduced.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; participants were seated in separate cubicles and could not see each other.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could choose any amount from their endowment to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Step-level public good: if group contributes at least 200 chips (out of 400 possible), the total is doubled and divided equally. If not, no multiplication. Effective MPCR is 0 if threshold not met, 0.5 if threshold met (since 400 chips become 800, divided equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a deduction: the lowest contributor has 30% of their final earnings subtracted. The cost to the punisher is not specified; punishment is automatic/system-administered.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a 30% deduction from the lowest contributor's final earnings. The effectiveness is fixed by design.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told the exact number of contribution sessions in advance, to minimize end play.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so effects on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The main outcome is contribution amount, not payoff or welfare.
- The punishment system is automatic and not peer-administered, so findings may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- Only the accuracy (procedural justice) of the sanctioning system is manipulated; other aspects of punishment (e.g., cost to punisher, severity) are not varied.
- The experiment uses a step-level (threshold) public good, which may not generalize to linear or other PGG variants.
- No mediating psychological processes (e.g., trust, motivation) are directly measured.
- No communication between participants; results may differ in settings with communication.
