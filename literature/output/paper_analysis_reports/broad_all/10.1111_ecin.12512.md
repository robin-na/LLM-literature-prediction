## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally studies the effect of exogenously imposed minimum return rules in a one-shot trust game. Four treatments are compared: no rule (0%), and minimum return rules of 10%, 20%, and 30% of the tripled investment. The main findings are: (1) Low minimum return rules (10%) reduce trust (investment), reciprocity (voluntary returns), and economic efficiency (ROI, group payoff) compared to no rule. (2) As the rule becomes more restrictive (20%, 30%), investment and efficiency recover, but trust and reciprocity do not. (3) Only the most restrictive rule (30%) restores or improves efficiency, but this is due to mechanical risk reduction, not increased trust. (4) The relationship between rule restrictiveness and efficiency is U-shaped: gentle rules are worse than no rule, but strong rules can restore efficiency. (5) The presence of rules crowds out voluntary cooperation and learning about partner trustworthiness.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing weak or moderate minimum return rules in trust-based games can reduce efficiency compared to no rule, due to crowding out of trust and reciprocity. Only highly restrictive rules (that nearly eliminate risk) can restore or improve efficiency, but at the cost of voluntary cooperation. The effect is non-monotonic: efficiency is lowest at low rule levels, recovers at high rule levels. This suggests that, in adjacent trust-game-like environments, the effect of punishment-like minimum standards on efficiency depends critically on the restrictiveness of the rule. The findings are most relevant for settings where the control (no-punishment) game already achieves moderate efficiency; in such cases, adding weak punishment or minimum standards may reduce efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot, two-player trust game, not a repeated or multi-player public goods game; generalizability to repeated or group settings is limited.
- The minimum return rule is a hard constraint, not a costly punishment or peer sanction; results may not transfer directly to punishment-based PGGs.
- No communication, reputation, or social context; findings may differ in richer environments.
- The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment cost or technology, only the restrictiveness of the minimum return rule.
- The effect of minimum standards is only tested for a small set of rule levels (0%, 10%, 20%, 30%).
