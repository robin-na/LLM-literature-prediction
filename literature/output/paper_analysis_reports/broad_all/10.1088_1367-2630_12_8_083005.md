Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the evolutionary dynamics and stationary fractions of strategies (cooperators, defectors, punishing cooperators, punishing defectors) in spatial public goods games. The main reported outcomes are the prevalence of these strategies and the overall cooperation level, not explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. While the cooperation level is related to efficiency, the paper does not directly report group payoff or efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in spatial public goods games, the introduction of punishing cooperators (PC) or punishing defectors (PD) can promote the spread of cooperation, but through different mechanisms. For punishing cooperators, increasing the punishment fine monotonically increases the cooperation level up to a threshold, after which non-punishing cooperators and defectors are eliminated. For punishing defectors, there is a non-monotonic (reentrant) effect: increasing the fine initially increases cooperation, but too high a fine reduces the fraction of punishing defectors, causing cooperation to collapse and the system to revert to defection. The results are presented as phase diagrams and time-evolution of strategy fractions, not as direct efficiency or group payoff measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and type of punishment mechanism, as well as the punishment cost and fine, can have large effects on the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games. However, because the outcomes are reported as fractions of strategies and cooperation level, not as group efficiency or total payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency. The results suggest that enabling punishment (especially by punishing cooperators) can increase cooperation and likely efficiency, but the relationship is mediated by the spatial structure and the specific punishment parameters. The non-monotonic effect for punishing defectors highlights the importance of tuning punishment severity. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment can improve efficiency in structured populations, but the mapping to efficiency must be inferred from cooperation levels.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player is on a square lattice and participates in 5 groups (self + 4 neighbors). The lattice size varies (L=400 to 3000), but the group size is fixed at 5. The results are said to be robust to reasonable changes in group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is run for up to 1,000,000 iterations until stationary fractions are reached. There is no fixed number of rounds as in a finite repeated game; the model is evolutionary and runs until equilibrium.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: cooperators (C, PC) contribute 1, defectors (D, PD) contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=3.8), with group size G=5, so MPCR = r/5. The effect of r is discussed in phase diagrams.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost c is a key parameter, varied in the phase diagrams. Lower cost allows punishment to be more effective in promoting cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment fine f is varied; the impact per unit cost is not explicitly calculated, but the effect of varying f and c is central. For PC, higher f increases cooperation up to a point; for PD, too high f reduces cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and computational, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are reported as fractions of strategies and cooperation level, not as direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The mapping from cooperation level to efficiency is not explicitly provided and must be inferred.
- The model assumes spatial structure (lattice), which may not generalize to well-mixed or other network structures.
- No human subjects or real-world data are used; results are based on evolutionary simulations.
