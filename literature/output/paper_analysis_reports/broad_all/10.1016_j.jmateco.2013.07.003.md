## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that contagious punishment (grim trigger strategies) in repeated random-matching social dilemmas can sustain full cooperation (i.e., maximal efficiency) if players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor), the cost of cooperation is not too high, and monitoring is sufficiently broad. The authors derive closed-form expressions for continuation payoffs and provide exact bounds on the discount factor and the cost of cooperation required to make cooperation self-enforcing. They show that increasing the breadth of monitoring (i.e., allowing players to observe more actions outside their own match) makes it easier to sustain cooperation, lowering the required discount factor and cost of cooperation. Conversely, if defection is not absorbing (i.e., if players can revert to cooperation), sustaining cooperation becomes harder. The results are general for random-matching repeated games with contagious punishment, and the framework is intended to inform both theory and laboratory design.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the presence and structure of punishment (specifically, contagious, unforgiving punishment) can enable high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas, even with random matching and anonymity. The key moderators are the discount factor, the cost of cooperation, and the breadth of monitoring (how many others' actions are observed). The results suggest that, in environments with strong punishment and sufficient monitoring, enabling punishment should increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment), provided the other parameters (patience, cost) are favorable. The paper also provides explicit formulas for the parameter thresholds needed to sustain cooperation, which could be used to predict when punishment will be effective in raising efficiency. However, the results are theoretical and apply to repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-type games, not public goods games per se, though the logic is closely related.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model is for repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-type games with binary actions, not public goods games per se, though the logic is closely related.
- Punishment is endogenous and decentralized (grim trigger), not an explicit costly punishment mechanism as in many lab PGG experiments.
- The model assumes infinite or indefinite repetition and common knowledge of the discount factor.
- No consideration of communication, reward, or identification of punishers.
- Results may not generalize to settings with finite horizons, explicit costly punishment, or richer action spaces.
