## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that impulsive choice and altruistic punishment are positively correlated: individuals who are more impulsive (prefer immediate rewards) are more likely to reject unfair offers in the Ultimatum Game, incurring personal cost to punish norm violations. Lowering serotonin via tryptophan depletion increases both impulsive choice and altruistic punishment, and the magnitude of these increases is correlated across individuals. The results suggest that altruistic punishment is more impulsive/emotion-driven than self-controlled. The study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it uses the Ultimatum Game and focuses on individual punishment behavior rather than group outcomes. However, it provides informative context about the psychological drivers of costly punishment, suggesting that impulsivity and emotional responses (modulated by serotonin) can increase the frequency of costly punishment. For prediction tasks, this implies that environments or manipulations that increase impulsivity or emotional arousal may increase punishment rates, but the effect on efficiency cannot be inferred from this study.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game is a two-player game (proposer and responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 48 one-shot (single-round) Ultimatum Games per session, each with a different proposer. No repeated interaction with the same partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions were via computer interface and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (rejection) is costly: responder forfeits their own potential earnings to punish the proposer. The cost is the amount the responder would have received if they had accepted.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is effective: rejection causes both players to receive nothing. The impact per unit cost is 1:1 for the responder (loses their share, proposer loses their share).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials (48) in the session; each is a one-shot game with a new proposer.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, not a public goods game or direct variant, so findings may not generalize to multi-player or repeated public goods settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual punishment behavior is measured.
- The sample size is small (n=22), and all participants are healthy volunteers from a limited demographic.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of unfair offers, which is a binary, all-or-nothing decision, not a continuous or multi-target punishment mechanism.
- The study does not manipulate or report on most game design dimensions relevant to public goods games (e.g., MPCR, group size, communication, etc.).
