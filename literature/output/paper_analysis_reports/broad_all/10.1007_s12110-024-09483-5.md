# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Nature and Motivation of Human Cooperation from Variant Public Goods Games
Authors: Zhang, Y. G., Zhu, Q., & Li, Z. Q.
Journal: Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- frequency of non-zero contributions
- punishment occurrence
- correlation between SVO and contribution
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on contribution behavior (amounts, conditional cooperation/free-riding) and the occurrence of punishment, but does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus. The closest outcome to efficiency is the discussion of contributions relative to the minimum needed to offset costs, but no explicit efficiency or group payoff metric is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a repeated PGG with essential costs and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, nearly all participants make non-zero contributions, and contributions are higher in intergroup competition than intragroup competition. The punishment mechanism ensures that absolute free-riding (zero contribution) is extremely rare, as punished individuals cannot benefit from the public good. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoffs, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed. The main behavioral outcome is contribution level, not payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that a punishment mechanism targeting the lowest contributor in a repeated PGG with essential costs leads to near-universal non-zero contributions and higher contributions in intergroup competition. However, because the study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment can strongly suppress absolute free-riding, but the efficiency implications (e.g., whether punishment increases or decreases group payoff relative to the no-punishment baseline) cannot be determined from this paper.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Group sizes varied across games (e.g., 98, 97, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4 per group), but the effect of group size on outcomes was not analyzed. Authors note inability to analyze group size effects as a limitation.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Number of rounds varied by game (2 to 7 rounds), but the effect of number of rounds on outcomes is not directly analyzed.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Explicitly stated that participants were instructed not to interact with each other during the game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (integer between 0 and 20), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public pool is multiplied by 2 and divided equally among N players, so MPCR = 2/N. N varies by group.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs 5 units per punisher per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment removes the punished participant's access to the public good for that round (they do not receive the group benefit). Only the lowest contributor(s) can be punished each round.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were unaware of the total number of rounds; the game was randomly terminated by the facilitator.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all participants were provided with information about each participant's contribution, endowment, and group ranking.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The effect of group size on contributions could not be analyzed due to design and sample limitations.
- Some games had too few effective rounds due to unexpected situations.
- The sample is limited to college students from one university, limiting generalizability.
