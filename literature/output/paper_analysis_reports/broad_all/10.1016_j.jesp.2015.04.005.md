# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On why hypocrisy thrives: Reasonable doubt created by moral posturing can deter punishment
Authors: Lönnqvist, J. E., Rilke, R. M., & Walkowitz, G.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment/rejection rates of unfair offers
- belief in proposer claims
- punishment by third parties
- use of coin flip as excuse for unfairness
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate at which unfair offers are punished (rejected) by responders or third parties. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare is reported. The focus is on behavioral responses to unfairness and the effect of moral posturing on punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in ultimatum games, proposers can reduce the likelihood of being punished for unfair offers by claiming that a random process (coin flip) determined the outcome. This effect holds even when responders do not believe the claim and is observed for both direct victims (responders) and third-party observers (judges). The reduction in punishment is not explained by responders' self-interest or gratitude. The studies do not report on group efficiency or total earnings, but the findings suggest that moral posturing can deter punishment and thus may allow unfair behavior to persist.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of plausible deniability (moral posturing) can reduce the frequency of punishment in bargaining games, even when punishment is available and even if the claim is not believed. However, the study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most relevant for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, especially the role of uncertainty or doubt about intent, rather than the direct effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game dyads (2 players: proposer and responder) in Studies 1-3; third-party/judge added in Study 4; large sample in vignette study (Study 5).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Single-shot (one-round) ultimatum games in all studies.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposers choose between two discrete splits: €8/2 (unfair) or €5/5 (fair); not a continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Studies 1-3, punishment is rejection (costly to responder: both get €0.5 instead of the proposed split). In Study 4, third-party punishment costs 1 cent per 3 cents punished.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Studies 1-3, rejection nullifies the proposed split (both get €0.5). In Study 4, each 1 cent spent by judge reduces proposer's payoff by 3 cents.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Study 4 mentions judges could also reward, but main analysis is on punishment. No analysis of reward effects.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Single-round games; participants know the structure.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, not a public goods game or direct variant, so findings may not generalize to multi-player public goods settings.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; only punishment/rejection rates are reported.
- Laboratory sample sizes are modest and may be underpowered for some effects.
- The strategy method (conditional responses) may differ from direct-response methods, though the authors argue this is not a major concern.
- The effect of moral posturing on punishment may depend on the severity of the transgression; only moderate unfairness is tested.
- No analysis of how reduced punishment affects long-run cooperation or efficiency.
