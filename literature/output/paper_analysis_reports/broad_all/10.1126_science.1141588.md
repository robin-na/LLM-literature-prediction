## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when individuals can choose whether to participate in the public goods game, costly punishment can emerge and become the dominant strategy, leading to high group efficiency and payoffs. In compulsory participation settings, defectors dominate and group efficiency is low, even if punishment is possible. The model predicts that voluntary participation is essential for the evolutionary stability and emergence of punishment as a mechanism for cooperation. The results are robust across a range of parameter values for group size, multiplication factor, and baseline income.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling voluntary participation in public goods games with punishment will result in higher group efficiency compared to compulsory participation. The presence of costly punishment alone is not sufficient to ensure high efficiency; the institutional context (voluntary vs. compulsory participation) is a critical moderator. For prediction tasks, the model suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on whether players can abstain from participation. Other design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and MPCR are discussed as parameters, but the main qualitative result is robust to their variation.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and imitation dynamics, which may not capture all real-world complexities.
- No explicit modeling of rounds, communication, or information structure as in laboratory experiments.
- No reward mechanism is included in the main analysis, though discussed in passing.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with strong group structure, reputation, or other institutional features not modeled.
