## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that graduated punishment (punishment increasing with the harm caused by selfish actions) is only efficient—i.e., maximizes group welfare—when two conditions are met: (1) there is heterogeneity among players in their sensitivity to utility differences, and (2) there is a nonzero probability of false accusation (punishing prosocials by mistake). In standard rational or homogeneous quantal response models, optimal punishment is not graduated but rather jumps from zero to a high level. With heterogeneity and error, the optimal punishment increases smoothly with harm, matching the graduated punishment observed in successful resource management. The paper provides explicit formulas for optimal punishment and shows how efficiency-maximizing punishment depends on model parameters.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games depends critically on the presence of player heterogeneity and error in monitoring. In standard homogeneous or error-free settings, punishment is either absent or severe, with little efficiency gain from graduation. When heterogeneity and error are present, graduated punishment is optimal and can improve efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive in environments with player heterogeneity and monitoring errors, and that the optimal punishment level should increase with the harm caused by defection. The paper's analytic results can inform parameterization of efficiency predictions as a function of harm, benefit, error rates, and heterogeneity.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Model is parameterized by N (number of players), but results are not directly about varying player count. N is used in payoff formulas and to define per capita welfare.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between two discrete actions: prosocial (cooperate) or selfish (defect).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Model is mapped to PGG: harm per defector (H) is analogous to marginal per-capita return (mpcr) in PGG. Explicit mapping in Discussion.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is parameterized as a variable fine (P) imposed on selfish players; the model explores how varying P affects welfare and behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- Punishment cost to the punisher or institution is not included in the welfare function, which may overstate the efficiency of punishment.
- The model is static (one-shot); repeated interactions and dynamic effects are not considered.
- No explicit modeling of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms.
- Results depend on the assumed form of heterogeneity (exponential distribution of sensitivity to utility difference) and error rates.
- Mapping to standard public goods game is approximate; some institutional and behavioral details may differ.
