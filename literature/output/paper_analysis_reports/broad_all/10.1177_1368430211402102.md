# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How the shadow of the future might promote cooperation
Authors: Van Lange, P. A. M., Klapwijk, A., & Van Munster, L. M.
Journal: Group Processes & Intergroup Relations
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- level of cooperation (number of coins given to partner)
- self-reported interaction goals (e.g., MaxJoint, MinDiff, MaxOwn, MaxRel)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the number of coins given to the partner (cooperation). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Self-reported goals are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the mere anticipation of repeated interaction (10 rounds vs. 1 round) increases cooperation (measured as coins given to the partner) among individualists, but not among prosocials, in a sequential give-some dilemma. Prosocials maintain high cooperation regardless of repetition, while individualists reciprocate more when repeated interaction is expected. The effect is robust to the presence or absence of 'noise' in the translation of choices. The study does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, nor does it report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that repeated interaction (longer games) can increase cooperation among individualistically oriented participants, even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward mechanisms. However, it does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not manipulate or include punishment, so its relevance to the prediction of efficiency under punishment is limited. The main implication is that repeated interaction may substitute for some effects of punishment among individualists, but this is not tested in a payoff or efficiency framework.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) sequential give-some dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Manipulated between 1 (single-trial) and 10 (repeated interaction) rounds. Only the first round is actually played, but participants believe there will be 1 or 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; participants were in separate cubicles and could not communicate.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can give any number of coins from 0 to 10.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each coin given is worth 0.5 Euro to the giver and 1 Euro to the receiver, so the marginal per-capita return for the group is 1.5 Euro per coin given, but for the giver, the marginal return is 1 Euro to the other per 0.5 Euro cost to self.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are explicitly told whether the game is single-trial or repeated (10 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the partner's choice (number of coins given) before making their own choice.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include or manipulate punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so it cannot directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual cooperation and self-reported goals are measured.
- The design is a dyadic sequential give-some dilemma, not a standard public goods game.
- Only the first round is actually played; repeated interaction is only anticipated, not experienced.
- Sample size is moderate and limited to university students.
- Findings may not generalize to larger groups or to settings with explicit punishment or reward options.
