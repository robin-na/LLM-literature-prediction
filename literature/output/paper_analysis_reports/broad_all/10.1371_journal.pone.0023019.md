## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- relative cooperator payoff (average payoff to cooperators divided by average payoff to defectors)
- average agent payoff (as a function of game production r and group size n*)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- excess assortment (degree to which cooperators cluster together)
- number of active games at equilibrium
- frequency of within-game cooperator frequencies
Outcome notes:
The main outcome is the relative cooperator payoff, which is not group efficiency per se, but rather the ratio of cooperator to defector payoffs. The model does not report group efficiency (total group payoff relative to full cooperation), but does analyze average payoffs and their determinants. No direct analysis of punishment or sanctions is performed; the paper focuses on institutional assortment mechanisms (capacity constraints) as an alternative to punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that capacity constraints (limits on the number of players per game) are a simple and effective institutional mechanism for promoting positive assortment of cooperators in an ecology of public goods games. When group size is limited, cooperators can cluster together and achieve higher payoffs relative to defectors, even without punishment or reputation. Budget constraints (limits on the number of games per player) are less effective. The model does not include punishment, but discusses it as a more complex and potentially costly alternative. The main outcome is the relative cooperator payoff, not group efficiency or total welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not model punishment or sanctions. However, it is relevant for understanding how institutional design (e.g., group size limits) can promote cooperation and higher payoffs without the need for punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that capacity constraints can increase the relative payoff of cooperators, but does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is not directly useful for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N is a model parameter, varied in sensitivity analyses (e.g., N=100, N=50-500). Larger populations take longer to reach equilibrium but the main results are robust to system size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is iterated until equilibrium is reached (hundreds of time steps); not a fixed number of rounds, but a dynamic process.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game production parameter r is varied (e.g., r=2.5, r=1-5), and group size n_j varies with capacity constraints. MPCR is r/n_j.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Agents have information about the payout of games when joining, but not about individual contributions.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanisms are modeled; the paper only discusses them as alternatives.
- The main outcome is relative cooperator payoff, not group efficiency or total welfare.
- Agents play fixed strategies (always cooperate or always defect); no learning, adaptation, or evolutionary dynamics.
- The model is theoretical and agent-based, not empirical or experimental.
- No direct analysis of the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff.
