# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Competition Between Organizational Groups: Its Impact on Altruistic and Antisocial Motivations
Authors: Goette, L., Huffman, D., Meier, S., & Sutter, M.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- punishment assigned (deduction points)
- beliefs about cooperation
- ingroup favoritism in cooperation
- antisocial punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates in a one-shot prisoners' dilemma and punishment behavior (deduction points assigned by third parties). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The design is adjacent to a public goods game but uses a two-player PD and a third-party punishment setup.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in a neutral environment, punishment is used prosocially to enforce cooperation, especially when the victim of defection is an in-group member. When competition between groups is introduced, punishment becomes antisocial: out-group members are punished more, regardless of whether they cooperate or defect. Competition increases in-group cooperation but does not reduce out-group cooperation. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the authors argue that antisocial punishment under competition is wasteful and could reduce overall efficiency, while increased in-group cooperation could enhance it. The net effect on efficiency is not measured.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of intergroup competition can fundamentally change the function of punishment in social dilemma games: from norm enforcement to antisocial, destructive punishment. While the design is not a standard repeated public goods game, the findings are highly relevant for predicting when punishment will be efficiency-enhancing versus efficiency-reducing. Specifically, in environments with salient group competition, enabling punishment may lead to resource-destroying antisocial punishment, undermining the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data means the findings should be used as qualitative guidance rather than quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 2 players per PD; Experiment 2: 4 players (2 A, 2 B) per game. The main punishment game is a 2x2 setup (A1, A2, B1, B2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players; strong anonymity conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: players either keep all points or pass all points.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the PD, passing 20 points results in the other player receiving 40 points (multiplier 2, 2 players), so MPCR = 2/2 = 1.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point costs the punisher (B-player) 1 point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the punished player's (A-player) payoff by 3 points.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; no uncertainty about the number of rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know the identity of the punisher or punished.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot prisoners' dilemma with third-party punishment, not a repeated or standard public goods game.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment) are analyzed.
- The subject pool consists of Swiss Army officer candidates, which may limit generalizability.
- The competitive manipulation is a bonus for the highest average payoff, but punishment cannot affect the competition outcome, which differs from many real-world or standard PGG tournament settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
