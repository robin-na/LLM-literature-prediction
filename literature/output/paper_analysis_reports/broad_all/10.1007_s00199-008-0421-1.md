Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable of interest is social welfare, defined as the total per-period payoff to the community, which is equivalent to average efficiency. The paper also analyzes the existence and properties of equilibria (good, bad, mixed) and the conditions under which cooperation is sustained.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that endogenous relationship formation and dissolution can sustain cooperation and high efficiency in a repeated prisoner's dilemma, but only if the fraction of always-defect (bad) types is neither too low nor too high. When the fraction of bad types is in an intermediate range, a 'good equilibrium' exists in which strategic players cooperate, and social welfare is maximized at the lower end of this range. If the fraction of bad types is too low, there is no credible punishment for defection, and cooperation unravels. If too high, the probability of forming a cooperative relationship is too low, and defection dominates. The model also characterizes mixed strategy equilibria and shows that welfare can drop discontinuously as parameters cross critical thresholds. The main outcome is social welfare (efficiency), which is explicitly calculated as a function of the type distribution and model parameters.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that endogenous social structure (relationship formation and dissolution) can substitute for explicit costly punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency, but only under certain conditions on the population's type distribution. The efficiency of the 'treatment' (i.e., the equilibrium with endogenous punishment via exclusion) is maximized when the fraction of bad types is just high enough to make the threat of exclusion credible, but not so high as to make cooperation infeasible. The model does not map directly onto standard public goods games with explicit punishment options, but it is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated games with endogenous partner choice or ostracism mechanisms. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency is non-monotonic and highly sensitive to the underlying population structure. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that the presence and credibility of punishment (even if not costly or explicit) can have strong, but non-linear, effects on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model uses a continuum of players (measure 1), but the key mechanism is pairwise matching in each period. The results are not sensitive to finite vs. infinite population, as long as rematching is random and ex-partners are not rematched.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated (infinite horizon, discrete time).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is implicit in the payoff matrix: (C,C) yields payoff a to each, (D,C) yields b to D and -l to C. The sum of payoffs is maximized at (C,C).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented via endogenous exclusion: defectors are forced into short-term, low-payoff relationships. The effectiveness of this punishment depends on the prevalence of bad types and the probability of rematching with a cooperator.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated; all players know the structure.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- Punishment is not implemented via explicit costly actions, but via endogenous exclusion and rematching; results may not transfer directly to standard public goods games with explicit punishment options.
- The model assumes a continuum of players and infinite horizon, which may differ from finite, small-group experimental settings.
- Type distribution (good, bad, opportunistic) is exogenous and fixed; the model does not endogenize type evolution except in a brief discussion.
- No communication, reputation, or information flows across matches are allowed, which may limit applicability to real-world or experimental settings with richer information structures.
