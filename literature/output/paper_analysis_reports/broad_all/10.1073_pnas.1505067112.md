# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Selfish third parties act as peacemakers by transforming conflicts and promoting cooperation
Authors: Halevy, N., & Halali, E.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate among disputants
- third-party intervention rate
- perceived costs and benefits of intervention
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates (i.e., proportion of cooperative choices by disputants) and intervention rates by third parties. The paper discusses collective gains and missed opportunities for increased payoffs, but does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes. Some discussion of payoffs is present in the context of the game's structure and missed opportunities, but the main analyses and results focus on behavioral outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main findings are: (1) The possibility and higher probability of third-party intervention increase cooperation rates among disputants in both interpersonal and intergroup contexts. (2) Third parties' willingness to intervene is highly sensitive to the risk and potential rewards of intervention; reducing risk or increasing potential gain increases intervention rates. (3) Despite the potential for increased collective gains, third parties often intervene less frequently than would be optimal, leading to missed opportunities for higher group payoffs. (4) The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as primary outcomes, but discusses them in the context of missed opportunities and the structure of the game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the introduction of a punishment/reward mechanism (via third-party intervention) can increase cooperation rates in social dilemma-like settings, even when the underlying game is not a standard public goods game. However, because the primary outcomes are behavioral (cooperation/intervention rates) and not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects. The results suggest that the design of punishment/reward mechanisms (including the risk and incentive structure for enforcers) is critical for their effectiveness. The evidence is most relevant for adjacent settings where third-party intervention is possible, but less directly transferable to standard PGGs with endogenous peer punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main experiments use three players per group: two disputants and one third party.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1 is one-shot; Experiment 2 and 3 use 10 consecutive decisions without feedback; Experiment 4 is one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; all decisions are made independently and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (triangle) or compete (square); not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (and reward) is implemented as side payments by the third party; the cost to the third party depends on the risk structure (gain/loss/fixed). The third party's payoff is affected by the outcome, but there is no explicit per-unit cost of punishment as in standard PGGs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the payoff structure: -1 for defection, +1 for cooperation (side payments). The impact per intervention is fixed by the game's design.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party intervention includes both rewards for cooperation and punishments for competition.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the third party for rewarding is embedded in the overall risk/gain structure; not specified as a per-unit cost.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is fixed by the game's design: +1 for cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of rounds in advance (e.g., 10 rounds in Experiments 2 and 3).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All decisions are anonymous; players do not know the identity of the third party or other disputants.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental paradigm (Peacemaker Game) is not a standard public goods game; it models a three-player conflict with third-party intervention, making direct transfer to PGGs limited.
- Primary outcomes are behavioral (cooperation and intervention rates), not direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as side payments by a third party, not as endogenous peer punishment among group members.
- No direct analysis or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff as a dependent variable.
- No manipulation or reporting of marginal per-capita return (mpcr), which is central to standard PGGs.
- No communication or feedback between rounds, which may limit generalizability to repeated or dynamic social dilemmas.
