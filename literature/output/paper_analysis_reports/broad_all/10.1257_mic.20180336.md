# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Spillover Effects of Institutions on Cooperative Behavior, Preferences, and Beliefs
Authors: Engl, F., Riedl, A., & Weber, R.
Journal: American Economic Journal-microeconomics
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group welfare (total group payoff)
- individual payoffs
- direct and spillover effects on welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- conditional cooperation preferences
- beliefs about others' cooperativeness
- heterogeneity in treatment effects (e.g., by age, reciprocity)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contributions (behavioral), but the paper also explicitly analyzes group welfare (total payoff) and decomposes the effects into direct and spillover effects on welfare. Beliefs and preferences are also measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Institutions that enforce cooperation in a public goods game (via a minimum contribution requirement, with full forfeiture for under-contribution) substantially increase group welfare in the regulated game and also increase welfare in an unregulated, otherwise identical game played by the same group. The direct effect on welfare is large, and the spillover effect is significant and economically meaningful (about 3-3.4 points on a 20-point endowment, or 30-40% of the direct effect). Both exogenous and endogenous institutions produce these effects, but the endogenous institution's spillover effect grows over time as the minimum requirement increases. The effect is mainly due to higher contributions among those already contributing, not by converting free riders. The positive effects on welfare and contributions persist after the institution is removed and extend to new partners. The mechanisms include increased beliefs about others' cooperativeness and stronger conditional cooperation preferences. No spillover is found to trust games. Heterogeneity exists: younger subjects and those with high positive reciprocity show smaller spillover effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a strong, central punishment institution (with full forfeiture for under-contribution) in a repeated linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and MPCR 0.5. The control (no institution) game shows declining contributions and welfare over time, while the treatment (with punishment) achieves near-full cooperation and much higher welfare. Critically, the paper quantifies both the direct and spillover effects on group welfare, allowing for prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design. The results are robust across exogenous and endogenous institution types, though the time dynamics differ. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with similar group size, round structure, and punishment technology (centralized, all-or-nothing, no cost to punisher). The absence of spillover to trust games suggests limits to generalizability across domains. The evidence is strongest for games with continuous contribution choices, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) in the repeated public goods games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: any integer from 0 to 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.5 (contributions doubled and split among 4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is administered by a central authority; cost to punisher is zero (punishment is automatic, not peer-administered).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is all-or-nothing: if a player contributes less than the minimum, they forfeit all income from that game in that period.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment (forfeiture) is used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 20 periods in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, players are shown the contributions of all group members (in descending order, not matched across games) and their payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is administered by a central authority; no identification of individual punishers (since not peer punishment).

## Important Limitations
- The punishment institution is centralized and costless to the punisher, which differs from peer punishment or costly punishment settings.
- The spillover effects are measured in a very similar, simultaneous public goods game; generalizability to more distant domains is limited (no spillover to trust games).
- The group size (n=4), number of rounds (20), and MPCR (0.5) are fixed; results may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- No communication is allowed; effects may differ with chat or communication.
- The punishment is all-or-nothing and very strong (full forfeiture); effects may differ with weaker or probabilistic punishment.
- No reward mechanism is present; results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects; field generalizability is uncertain.
- The institution is either exogenously imposed or endogenously chosen by group vote, but the endogenous mechanism is specific (minimum of votes, unanimity or majority); other forms of endogeneity may yield different results.
- The study does not test for long-term persistence of spillover effects beyond the immediate removal of the institution.
