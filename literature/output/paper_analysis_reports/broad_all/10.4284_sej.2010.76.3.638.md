## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment compares symmetric and asymmetric punishment institutions in a standard public goods game with punishment. Asymmetry is introduced by giving one player higher punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) than the other three. The main findings are: (1) Asymmetric punishment institutions are as effective as symmetric ones in sustaining cooperation and efficiency, as measured by group earnings. (2) There is no significant difference in efficiency (group earnings) between symmetric and asymmetric punishment institutions, controlling for average punishment effectiveness. (3) Higher average punishment effectiveness increases contributions, but does not significantly increase efficiency. (4) Strong players (with higher punishment effectiveness) punish more and earn more than weak players, but do not contribute less. (5) The behavioral regularities of symmetric punishment institutions (e.g., punishment targeting free riders, response to punishment) carry over to asymmetric institutions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the presence of asymmetry in punishment effectiveness (i.e., one player can punish more effectively than others) does not significantly affect group efficiency compared to symmetric punishment, as long as average punishment effectiveness is held constant. Thus, for the prediction task, the efficiency effect of enabling punishment should be predicted primarily based on the average punishment effectiveness, not on whether punishment power is symmetric or asymmetric. The paper also confirms that higher average punishment effectiveness increases contributions, but this does not necessarily translate into higher efficiency due to the costs of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between symmetric and asymmetric punishment with the same average effectiveness.

- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.

- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per point) is varied across treatments and between players. Higher average effectiveness increases contributions, but not efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment only examines groups of 4 players and 10 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- No control (no-punishment) condition is reported in this paper, so the effect of enabling punishment versus no punishment is not directly estimated.
- All treatments use fixed matching (partners), which may affect cooperation and punishment dynamics compared to random matching.
- The study does not examine the effect of allowing counter-punishment or reputation formation.
- The findings are limited to laboratory settings with student participants and may not generalize to field or naturally occurring environments.
