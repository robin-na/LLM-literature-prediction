## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms leads to a large increase in group efficiency and welfare, as measured by group payoff and normalized utility. The model, calibrated to Fehr & Gächter (2000), matches the observed jump in average contributions and welfare when punishment is enabled. The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations. The model further predicts that the relationship between societal value of cooperation and punishment is non-monotonic in the lab, but always positive in the field.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The model is directly calibrated to the Fehr & Gächter (2000) design (4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, specific punishment cost/tech), and matches the observed efficiency increase. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical, with quantitative calibration to existing experimental data rather than new experiments.
- Most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG with specific punishment cost/tech; generalization to other designs is model-based.
- The model assumes a fixed fraction of internalizers (acolytes) in the lab, which may not capture all sources of heterogeneity or learning dynamics.
- Efficiency effects are estimated for the final round after learning; early rounds may not reflect the full effect of punishment.
- The paper does not analyze the effect of all design dimensions (e.g., chat, default contribution) in detail; some are held constant or not present in the calibration.
- Cross-cultural and field generalizations are discussed but not directly tested with new data.
