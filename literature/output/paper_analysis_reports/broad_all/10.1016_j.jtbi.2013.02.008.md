## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency of punishment (defined as cooperation level divided by average cost to maintain it)
- cooperation level (fraction of cooperators and punishers)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- stationary densities of strategies (cooperators, defectors, conditional punishers, unconditional punishers)
- phase transitions between strategy mixtures
- spatial pattern formation
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the stationary densities of strategies and phase diagrams showing which strategies survive under different parameter regimes. The paper defines 'efficiency of punishment' as the ratio of cooperation level to average cost, which is a cost-effectiveness measure rather than group payoff or total welfare. There is no direct calculation or reporting of group efficiency as the ratio of actual group payoff to the maximum possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that conditional punishment, where the severity of punishment is proportional to the number of punishers in a group, is more effective than unconditional punishment in promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when punishment is costly. Conditional punishers can maintain cooperation at lower fines and costs, and are more efficient in terms of cooperation per unit cost. Unconditional punishers only outperform conditional punishers when punishment is very cheap. The results are robust across different values of the multiplication factor (mpcr), and the findings are explained through the mechanism of indirect territorial competition and pattern formation on the spatial grid.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the design of the punishment mechanism (conditional vs. unconditional) and the cost-to-fine ratio are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperation and cost-effectiveness. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the results suggest that conditional punishment is more likely to yield higher cooperation per unit cost, especially when punishment is not cheap. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency as a fraction of the maximum possible payoff, so predictions about absolute efficiency should be made with caution. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and no communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group has 5 players (G=5), arranged on a square lattice with overlapping groups. The total population size L^2 is varied for simulation accuracy, but the group size is always 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10^5 to 10^7 Monte Carlo steps (MCS) until stationary state is reached. There is no fixed number of rounds as in laboratory experiments; the system evolves until equilibrium.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a fixed amount (1) for cooperators and punishers; defectors contribute 0. There is no continuous contribution choice.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=3.8, r=3.5). The critical value for cooperation is r=3.74. The effect of r on phase transitions and strategy survival is analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (gamma) is systematically varied. The cost-to-fine ratio is a key moderator: conditional punishment is more effective when punishment is costly.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment fine (beta) and cost (gamma) are varied. Unconditional punishers always impose the maximal fine; conditional punishers impose a fine proportional to the number of punishers in the group. The effectiveness of punishment is a central focus.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Efficiency is not reported as group payoff relative to the maximum possible, but rather as cooperation per unit cost.
- The model assumes spatial structure (square lattice) and local imitation, which may not generalize to well-mixed or real-world settings.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included.
- Contribution is all-or-nothing; continuous contribution choices are not modeled.
- Findings may not directly translate to laboratory or field public goods games with different structures or additional features.
