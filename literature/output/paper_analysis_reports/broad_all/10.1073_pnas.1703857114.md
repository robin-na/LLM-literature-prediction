## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper uses agent-based simulations to show that norm internalization evolves more readily and has larger effects on cooperation when peer punishment of free riders is promoted. In public-goods-like ('us-vs.-nature') games, the evolution of norm internalization and punishment increases cooperation and material payoffs (efficiency). In intergroup contest ('us-vs.-them') games, norm internalization and punishment increase cooperation but reduce material payoffs due to overproduction and rent dissipation. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore positive in public-goods-like settings and negative in intergroup contest settings. The model also predicts that group size, punishment cost, and the strength of social norms moderate these effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends on the type of collective action problem. In standard public goods (us-vs.-nature) games, enabling punishment increases efficiency by reducing free riding. In intergroup contest (us-vs.-them) games, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive effort and resource dissipation. The model also suggests that group size and punishment cost are important moderators. For prediction, the context of the collective action (public goods vs. contest), group size, and punishment parameters should be considered.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size n is explicitly varied in simulations (n=8, 16, 24, and up to 64 in discussion). Larger groups make norm internalization and cooperation harder to evolve, especially in us-vs.-them games. In us-vs.-nature games, the effect of group size is more complex and depends on punishment cost.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Each generation consists of Q rounds, but the number of rounds is not systematically varied or analyzed for its effect.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment choices are binary (x=0 or 1, y=0 or 1).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by benefit parameter b, group size n, and the collective action function. Explicitly parameterized in the payoff equations.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Cost to punisher (δ) and cost to punished (κ) are explicit parameters. Effects of varying these costs are discussed: higher punishment cost can reduce norm internalization and cooperation, but effects are generally small.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is modeled via the cost to the punished (κ). Stronger punishment (higher κ) increases cooperation and norm internalization.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and based on agent-based simulations; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The models assume binary (all-or-nothing) contribution and punishment choices, which may not capture the full range of behaviors in real public goods games.
- Reward mechanisms are not modeled, so the comparative effects of reward vs. punishment are not addressed.
- The models do not include communication or information-sharing between players.
- The distinction between 'us-vs.-nature' and 'us-vs.-them' games is stylized and may not map perfectly onto real-world public goods or contest environments.
- Some parameters (e.g., number of rounds, migration rates) are not systematically varied or analyzed for their effects.
