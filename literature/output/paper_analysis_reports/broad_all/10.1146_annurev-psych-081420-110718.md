The review discusses behavioral outcomes such as cooperation, punishment, reward, and norm enforcement in public goods and related games. It references efficiency and welfare as theoretical constructs but does not report or synthesize quantitative payoff or efficiency results.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review highlights that the inclusion of punishment and reward mechanisms in repeated public goods games and other social dilemmas enables partner control, which can promote cooperation and the development of group norms. It notes that punishment is typically costly to the punisher but more costly to the target, and that such mechanisms can shape future cooperation through reputation and indirect reciprocity. The review also discusses moderators such as group heterogeneity, intergroup competition, and cross-cultural variation in norm enforcement. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative synthesis on how punishment affects efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and reward mechanisms can promote cooperation in public goods games and related environments, especially in repeated interactions. It identifies key design features (e.g., repeated play, sanctioning options, partner selection) that are relevant to predicting the impact of punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to calibrate the magnitude of punishment's effect on efficiency. Its value for prediction is mainly in identifying which game dimensions are theoretically important moderators.

## Important Limitations
- This is a theoretical review and does not provide quantitative or meta-analytic estimates of punishment's effect on efficiency or group payoff.
- Payoff-based outcomes such as efficiency, welfare, or total earnings are discussed as theoretical ideals but not systematically analyzed.
- The review synthesizes findings from many studies but does not report original data or effect sizes.
- Moderators such as player count, number of rounds, and cost/effectiveness of punishment are discussed in general terms but not systematically varied or analyzed.
- The review does not distinguish between different implementations of punishment (e.g., peer vs. centralized, second- vs. third-party) in terms of their quantitative impact on efficiency.
