## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment used a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with three players per group, five rounds, and random rematching (perfect stranger design). After each contribution stage, subjects could punish others at randomly assigned costs per unit of punishment. The main findings are: (1) punishment is used even when costly and even when there is no strategic benefit; (2) the amount of punishment is strongly negatively related to its cost (law of demand); (3) punishment is mainly directed at free riders, but a significant amount of punishment is also directed at high contributors by low contributors; (4) higher contributions are observed when punishment is available and especially when it is cheap, but efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is actually lower when punishment is cheap, due to the direct costs of punishment. Efficiency is highest when punishment is more expensive, as less punishment is used. Thus, the availability and cost of punishment have a non-monotonic effect on efficiency: punishment can sustain cooperation, but its costs can outweigh its benefits for group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the cost of punishment. When punishment is cheap, it is used more, leading to higher contributions but lower efficiency due to the costs incurred by both punishers and targets. When punishment is expensive, it is used less, contributions are somewhat lower, but efficiency is higher. For prediction tasks, this means that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are key moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's design and results allow inference about the relative efficiency across different punishment cost regimes. The findings suggest that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the net effect depends on the balance between increased cooperation and the direct costs of punishment. Game designs with low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment are likely to see the largest increases in contribution but also the largest efficiency losses.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per game, fixed throughout all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Five rounds per subject, each with a new group (perfect stranger design).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (integer from 0 to 10), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.6 (group account returns 0.6 times total group contribution, split equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit is randomly assigned each round from a set of five values, varying by treatment (e.g., 0, 30, 60, 90, 120 cents; 0, 5, 10, 20, 30 cents; 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 cents). Lower punishment cost leads to more punishment, higher contributions, but lower efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is 1:1 (each unit purchased reduces target's earnings by 1 unit); cost to punisher varies. When cost to punisher exceeds cost to target, punishment is still observed but less frequent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told there are five rounds in total.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, each subject sees the contribution of each other group member before making punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: subjects only see the total amount they were punished, not who punished them or how much each group member assigned.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a perfect stranger design (no repeated interaction with the same individuals), so results may not generalize to settings with repeated or reputational effects.
- Punishment is always anonymous; the effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
- No control condition without punishment is reported in this paper, so the direct effect of enabling punishment versus no punishment on efficiency is inferred rather than directly measured.
- The number of rounds is small (five), which may limit the observation of long-term dynamics.
- The group size is fixed at three; results may differ with larger groups.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The study is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
