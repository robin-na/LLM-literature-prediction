## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that, in large enough populations and with sufficiently patient players, efficient (cooperative) outcomes can be sustained in equilibrium in repeated anonymous random matching games, even when players only observe their own transactions and have no information about others' actions or identities. The mechanism involves a trust-building phase followed by community enforcement (contagion-based punishment). The set of achievable equilibrium payoffs includes those arbitrarily close to the efficient frontier, as long as the stage game has a strict Nash equilibrium and a one-sided incentive profile. The paper provides detailed conditions and mathematical proofs for when and how this is possible.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical support that, in repeated games with random matching, minimal information, and community enforcement (contagion-based punishment), the introduction of a punishment mechanism (in the form of Nash reversion triggered by observed deviations) can enable the group to achieve efficiency levels arbitrarily close to full cooperation, provided the population is large enough and players are sufficiently patient. The results are not specific to the public goods game but apply to a broad class of two-player games with certain properties. The findings suggest that, in environments with similar structure (random matching, minimal information, repeated play, and the possibility of community-wide punishment), enabling punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, even when direct monitoring is not possible. However, the results are theoretical and depend on equilibrium reasoning, not empirical observation.

## Important Limitations
- The results are entirely theoretical and depend on equilibrium reasoning; no empirical or simulation evidence is provided.
- The model is not a public goods game but a general two-player game with certain properties; results may not transfer directly to standard PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as Nash reversion (community-wide breakdown), not as a costly, targeted action; this differs from typical experimental punishment mechanisms.
- The construction requires infinite repetition and common knowledge of calendar time; results may not hold in finitely repeated or uncertain-horizon games.
- Players only observe their own matches; the results may not generalize to settings with richer information or communication.
- The mechanism is not robust to mandatory random mistakes (trembling hand with positive probability in every period).
