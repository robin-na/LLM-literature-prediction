# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Dual-Process Approach to Human Sociality: Meta-Analytic Evidence for a Theory of Internalized Heuristics for Self-Preservation
Authors: Capraro, V.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Outcome notes:
The paper is a meta-analytic review of dual-process (intuition vs. deliberation) effects on a range of social behaviors, including cooperation in public goods games and punishment in social dilemmas and ultimatum games. The primary outcomes are behavioral (e.g., cooperation, punishment, rejection, giving, lying), not efficiency or group payoff. Efficiency or welfare is not directly measured or meta-analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that promoting intuitive processing (via time pressure, ego depletion, conceptual primes, etc.) increases negative reciprocity (i.e., punishment of norm violations) in economic games, including public goods games with punishment phases and ultimatum games. This is supported by a meta-analysis showing a small but significant increase in punishment/rejection rates when intuition is promoted. However, the review does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not addressed; the focus is on the cognitive basis of the decision to punish, not on the downstream consequences for group outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that intuitive processing increases the likelihood of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, but it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this review is not directly informative, as it does not report or synthesize efficiency or welfare outcomes. It is useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior (e.g., cognitive manipulation, individual differences), but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. Use this paper to inform models of punishment frequency or behavioral response, not efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Player count is described for public goods games (N players) and other games (e.g., 2 in prisoner's dilemma, ultimatum game), but not systematically varied or analyzed as a moderator of punishment or efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both one-shot and repeated games are discussed, but most meta-analytic evidence is from one-shot games. Number of rounds is not systematically analyzed as a moderator.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both binary (all-or-nothing) and continuous contribution versions of public goods games are mentioned (e.g., 'binary PGG' in Table 2), but not systematically analyzed as a moderator.
- default contribution framing: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Default options are discussed as a potential moderator in dictator games and possibly relevant for status quo bias, but not for public goods games or punishment.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is described as a key parameter in public goods games. One study (Kessler et al., 2017) finds that the effect of intuition on cooperation depends on the benefit-to-cost ratio (b/c), which is closely related to mpcr.
- punishment cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment is described as costly (e.g., 'incur a cost to decrease the payoff of others'), but specific cost parameters are not systematically reported or analyzed.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Positive reciprocity (rewarding) is discussed as a behavioral domain, but not as a feature of public goods games with punishment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Standard public goods and punishment games typically provide feedback about others' actions, but the review does not systematically analyze this as a moderator.

## Important Limitations
- The review does not analyze or report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus as outcomes in public goods games or punishment games.
- All meta-analytic results are for behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation, punishment, rejection), not for payoff-based outcomes.
- Game design dimensions (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost) are not systematically varied or analyzed as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- Most included studies are from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) populations; generalizability is limited.
- The review does not distinguish between different punishment technologies or costs in a way that would inform efficiency predictions.
- No direct evidence is provided for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games; the review focuses on the cognitive basis of punishment behavior.
