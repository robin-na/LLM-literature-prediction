## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- All results depend on the specific evolutionary model (Moran process, rare mutations, well-mixed population) and may not generalize to real-world settings.
- The mutation structure (kernel) is a modeling assumption and may not correspond to actual biological or cultural mutation processes.
- No direct measurement of efficiency or payoff in empirical games; all outcomes are theoretical stationary distributions.
- No consideration of communication, reward, or identification of punishers, which may be relevant in real-world public goods games.
