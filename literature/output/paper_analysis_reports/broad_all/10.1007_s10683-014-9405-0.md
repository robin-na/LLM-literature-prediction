## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both informal (peer) and formal (rule-based) sanctions increase group efficiency in public goods games relative to a no-sanction baseline. When formal sanctions have no fixed cost, they are more popular and at least as efficient as informal sanctions. However, when formal sanctions require a fixed cost, groups often choose informal sanctions, which then deliver higher efficiency than formal sanctions with a fixed cost. Over time, experience with informal sanctions leads to improved targeting and deterrence, further increasing efficiency. Endogenous (voted) choice of sanctioning regime, especially informal sanctions, results in higher efficiency than exogenously imposed regimes, likely due to self-selection and signaling effects. The study also finds that the effectiveness of informal sanctions is robust to allowing counter-punishment (identifying punishers), and that both types of sanctions can make cooperation individually rational by reversing the incentive to free ride.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (either informal or formal) in a standard public goods game increases efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is especially strong for informal sanctions when formal sanctions are costly. The efficiency gain from punishment is moderated by the cost structure of the punishment institution (fixed and variable costs), the endogenous choice of institution, and experience over repeated rounds. For prediction, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially if chosen by vote and with low or no fixed cost) should be expected to increase efficiency substantially, often approaching the full-cooperation benchmark. The findings also suggest that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and whether punishment is formal or informal are key moderators. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is much lower than with punishment enabled, and the difference is largest when punishment is well-targeted and not too costly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 5 players (n=5) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 periods per session, grouped into 6 phases of 4 periods each.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject allocates any amount (0-20) to public/private account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each point to public account returns 0.4 to each of 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS, each point of punishment costs the punisher 1 and the target 4. In FS, variable and fixed costs: fixed cost is 0 or 5 per period per player; variable cost is 1/15 of sanction per player (shared).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS, 1 point spent reduces target's earnings by 4. In FS, sanction rate is chosen (0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2 per unit kept in private account), and is imposed with certainty.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of periods and phases in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects see the contributions of each other group member (in random order).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In main treatments, punishment is anonymous; in additional treatments, identities and punishment histories are revealed to allow counter-punishment. The effect on efficiency is neutral or positive (no reduction in efficiency).

## Important Limitations
- All groups are of fixed size (n=5); results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Laboratory setting with student subjects may limit external validity.
- Formal sanctions are implemented with certainty and perfect information, which may not reflect real-world enforcement.
- Informal sanctions assume costless and accurate monitoring, which may not hold outside the lab.
- No reward mechanisms are studied; only punishment.
- The design does not allow for simultaneous use of both formal and informal sanctions.
- Endogenous institution choice may be influenced by self-selection and signaling effects that differ from real-world settings.
