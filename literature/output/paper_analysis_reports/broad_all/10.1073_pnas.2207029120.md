## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that specialized third-party punishment can enable and sustain high-efficiency cooperative equilibria in social dilemmas where direct and indirect reciprocity fail. When enforcers are incentivized (via reputation and meta-enforcement) to punish defectors, producers cooperate, and both groups achieve higher payoffs. The efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the cost of information, and the destructiveness of enforcer conflict. If punishment is too costly or weak, or if enforcer conflict is not destructive enough, the system collapses to a low-efficiency defection equilibrium. The model also shows that the cooperative equilibrium is robust to the presence of alternative (non-cooperation-enforcing) reputation systems, but only if the key parameter thresholds are met.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment by specialized third-party enforcers can substantially increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, but only if the punishment is sufficiently severe, not too costly, and enforcers are themselves disciplined (e.g., via meta-enforcement and reputation). The model clarifies which game design dimensions (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, information cost, severity of punishment, and the structure of enforcement interactions) are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. For prediction, the presence of third-party punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, provided the relevant costs and incentives are favorable. However, if punishment is weak, costly, or undisciplined, efficiency gains may not materialize.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N is a parameter in the model; simulations use N=50 (n_P + n_E). Producers are paired in twos; enforcers are also paired. The model is robust to large population size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Game is repeated with continuation probability delta; expected number of rounds is 1/(1-delta). Higher delta (more rounds) increases the likelihood of cooperation and efficiency gains from punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (pay cost c, give benefit b) or defect (no cost, no benefit).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b/c for the prisoner's dilemma between two producers. b and c are varied in simulations.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to enforcer is v per punished producer (variable cost) and f (fixed cost for information). Higher v or f reduces cooperation and efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is p (cost imposed on punished producer). Higher p (relative to c and (1-tau)) is required for cooperation; more effective punishment increases efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Game is repeated with continuation probability delta; players know the structure (indefinite repetition). Higher delta (more rounds) increases cooperation and efficiency.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Enforcers can acquire information about their clients' actions and about other enforcers' reputations by paying a fixed cost f. This information is necessary for effective punishment and meta-enforcement.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Enforcers' actions are tracked via a reputation system; other enforcers can identify who failed to punish and can punish/attack accordingly. Identification is necessary for meta-enforcement.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based; it is not empirically tested in laboratory or field settings.
- The structure is stylized and abstracts from many real-world complexities (e.g., only two types of agents, binary actions, no communication, no reward).
- The model assumes perfect or near-perfect information for enforcers who pay the fixed cost, which may not generalize to environments with noisy or incomplete information.
- The model does not address heterogeneity in group size, network structure, or more complex public goods settings beyond the two-player prisoner's dilemma.
- Parameter thresholds for cooperation are sharp; small changes in cost or effectiveness can lead to collapse of cooperation, which may not reflect gradual transitions in real systems.
