Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group repayment performance, operationalized as days early or late (higher is better). Alternative measures include partial payment timing and default hazard rate. Non-payoff outcomes are explored in ethnographic analysis, focusing on mechanisms of enforcement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that group repayment (a direct measure of economic cooperation/efficiency) is highest in groups with moderate social cohesion and no disconnected subgroups. Both very high and very low cohesion are associated with worse repayment. Disconnected subgroups further reduce repayment, and the negative effect is mitigated by higher overall cohesion. The mechanisms are traced to a trade-off between ability and willingness to punish: high cohesion increases ability but reduces willingness to enforce, while disconnected subgroups increase willingness to punish out-group members but reduce ability to do so effectively. The net effect on efficiency is determined by the lesser of ability or willingness. These results are robust to controls for financial ability, group composition, and alternative outcome measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of informal punishment mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like settings is highly sensitive to group social structure. In environments where punishment is informal and depends on social ties, both the ability and willingness to punish must be considered. Efficiency is maximized at moderate levels of cohesion; too much cohesion reduces willingness to punish, undermining enforcement. Disconnected subgroups are detrimental to efficiency, even if willingness to punish out-group members is high, because ability is low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not monotonic in group connectedness and is context-dependent. The findings are most relevant for settings with informal, endogenous punishment and may not generalize to formal, externally imposed punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups are typically of size 4-5 (mean 4.58, SD 0.49, min 4, max 5). Group size is included as a control variable in all models.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Loans are repaid in monthly installments over 6-12 months; each loan has multiple scheduled payments (5,487 repayment transactions for 741 loans).
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Groups are self-formed and interact in person; qualitative data indicate frequent communication in cohesive groups. No explicit chat intervention, but naturalistic communication is present.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly calculated, but the group lending contract is joint liability: if one member defaults, all lose access to future credit. The group outcome is shared, but the exact MPCR is not specified.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is informal (social pressure, shaming, ostracism); costs are social/emotional, not monetary, and vary by group structure. No explicit cost per unit.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness depends on group structure: more cohesive groups have more opportunities (higher ability), but effectiveness is limited by willingness. No explicit quantitative measure.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards (e.g., encouragement, support) are mentioned as part of informal social enforcement, but not systematically analyzed or measured.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Loan terms (number of payments) are known in advance; repayment schedule is fixed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Group members are aware of each other's repayment behavior; social pressure and shaming are based on public knowledge of compliance.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is informal and face-to-face; the identity of punishers is always known.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and based on naturally occurring group structure; causal inference is limited by potential unobserved confounders, though extensive controls are used.
- Punishment is informal and endogenous, not externally imposed or experimentally manipulated; results may not generalize to formal or laboratory punishment mechanisms.
- The context is group lending in Sierra Leone; generalizability to other cultures, group sizes, or types of public goods is uncertain.
- The effect of group size is not systematically varied (most groups are size 4-5).
- No explicit manipulation or measurement of punishment cost or effectiveness per unit.
- No direct mapping to standard public goods game parameters (e.g., MPCR, explicit punishment tokens).
