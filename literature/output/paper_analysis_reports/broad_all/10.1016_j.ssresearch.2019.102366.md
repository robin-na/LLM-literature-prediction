# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputations in mixed-role markets: A theory and an experimental test
Authors: Kas, J., Corten, R., & van de Rijt, A.
Journal: Social Science Research
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- success rate (fraction of interactions in which trust is placed and honored)
- trustworthiness rate (fraction of interactions in which the trustee honored trust, if trust was placed)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust rate (fraction of interactions in which the trustor placed trust)
- determinants of trustor and trustee decisions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the trustor's decision to place trust (a behavioral outcome). The paper also reports 'success rate' (trust placed and honored) and 'trustworthiness rate' (honoring), which are related to group welfare but do not directly report group efficiency as a share of maximum possible group payoff. No explicit efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in a repeated dyadic trust/lending game, providing information about a participant's past behavior in both roles (mixed-role reputation) increases the rate at which trust is placed and honored in the Lending Game (free exchange, where lending is costly and not compensated), but not in the Trust Game (paid exchange, where lending is compensated). Specifically, in the Lending Game, the trust rate and the 'success rate' (trust placed and honored) are significantly higher under mixed-role reputation than under single-role reputation. In the Trust Game, there is no significant difference between reputation conditions. The paper interprets these results as evidence that mixed-role reputation systems facilitate reciprocal lending and increase overall cooperation and welfare in free exchange settings.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving public-goods-like environments where the main intervention is a change in the information structure (e.g., adding mixed-role reputation), this paper provides evidence that such interventions can increase efficiency-related outcomes (success rate) in free exchange settings, but not in paid exchange settings. The results are most relevant for environments where cooperation is costly and not directly compensated, and where reputation can be built across multiple roles. The findings suggest that enabling richer reputation signals (beyond single-role) can improve group outcomes in these contexts. However, the paper does not directly study classic public goods games or explicit punishment mechanisms, so the transfer to PGGs with punishment should be made with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 8, 10, or 12 participants per session; random dyadic matching within group each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 36 rounds per session; each participant played 18 times as trustor and 18 times as trustee.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication between participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/trust decision is binary: trust or not; honor or abuse.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed that the game lasts 36 rounds; round number shown on screen.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, both players were informed about their own and their partner's choice and payoff for that round, and their own total payoff. In the reputation conditions, trustors saw summary statistics of the trustee's past behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not use a public goods game or a direct analog; it uses dyadic trust/lending games, which are adjacent but not equivalent to PGGs.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only reputation information is manipulated.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as group payoff relative to maximum possible, but as 'success rate' (trust placed and honored), which is only a proxy.
- The findings may not generalize to larger groups or classic PGG settings with punishment.
- The main outcome is behavioral (trust placement), not direct group efficiency or welfare.
- The experiment is conducted with university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
