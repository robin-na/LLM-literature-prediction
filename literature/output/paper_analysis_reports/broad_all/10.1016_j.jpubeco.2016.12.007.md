## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a linear public goods game with two majority players (who benefit from the public good) and one minority player (who is harmed by the public good), punishment institutions do not increase contributions or efficiency. Punishment is used ineffectively, with minority players sometimes punishing contributors, and majority players reluctant to punish free riders. As a result, efficiency is significantly lower in the punishment treatments compared to the baseline. Communication does not improve outcomes in the punishment condition. In contrast, when a reward institution is available and communication is allowed, majority players compensate the harmed minority, which increases majority contributions and efficiency. The presence of a harmed minority undermines the usual positive effects of punishment and communication on cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that in public goods games with a harmed minority, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency and may even reduce it, relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect is robust to the presence or absence of communication. The game design features—three players, 30 rounds, continuous contributions, known group composition, and a high punishment impact (3:1 ratio)—do not rescue the effectiveness of punishment in this context. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in similar settings (especially with harmed minorities), enabling punishment is likely to have a neutral or negative effect on efficiency, even if control efficiency is moderate. The paper also shows that reward institutions, especially with communication, can increase efficiency by enabling compensation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players: 2 majority (benefit from public good), 1 minority (harmed by public good).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds, played in fixed groups (partner design), with restarts after 10 and 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) was a treatment variable. In punishment treatments, chat did not increase efficiency or contributions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: each player could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Majority players: mpcr = 0.8; Minority player: mpcr = -0.4; average mpcr = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment token cost the punisher 1 token and reduced the punished player's payoff by 3 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 token spent reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens (3:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward institution was a treatment arm, separate from punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward token cost the rewarder 1 token and increased the recipient's payoff by 1 token (1:1 ratio).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 token spent increases target's payoff by 1 token (1:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the total number of rounds (30), with restarts after 10 and 20 rounds announced in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observed others' contributions and payoffs each round (implied by standard VCM and explicit in design).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a variant of the standard public goods game, with a harmed minority (one player with negative mpcr), so results may not generalize to universal-benefit PGGs.
- Group size is fixed at 3, with a specific 2:1 majority-minority structure; effects may differ in larger or more heterogeneous groups.
- Punishment and reward institutions are not present simultaneously; only one is enabled per treatment.
- The punishment institution is relatively strong (3:1 impact), but still fails to increase efficiency in this context.
- Communication is only allowed at specific intervals (every 5 rounds), not continuously.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
