# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When punishment fails: Research on sanctions, intentions and non-cooperation
Authors: Houser, D., Xiao, E., McCabe, K., & Smith, V.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- percentage of tripled investment amount returned
- actual back-transfer
- actual back-transfer as a percentage of request
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation (return at least requested amount)
- complete defection (return nothing)
- weak non-cooperation (return positive amount less than requested)
- variance of return distribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: amount returned, cooperation/defection rates, and types of non-cooperation. Group or total payoffs, efficiency, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed. Payoff-related outcomes are reported as individual-level returns, not group efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that threats of punishment (a $4 fine for not meeting the requested back-transfer) do not increase cooperation and can even reduce it, especially when the requested return is high relative to the sanction. This effect is the same whether the punishment is intentionally imposed by the investor or randomly assigned by nature, indicating that incentives, not intentions, drive the effect. When the sanction is used to enforce a low request, it increases full cooperation; when used to enforce a high request, it increases complete defection. The main outcomes are the percentage of the tripled amount returned and the distribution of cooperation/defection types. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, so direct implications for efficiency are not available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation in trust/investment games depends on the relationship between the sanction size and the requested return. Punishment can reduce cooperation when the sanction is small relative to the demand. However, because the study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, it does not directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral responses (cooperation/defection) to punishment, especially in one-shot, two-player, trust-like games with continuous contribution and fixed punishment cost/effectiveness. The lack of efficiency or group payoff data limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played in pairs (2 players: investor and trustee).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and back-transfer choices are continuous (any integer amount within range), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 3 (amount sent is tripled for the trustee).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs the trustee $4 if the back-transfer is less than requested. The cost to the punisher (investor) is zero; the fine does not accrue to the investor.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed $4 deduction from the trustee's earnings if the condition is met (per instance, not per unit).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are explicitly told the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trustees know whether the punishment threat is present and whether it is intentional (by investor) or random (by nature). Identities are anonymous, but the source of the punishment (intentional vs. random) is known.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a one-shot, two-player trust/investment game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods settings.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual-level behavioral outcomes (amount returned, cooperation/defection) are analyzed.
- Punishment is a fixed fine with no cost to the punisher and does not benefit the punisher, which differs from many public goods game punishment designs.
- The study does not examine dynamic or repeated interactions, which are common in public goods game research.
- The effect of punishment is shown to depend on the relationship between sanction size and requested return, but the implications for group efficiency are not directly tested.
