## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a public goods game with both peer and centralized punishment, the presence of corruption undermines the effectiveness of strong centralized punishment. When centralized punishment is severe and corruption is possible, corruptors exploit the system, leading to lower cooperation and more defectors. Weaker centralized punishment allows peer-punishers to maintain cooperation even in the presence of corruption. Introducing a hybrid strategy (paying both the pool and peer-punishing) can maintain high cooperation across all levels of centralized punishment. The model suggests that a mix of centralized and decentralized enforcement is necessary for robust cooperation, especially in the face of corruption.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency (or cooperation) in public goods games depends critically on the structure of punishment (peer, pool, hybrid), the possibility of corruption, and the severity of centralized punishment. In environments where only centralized punishment is available and corruption is possible, increasing punishment severity can reduce efficiency by enabling corruptors to exploit the system. Allowing peer or hybrid punishment can mitigate this effect and restore cooperation. For prediction, the presence of corruption, the type of punishment available, and the severity of punishment are key moderators. The findings suggest that simply enabling (strong) punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the institutional context and the possibility of corruption must be considered.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size M=100, group size N=5 in simulations; model allows for variable group size N ≤ M.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model is evolutionary with repeated random matching; simulation runs for S=100,000 steps. No fixed number of rounds per group, but repeated play is assumed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each individual chooses whether or not to contribute a fixed amount c>0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N, with r=3.0 and N=5 in main simulations (so MPCR=0.6).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Peer punishment cost per act is gamma=0.7; pool punishment cost is G=0.7; corruptors pay K*G (K=0.5) to avoid punishment. The model varies the severity of punishment (B) and analyzes its effects.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized: peer-punishers impose fine beta=0.7 per defector; pool-punishers impose fine B per non-contributor (B varied from 0.7 to 7). The effect of increasing B is a central focus.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported; outcomes are inferred from the prevalence of cooperative strategies.
- The model assumes random matching and does not include network or repeated interaction effects beyond evolutionary updating.
- Corruption is modeled as a fixed fee to avoid punishment, which may not capture all real-world forms of corruption.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are considered.
- No communication, reputation, or information feedback mechanisms are included.
- The model does not address perceptual errors or aggregation of information by institutions.
