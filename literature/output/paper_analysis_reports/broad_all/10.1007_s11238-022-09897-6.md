# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When punishers might be loved: fourth-party choices and third-party punishment in a delegation game
Authors: Li, Y. Z., Luo, J., Niu, H., & Ye, H.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average income of third-party punishers, helpers, and non-actors in subsequent games (ultimatum game, delegation game)
- amounts offered to third-party types in ultimatum game
- amounts entrusted in trust game
- payoff to fourth-party choosers in delegation game
Non-payoff outcomes:
- partner/delegate choice rates (proportion of punishers/helpers/PDN chosen)
- trust ratings (Likert scale)
- rejection rates of unfair offers
- third-party choice rates (punish, help, do nothing)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based (income, offers, entrusted amounts), but the paper also reports behavioral outcomes such as partner choice and trust ratings.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three lab experiments, the paper shows: (1) Helpers are more trusted and preferred as partners in trust games, but punishers are more likely to be offered higher amounts in ultimatum games due to their deterrence signal, resulting in higher actual payoffs for punishers. (2) In a delegation game, when the proposer's knowledge of the delegate's punishment history is made explicit, fourth-party players prefer to select punishers as their delegates, and this choice leads to higher expected payoffs for both the delegate and the principal. (3) The effect is contingent on the visibility of the punishment signal; when the proposer's information is limited, helpers are preferred. Thus, punishment increases efficiency/payoff in environments where its deterrence is visible and relevant.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in games adjacent to public goods games (third-party punishment, ultimatum, and delegation games), enabling punishment increases the efficiency (payoff) of punishers and those who select them as delegates, especially when the punishment signal is visible to others. The effect is context-dependent: punishment is less effective in trust-based settings but more effective in settings where deterrence is valuable and observable. For prediction tasks, the presence of a visible punishment mechanism, the structure of the game (ultimatum/delegation vs. trust), and the information available to other players are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is lower than with punishment in deterrence-relevant settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player count varies by stage: 3 in third-party punishment game (dictator, recipient, third-party), 2 in trust/ultimatum games, 3 in delegation game (principal, delegate, proposer). Delegation game is a 3-player game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) interactions; no repeated rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/punishment/helping choices are all-or-nothing: third-party can choose to punish (pay 5 to reduce 5), help (pay 5 to increase 5), or do nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 5 RMB to reduce 5 RMB from the dictator (1:1 cost-impact ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (pay 1 to reduce 1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party can choose to help (pay 5 to increase recipient's payoff by 5), which is a form of reward/compensation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward/help cost is 5 RMB to increase 5 RMB for the recipient (1:1 cost-impact ratio).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (pay 1 to increase 1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the structure and that the game is one-shot; number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Fourth-party players are informed of the third-party's choice (punish/help/do nothing) before making their own decisions in subsequent games.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the 'known' condition, the proposers are informed of the delegate's punishment/helping history, which increases the efficiency/payoff for punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental games are not standard public goods games but are adjacent (third-party punishment, ultimatum, and delegation games), so direct generalization to PGGs may be limited.
- All games are one-shot; repeated interaction effects are not studied.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and relies on the visibility of the punishment signal to others.
- Sample sizes are moderate and drawn from a single university population.
- Some deception was used in the delegation game, which may affect external validity.
- No direct manipulation of group size beyond the specific roles in each game.
