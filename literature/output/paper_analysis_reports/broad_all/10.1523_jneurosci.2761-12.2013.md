# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Serotonin Modulates Striatal Responses to Fairness and Retaliation in Humans
Authors: Crockett, M. J., Apergis-Schoute, A., Herrmann, B., Lieberman, M., Müller, U., Robbins, T. W., & Clark, L.
Journal: Journal of Neuroscience
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rate of unfair offers (second-party punishment)
- amount spent to punish in third-party punishment
- reaction times
- fairness ratings
- fMRI BOLD responses in striatal regions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (rejection rates, amount spent to punish) and neural (BOLD responses). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare is reported. Payoff consequences are implicit in the structure of the games but not analyzed as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that lowering serotonin via ATD increases costly punishment (rejection of unfair offers) in the ultimatum game (second-party context), but tends to decrease costly punishment in third-party punishment games. The behavioral effects are accompanied by neural changes: ATD reduces ventral striatal responses to fairness and increases dorsal striatal responses during retaliation. The results suggest that serotonin modulates the drive for retaliation rather than fairness preferences. The games are one-shot, with no repeated interaction, and the main outcomes are behavioral and neural, not payoff-based. There is no direct analysis of efficiency or group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and neurobiological mechanisms underlying costly punishment, showing that serotonin depletion increases second-party punishment but decreases third-party punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, and the games are not public goods games but rather ultimatum and third-party punishment games. The findings may inform predictions about the psychological drivers of punishment in similar environments, but do not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design features (one-shot, no communication, binary accept/reject, known costs of punishment) are informative for mapping to game dimensions, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: 2 players per round (proposer, responder). Third-party punishment: 3 players per round (proposer, receiver, observer/punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: 96 human proposer trials, 48 computer proposer trials, 48 no-choice trials per session; all are one-shot (no repeated interaction with same partner). Third-party punishment: 16 rounds per session, all one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: responder's choice is binary (accept or reject). Third-party punishment: punisher can choose how much to spend (continuous up to 50p).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a public goods game, but in third-party punishment, each 1p spent reduces the proposer's payoff by 3p (punishment multiplier 1:3).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party punishment: up to 50p can be spent to reduce proposer's payoff at 1:3 ratio. Second-party (ultimatum): rejection is costly (responder forgoes own payoff).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party punishment: 1p spent reduces 3p from proposer (1:3 effectiveness). Second-party: rejection removes all payoffs for both players.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials and that each is one-shot; one round is randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the offer, the stake, and photographs of the proposer (and receiver in third-party). No summary statistics about others' behavior.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see photographs of proposers and receivers, but it is not specified whether punished individuals know the identity of the punisher. Likely anonymous from the punished party's perspective.

## Important Limitations
- The games studied are ultimatum and third-party punishment games, not public goods games; thus, findings may not generalize directly to PGG environments.
- No direct analysis or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus; only behavioral and neural outcomes are reported.
- All games are one-shot; there is no repeated interaction or opportunity for cooperation to build over time.
- The punishment mechanism differs between second-party (binary rejection) and third-party (continuous costly punishment), limiting comparability.
- The study manipulates serotonin pharmacologically, which is not a typical game design dimension.
- Sample size is moderate (n=28 after exclusions), and all participants are healthy volunteers from a single site.
