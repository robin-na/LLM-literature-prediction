## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that higher punishment for negative cooperation (defection) increases both the rate of positive cooperation and the average payoff (\bar{U}) in the network, especially when punishment exceeds the development cost. When punishment is low, negative cooperation is more attractive and average payoffs are lower. Knowledge spillover reduces cooperation and can increase payoffs for defectors, but if punishment is high, spillover has less effect. The most efficient and cooperative outcome is achieved when profits are split equally between the two sides. The findings are based on simulation of the theoretical model, not on lab or field experiments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical/simulation evidence that, in a networked, repeated, two-population cooperation game with punishment, increasing the punishment for negative cooperation increases group efficiency (average payoff), especially when punishment exceeds the cost of cooperation. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related. The results suggest that, for similar networked cooperation games, enabling or increasing punishment should be predicted to increase efficiency, particularly when the punishment is substantial relative to the cost of cooperation. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with network structure, two-sided cooperation, and payoff sharing, but the qualitative direction is likely to generalize to PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model is based on a real network with 309 nodes (industries and universities/research institutes), but the simulations and analysis focus on network structure rather than explicit player count manipulation.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model is iterated for a large number of rounds (e.g., 2000 or 6000 iterations) until steady state is reached, but the number of rounds is not a manipulated variable.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players choose between 'positively cooperate' and 'negatively cooperate', i.e., a binary choice.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (E) is a key parameter, directly manipulated in simulations. Higher punishment increases efficiency (average payoff).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a direct transfer from the negative cooperator to the positive cooperator; effectiveness is determined by the value of E relative to cost c.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not based on laboratory or field experiments with human subjects.
- The game structure is not a standard public goods game but a two-population, networked, repeated cooperation game with custom payoff structure.
- Parameter values and network structure are based on real data, but the behavioral assumptions are not empirically validated.
- No direct manipulation or analysis of several PGG-relevant dimensions (e.g., communication, marginal per-capita return, reward mechanisms, information structure).
- Findings may not generalize to all PGG-like environments, especially those without network structure or with different payoff sharing rules.
