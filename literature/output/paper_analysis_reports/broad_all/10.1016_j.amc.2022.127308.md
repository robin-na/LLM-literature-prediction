## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both institutional punishment and reward, when applied optimally, can drive a population to full cooperation in a public goods game. However, the cumulative cost to the institution is lower for optimal punishment than for optimal reward across almost all initial conditions. The optimal control protocols for both incentives are derived analytically, and numerical examples confirm that these protocols minimize the cost of achieving cooperation compared to other fixed or heuristic strategies. The evolutionary trajectory of cooperation is identical under both optimal strategies, but punishment is more cost-efficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in a standard public goods game with institutional incentives, enabling punishment (with optimally chosen intensity) is more cost-effective for achieving full cooperation than enabling reward, given the same game parameters. While the paper does not report group efficiency in the standard sense, the cumulative cost minimization is closely related to institutional efficiency. The results are most applicable to settings with well-mixed populations, no communication, continuous contribution, and institutional (not peer) punishment or reward. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutional punishment (with optimal tuning) will yield higher efficiency (lower cost to reach cooperation) than enabling institutional reward, and both are superior to no incentive. However, the model does not address peer punishment, finite groups, or stochastic effects.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Population is assumed to be infinitely large and well-mixed; results may not generalize to finite or structured populations.
- Punishment and reward are institutional (top-down), not peer-based; findings may not apply to peer punishment settings.
- The main outcome is cumulative institutional cost to reach cooperation, not group payoff or efficiency in the standard sense.
- No stochasticity, noise, or implementation errors are modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or history effects are included.
- The model does not address the time required to reach cooperation as a primary outcome, though it is discussed as a possible extension.
