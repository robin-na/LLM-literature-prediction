# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Unintended Consequences of Cooperation Inducing and Maintaining Mechanisms in Public Goods Dilemmas: Sanctions and Moral Appeals
Authors: Chen, X. P., Pillutla, M. M., & Yao, X.
Journal: Group Processes & Intergroup Relations
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good (amount invested in joint account or group project)
- trust in others' cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good (cooperation), not group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. Trust in others is also measured. No direct analysis of efficiency or group payoff is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Sanctions (punishment or reward) in public goods games increase cooperation while present, but when removed, cooperation drops sharply—sometimes to levels below those in groups that never had sanctions. Moral appeals also increase cooperation, but this effect is more persistent after the appeal is removed. The drop in cooperation after removing sanctions is mediated by reduced trust in others' cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings are based on contribution rates, not direct payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment (and reward) increases cooperation in the short term, but this effect is not sustained after removal, and may even backfire. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes for games with punishment enabled versus disabled. The findings are most useful for predicting contribution rates and for understanding the psychological mechanisms (trust) that may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. For efficiency prediction, the results suggest caution: increased cooperation under punishment may not translate to sustained efficiency gains, especially if punishment is later removed.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players in both experiments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 2 stages of 5 rounds each (10 rounds total). Experiment 2: 3 stages (weeks) of 5 rounds each (15 rounds total).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: participants could allocate any amount from 0 to 10 (Exp 1) or 0 to 10 hours (Exp 2) per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 0.5 (100% return divided among 4 players) in both experiments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous: in Exp 1, a reduction of 6 points for low contributors; in Exp 2, a fine of 25 hours for lowest contributor. No cost to other players; punishment is imposed by the experimenter.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction (6 points or 25 hours) for lowest contributor; not a variable or endogenous punishment technology.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward condition present: highest contributor receives bonus (6 points in Exp 1, 25 hours in Exp 2).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is exogenous: bonus provided by experimenter, not at a cost to other players.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is a fixed bonus (6 points or 25 hours) for highest contributor; not a variable or endogenous reward technology.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the number of rounds (trials) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round (Exp 1) or stage (Exp 2), participants were shown each member's contribution and associated payoff by ID.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Feedback included member IDs and who received punishment/reward.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution (cooperation), not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- Punishment and reward are exogenously imposed by the experimenter, not chosen by participants, so findings may not generalize to endogenous punishment settings.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or group payoff is reported, limiting direct applicability to efficiency prediction tasks.
- Short time horizon (10-15 rounds) and student samples may limit generalizability.
- The effect of punishment is only measured while present and immediately after removal; no long-term or repeated reintroduction/removal cycles are studied.
