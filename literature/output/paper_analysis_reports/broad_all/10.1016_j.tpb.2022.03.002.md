## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma with stochastic absence of resources for cooperation, the evolutionarily stable outcome is often a coexistence of trustful cooperators and trustful defectors, provided the cost-to-benefit ratio, the probability of lacking resources, and the exogenous break-up rate are not too high. Trustful strategies (those that keep interacting with partners who defect due to lack of resources) are robustly favored by natural selection, and this leads to higher equilibrium frequencies of cooperation and higher group payoffs (efficiency). When parameters are unfavorable (high cost, high resource absence, high break-up), defectors dominate and efficiency is low. The model does not include explicit punishment or costly sanctioning, but the 'walk-away' (partner switching) mechanism is functionally adjacent to punishment in that it can enforce cooperation by breaking ties with defectors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not model a public goods game or explicit punishment, it analyzes a closely related repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with partner choice (walk-away) and stochastic resource constraints. The key design dimensions mapped are the cost-to-benefit ratio (analogous to MPCR), the probability of resource absence (a form of noise or cooperation constraint), and the exogenous break-up rate (analogous to the probability of game continuation or group stability). The main result is that mechanisms allowing players to break ties with defectors (even when defection is due to lack of resources) can support high efficiency, provided the game is not too harsh (low cost, low noise, stable groups). This suggests that in PGG-like environments, enabling partner choice or walk-away (an adjacent form of sanction) can increase efficiency, especially when the baseline efficiency is not too low. However, the absence of explicit punishment means the results are not directly transferable to punishment-enabled PGGs, but the logic of positive assortment and the conditions for efficiency gains are highly relevant.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a pairwise (2-player) repeated game throughout; all results are for dyads.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for an indefinite number of rounds, with the expected number of rounds determined by the exogenous break-up rate (rho).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: cooperate (pay cost, give benefit) or defect (do nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost-to-benefit ratio (c/b) is the key parameter; lower c/b (higher MPCR) increases the likelihood of cooperation and efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The expected number of rounds is determined by the exogenous break-up rate (rho); the game is indefinite but with a known continuation probability.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe both the partner's action (cooperate/defect) and whether the partner had resources for cooperation in each round.

## Important Limitations
- The model is strictly pairwise (2-player) and does not generalize directly to n-player public goods games.
- No explicit punishment or costly sanctioning is modeled; only partner choice (walk-away) is considered.
- The model assumes infinite population size and deterministic evolutionary dynamics.
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are theoretical.
- The model assumes that information about resource availability and actions is perfectly observable.
- No costs for searching for new partners are included, which may bias results in favor of walk-away strategies.
- The model does not address heterogeneity in resource availability across individuals.
- The model does not include communication, reputation, or more complex memory strategies.
