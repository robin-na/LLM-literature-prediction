## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model predicts that in standard public goods games without punishment, the equilibrium is typically full defection and low efficiency, matching empirical data. When a punishment stage is added (costly to the punisher, but more costly to the punished), the threat of punishment by inequity-averse players can sustain full cooperation and thus high efficiency, even if most players are selfish. The model provides explicit conditions under which this occurs, and shows that the presence of even a single sufficiently inequity-averse 'enforcer' can be enough to sustain efficient cooperation. The theory also predicts that the effect of punishment on efficiency is stronger when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is higher, and when the cost of punishment is lower.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, often dramatically, provided there is at least a small fraction of inequity-averse players. The model gives explicit conditions relating group size, mpcr, and punishment cost to the likelihood of efficient cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, for typical lab PGG designs, adding punishment will shift efficiency from low (near zero) to high (near one), unless punishment is extremely costly or the group is very large and inequity aversion is rare. The model also suggests that the marginal effect of punishment is moderated by mpcr and punishment cost.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The model and discussion explicitly analyze the effect of group size (n) on the ability to sustain cooperation with and without punishment. Larger groups make it harder to sustain cooperation without punishment, but with punishment, a single enforcer can sometimes suffice. The effect of n is ambiguous for punishment: as n increases, the condition for sustaining cooperation becomes harder, but the probability of having at least one enforcer increases.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model focuses on equilibrium (final period) behavior and references repeated games, but does not model learning or dynamics explicitly. Empirical evidence is cited for final period outcomes.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public goods game is modeled with continuous contribution choices (g_i in [0, y]), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return (a) is a key parameter in the model. Higher a (mpcr) increases the prospects for cooperation and efficiency, both with and without punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (c) is explicitly modeled. Lower punishment cost makes it easier to sustain cooperation and high efficiency. The model gives explicit conditions involving c.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the cost to the punisher (c) vs. the loss to the punished (1 per unit). Higher effectiveness (lower c) increases the ability to sustain cooperation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes that after the contribution stage, all players observe the full contribution vector (g_1,...,g_n) before punishment decisions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical model and does not present new empirical data; its predictions are compared to existing experiments.
- The model focuses on equilibrium (final period) outcomes and does not address learning, dynamics, or short-run effects.
- The model assumes some fraction of inequity-averse players; if all players are selfish, punishment does not sustain cooperation.
- The model does not address the effect of communication (chat), reward mechanisms, or framing effects.
- The model does not specify the effect of identifying punishers (show_punishment_id) or the impact of information about the number of rounds.
