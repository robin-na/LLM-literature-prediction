# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social learning and voluntary cooperation among like-minded people
Authors: Gächter, S., & Thöni, C.
Journal: Journal of the European Economic Association
Year: 2005

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group contribution
- mean income reduction due to punishment
- average earnings (implied, not directly reported as efficiency)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- group composition effects
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average group contribution. Mean income reduction due to punishment is reported, but efficiency (total group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly calculated or reported. Earnings are discussed in context but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study uses a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.6, 3 players, 10 rounds) to examine how group composition and the availability of punishment affect cooperation. Groups are either sorted by initial cooperative attitude (measured in a one-shot game) or randomly composed. In the no-punishment condition, sorted groups of high cooperators maintain very high contributions, while randomly composed groups show lower and declining cooperation. When punishment is available, average contributions increase substantially in both sorted and random groups, but the effect is strongest in randomly composed groups. In sorted groups of high cooperators, punishment is rarely used and does not increase contributions further. In sorted groups of low cooperators, punishment is used frequently but does not substantially raise contributions. The paper reports average contributions and income reduction due to punishment, but not efficiency per se. The findings suggest that punishment is most effective at raising cooperation (and likely efficiency) in heterogeneous groups, while homogeneous groups of cooperators do not need punishment to achieve high outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard repeated linear public goods games, enabling punishment increases average group contributions, especially in randomly composed (heterogeneous) groups. The effect on efficiency is positive, but the net gain depends on the magnitude of punishment costs. In homogeneous groups of high cooperators, punishment is unnecessary and does not improve outcomes. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, particularly when group composition is random. The specific game parameters (3 players, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.6, punishment cost 1:3) are informative for parameter-matched predictions. However, efficiency gains may be offset by punishment costs, especially if punishment is used frequently. The paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions should be based on the reported contribution and punishment patterns.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds in the repeated public goods game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0-20 ECU per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.6 (each ECU contributed returns 0.6 ECU to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 1 ECU per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's income by 3 ECU (1:3 cost-impact ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told in advance that there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are informed about their group members' individual contributions.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (total group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported; only average contributions and punishment costs are shown.
- The experiment uses only 3-player groups and 10 rounds, which may limit generalizability to larger or longer games.
- Subjects are university students from a single institution, which may affect external validity.
- No direct analysis of the net effect of punishment on efficiency (i.e., whether increased contributions outweigh punishment costs in all cases).
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results do not generalize to games with both punishment and reward.
