# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Counterproductive punishment in closed communities: Experimental evidence from Japanese fishery
Authors: Goto, J., & Matsui, T.
Journal: Marine Policy
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate (percentage of endowment contributed to public good)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of endowment contributed to the public good. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a share of the social optimum is reported. The paper does report average payments, but does not analyze efficiency or welfare as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that, before punishment is introduced, average contributions are similar in closed and open communities. After introducing the (indirect, weak) punishment mechanism, contributions decrease substantially in closed communities (by 17 percentage points) and increase slightly in open communities (by 4 percentage points). Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect in closed communities is robust to controls and alternative specifications. The mechanism appears to be that punishment in closed communities is perceived as excessive peer pressure, undermining trust and cooperative norms. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, but the direction of contribution changes suggests that punishment may reduce efficiency in closed communities and slightly increase it in open communities.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment depends on community structure: in environments with strong pre-existing cooperative norms and high internal communication (closed communities), even weak, indirect punishment can reduce contributions and likely efficiency. In more open communities, punishment has a small positive effect. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so predictions must be inferred from contribution rates. The punishment mechanism is indirect and low-cost, so results may not generalize to direct, costly punishment systems. The findings are most relevant for settings with similar social structure and weak, reputation-based punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experimental group consists of 4 players. Group membership is fixed throughout the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per session: 5 rounds without punishment, 5 rounds with punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed during the experiment, except for the indirect punishment mechanism (expression of dissatisfaction).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how much of their 3000 yen endowment to contribute (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total group contribution is doubled and split equally among 4 players. MPCR = 0.5 (each yen contributed returns 0.5 yen to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to send a dissatisfaction message is 10 yen per round per subject.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is indirect: sending a dissatisfaction message costs 10 yen but does not reduce others' payoffs; only conveys dissatisfaction anonymously.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told there are 10 rounds, with a rule change after round 5.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are shown the amounts contributed by others in random order, without identifying information.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (dissatisfaction messages) is anonymous; recipients cannot identify who sent the message.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- The punishment mechanism is weak and indirect (expression of dissatisfaction), not direct monetary punishment; results may not generalize to direct punishment systems.
- The experiment is conducted with real-world fishing community members in Japan; findings may not generalize to other populations or contexts.
- The study does not analyze or report group payoff, welfare, or efficiency as a share of the social optimum.
- The mechanism for the negative effect in closed communities is inferred from contribution changes and survey data, not directly observed.
- The classification of communities as 'closed' or 'open' is based on a specific network measure; other definitions may yield different results.
