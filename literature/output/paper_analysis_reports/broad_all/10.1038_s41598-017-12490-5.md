# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On the long-run efficacy of punishments and recommendations in a laboratory public goods game
Authors: Chaudhuri, A., & Paichayontvijit, T.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings (efficiency)
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- punishment points assigned
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is efficiency, measured as average earnings relative to the social optimum. Contribution and punishment behavior are also reported but are secondary.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated linear public goods games with groups of four, both peer punishment and exhortative recommendations (public messages) increase efficiency compared to a control with no intervention. Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. Punishment starts with lower efficiency due to the cost of punishing but shows an upward trend over time, eventually catching up to or surpassing recommendations in some conditions, particularly with longer time horizons. However, in these experiments, punishment never outperforms the restart recommendation treatment. The effect of punishment is stronger with fixed matching than with random rematching. The cost-effectiveness of punishment depends on the punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) and the length of the game. The study provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, with detailed efficiency data for control, recommendation, and punishment treatments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear public goods games. It reports efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the social optimum) for control and punishment treatments, with and without recommendations, across different matching protocols and time horizons. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Experiment 1: 20 rounds (10 pre, 10 post-intervention); Experiment 2: 30 rounds (10 pre, 20 post-intervention). Longer games allow punishment to catch up or surpass recommendations in efficiency.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is allowed, except for the public recommendation message in the relevant treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions are made in whole tokens from 0 to 10 (discrete, not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each token contributed is doubled and split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Each punishment point costs the sender $0.05 (1 token), and reduces the receiver's earnings by 10% per point, up to 100% for 10 points. The cost/impact ratio is 1:2 (1 token cost, 2 tokens max impact per point if receiver has full earnings).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment technology: 1 token cost to punisher reduces receiver's earnings by 10% of their round payoff per point, up to full forfeiture at 10 points.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are fully informed about the total number of rounds in advance, except for the 'restart' treatment where a surprise extension is used.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see the individual contributions of other group members (by ID number) and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not know who punished them. The paper notes that anonymity avoids revenge and may result in higher efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) is different from some other studies, which may affect generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The effect of a 'restart' is only tested with recommendations, not with punishment, so the full potential of punishment with restarts is not assessed.
- All participants are university students from New Zealand, which may limit external validity.
- The number of rounds is limited to 20 or 30, so very long-run effects are not observed.
- Contribution choices are discrete (whole tokens), not continuous.
