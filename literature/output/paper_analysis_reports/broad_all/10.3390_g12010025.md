The main dependent variables are average earnings for distributors and receivers, total surplus produced, and group-level efficiency. Contribution rates and cooperation rates are also reported, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three treatments: (1) Baseline (forced cooperation, exogenous re-matching), (2) Re-match (voluntary cooperation, exogenous re-matching), and (3) Endogenous-match (voluntary cooperation, endogenous group formation). Allowing refusal to cooperate (punishment) increases average contributions and receiver earnings, and decreases distributor earnings, relative to forced cooperation. However, allowing repeated interaction with the same group (endogenous matching) does not further increase average efficiency or payoffs compared to exogenous re-matching. Instead, repeated interaction leads to self-selection: some groups are highly cooperative and efficient, while others are unstable and inefficient. The most efficient groups are composed of 'greedy' distributors (who contribute more to avoid rejection) and 'modest' receivers (who accept lower offers). Overall, the possibility of punishment (refusal to cooperate) increases efficiency, but repeated interaction does not further increase average efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment via refusal to cooperate (i.e., making cooperation voluntary) increases efficiency and average payoffs compared to forced cooperation in a surplus allocation game with asymmetric bargaining power. However, simply allowing repeated interaction and endogenous group formation does not further increase average efficiency or payoffs, though it does create heterogeneity in group outcomes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (even if only via refusal to cooperate) is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment. The effect of repeated interaction/endogenous matching is not to further increase average efficiency, but to increase variance across groups. The findings are directly relevant for settings where punishment is implemented as the ability to refuse cooperation, but less so for costly punishment mechanisms typical in standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players (2 distributors, 2 receivers) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 30 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; full anonymity was maintained.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cooperation was all-or-nothing: all group members had to agree to cooperate for surplus to be produced; otherwise, no surplus was generated.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Surplus of 20 ECUs was produced only if all 4 members cooperated; the surplus was then allocated by the two distributors. The marginal per-capita return is not standard as in PGGs, but the group surplus is only produced if all cooperate.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as the ability to refuse cooperation, which is costless to the punisher (but results in zero payoff for all group members that round). There is no explicit costly punishment mechanism.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: refusal to cooperate prevents surplus production for the entire group in that round.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Before each round, all subjects were informed about how the matched distributors allocated surplus in the last three rounds.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a surplus allocation game with asymmetric bargaining power and all-or-nothing cooperation, so results may not generalize to standard PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as refusal to cooperate (costless to the punisher, but costly to all), not as a costly punishment mechanism typical in PGGs.
- Group size and structure (2 distributors, 2 receivers) are fixed and may not generalize to larger or differently structured groups.
- No communication or identification of punishers; all actions are anonymous.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- The findings are qualitative and may not map directly to real-world or other experimental settings.
