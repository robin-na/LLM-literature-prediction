# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Retribution and emotional regulation: The effects of time delay in angry economic interactions
Authors: Wang, C. S., Sivanathan, N., Narayanan, J., Ganegoda, D. B., Bauer, M., Bodenhausen, G. V., & Murnighan, K.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency (ultimatum rejection rate)
- punishment magnitude (points spent on punishment)
- anger/negative emotion (self-report and coded writing)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency of costly punishment (e.g., rejection of unfair ultimatum offers, points spent to punish in third-party trust game) and measures of negative emotion. No direct group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across five lab experiments, the authors show that introducing a time delay before making punishment decisions (e.g., rejecting unfair offers or punishing norm violators) generally reduces costly punishment, but only if the delay is accompanied by distraction or cognitive reappraisal. If participants ruminate during the delay, punishment increases. The effect is mediated by negative emotions (anger). The experiments use one-shot or single-decision ultimatum games and third-party punishment trust games, with punishment measured as rejection rates or points spent. No direct group efficiency or total payoff outcomes are reported, but the reduction in costly punishment implies less wasteful destruction of resources. The findings highlight the importance of emotional regulation in moderating punitive impulses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It shows that the context and psychological state of punishers (e.g., time delay, distraction, rumination) can strongly moderate the frequency and magnitude of costly punishment. While the games are not public goods games, the results suggest that interventions reducing negative emotion or promoting reappraisal/distraction could reduce inefficient punishment and thus improve group payoffs. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, and the games are not repeated or multi-player PGGs, the findings should be used as informative but indirect evidence for prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: 2 players (proposer, responder); Third-party punishment trust game: 3 players (A, B, C), but only C is a real participant.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) decisions.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all decisions are made privately.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: responder's choice is all-or-nothing (accept or reject). Third-party punishment: punishment is continuous (points allocated).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum: rejection is costly (responder gets $0 instead of $2). Third-party: each point spent on punishment costs the punisher 1 point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party punishment: each point spent reduces target's payoff by 2 points (punishment multiplier = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a single decision (one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In third-party punishment, participants are shown the actions and payoffs of other players (e.g., Player A sent all points, Player B returned nothing).

## Important Limitations
- The games studied are not public goods games or close variants; they are ultimatum and trust games with punishment.
- No direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff are reported; only costly punishment rates and magnitudes are measured.
- All experiments are one-shot (single round), so findings may not generalize to repeated or multi-round environments typical of public goods games.
- The effect of punishment on cooperation or group outcomes is not studied; only the antecedents and moderators of punishment behavior are analyzed.
- Emotion regulation manipulations (distraction, rumination, reappraisal) may not map directly onto institutional or design features in public goods games.
