# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: A preference to learn from successful rather than common behaviours in human social dilemmas
Authors: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & D'Amico, V.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- preference for social information (success vs. common behavior)
- frequency of matching social information
- decline in cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good. The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings as a primary outcome, though it discusses how learning from success leads to lower cooperation and thus lower group payoffs in principle.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Participants in a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4, groups of 4, 6 rounds) were more motivated to learn from and copy successful (high-earning) behaviors than to conform to common behaviors. When shown both the average (common) contribution and the average contribution of the highest earners from a previous experiment, participants contributed significantly less than when shown only the common behavior. This decline in cooperation was not explained by simply knowing about free riders, but specifically by the desire to learn from success. Most participants preferred to see information about successful behaviors over common behaviors. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the lower contributions in the 'success' information condition imply lower efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games without punishment or reward, providing information about successful (high-earning) behaviors leads to lower cooperation and thus likely lower efficiency, compared to providing only information about common behaviors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of success-based social information (even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward) can undermine efficiency. However, the study does not manipulate or analyze punishment directly, and so its direct relevance to predicting the effect of enabling punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of social information and learning in public goods games, and suggest that efficiency may be lower when participants can observe and learn from successful free-riding.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 participants in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 6 rounds in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0-20 MU per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total multiplier 1.6, 4 players).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told there would be 6 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Participants were shown summary information about average contributions (common pattern) and/or average of highest earners (success pattern) from a previous experiment, not from their own group.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include a punishment or reward mechanism in the current experiment, so findings do not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured or reported; conclusions about efficiency are inferred from contribution levels.
- Social information is based on behavior from a previous experiment, not from participants' own group, which may limit ecological validity.
- The participant pool is limited to students at a Swiss university, which may affect generalizability.
- The study focuses on the effect of social information (success vs. common behavior) rather than institutional design features like punishment or reward.
