The paper is a theoretical review focused on the explanatory power of moral preferences (personal norms) for unselfish behavior in one-shot, anonymous economic games. Outcomes discussed are primarily behavioral (e.g., giving, cooperation, punishment, truth-telling), not efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that a wide range of unselfish behaviors in one-shot, anonymous economic games are better explained by a 'moral preference hypothesis'—that is, people have preferences for following their own personal norms about what is right or wrong, beyond the monetary consequences of their actions. This framework organizes and explains empirical deviations from standard social preference models in games such as the dictator game, ultimatum game (altruistic punishment), Prisoner's Dilemma (cooperation), trust game (trustworthiness), sender-receiver game (truth-telling), and trade-off games (equality vs. efficiency). The review also discusses the practical implications of targeting personal norms (rather than social or injunctive norms) in interventions to promote pro-sociality, and proposes a formal utility function incorporating personal norms. However, the paper does not provide new data or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of punishment or sanctions in multi-player, repeated, or public goods games. Its main contribution is theoretical: it suggests that personal moral norms are a key driver of unselfish behavior (including punishment) in one-shot, anonymous games, and that interventions targeting personal norms may be more effective than those targeting social norms. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides only indirect, mechanistic context about why people might punish or cooperate, but not quantitative or design-specific evidence about efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The review discusses games with varying player counts (e.g., dictator game: 2 players; Prisoner's Dilemma: 2 players; public goods game mentioned as a variant with more than two players), but does not analyze or manipulate player count as a variable.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The review focuses on one-shot (single-round) games and explicitly contrasts one-shot with repeated interactions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes.
- No direct evidence is provided for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or repeated games; the focus is on one-shot, anonymous, mostly two-player games.
- Punishment is discussed only in the context of altruistic punishment (ultimatum game rejection), not as a design feature in public goods or multi-player cooperation games.
- No quantitative or design-specific evidence is provided for the effect of any game dimension on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- The review does not address the effect of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, or other institutional design features relevant to public goods games.
