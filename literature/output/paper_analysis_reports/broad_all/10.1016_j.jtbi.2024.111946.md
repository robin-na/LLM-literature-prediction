## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- fixation probability of cooperators
- mean group payoff (fitness)
- mean number of offspring produced by groups

Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the fixation probability of cooperators (interpreted as the probability the population reaches the all-cooperator absorbing state), the mean group fitness (payoff), and the stability of equilibria. The paper does not report contribution rates or punishment frequency, as there is no punishment mechanism in the model.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in the 'others-only' n-person prisoner's dilemma (a strong altruism scenario), cooperation cannot evolve under asexual reproduction or sexual reproduction with selfing, but can evolve and become stable under biparental sexual reproduction if the group benefit multiplier (r) is sufficiently large relative to group size (n) and the cost of cooperation (c) is not too high. The model does not include punishment or reward, and the main mechanism enabling cooperation is the genetic structure imposed by biparental reproduction. The main outcome is the probability that the population reaches the all-cooperator state (fixation probability), which is a direct measure of group efficiency or welfare. The results are summarized in phase diagrams showing regions where cooperation is stable, bistable, or unstable, as a function of c, r, and n. The findings are robust to demographic noise and extend to dioecious populations. The paper does not provide evidence about the effects of punishment, communication, or other institutional design features.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments that lack punishment or reward mechanisms, especially when the game structure is the 'others-only' variant of the n-person prisoner's dilemma. It shows that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency (measured as fixation of cooperation and group payoff) is only achievable under specific genetic and demographic conditions—namely, biparental sexual reproduction, sufficiently high group benefit multiplier (r), and not-too-large group size (n) or cost (c). The results suggest that, without punishment, efficiency is generally low unless these conditions are met. However, the paper does not provide any evidence about the effect of enabling punishment or sanctions, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency difference between control and punishment-enabled conditions. Its main value is in establishing a baseline: in the absence of punishment, efficiency is generally low except under the specified genetic/demographic conditions. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not manipulate or analyze punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size n is a key parameter; increasing n makes cooperation less likely to fixate (higher r required for cooperation).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is generational, with repeated group formation and reproduction until fixation; not a fixed number of rounds, but an evolutionary process.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: individuals either contribute c or not (cooperator or defector).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The group benefit multiplier r is analogous to MPCR; higher r increases the likelihood of cooperation fixation and group efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- No punishment or reward mechanisms are present; the paper cannot inform about the effect of enabling punishment.
- The model assumes binary contribution (all-or-nothing) and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- The evolutionary process is not structured as a fixed-round game; results may not transfer directly to finite-round laboratory public goods games.
- The main outcomes are fixation probabilities and equilibrium stability, not average efficiency per round as in typical experimental PGGs.
- The model assumes random group formation and no positive assortment; real-world settings may differ.
- The results depend on the genetic structure (biparental sexual reproduction); findings may not generalize to all social dilemmas or human experiments.
- No direct analysis of communication, information, or institutional design features.
