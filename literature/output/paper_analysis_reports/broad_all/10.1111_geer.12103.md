# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Designing Institutions for Social Dilemmas
Authors: Rockenbach, B., & Wolff, I.
Journal: German Economic Review
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (sum of individual payoffs as a fraction of the social optimum)
- group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- rule component usage (e.g., punishment, communication, feedback)
- rule adaptation over time
Outcome notes:
Efficiency is the main dependent variable, defined as the sum of individual payoffs (net of rule costs) divided by the maximum possible group payoff. Contribution rates and rule design choices are also tracked, but efficiency is the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when subjects are allowed to design institutions for a repeated public goods game, they initially favor punishment or redistribution mechanisms, but these are associated with lower efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) compared to rules that emphasize communication (framing, appeals) or participative elements (endogenous rule change). Over the course of the experiment, the use of punishment declines, and efficiency increases, suggesting that punishment is not an efficient solution in this context. Communication and participative rule features are positively associated with efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that punishment, as typically implemented by endogenous designers, tends to reduce efficiency, mainly due to its costs, and that designers learn to move away from it.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with endogenous institution design, enabling punishment mechanisms tends to reduce efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) baseline, especially when punishment is costly and not peer-administered. The effect is robust across multiple rounds and rule sets, with efficiency increasing as designers move away from punishment toward communication and participative mechanisms. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment (especially costly, centralized, or automatic forms) is likely to result in lower efficiency than the control, unless the punishment is highly refined or replaced by redistribution mechanisms with lower waste. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played over 25 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No open chat or peer-to-peer communication is implemented, except for rare signaling/voting in the final tournament.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses an integer amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.4 (total contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided by 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment and redistribution mechanisms are designed by subjects, with costs attached to their implementation. Punishment is costly and these costs reduce efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is typically automatic or centralized, not peer-to-peer; effectiveness is limited and often wasteful.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some rule sets include redistribution or 'big bonus' mechanisms, which can be interpreted as rewards for high contributors.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Redistribution and bonus mechanisms have costs, but details are not always specified.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is not directly analyzed; some mechanisms transfer points to high contributors.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is played over 25 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Aggregate contribution information is typically shown; individual contribution feedback is less common and associated with lower efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a small sample size (24 students in two seminar groups), limiting statistical power and generalizability.
- The institution design process is highly endogenous and exploratory, making it difficult to isolate the effects of individual rule components due to potential interactions.
- Punishment mechanisms are not standardized; their form, cost, and implementation vary across rule sets, complicating direct comparison to typical experimental punishment treatments.
- The study focuses on highly experienced and motivated participants (seminar students), which may not reflect typical public goods game populations.
- Some rule sets combine multiple components, making it hard to attribute efficiency effects to punishment alone.
