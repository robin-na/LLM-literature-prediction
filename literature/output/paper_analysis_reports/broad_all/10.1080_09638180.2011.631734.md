Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are superior payoff, subordinate payoff, and slack (difference between reported and actual costs, normalized). Willingness to audit, audit frequency, and reputation measures are also analyzed. Contribution/cooperation rates are not directly measured, but slack is a proxy for misreporting.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Audits (punishment) reduce slack and increase the superior's payoff (efficiency) in all random matching treatments, regardless of whether reputation information is provided. In these settings, subordinates reduce misreporting to avoid punishment, and superiors' willingness to audit is responsive to subordinates' past behavior. However, in fixed matching (repeated dyads), enabling audits leads to higher slack and lower superior payoff than in the random matching case, and even compared to the no-audit control. This is attributed to negative reciprocity: subordinates retaliate against superiors who punish them by increasing slack in later rounds, making punishment inefficient in repeated relationships. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus positive in random matching but negative or null in fixed matching.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the matching protocol: punishment increases efficiency (superior payoff, lower slack) in random matching (analogous to one-shot or stranger settings), but not in fixed matching (repeated dyads), where it can decrease efficiency due to retaliation. For prediction, the presence of repeated interaction with the same partner (fixed matching) is a key moderator: enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in random matching but may have no effect or even reduce efficiency in fixed matching. Other design dimensions (e.g., reputation information) have smaller or secondary effects. The control efficiency (no-punishment) baseline is necessary for quantifying the effect size, but the direction of the effect is strongly moderated by the matching structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of a dyad: one superior and one subordinate (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 8 rounds.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but the marginal return to the superior for each unit of cost not reported as slack is 1 (since every unit of slack reduces superior's payoff by 1). The project revenue is fixed at 240, and the superior receives 220 minus reported costs.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Audit (punishment) costs the superior 20 per audit, regardless of outcome.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. If slack is detected, the superior can impose a fine up to the amount of slack (fine = slack, max). The fine is a deadweight loss (does not benefit the superior).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of rounds (8) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In treatments with reputation, players are shown summaries of counterpart's past actions (cost reports, audit choices, fines). In all treatments, participants receive a summary of all prior rounds at the end of each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In fixed matching, identity is persistent (same partner), so punishment is always attributable. In random matching, identity is not persistent, but reputation information is provided in some treatments.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental setting is not a standard public goods game but a capital budgeting game with asymmetric roles (superior/subordinate) and private information.
- Groups are dyads (2 players), not larger groups as in typical PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as audits with fines, which are deadweight losses and do not benefit the punisher.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the matching protocol (random vs. fixed matching), which may not generalize to all repeated group settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present.
- The experiment uses a fixed number of rounds (8), which may affect endgame behavior.
- The magnitude of fines and audit costs may not match real-world settings.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit generalizability to settings with richer social interaction.
