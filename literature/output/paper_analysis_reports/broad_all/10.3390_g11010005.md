The main dependent variable is the average profit per round, which is directly tied to group efficiency. The paper also reports on behavioral outcomes such as investment choices and feedback provision, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated intertemporal common-pool resource game with five players and ten rounds, the study compares the effects of (1) binding contracts, (2) non-binding promises, (3) private social feedback, and (4) public social feedback, against a no-regulation control. Binding contracts, when unanimously signed, lead to the highest group efficiency (average profit per round close to the cooperative optimum). Non-binding promises increase sustainable investment and efficiency initially, but the effect decays over time. Private feedback (where only the recipient sees ratings) does not improve efficiency over the control. Public feedback (where feedback scores are visible to all) significantly increases sustainable investment and group efficiency, with the effect being more stable over time than promises. The study provides direct evidence that public, but not private, social feedback can substitute for formal sanctions in promoting efficiency in CPR dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated CPR games with continuous contribution choices, public social feedback (where feedback is visible to all) can substantially increase group efficiency, even in the absence of formal punishment or monetary sanctions. Private feedback is ineffective. Binding contracts are most effective but require unanimous agreement. For prediction tasks, the presence of public feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = yes) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, while private feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = no) should not. The absence of monetary punishment or reward mechanisms means that the findings are most relevant for settings where social (non-monetary) sanctions are possible. The game design dimensions (player_count = 5, num_rounds = 10, continuous contributions, no chat, known rounds, full feedback on others' choices and payoffs) are well-specified and should be used as reference points for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions used groups of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each part of the experiment consisted of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or face-to-face communication was allowed; all interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment choices were continuous (0-100 MU per round), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return for Asset B is 5% (0.05), for Asset A is 35% (0.35), but Asset A also generates a negative externality of 2% per unit invested, shared by all.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told in advance that there would be 10 rounds per part.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Subjects saw the history of their own and others' decisions, total investments, accumulated externalities, and payoffs each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the public feedback condition, feedback scores (number of positive minus negative ratings) were displayed next to each player's number and visible to all group members. In the private feedback condition, feedback was only visible to the recipient.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a common-pool resource game, not a standard public goods game; the structure is adjacent but not identical.
- No monetary punishment or reward mechanisms were implemented; only non-monetary social feedback was studied.
- The effect of public feedback may be partly due to status incentives, not just shame aversion, but this is not disentangled.
- The contract condition requires unanimous agreement, which was not always achieved; results for contract condition only apply when the contract is in effect.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with different group sizes, round numbers, or with monetary sanctions.
- The effect of social feedback may depend on cultural or contextual factors not tested here.
