## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the mechanisms often posited by CGS models to stabilize cooperation—such as conformist social learning and a norm psychology that motivates indiscriminate punishment—are not strongly supported by empirical evidence. Instead, individuals tend to use adaptive, context-sensitive strategies, and punishment is typically self-interested, aimed at increasing future cooperation or personal payoff rather than enforcing norms for their own sake. The review also notes that institutions facilitating cooperation often arise endogenously through rational design rather than through stochastic group-level processes. While punishment can stabilize cooperation, its effectiveness and the resulting group efficiency depend on the alignment of individual and group interests, the structure of institutions, and the presence of incentives. The review does not provide new quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants, but it synthesizes theoretical and empirical arguments relevant to the design and interpretation of such experiments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides important conceptual guidance for predicting the effects of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It suggests that the impact of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on whether punishment is self-interested (aligned with individual and group payoffs) or norm-based (potentially maladaptive). The review argues that, in practice, punishment is usually self-interested and embedded in institutional contexts that align individual incentives with group benefit, which tends to support higher efficiency. However, it cautions that models assuming indiscriminate or maladaptive punishment may not reflect real human behavior. For prediction tasks, this implies that game designs with punishment mechanisms are more likely to increase efficiency when they include features that align individual and group interests (e.g., rewards for punishers, reputation systems, endogenous institution formation), and less likely to do so when punishment is costly, norm-based, and not incentivized. The paper does not provide direct empirical estimates but offers a framework for interpreting when and why punishment may or may not improve efficiency.

## Important Limitations
