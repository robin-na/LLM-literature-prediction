The main outcomes are the maximum sustainable cooperation (which maps to efficiency) and social welfare. Behavioral outcomes like punishment or ostracism are discussed as mechanisms, not as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the optimal way to sustain cooperation in repeated public-goods-like games depends on the effectiveness of the punishment technology. If specialized enforcers can punish at low cost and high impact, then pure enforcer punishment (with immediate return to cooperation) maximizes efficiency. If specialized enforcement is less effective, then combining enforcer punishment with community enforcement (e.g., temporary withdrawal of cooperation or ostracism) is optimal. The paper provides explicit characterizations of the maximum sustainable cooperation (efficiency) in each regime, and shows that the presence of effective specialized enforcement can increase group welfare compared to pure community enforcement. However, if the punishment technology is very weak, community enforcement alone is nearly optimal. The results generalize to both small and large groups, and to settings with imperfect monitoring.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of introducing punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on the effectiveness of the punishment technology and other game design parameters. It shows that, when punishment is highly effective (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the harm inflicted), enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency by supporting higher cooperation. However, if punishment is costly or ineffective, the efficiency gain is limited, and community enforcement may be nearly as good. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency as a function of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and discount factor. The results also highlight that the structure of punishment (one-time vs. repeated, enforcer vs. peer) and the possibility of ostracism or repentance are important moderators.

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is modeled explicitly: enforcer pays y_i to inflict g(y_i) on the target. The effectiveness of punishment (cost/impact) is a key moderator.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment technology (g function) determines how much harm is inflicted per unit cost. Higher effectiveness (steeper g) makes specialized enforcement more beneficial.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Infinite horizon with known structure; players know the game continues with discount factor δ.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Perfect monitoring (all actions observed) and private monitoring (only own and partners' matches) are both analyzed. Information structure is a key moderator.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the model, punishment is perfectly observed and attributed to enforcers. In some variants, anonymity is discussed, but enforcer identity is generally known at the punishment stage.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model assumes rational, forward-looking agents and equilibrium play, which may not capture all real-world behavioral factors.
- The public goods environment is stylized and may not capture all institutional or psychological complexities of real-world cooperation.
- Punishment is modeled as a continuous, costly action with a specific technology function, which may not match all experimental or field settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are considered.
- Communication, reputation outside the modeled group, and other social factors are not included.
- The model assumes either perfect or well-specified private monitoring; real-world information structures may be more complex.
