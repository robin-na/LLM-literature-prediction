# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The endogenous formation of common pool resource coalitions
Authors: Chávez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., Quezada, F. J., & Stranlund, J. K.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual earnings
- group earnings
- total time spent harvesting (resource exploitation)
- insider and outsider earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- coalition formation frequency
- coalition size
- probability of investing in monitoring
- probability of poaching
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are individual and group earnings (payoff), as well as resource exploitation (total harvest time). Behavioral outcomes such as coalition formation, monitoring, and poaching are also reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that endogenous coalition formation, combined with the ability to monitor and sanction outsider poaching (via government-imposed fines), leads to larger coalitions, reduced resource exploitation, and higher individual and group earnings compared to open access. These effects are strongest when monitoring costs are low, enabling the grand coalition to form and fully deter poaching. When monitoring is not possible or is too costly, only small coalitions form and efficiency gains are minimal. Among fishers, coalition formation and monitoring are frequent and effective, while among students, coalitions are smaller, monitoring is rare, and efficiency gains are weak or absent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that enabling punishment (here, government-imposed fines for poaching, triggered by coalition monitoring) increases efficiency in a CPR game, especially when monitoring costs are low. The effect is mediated by coalition size and the ability to deter outsiders. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the cost and effectiveness of monitoring and punishment, the ability to form coalitions, and the baseline efficiency under open access. The results suggest that, in environments where monitoring and punishment are feasible and not too costly, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency relative to control. However, if monitoring is impossible or prohibitively expensive, the efficiency gains from punishment are minimal. The findings are most applicable to real-world user groups with experience in collective management.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experimental groups had 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 15 independent rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication among subjects was never allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvest) choices were discrete: all, half, or none of time endowment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost to coalition for monitoring (enabling punishment) is varied across treatments (e.g., 1,100, 2,200, 2,400 pesos).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by monitoring probability (p=0.5 or 1.0) and fine size; treatments vary these parameters.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the session consisted of 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Aggregate time spent harvesting for the group was announced at the end of each round; payoff tables were visible.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game but a CPR/coalition formation game with endogenous group formation and government-imposed sanctions.
- Punishment is only possible via group monitoring and government fines; there is no peer-to-peer punishment.
- The results may not generalize to environments without real-world experience in collective management (e.g., student samples showed much weaker effects).
- Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is not defined as in standard PGGs, limiting direct comparability.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward effects.
- The effect of punishment is confounded with coalition formation and monitoring decisions.
