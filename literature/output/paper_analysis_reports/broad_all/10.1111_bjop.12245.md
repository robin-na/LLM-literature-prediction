# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Perceived warmth and competence of others shape voluntary deceptive behaviour in a morally relevant setting
Authors: Azevedo, R. T., Panasiti, M. S., Maglio, R., & Aglioti, S. M.
Journal: British Journal of Psychology
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of lying (self-gain lies, altruistic lies)
- subjective ratings (guilt, identification, apprehension, competitiveness)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the binary decision to lie or tell the truth, further categorized as self-gain (egoistic) or other-gain (altruistic) lies. No direct analysis of group or individual efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The game is zero-sum between two players, and the focus is on the psychological and social determinants of lying behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the perceived warmth and competence of the target modulate the likelihood of lying for self-gain: participants lie less to high-warmth individuals and more to high-competence individuals. The risk of disclosure (reputation risk) reduces lying, particularly toward low-warmth/low-competence targets. These effects are interpreted as reflecting malleable personal moral standards and social evaluation processes. However, the study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the game is not a public goods game or direct variant. The outcomes are behavioral (lying frequency) rather than payoff-based.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how social context, reputation risk, and target characteristics can modulate dishonest behavior in a zero-sum, two-player setting. It does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The manipulation of reputation risk is conceptually adjacent to punishment/sanction mechanisms, but there is no explicit punishment, and the outcome is not efficiency. Thus, the paper is of limited direct use for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, but it may inform understanding of how social evaluation and disclosure risk affect norm-violating behavior.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always played between two players: participant and one opponent per block.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 240 trials (20 trials per block, 12 blocks).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The decision is binary: lie or tell the truth (all-or-nothing choice per trial).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of blocks and trials per block; the structure is explicit.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or a direct variant; the game is a two-player, zero-sum deception task.
- No explicit punishment or sanction mechanism is present; the 'reputation risk' manipulation is only conceptually adjacent to punishment.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed; all outcomes are behavioral (lying frequency) or subjective (guilt, identification).
- The findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods contexts with explicit punishment or reward mechanisms.
- The game structure (one player has all power, the other is passive) differs fundamentally from standard public goods or cooperation games.
