# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Co-enforcement of Common Pool Resources to Deter Encroachment: Evidence from a Field Experiment in Chile
Authors: Chávez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., & Stranlund, J. K.
Journal: Environmental & Resource Economics
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- insider cumulative earnings
- insider cumulative harvests
- outsider net earnings from poaching
- duration of resource (terminal period)
- monitoring costs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- insider monitoring investments
- outsider poaching levels
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cumulative earnings and harvests for insiders (resource users), net earnings from poaching for outsiders, and the number of rounds the resource lasts. Monitoring investments and poaching levels are also analyzed, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The field experiment with Chilean fishers shows that co-enforcement (insider monitoring plus government sanctions) reduces poaching and increases insiders' harvests and earnings relative to a no-enforcement baseline. However, the costs of monitoring largely offset these gains, so net efficiency improvements are modest. Voting on monitoring investments and higher fines both help insiders coordinate to invest enough in monitoring to eliminate average poaching gains, but poaching persists and is not highly sensitive to the level of monitoring or fines. The results suggest that while co-enforcement can improve outcomes, the ongoing cost of monitoring is a major constraint on efficiency gains.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that introducing punishment (in the form of exogenous government sanctions for poaching, combined with group monitoring) increases group efficiency/payoff relative to a no-enforcement baseline, but the net gains are limited by the costliness of monitoring. The design is not a standard public goods game but is a dynamic common pool resource game with poaching by outsiders. The findings suggest that, in similar CPR settings, enabling punishment/sanctions will likely increase efficiency, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the cost structure of monitoring and the ability of insiders to coordinate. Voting and higher fines can help achieve deterrence, but unless monitoring costs are low, efficiency gains may be small. For prediction, the control (no-punishment) efficiency is lower than with punishment enabled, but the treatment effect is muted by monitoring costs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 6 players (3 insiders, 3 outsiders) per session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each part lasted a maximum of 15 periods, but could end earlier if the resource was depleted. Each session had two parts.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Insiders (resource users) could communicate face-to-face for up to 3 minutes before each round; outsiders could not communicate.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Harvest and monitoring contributions are made in discrete units (harvest up to 6 units per period; monitoring in 100-peso increments).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Insiders pay for monitoring (cost per unit of monitoring probability); punishment (sanction) is exogenous and costless to insiders, but monitoring is costly. Higher fines (punishment effectiveness) reduce the monitoring cost needed for deterrence.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the fine per unit poached (650 or 1300 pesos). Higher fines make deterrence cheaper and more likely.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the maximum number of rounds (15), but the game could end earlier if the resource was depleted.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, aggregate harvest and monitoring decisions were publicly announced; individual decisions were private.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a dynamic common pool resource game with poaching by outsiders, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to standard PGG settings.
- Punishment is exogenous (government-imposed) and not peer-administered; monitoring is costly and must be paid by insiders, which differs from typical PGG punishment designs.
- Efficiency gains from punishment are limited by the ongoing cost of monitoring, which may not be present in other punishment settings.
- Poaching is not fully eliminated even when average poaching gains are negative, suggesting behavioral inertia or other unmodeled factors.
- The sample is artisanal fishers in Chile; external validity to other populations or contexts may be limited.
