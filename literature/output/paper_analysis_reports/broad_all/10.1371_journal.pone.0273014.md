# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Long-lasting effects of incentives and social preference: A public goods experiment
Authors: Nakagawa, M., Lefebvre, M., & Stenger, A.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- individual contributions
- proportion of zero/full contributors
- heterogeneity by social preference type
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the contribution (tokens contributed to the public good). No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare is reported. All results are in terms of contribution rates and their persistence.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that all three incentive mechanisms (monetary punishment, non-monetary reward, recommendation) significantly increase contributions to the public good during the policy phase. Monetary punishment is most effective in raising contributions, but this effect is not persistent: contributions drop sharply after the punishment phase ends. Non-monetary reward and recommendation also increase contributions, with some evidence of longer-lasting effects, especially for recommendation among certain types (e.g., free-riders). However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The study also finds that the effect of incentives varies by social preference type: monetary punishment increases contributions across all types, while non-monetary reward and recommendation mainly affect strong conditional cooperators.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling monetary punishment in a repeated linear PGG increases contribution rates during the intervention, but the effect is not persistent after removal. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency outcomes is not possible. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified and match standard PGG lab experiments. The findings support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) during the intervention, but the lack of payoff/efficiency data means the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred. The results are most useful for predicting contribution rate changes, not efficiency per se.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 subjects in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods per session: 5 pre-policy, 10 policy, 15 post-policy.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject chooses how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token contributed yields 0.4 tokens to each of 4 group members).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 0.25 tokens per punishment point assigned; each point reduces target's income by 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1 token lost per 0.25 token spent (4:1 impact/cost ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Non-monetary reward condition: subjects can assign approval points (0-10) to others, no monetary effect.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward points are costless to assign and have no monetary effect.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward points have no direct payoff impact; purely symbolic/approval.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the experiment consists of 30 periods; end is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed of the contribution levels of other group members (in ascending order, anonymized).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward points are assigned anonymously; subjects cannot identify who assigned points.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; no direct analysis of efficiency or total group earnings is reported.
- No direct control group with punishment disabled in the same session; pre-policy phase serves as baseline.
- No communication or chat between players, which may limit external validity for real-world settings.
- The experiment does not allow for long-term investments or carry-over of tokens, which may affect the persistence of incentive effects.
- Sample size per treatment is moderate (n=40), and post-hoc power analysis suggests some tests may be underpowered.
- Findings on persistence of effects are based on contribution rates, not on efficiency or cost-effectiveness.
