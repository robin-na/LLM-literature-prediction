# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Asymmetric obligations
Authors: Riedel, N., & Schildberg-Hörisch, H.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- pairwise contribution levels
- probability of undercutting obligation
- fraction contributing less than threshold
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels and compliance with minimum obligations. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported, though the design allows for such calculation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that both high and low minimum contribution obligations (even when only weakly enforced) increase contributions in the initial periods compared to no obligation, but this effect is not sustained in the long run. Asymmetric obligations are as effective as symmetric ones: for a given own obligation, the partner's obligation does not affect behavior. The effect of obligations is interpreted as arising from an emotional cost of disobeying, not from expectations about others' behavior. No direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that weakly enforced minimum contribution obligations can temporarily increase contributions in public goods games, regardless of whether obligations are symmetric or asymmetric. However, since the study does not include a standard punishment institution (where players can assign costly punishment to others), and the 'punishment' here is a very weak, probabilistic, externally imposed fine for not meeting a minimum, the results are only adjacent to the prediction task. The findings suggest that expressive law (weak obligations) can have a short-term effect on cooperation, but do not inform about the efficiency effects of endogenous peer punishment. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of a standard punishment treatment limits direct applicability to the prediction of efficiency under peer punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in fixed pairs (groups of 2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants is explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: any integer between 0 and 100.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.6 (multiplier 1.2, divided by 2 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is externally imposed: if monitored (probability 1/12), a fine of 1.2 per unit below the minimum is deducted. This is not peer punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is 1.2 ECU per unit below minimum, only if monitored (probability 1/12). Not peer punishment; effectiveness is very low.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. If monitored and contribution exceeds minimum, a reward of 1.2 per unit above minimum is given.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is externally imposed, not chosen by players; cost is 1.2 per unit above minimum, paid by experimenter.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is 1.2 per unit above minimum, only if monitored (probability 1/12).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants see both their own and the other player's contributions, whether anyone was controlled, and payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include a standard peer punishment treatment; punishment is externally imposed, weak, and probabilistic.
- No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported; only contribution levels and compliance are analyzed.
- The findings on expressive law and minimum obligations may not generalize to settings with endogenous, costly peer punishment.
- The effect of obligations is only temporary; the increase in contributions fades over time.
- The experiment uses only two-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
