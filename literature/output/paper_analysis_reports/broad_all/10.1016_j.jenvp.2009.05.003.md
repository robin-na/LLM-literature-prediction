# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Public commitment making as a structural solution in social dilemmas
Authors: Lokhorst, A. M., van Dijk, E., & Staats, H.
Journal: Journal of Environmental Psychology
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- willingness to install a public commitment system
- self-reported motive to maximize collective outcomes
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the willingness to invest in a system of public commitments (a structural solution), not actual contributions, efficiency, or group payoff. The motive to maximize collective outcomes is measured as a self-report, not as an actual payoff or efficiency outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both dispositional trust and situational expectations interact to predict willingness to invest in a public commitment system in a public good dilemma. High trusters are more willing to install a commitment system when situational expectations are low, while low trusters are more willing when situational expectations are high. The motive to maximize collective outcomes partially mediates this effect. However, the study does not measure actual contributions, group payoff, or efficiency, and thus does not provide direct evidence on the effect of commitment systems (or punishment) on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is relevant for understanding when participants are likely to support or invest in structural solutions (like public commitment systems), but does not report on actual efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment or sanctions. The findings may be useful for understanding the adoption of commitment or sanctioning systems, but not for predicting their efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 2 uses groups of 5 participants in the lab experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment appears to be a one-shot (single round) public good dilemma.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each chip contributed is doubled and distributed among all group members (MPCR = 2/5 = 0.4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the structure of the game; it is a one-shot game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Situational expectations are manipulated by telling participants about others' average contributions in previous studies.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not implement or measure punishment, sanctions, or reward systems; it focuses on willingness to install a public commitment system.
- No actual efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes are measured or reported.
- The main outcome is willingness to invest in a commitment system, not actual behavioral change or efficiency.
- Findings may not generalize to real-world settings where dispositional trust and situational expectations are confounded.
- The study does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
