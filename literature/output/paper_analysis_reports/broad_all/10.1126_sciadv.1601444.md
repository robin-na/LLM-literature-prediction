# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Onymity promotes cooperation in social dilemma experiments
Authors: Wang, Z., Jusup, M., Wang, R. W., Shi, L., Iwasa, Y., Moreno, Y., & Kurths, J.
Journal: Science Advances
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- median payoff per round
- correlation between strategy use and payoff per round
- final score (converted to real currency)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- frequency of punishment
- conditional strategies (responses to C, D, P)
- participant ranking
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is payoff per round, with extensive analysis of how strategy use (cooperation, defection, punishment) relates to payoffs. Behavioral outcomes (cooperation, defection, punishment frequencies) are also reported, but payoff is the primary focus for treatment comparisons.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma with three available actions (cooperate, defect, punish), the use of costly punishment is negatively correlated with individual payoffs in both anonymous and onymous conditions. Onymity (making opponents identifiable) increases cooperation and median payoffs, but punishment does not promote efficiency or cooperation. Instead, those who punish more tend to earn less ('winners don't punish' effect). Control trials without punishment show similar qualitative effects of onymity, suggesting that punishment is not necessary for increased cooperation or efficiency. The paper provides direct evidence that punishment, as implemented here, does not improve group efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma games with costly punishment, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency or group payoff. In fact, punishment is associated with lower payoffs for those who use it. The findings suggest that, all else equal, adding a costly punishment option to a similar game design is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, discrete-choice social dilemmas with costly punishment, and should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to multi-player public goods games. However, the negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust in this setting.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are pairwise (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pairwise interaction is repeated for an uncertain number of rounds, with a 75% probability of continuation after each round. The expected number of rounds per interaction is not fixed but is stochastic.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is allowed; only onymity (name revealed) is manipulated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: cooperate, defect, or punish (no continuous contribution).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the cooperation action, a player pays 1 unit for the opponent to receive 2 units. This is not a standard PGG MPCR, but the marginal return to the other is 2 per 1 paid.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs the punisher 1 unit and reduces the opponent's payoff by 4 units per punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4 units lost by the target per 1 unit spent by the punisher.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is discussed in the introduction as a possible mechanism, but not implemented in the experiment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds; continuation probability is 75% per round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants can see the result of the preceding round and their total payoff. No mention of seeing the other player's full history or summary.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the onymous treatment, players know the identity (name) of their opponent, so punishment is attributable. In the anonymous treatment, it is not.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a pairwise repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a multi-player public goods game; generalization to larger groups or standard PGGs may be limited.
- Punishment is implemented as a discrete action with fixed cost and impact; other punishment forms or parameterizations may yield different results.
- The sample consists of Chinese undergraduate students who are classmates, which may affect generalizability to other populations or levels of onymity.
- The number of rounds per interaction is stochastic and not known in advance, which may affect strategic behavior.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
