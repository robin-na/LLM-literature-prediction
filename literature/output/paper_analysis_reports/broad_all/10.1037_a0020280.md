# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Predicting Behavior in Economic Games by Looking Through the Eyes of the Players
Authors: Mellers, B. A., Haselhuhn, M. P., Tetlock, P. E., Silva, J. C., & Isen, A. M.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Psychology-general
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- monetary offers in ultimatum and dictator games
- rejection rates in ultimatum game
- exit behavior in dictator game with exit option
- anticipated pleasure and fairness ratings
- effect of positive mood induction on offers
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes such as offers made, rejections, and exit choices, as well as psychological constructs (want/ought selves, anticipated pleasure, fairness). It does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare in a public goods or repeated game context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main findings are: (1) Players' self-defined want and ought motives predict their offers in ultimatum and dictator games; (2) in the ultimatum game, more players act according to what they believe is fair (ought), while in the dictator game, want and ought are equally influential; (3) positive mood induction increases the likelihood of making equal offers in the dictator game; (4) when given an exit option, players experiencing greater conflict between want and ought are more likely to exit. The paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment in public goods or repeated games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment institutions, marginal per-capita return, or group efficiency. Its findings on rejections in the ultimatum game and exit behavior in the dictator game are only loosely related to the prediction task. The main value is in highlighting the psychological mechanisms (want/ought selves) that may underlie cooperation and sanctioning, but it does not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games were played in sessions with at least 8 participants for anonymity, but the actual game structure is two-player (ultimatum and dictator games).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played each game only once (one-shot games).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; anonymity was emphasized.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/offer choices were discrete (even-dollar amounts from $0 to $10), not strictly all-or-nothing, but not continuous.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was one-shot; instructions made clear the structure.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use public goods games or repeated games; it focuses on ultimatum and dictator games.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning institution is present; rejections in the ultimatum game are not parametrically varied as punishment.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual offers, rejections, and exit behavior are analyzed.
- No manipulation or analysis of key game design dimensions relevant to public goods games (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost, group size).
- Findings are based on one-shot, two-player games with university students, limiting generalizability to other settings.
