# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Voluntary play increases cooperation in the presence of punishment: a lab in the field experiment
Authors: Pancotto, F., Righi, S., & Takács, K.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment behavior
- participation/opt-out (loner) choice
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution level (i.e., how much is contributed to the public good), not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. Punishment behavior and participation/opt-out are also analyzed. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, though these could be inferred from contribution data.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that punishment increases contributions in public goods games, both when participation is compulsory and when it is optional. The effect is strongest when participation is compulsory and punishment is available. Optional participation (opt-out) alone does not significantly increase contributions compared to compulsory participation. Loners (those who opt out) contribute less when forced to participate, but are not responsible for anti-social punishment. Instead, low contributors (free-riders) are the main source of anti-social punishment. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the contribution data suggest that punishment increases group welfare, especially in compulsory settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in discrete public goods games with 4 players, 1:3 punishment technology, and no communication, enabling punishment increases contributions, especially when participation is compulsory. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions strongly suggests that efficiency would also increase with punishment, particularly in compulsory games. The design dimensions are well specified, allowing for mapping to prediction tasks, but the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff data means that predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes. The results are most informative for settings with similar group size, discrete contributions, and punishment technology.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per session; all games played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Number of rounds is not explicitly stated in the main text; design involves multiple stages but the exact number of rounds per stage is not specified.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; oral communication was forbidden during sessions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: 0, 10, 20, 30, or 40 tokens (not all-or-nothing, but not fully continuous).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (the sum of contributions is doubled and split among 4 players). In optional games, MPCR per participant decreases as the number of participants decreases.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 4 or 8 tokens to reduce the target's payoff by 12 or 24 tokens, respectively (1:3 punishment technology).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment technology is 1:3 (each token spent reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants observe the contribution levels of other group members, but not the identity of contributors.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is assigned based on contribution level, not on identity; participants do not know who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution level, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency must be inferred.
- Number of rounds per stage is not explicitly reported.
- No direct analysis of group welfare, surplus, or total earnings.
- Sample is not fully representative of the general population (over-represents females, students, and younger individuals).
- No communication or reputation mechanisms are present; results may not generalize to settings with these features.
- Punishment is implemented via the strategy method and is not targeted at specific individuals, only at contribution levels.
