# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment as a Means of Competition: Implications for Strong Reciprocity Theory
Authors: Paál, T., & Bereczkei, T.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution per round
- amount of punishment imposed and received
- relationship between contributions, profits, and punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution rate. The paper does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare directly. It does analyze the relationship between profit and punishment, but does not provide group-level payoff or efficiency statistics.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a 10-round, 4-player PGG with a competitive, rank-based payoff and costly punishment enabled from round 6 onward, average contributions declined steadily and did not increase after punishment was introduced. Punishment was not targeted at low contributors but at those with higher accumulated profits, indicating strategic rather than norm-enforcing use. There was no association between contributions and punishment received or imposed. The results suggest that in competitive environments, punishment can become a tool for rivalry rather than for sustaining cooperation, and does not reverse the decline in cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that in a PGG with a competitive, tournament-style payoff structure and full information about others' profits, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency or group payoff. Instead, punishment is used for strategic competition, and cooperation continues to decline. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent: in competitive, rank-based settings, punishment may not improve and may even undermine group efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so this inference is based on contribution behavior and the described use of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players, fixed throughout the game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; players were in separate rooms and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: players could choose any amount from 0 to 1000 points per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round: contributions to the public good are doubled and divided equally among 4 players. MPCR = (2/4) = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 25% of the amount imposed (e.g., to punish 100 points, punisher pays 25 points).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (for each point spent, 4 points are deducted from the target's account, since 25% cost).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment (reduction) was available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were informed of the current round and total number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw others' contributions and total profits at the end of each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare directly; all inferences about efficiency are based on contribution rates and punishment behavior.
- The competitive, tournament-style payoff structure is not standard for PGGs, limiting generalizability to more typical public goods environments.
- Sample size is moderate (80 participants, 20 groups), which may limit statistical power for some analyses.
- The findings may not generalize to settings where punishment is not anonymous or where communication is allowed.
- The study only examines one set of parameter values (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, group size), so effects of varying these are not tested.
