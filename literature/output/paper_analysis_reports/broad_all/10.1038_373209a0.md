## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical models show that punishment can be stable and effective in animal societies, especially when dominants can punish subordinates at low cost and with high impact. Punishment can enforce cooperation, deter cheating, and maintain social order. The models identify conditions under which punishment is likely to evolve, such as strong asymmetries in power or fighting ability. Empirical examples illustrate that punishment is widespread in animal societies and can modify behavior, but the paper does not report quantitative effects on group efficiency or welfare. The findings suggest that punishment can increase the fitness of punishers by reducing the frequency of damaging actions by others, but do not directly address group-level efficiency as in PGGs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual and theoretical support for the idea that punishment can enforce cooperation and deter selfishness, which is relevant to predicting the effects of punishment in PGG-like games. However, the models are dyadic, focus on fitness rather than explicit group payoff, and do not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency changes. The paper suggests that punishment is more likely to be effective when the cost to the punisher is low and the impact on the punished is high, and when there are clear asymmetries in power. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation and (by analogy) efficiency, but does not provide direct empirical or quantitative evidence for use in predictive modeling.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Models are dyadic (two players: dominant and subordinate), not group-based as in standard PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Models are repeated games with N rounds; learning and behavior change occur over multiple rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Choices are typically binary: transgress/desist or cooperate/non-cooperate.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher (d1) is a key parameter; models analyze when punishment is stable based on cost-benefit tradeoffs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost (damage to punished, d2) is a key parameter; effectiveness of punishment is central to model results.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Number of rounds (N) is a parameter in the models; assumed known to players.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is always dyadic and the identity of the punisher is known by construction.

## Important Limitations
- Theoretical models are dyadic (two players), not group-based as in public goods games.
- Outcomes are framed in terms of evolutionary fitness, not explicit group efficiency or welfare.
- No quantitative or experimental data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare effects of punishment.
- Empirical evidence is qualitative and descriptive, not quantitative or experimental.
- No analysis of group size, marginal per-capita return, or other key PGG parameters.
