# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation, punishment, and group change in multilevel public goods experiments
Authors: Otten, K., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., Cherki, B., & Israel, S.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total contributions
- local contributions
- global contributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned
- punishment received
- normative expectations
- personal normative views
- empirical expectations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels (local, global, total) and punishment behavior. Efficiency (as group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported, but can be inferred from contribution data and the payoff structure. Norms and expectations are also measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that peer punishment increases cooperation in standard (single-group) public goods games, sustaining high contribution levels and preventing decay. In multilevel public goods games (where participants can contribute to private, local, or global accounts), punishment only increases total and global contributions if participants previously played a single-group PGG. If the multilevel PGG is played first, punishment does not significantly increase cooperation or efficiency. The effect of punishment is thus moderated by the order in which group structures are experienced. Normative expectations and punishment patterns suggest that prior experience with single-group PGGs helps groups coordinate on efficient (global) cooperation norms in multilevel settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the game (single-group vs. multilevel) and the order in which participants experience these structures. For prediction, the presence of a multilevel structure and whether participants have prior experience with single-group PGGs are key moderators. In standard PGGs, punishment reliably increases efficiency. In multilevel PGGs, punishment only increases efficiency if participants have previously played a single-group PGG. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are clearly specified, and the absence of communication and anonymity of punishment are also relevant. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency in single-group PGGs and in multilevel PGGs only if there is prior single-group experience.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each part consists of 10 rounds (with some uncertainty for participants, but always 10 rounds per part in the experiment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-20 MU per account), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Private account: 1.0; Local account: 0.7; Global account: 0.5. (Marginal per-capita return for local: 0.7; for global: 0.5.)
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 MU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's payoff by 3 MU (punishment effectiveness 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of rounds is between 8 and 12, but in practice always 10 rounds per part. The exact number is not known in advance to reduce endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see how much each group member contributed to the local and global accounts.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants see how many punishment points they received, but not by whom.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio of group payoff to maximum possible, but can be inferred from contribution data and payoff structure.
- The experiment uses only 4-player groups and a specific multilevel structure (2 local groups of 2 within a global group of 4); generalizability to other group sizes or structures is untested.
- No communication is allowed, which may limit external validity for real-world settings where discussion is possible.
- Punishment is always anonymous; effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
- No reward mechanism is present, so interaction between punishment and reward is not addressed.
- Order effects (single-group before multilevel vs. vice versa) are central; other forms of experience or repeated play are not tested.
- Norm elicitation may have influenced behavior by making norms more salient.
- Participants are university students in the Netherlands, which may limit generalizability.
