# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Evolution of trust in a hierarchical population with punishing investors
Authors: Sun, K. T., Liu, Y., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A.
Journal: Chaos Solitons & Fractals
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust (first-mover choice to trust)
- trustworthiness (second-mover choice to reciprocate)
- voting for/against punishment mechanism
- cognitive ability and voting behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust and trustworthiness (i.e., behavioral choices in a binary trust game), and voting for the punishment institution. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes, though the theoretical model discusses expected payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that exogenously imposed collective punishment in a binary trust game significantly increases trustworthiness (from 38.5% to 82.3%) and somewhat increases trust (from 28.1% to 39.6%). When the adoption of the punishment mechanism is determined by majority vote, most subjects vote against it, and the endogenous adoption does not further increase trust or trustworthiness compared to exogenous imposition. Cognitive ability predicts voting for punishment. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff outcomes, and the main focus is on behavioral measures (trust, trustworthiness, voting).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that collective punishment can increase trustworthiness and trust in a binary trust game, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it is a binary trust game rather than a multi-player PGG. The punishment mechanism is collective and endogenous in some treatments, but the main outcomes are behavioral. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides indirect evidence that punishment can improve cooperative behavior, but does not allow direct inference about efficiency effects. The absence of efficiency or payoff data limits its direct utility for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 6 subjects; each subject plays with one other group member per treatment (one-shot, random matching within group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment is a one-shot game; three treatments per subject (Baseline, Exogenous, Voting), no repeated rounds within a treatment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Binary trust game: first mover chooses to trust or not (all-or-nothing); second mover chooses to reciprocate or cheat (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a penalty (social sanction) imposed on cheaters; the cost is not paid by individuals but is a reduction in the cheater's payoff proportional to the number of trustworthy others (parameter z). No explicit cost to punishers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized by z (severity of sanction per trustworthy other); the penalty to cheaters is z*q, where q is the fraction of trustworthy others.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know each treatment is a one-shot game; the number of games/treatments is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the main treatments, no feedback is given until the end of the session. In the Voting-IF treatment, subjects are shown aggregate behavior (number of trustful/trustworthy choices) before voting.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a binary trust game, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to PGG environments.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (trust, trustworthiness, voting) are measured.
- Punishment is collective and endogenous in some treatments, but the cost structure differs from standard PGG punishment (no explicit cost to punishers).
- One-shot games only; no repeated interaction or learning.
- No communication allowed; effects of chat or discussion are not tested.
- Small group size (6); not varied across treatments.
- Limited external validity due to lab setting and student sample.
