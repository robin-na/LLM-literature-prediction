# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Acute tryptophan depletion in healthy subjects increases preferences for negative reciprocity
Authors: Bengart, P., Gruendler, T., & Vogt, B.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy choice (Hawk or Dove)
- negative reciprocity (Hawk in response to expected Hawk)
- positive reciprocity (Dove in response to expected Dove)
- beliefs about opponent's action
- risk attitude
- mood
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: strategy choice (Hawk/Dove), negative reciprocity, and beliefs. No group or individual efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed as primary outcomes. Payoff is discussed in the context of the game's structure, but not as an outcome variable.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that lowering serotonin via ATD increases negative reciprocity in the Hawk-Dove game: participants in the ATD group are more likely to choose Hawk in response to an expected Hawk, compared to placebo. This effect is specific to negative reciprocity and does not extend to positive reciprocity or beliefs about the opponent's action. The incentive for mutual cooperation (payoff for mutual Dove) reduces Hawk choices. Risk attitude and mood are unaffected by ATD and do not predict strategy choice. The study does not analyze or report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that biological factors (serotonin availability) can modulate negative reciprocity in a competitive, dyadic game, but it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment mechanisms or sanctions in public goods or similar games. The Hawk-Dove game is structurally adjacent to public goods and punishment games, but lacks explicit punishment or sanctioning stages, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Therefore, the findings are only weakly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The main relevance is in showing that negative reciprocity (costly, non-payoff-maximizing responses to perceived unkindness) can be increased by serotonin depletion, but the translation to efficiency or group payoff in multi-player, repeated, or sanction-enabled games is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always played with 2 players (dyadic Hawk-Dove game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays 12 one-shot versions of the Hawk-Dove game, each with a different mutual cooperation incentive. No repeated interaction with feedback; each version is independent and only one is payoff-relevant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/choice is all-or-nothing: each player chooses either Hawk or Dove (binary choice).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know they will play 12 versions of the game, each with a different incentive, and that only one is payoff-relevant.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Hawk-Dove game, which is structurally different from public goods games and does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed; only behavioral outcomes (negative reciprocity, strategy choice) are measured.
- Sample is limited to healthy young males; results may not generalize to females or broader populations.
- The experiment is between-subjects and may be affected by unobserved individual differences.
- The findings on negative reciprocity may not generalize to less competitive or more cooperative environments.
- The study does not distinguish between different motives for reciprocal behavior (e.g., inequity aversion vs. harm aversion).
