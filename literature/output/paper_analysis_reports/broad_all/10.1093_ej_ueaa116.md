Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variables are effort levels (interpreted as public good contributions), but the central outcomes of interest are group welfare, total surplus, and the distribution of payoffs (inequality) between elites and non-elites. The paper explicitly analyzes how punishment regimes affect these payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model assumes perfect monitoring of effort and infinite horizon, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- Punishment is administered by a central authority, not by peers, which differs from many lab public goods games.
- The model does not include positive rewards beyond the carrot of future cooperation.
- The model focuses on elite vs. non-elite distinctions, which may not map directly onto typical experimental designs.
- The cost to the punisher is not explicitly modeled; only the disutility to the punished is parameterized.
- The model assumes a continuum of agents or large populations, not small groups.
