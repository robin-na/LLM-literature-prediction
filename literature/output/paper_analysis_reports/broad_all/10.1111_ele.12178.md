Outcome notes:
The model's main outcomes are group-level carrying capacity, population size, and the stability of cooperation, all of which are direct proxies for efficiency or welfare. Behavioral outcomes (e.g., frequency of cooperators/defectors) are also tracked, but the focus is on population-level payoffs and the ability of institutions to support large-scale cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the co-evolution of institutions with sanctioning (punishment) and demography enables the stable emergence and maintenance of large-scale cooperation, resulting in a dramatic increase in group carrying capacity and efficiency. In the absence of institutionalized punishment, cooperation cannot be stably maintained, and group size/efficiency remains low. The model demonstrates that the optimal institution invests most, but not all, of the public good into technological improvement (increasing carrying capacity), while retaining enough for sanctioning to deter defectors. Sensitivity analyses show that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to changes in key parameters, though it is diminished by high migration rates or low benefits of cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutionalized punishment in public-goods-like environments will increase group efficiency, especially in structured populations. The model directly links the presence and effectiveness of punishment to higher group payoffs and larger sustainable group sizes. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, introducing or strengthening punishment mechanisms should increase efficiency, provided the cost of punishment is not prohibitive and the benefit from cooperation is sufficient. The model also highlights the importance of population structure (limited migration) and the allocation of resources between sanctioning and productive investment. While the model is not an empirical experiment, its detailed mapping to public goods game structure and explicit focus on efficiency make it highly informative for predicting the impact of punishment on efficiency outcomes.

Group (patch) size is a key variable; initial patch size (carrying capacity) is manipulated, and the model tracks how institutions allow group size to increase. Larger groups are only sustainable with institutionalized punishment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The benefit of cooperation (B) is directly manipulated; higher B increases the range of group sizes where cooperation and efficiency can be sustained.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The cost of institution formation (I) is manipulated; higher I reduces the range of group sizes where cooperation can invade and be sustained.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The effectiveness of sanctioning is varied in model extensions (Appendix S4); higher effectiveness supports cooperation and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested; results are based on simulations and mathematical analysis.
- The model abstracts away from micro-level behavioral mechanisms, such as explicit monitoring, communication, or individual-level punishment decisions.
- No explicit time horizon or round structure; the evolutionary framework may not map directly onto short-term experimental public goods games.
- No reward mechanisms are considered; only punishment/sanctioning is modeled.
- Assumes vertical cultural transmission and specific forms of population structure, which may not generalize to all real-world settings.
- Parameter values (e.g., cost of institution, benefit of cooperation) are chosen for model tractability and may not correspond to empirical values.
