Non-payoff outcomes such as cooperation frequency and strategy prevalence are also reported, but are interpreted in terms of their impact on group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that prior commitment strategies, where players agree in advance to cooperate and specify compensation for defection, can achieve higher levels of cooperation and average payoff than traditional costly punishment, provided the arrangement cost is not too high. Sharing the cost of commitment further increases the viability and efficiency of these strategies. The efficiency gains are robust across a range of compensation and arrangement cost parameters, and commitment-based cooperation is less susceptible to antisocial punishment. The findings are derived from analytical risk-dominance conditions and evolutionary simulations, focusing on the stationary distribution of strategies and average payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that mechanisms analogous to prior commitment with compensation (distinct from standard punishment) can yield higher efficiency than costly punishment, especially when the cost of arranging the agreement is low or shared. While the model is based on the one-shot PD rather than a public goods game, the efficiency results are likely to transfer to PGG-like environments with similar cost/impact structures. The findings imply that, for games with low punishment/arrangement costs and high punishment/compensation effectiveness, enabling commitment or cost-sharing mechanisms should increase efficiency relative to standard punishment. The paper also highlights the importance of cost-to-impact ratios and the potential for free-riding if arrangement costs are high and not shared.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main model is a pairwise (2-player) Prisoner's Dilemma. Population size N=100 is used in simulations, but the game itself is always pairwise.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round) PD; no repeated interactions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD is a binary choice: cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD is parameterized as the Donation game: T=b, R=b-c, P=0, S=-c. The effective MPCR is (b-c)/b for cooperation.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/arrangement cost (epsilon) is a key parameter. Lower epsilon leads to higher efficiency and more stable cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/compensation effectiveness (delta) is varied. Higher delta (compensation) increases the viability of commitment strategies up to a threshold.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly one-shot; players know there is only one round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. In the commitment model, both parties are aware of the agreement and who is responsible for compensation. This is not the same as identifying punishers in a PGG, but the identity of the punisher is always known in the commitment context.

## Important Limitations
- The model is based on the one-shot, pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma, not a multi-player public goods game. Results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- All results are theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The commitment mechanism is modeled as a prior agreement with compensation, which is not identical to standard punishment in PGGs.
- No repeated interactions, reputation, network structure, or group selection are considered.
- No communication, chat, or feedback mechanisms are included.
- The model assumes perfect enforcement of compensation; real-world enforcement may be imperfect.
