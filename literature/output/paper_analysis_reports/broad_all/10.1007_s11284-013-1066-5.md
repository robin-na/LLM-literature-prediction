## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a coupled socioeconomic-bioeconomic model of a fishing community, where individuals choose to cooperate with management or not, and non-cooperators experience psychological punishment ('social pressure') that depends on both internal (community-based) and external (outside community) sources. The model shows that when both internal and external social pressures are sufficiently strong, the system can reach full cooperation, resulting in sustainable fish stocks and maximum sustainable yield (MSY)—a proxy for group efficiency. If only one type of social pressure is strong, or both are weak, the system may stabilize at lower cooperation and lower stock levels. The model highlights the importance of both community relationships and external societal pressure in promoting cooperation for resource management.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence that psychological punishment mechanisms (social pressure) can increase group efficiency in common-pool resource settings analogous to public goods games, especially when both internal and external sources are present. While the model is not a standard PGG and does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, it shows that the presence and strength of punishment-like mechanisms (social pressure) are critical moderators of group outcomes. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling both internal and external punishment/sanction mechanisms is likely to increase efficiency, while the absence or weakness of either reduces the effect. The model also suggests that the effect of punishment is moderated by group size (player count), as social pressure per individual decreases with larger groups.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Model includes parameter n (number of players); discussion notes that social pressure per individual decreases as n increases, making cooperation harder in larger groups.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Model is continuous-time (differential equations), not discrete rounds, but the dynamic is analogous to repeated interaction.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between two discrete options: cooperate with management or not (binary choice).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Analogous to PGG: the marginal return to cooperation is determined by fishing coefficients (f_C, f_N) and stock dynamics. Not directly labeled as MPCR, but the model structure is similar.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is psychological/social (not monetary); cost is modeled as negative utility (social pressure) for non-cooperators. The 'cost' to the punisher is not explicit, but the effect on the punished is parameterized.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is parameterized by coefficients (φ, γ, λ) for internal and external social pressure. Higher values increase the effect on cooperation and group outcome.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Social pressure depends on the observed fraction of cooperators (x) and stock level (y), implying that players are aware of others' behavior and outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- Payoff-related outcomes are proxies (stock biomass, MSY) rather than direct group payoff or efficiency ratios as in standard PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as psychological/social pressure, not as explicit costly punishment with direct costs to punishers.
- The model assumes binary choices (cooperate or not), not continuous contribution levels.
- Continuous-time dynamics differ from discrete-round PGGs.
- No explicit modeling of communication, reward, or identification of punishers.
- The effect of group size is only discussed, not systematically analyzed.
- Assumes perfect observability of cooperation and stock levels.
