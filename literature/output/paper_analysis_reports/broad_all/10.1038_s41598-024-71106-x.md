# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The relationship of the source of punishment and personality traits with investment and punishment in a public goods game
Authors: Rodrigues, J., Leipold, N., Hewig, J., & Hein, G.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment in the public good
- amount of punishment assigned
- choice of punishment source (own money vs. pool)
- certainty of punishment source choice (mouse trajectory AUC)
- trait-behavior associations (anger, empathy, altruism)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are investment (contribution), punishment amount, and punishment source choice. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare is reported. Payoff is described in the methods, but not as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that allowing punishment from the common pool (i.e., at no personal cost) or giving participants a choice between punishment sources increases investment in the public good compared to a no-punishment baseline. When punishment must be paid from one's own money, investment does not increase relative to the no-punishment condition. Punishment is more frequent and higher in magnitude when it can be taken from the pool. Personality traits (anger, altruism, empathy) modulate investment and punishment behaviors, but these effects are secondary to the main manipulation. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (who pays the cost) affects contribution rates in public goods games. Specifically, punishment that is free to the punisher (taken from the pool) increases contributions, while punishment that is costly to the punisher does not. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on whether punishment is personally costly or not, and that punishment from the common pool may increase contributions but could also reduce total group resources due to the cost being taken from the pool. For prediction tasks, this paper is most useful for understanding how punishment design affects contribution behavior, but less so for direct efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played with 4 players (1 real participant, 3 computer-controlled).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each block consists of 50 trials; 4 blocks per participant (no punishment, punishment self, punishment pool, punishment choice).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; all other players are computer-controlled and not real.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose any integer from 0 to 20 credits to invest.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (the sum of all investments is multiplied by 0.4 and redistributed equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is either paid from own credits (own-money-punishment) or from the common pool (pool-money-punishment). In the own-money condition, cost is 1:1; in the pool condition, there is no personal cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is 1:1: each credit spent reduces the punished player's credits by 1. In the pool condition, punishment is paid from the pool, not from the punisher's own credits.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials per block (50) and the number of blocks (4).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are shown the investment of each player and the final distribution of credits for each player.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; only investment and punishment behaviors are analyzed.
- All other players are computer-controlled; only one real participant per session, which may affect ecological validity and strategic behavior.
- Punishment from the pool is not personally costly, which may not generalize to real-world or standard PGG punishment settings.
- The study uses deception (fictive players), which may influence participant behavior.
- The compensation method (fixed payment, not based on actual game outcome) may reduce the incentive compatibility of the task.
- The study was not preregistered, and sample size was based on previous studies rather than a pilot.
