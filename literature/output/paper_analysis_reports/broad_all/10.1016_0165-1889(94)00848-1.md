## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main findings are: (1) In a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with boundedly rational agents (finite automata), cooperation and high efficiency can evolve even from hostile initial conditions, as long as there are no costs for strategy complexity. (2) Imposing a cost on the size of the automaton (i.e., the 'storage' or monitoring cost) destroys cooperation and reduces efficiency, with populations converging to simple, defecting strategies. (3) Imposing a cost on the frequency of switching states (i.e., 'retrieval' or attention cost) does not harm, and may even promote, cooperation and efficiency, especially if the cost is quadratic. (4) The results are robust to a range of simulation parameters. (5) The findings suggest that the form of cognitive or implementation cost is a critical moderator of whether punishment-like strategies (contingent defection) can sustain cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated two-player games structurally similar to public goods games (but not group PGGs), the ability to implement contingent punishment (e.g., Tit-for-Tat or more sophisticated automata) is crucial for sustaining high efficiency. However, if the game design imposes costs on monitoring or strategy complexity (analogous to high punishment cost or high cognitive burden), efficiency collapses and simple, non-cooperative strategies dominate. In contrast, costs on the frequency of switching (analogous to costs for changing behavior or attention) do not undermine efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that the presence and form of punishment, and especially the cost structure for implementing contingent strategies, are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The results are most directly relevant to two-player, long-horizon, repeated games with deterministic feedback and no communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (Prisoner's Dilemma). Population size is 30, but each game is 2 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each repeated game is 150 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each move is a binary choice: cooperate or defect.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. There is no explicit punishment stage, but contingent defection (punishment) is implemented via automata. The 'cost' of punishment is operationalized as the cognitive/implementation cost of using complex strategies (size of automaton or frequency of switching). When the cost of complexity (size) is high, punishment strategies (contingent defection) are not used, and efficiency collapses.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is determined by the ability to implement contingent strategies. When complexity costs are low, punishment (contingent defection) is effective and supports cooperation; when costs are high, punishment is not used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (150) is fixed and known to the automata (by construction).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Automata observe the opponent's move each round (needed for contingent strategies). No aggregate summaries, but full round-by-round feedback.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses simulated agents (finite automata) rather than human subjects, so results may not generalize to human behavior.
- The game is a two-player repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a multi-player public goods game; group dynamics and free-riding are not present.
- Punishment is not implemented as a separate costly action, but as contingent defection; thus, the mapping to explicit punishment in PGGs is indirect.
- No communication or social context is modeled.
- The cost structures studied (complexity and switching) are cognitive/implementation costs, not direct monetary costs for punishing or rewarding.
- Findings may not generalize to games with more than two players, shorter horizons, or stochastic feedback.
