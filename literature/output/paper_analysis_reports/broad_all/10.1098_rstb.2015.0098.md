## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proposes that the ability to create and enforce institutional rules, including sanctioning mechanisms, is a key driver of large-scale human cooperation and division of labor. Institutions can transform the default equilibrium of social dilemmas (such as public goods games) to more cooperative, higher-payoff outcomes by enabling coordination, information sharing, and self-enforcing sanctioning. Theoretical arguments and historical/anthropological examples are used to support the claim that institutions can increase group efficiency and welfare, but the paper does not provide new empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment or institutional design.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual support for the idea that enabling punishment or sanctioning mechanisms within an institutional framework can increase efficiency and group payoffs in public-goods-like environments, especially as group size increases. However, it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence that can be directly used to predict efficiency changes as a function of game design dimensions or control efficiency. Its value for prediction is in supporting the plausibility and mechanism of positive effects of institutions (including punishment) on efficiency, but it does not inform the magnitude or specific moderators of these effects.
