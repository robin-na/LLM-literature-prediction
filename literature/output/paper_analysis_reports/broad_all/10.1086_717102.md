# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Time and Punishment: How Individuals Respond to Being Sanctioned in Voluntary Associations
Authors: Doering, L., & Ody-Brasier, A.
Journal: American Journal of Sociology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of individual contributions allocated to the collective fund (prosocial contribution)
- response to being sanctioned (change in prosocial contribution)
- group tenure (duration)
- receipt of emergency funds (as a proxy for dependence)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of a member's total monetary contribution at each meeting allocated to the collective fund, interpreted as a measure of prosociality. No direct measures of group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare are reported. The study focuses on behavioral responses to sanctions (punishment) in a real-world voluntary association context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the effect of being sanctioned (fined) for absence in voluntary microsavings groups changes over time. Early in group tenure, sanctioned individuals reduce their prosocial contributions to the collective fund, but as they become more dependent on the group (measured by time in the group or by having received emergency funds), their response to sanctions becomes increasingly prosocial, with higher allocations to the collective fund after being fined. The effect is robust to various controls and alternative specifications. The study does not measure group efficiency or total group payoff, but the findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting prosocial behavior is temporally and relationally dependent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment on prosocial behavior (here, contributions to a collective fund) is not static but depends on group tenure and individual dependence on the group. For prediction tasks involving efficiency or payoff in public-goods-like environments, this suggests that the impact of punishment may be weaker or even negative early in group formation, but can become more positive as group members become more dependent on each other. However, because the outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, but rather individual prosocial allocation, the findings should be transferred to efficiency prediction with caution. The study is most informative about the temporal dynamics and social context moderating punishment effects, rather than about the direct effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups had about 15 members on average (mean members present per meeting: 14.93, SD 3.21).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups met approximately fortnightly over about one year; group duration ranged from 0 to 504 days (mean 113 days). Number of meetings is not explicitly stated, but can be inferred as 10-30+ per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Meetings were social, with open discussion, coffee, snacks, and local gossip. Members could communicate freely.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: members could choose how much to contribute to both individual and collective funds at each meeting.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (fine) amounts were set by the group at the first meeting and did not change. Typical fines: 500-1000 pesos (about $0.16-$0.31 USD), meaningful for the population.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was a monetary fine; the impact was the loss of money for the punished individual, with the fine amount set by the group. The fine was paid into the collective fund.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Members understood that the groups were time bound, but could continue after the program formally concluded. The total number of meetings was not strictly fixed, but the time horizon was known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contributions were made publicly in meetings; members knew what others gave, creating social pressure.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctions (fines) were public and known to all group members; the process was transparent.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, but rather the individual's allocation to a collective fund, interpreted as prosociality.
- The study is observational and field-based, not a controlled experiment; causality is inferred but not established by random assignment.
- The context is a real-world voluntary association (microsavings groups in Colombia), which may differ from standard public goods games in laboratory settings.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or group payoff structure is specified, limiting direct comparability to standard PGGs.
- The effect of punishment is shown to be dynamic and dependent on group tenure and individual dependence, which may not generalize to one-shot or short-term groups.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings are limited to punishment effects.
- Some demographic and status data are missing or incomplete, limiting subgroup analyses.
- Attrition is low but present; robustness checks suggest findings are not driven by exiting members.
