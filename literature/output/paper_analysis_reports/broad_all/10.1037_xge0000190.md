# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reflexive Intergroup Bias in Third-Party Punishment
Authors: Yudkin, D. A., Rothmund, T., Twardawski, M., Thalla, N., & Van Bavel, J. J.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Psychology-general
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- percentage of transgressor's money deducted as punishment
- intergroup bias in punishment (difference in punishment assigned to in-group vs. out-group)
- response time
- effect of cognitive load on punishment decisions
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the percentage of the transgressor's money deducted as punishment. No group payoff, efficiency, welfare, or total earnings are reported or analyzed. Outcomes are behavioral, not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three experiments, participants assigned to a third-party role punished out-group transgressors more harshly than in-group transgressors, especially under conditions promoting reflexive judgment (fast response or cognitive load). The effect is robust across different group identity manipulations (sports teams, nationality). The punishment is costless to the punisher and only removes money from the transgressor. The study does not analyze or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. The findings are about behavioral bias in punishment assignment, not about the impact of punishment on cooperation or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological determinants of punishment assignment (intergroup bias, reflexivity) in a third-party punishment context, but does not provide data on efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment on cooperation. The game structure is adjacent to public goods games but is a one-shot, three-player 'stealing and punishment' scenario with costless punishment. Thus, the findings are not directly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in public-goods-game-like environments with or without punishment. The main relevance is for understanding when and how punishment is assigned, not its effect on group welfare.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use a three-player setup: Player A (transgressor), Player B (victim), Player C (punisher/participant).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round) in all experiments.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players can enter a greeting at the start, but there is no evidence of strategic or ongoing chat/communication relevant to the game outcome.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (stealing) and punishment choices are continuous: Player A can steal any amount, Player C can deduct any amount up to the transgressor's total.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher: deducting money from the transgressor does not reduce the punisher's own payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is 1:1: each cent deducted from the transgressor is removed from the game, not transferred to anyone else.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the game is a one-shot scenario; the number of rounds is clear.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the amounts held by each player after the stealing phase and after punishment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players are identified by group (team/nationality) and role, but it is not specified whether the punished player knows the identity of the punisher beyond group membership.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a repeated social dilemma; it is a one-shot, three-player 'stealing and punishment' scenario.
- Punishment is costless to the punisher, which differs from most public goods game punishment paradigms.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported or analyzed; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The findings are about intergroup bias in punishment assignment, not about the effect of punishment on cooperation or group outcomes.
- The study does not address the impact of punishment on future behavior, norm compliance, or group-level efficiency.
- All experiments are conducted online with simulated partners, which may limit ecological validity.
- The victim is always an unrelated third party, not a member of the punisher's group.
