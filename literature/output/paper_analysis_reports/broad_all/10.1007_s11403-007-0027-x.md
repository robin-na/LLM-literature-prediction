Outcome notes:
The paper is primarily theoretical, focusing on the quantitative prediction of the propensity to punish (k) in public goods and related games. It compares these predictions to experimental data from other studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2002), but does not report new empirical efficiency or group payoff results. The main dependent variable is the level of punishment assigned, not efficiency or total group payoff, though the theory is motivated by efficiency considerations.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proposes that the observed level of punishment in public goods and related games can be quantitatively predicted by a cost-benefit analysis rooted in evolutionary feedback selection. The theory is applied to three canonical games: the third-party punishment game, the ultimatum game, and the public goods game with punishment (as in Fehr & Gächter 2002). The predicted punishment propensities (k) closely match experimental data from these games. Agent-based simulations show that the predicted punishment level is an emergent property of evolutionary dynamics. The theory suggests that punishment is tuned to maximize group efficiency, but the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes as dependent variables.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a theoretical rationale for why punishment increases cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency in public goods games. It offers quantitative predictions for the level of punishment as a function of game parameters (e.g., group size, punishment efficiency, marginal per-capita return). However, it does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report new experimental data on group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation and likely efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly, but does not provide direct efficiency data for model training or calibration.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Theoretical predictions and simulations explicitly vary group size (n), with examples for n=3 (third-party), n=2 (ultimatum), n=4 (public goods), and n=8 (simulations). The effect of player count on punishment propensity is analyzed (see Figure 5), but not directly on efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Theoretical analysis assumes one-shot and repeated games; agent-based simulations use T=500 rounds. The effect of number of rounds on efficiency is discussed but not directly analyzed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0 to 20 MUs) in the public goods game; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is specified as r1/n (e.g., r1=1.6, n=4, so mpcr=0.4). Used in theoretical predictions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is specified (e.g., 1 MU spent reduces target's payoff by r_p MUs; r_p=3 in Fehr & Gächter 2002).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment efficiency (r_p) is varied in theory and simulation. Higher r_p increases the effectiveness of punishment and supports higher cooperation in simulations.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is discussed as a theoretical possibility and symmetry to punishment, but not implemented in the main games analyzed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe others' contributions and payoffs, as punishment is based on these observations.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and does not report new experimental or empirical data on efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not a dependent variable; the main focus is on predicting punishment propensity (k) and matching it to prior experimental data.
- Agent-based simulations are stylized and may not capture all relevant features of real-world or laboratory public goods games.
- The theory assumes evolutionary adaptation of punishment propensities, which may not fully account for cultural, contextual, or institutional moderators.
- No direct analysis of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in the presence versus absence of punishment; implications for efficiency are inferred rather than measured.
