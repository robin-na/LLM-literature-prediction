## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that sanctions can have an expressive function, conveying moral norms and motivating compliance for moral reasons rather than purely instrumental ones. It reviews evidence that (1) retributive sanctions are more likely to induce moral norms than compensatory ones, (2) punishments are more effective than rewards at conveying obligatory moral rules, and (3) severe (but not unreasonably severe) sanctions are more likely to induce moral disapproval and compliance, especially when implemented by trustworthy and fair authorities. However, the paper also notes that sanctions can backfire, especially among those already engaging in the undesired behavior, and that excessive severity or unfairness can undermine moral norms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and psychological context for how punishment may affect compliance and norm internalization in public-goods-like environments, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. It suggests that the design and framing of punishment (retributive vs. compensatory, punishment vs. reward, severity, authority trustworthiness, and fairness) can moderate the effectiveness of sanctions in changing behavior. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, as it does not report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff data. Its main value is in highlighting psychological mechanisms and moderators that could influence the effectiveness of punishment in experimental designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Rewards are discussed as an alternative to punishment; evidence suggests rewards are less effective at conveying moral norms and obligatory rules than punishments.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and synthesizes findings from other empirical studies; it does not report new experimental or observational data.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- Most outcomes discussed are psychological (moral norm internalization, disapproval, compliance intentions) or behavioral (contribution, lateness, cheating), not payoff-based.
- The relevance to public goods games is indirect; some referenced studies use social dilemma or public-goods-like settings, but the paper does not provide detailed game design parameters.
- No information is provided on key game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, or technical implementation.
