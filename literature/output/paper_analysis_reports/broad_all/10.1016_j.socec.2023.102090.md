# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social distancing in networks: A web-based interactive experiment
Authors: Gallo, E., Barak, D., & Langtry, A.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of practicing social distancing
- change in social distancing behavior in response to fines and nudges
- heterogeneity in distancing by network position, contagion rate, and individual characteristics
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the binary decision to practice social distancing. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff structure is described, but outcomes are reported in terms of distancing behavior, not realized payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Fines (punishment) for not practicing social distancing significantly increase the probability that participants choose to distance, compared to a baseline with no fine. Nudges (informational videos) have a smaller and less robust effect. The effect of fines is immediate and persistent, while the nudge effect is weaker and context-dependent. Network structure (star vs. complete) and position (superspreader vs. peripheral) influence distancing: superspreaders distance more. High disease contagiousness increases distancing. Political ideology, risk preferences, and social preferences also moderate distancing behavior. However, the experiment does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing fines (punishment) increases compliance with a costly pro-social action (distancing) in a networked environment structurally similar to a public goods game. However, because the main outcome is behavioral (distancing) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the results should be used as indirect evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, binary action, fixed cost/benefit, punishment cost and impact, no reward, no feedback) are well specified, so the paper is informative about the likely direction of punishment effects on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency. Use this paper as supportive evidence that punishment increases pro-social behavior in similar environments, but do not use it for quantitative efficiency predictions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 5 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group plays 20 rounds of baseline, then 20 rounds of intervention (fine or nudge).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between participants is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Decision to practice social distancing is binary (yes/no).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not labeled as MPCR, but the benefit structure is: healthy individuals get 100 points, distancing costs 35 points, infection probability depends on network and distancing. The marginal return to distancing is determined by the contagion process and network structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Fine for not distancing is 15 points, paid by the non-distancer. This is a fixed cost per violation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is a fine of 15 points per non-distancing decision; the impact is direct and certain.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only fines (punishment) and nudges are tested.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds (20 baseline, 20 intervention).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are not informed about the decisions or outcomes of other group members at any point.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (fine) is automatic and not assigned by other players; no identification of punisher is possible.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is behavioral (distancing) rather than efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No direct analysis or reporting of group efficiency, total payoff, or surplus.
- The game is a framed social distancing scenario, not a standard public goods game; mapping to PGG is indirect.
- Punishment is implemented as an automatic fine, not peer punishment.
- No reward mechanism is tested.
- No communication or feedback about others' actions or payoffs.
- Results may not generalize to standard PGGs or to settings with different payoff structures.
- Nudge intervention is limited to a single informational video; other nudge types are not tested.
