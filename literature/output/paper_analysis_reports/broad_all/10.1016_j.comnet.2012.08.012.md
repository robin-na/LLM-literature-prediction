## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of cooperators, non-cooperators, and loners over time
- stability and persistence of cooperation
- cyclic dominance of strategies
- invasion or extinction of strategies
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the emergence and persistence of cooperation, non-cooperation, and loner strategies in a repeated Rock-Scissors-Paper (RSP) game as a model for P2P systems. Outcomes are primarily behavioral (strategy frequencies, stability, and cyclic patterns), not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. Payoff is discussed in terms of the game matrix, but aggregate efficiency or group payoff is not a reported outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated RSP game modeling P2P interactions, the introduction of a 'loner' strategy (voluntary withdrawal) allows for stable coexistence of cooperators, non-cooperators, and loners, preventing the collapse of cooperation even in the absence of incentives or punishment. The system exhibits cyclic dominance of strategies, and the stability of cooperation is sensitive to factors such as population size, network overlay dynamism, and initial population composition. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as outcomes. Simulations show that large, weakly dynamic populations are more likely to sustain stable cycles, while small or highly dynamic overlays are prone to domination by a single strategy.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. Its main relevance is in showing that, in a repeated game with voluntary participation (the 'loner' option), stable cooperation can emerge without explicit punishment or reward. The model is adjacent to PGGs but differs in key respects: it is a zero-sum RSP game, not a public goods game, and does not manipulate or analyze punishment, reward, or efficiency. Therefore, it should not be used as direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs, but may be useful for understanding the role of voluntary participation and cyclic dominance in sustaining cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Simulations vary population size (e.g., 100, 500, 1000 nodes). Larger populations are more likely to sustain stable coexistence and cycles; small populations are prone to domination by a single strategy.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations use repeated rounds and generations (e.g., 500 generations of 20 rounds each), but the effect of number of rounds per se is not isolated.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game; it is a repeated zero-sum Rock-Scissors-Paper game with three strategies (cooperate, non-cooperate, loner).
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are present in the main model; punishment is only discussed in a brief simulation variant.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are not reported outcomes; the focus is on behavioral dynamics and stability.
- Findings may not generalize to PGGs or environments with explicit punishment/reward mechanisms.
- Simulation results depend on specific assumptions about population size, overlay dynamics, and the RSP payoff matrix.
