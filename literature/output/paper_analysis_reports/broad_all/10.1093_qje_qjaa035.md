Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical analysis of a repeated trust/relational contracting game with coordinated punishment and the possibility of extortion. The main dependent variables are surplus, payoffs, and effort, which map to efficiency. Non-payoff outcomes like cooperation, extortion, and communication are discussed as mechanisms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in relational/trust games with coordinated punishment, the possibility of extortion (i.e., agents threatening to trigger punishment unless paid, regardless of their effort) can completely undermine cooperation and efficiency. In the baseline model with extortion, equilibrium effort and surplus are zero. However, if institutions can tie the ability to trigger punishment (leverage) to actual effort—such as through public signals of effort or strong dyadic relationships—then positive effort and efficiency can be restored, though often at the cost of rents to agents or the need for strong relationships. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not universally positive: it depends critically on whether misuse/extortion is possible and whether institutional features can prevent it.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated cooperation games is highly sensitive to the possibility of extortion and the institutional context. If punishment can be misused (e.g., via action-contingent threats), enabling punishment may reduce efficiency to zero. However, if the game design includes mechanisms that tie punishment power to actual cooperative behavior (e.g., public effort signals, strong dyadic relationships), punishment can support high efficiency. For prediction, this means that the presence or absence of extortion opportunities, and the presence of institutional safeguards, are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. Game design dimensions that affect the ability to make credible threats, the observability of effort, and the structure of relationships are especially important.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model is not a standard public goods game, but rather a repeated trust/relational contracting game with a principal and a sequence of agents.
- Punishment is implemented via coordinated group response to public messages, not via costly individual punishment actions.
- The model assumes perfect rationality and equilibrium play.
- The possibility of extortion is modeled as agents being able to make action-contingent threats; real-world applicability may depend on the credibility and observability of such threats.
- The analysis focuses on infinite-horizon or indefinitely repeated games; finite-horizon effects are not directly addressed.
- No explicit analysis of group size effects beyond the principal-agent structure.
- No explicit reward mechanisms are modeled.
