## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a centralized sanctioning authority into a repeated trust game with incomplete information can increase trust and reciprocity, especially when actors believe the authority is likely to punish defectors. The model shows that the threat of centralized punishment relaxes the conditions under which trust is placed and honored, compared to the baseline trust game without an authority. The effect is strongest when there is optimism about the authority's willingness to punish. The model predicts that centralized sanctioning can mitigate the end-game effect (decline in cooperation in final rounds). However, the analysis is theoretical and does not provide explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that adding a centralized punishment mechanism to a trust-based social dilemma can increase trust and cooperation, especially under incomplete information. While the model is not a public goods game, it is adjacent and relevant for predicting the effect of centralized punishment in repeated social dilemmas. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling centralized punishment is likely to increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoffs or efficiency, particularly when the punishment is sufficiently strong and actors believe the authority is likely to punish. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency or group payoff, so its value for direct efficiency prediction is limited to qualitative inference.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a three-player game: trustor, trustee, and authority. The baseline is two players; the two-level model is three.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is finitely repeated, with explicit analysis for two periods (n=2).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust placement and honoring are binary (place trust or not; honor trust or abuse).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment by the authority is costly (cost C3 to authority per punishment). The cost affects equilibrium strategies and the likelihood of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is parameterized as psi (reduction in trustee's utility). Higher psi increases the deterrent effect and likelihood of trust and cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Authority receives a reward R3 if trust is placed and honored, but there is no reward mechanism for trustor or trustee to reward each other.
- reward cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Authority's cost C3 is for punishment, not for rewarding. No explicit cost for rewarding.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. All actors know the exact number of periods (finite, n=2 in analysis).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is imposed by a unique authority; the identity of the punisher is always known.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- No explicit efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are calculated; only equilibrium strategies and probabilities of trust/cooperation are analyzed.
- The model is a trust game, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- The analysis is limited to two periods (n=2); longer or infinite horizon cases are not analyzed.
- The model assumes incomplete information about types but does not address learning or updating over many rounds.
- No analysis of heterogeneity in punishment cost or effectiveness across authorities.
