## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- cooperation level (fraction of cooperative actions, equivalent to group efficiency in the repeated donation game)
- long-term average payoff
- fitness (average payoff against group members)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy class prevalence (efficient, rival, friendly rival)
- memory length of strategies
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the cooperation level, which in the context of the repeated donation game is equivalent to group efficiency (i.e., the fraction of time mutual cooperation is achieved, which maximizes group payoff). The paper also analyzes the prevalence of different strategy types and memory lengths, but these are not payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that group structure, when combined with the availability of longer-memory strategies (which allow for friendly rival strategies), leads to the evolution of nearly full cooperation and maximal efficiency in the repeated donation game. In well-mixed populations, longer memory does not have this effect. The results are robust to the benefit-to-cost ratio and to details of the evolutionary process. The paper does not study public goods games or explicit punishment mechanisms, but the repeated donation game is adjacent to PGGs, and friendly rival strategies function as a form of endogenous sanctioning (never allowing the co-player to earn more). Efficiency is measured as the fraction of time mutual cooperation is achieved, which is equivalent to group payoff relative to the maximum.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player repeated donation games (adjacent to PGGs), the combination of group structure and the availability of longer-memory strategies (enabling friendly rival strategies) can dramatically increase efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise be difficult. While there is no explicit punishment mechanism, friendly rival strategies act as endogenous enforcers of cooperation by never allowing the co-player to earn more. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in environments with group structure and sufficient strategic complexity, efficiency can be much higher than in well-mixed or memory-limited settings, even without explicit punishment. However, the results may not transfer directly to multi-player PGGs or to settings with explicit costly punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The main model is a two-player repeated donation game (N=2 per group), but the number of groups (M) is varied. In well-mixed populations, N is varied (see Fig 4). Larger N in well-mixed populations can increase cooperation if the benefit is high, but in group-structured populations, N=2 is used throughout.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The repeated donation game is played for infinitely many rounds (long-run average payoff is used). There is no fixed or known number of rounds; the game is modeled as infinite horizon.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round, players choose to cooperate (donate) or defect (not donate); the action is binary (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b (benefit to recipient) per unit cost (cost=1). b is varied (e.g., b=1.5, 3, 6). Higher b increases cooperation and efficiency in well-mixed populations, but in group-structured populations with longer memory, high efficiency is achieved even at low b.

## Important Limitations
- The model is limited to two-player repeated donation games (iterated Prisoner's Dilemma), not multi-player public goods games.
- There is no explicit punishment or reward mechanism; enforcement is endogenous via strategy selection (friendly rivals).
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit costly punishment, multi-player interactions, or finite repeated games.
- All strategies are deterministic; mixed strategies are not considered, and friendly rivals are rare or measure zero in mixed strategy space.
- The model assumes infinite horizon (no endgame effects) and no communication or reputation.
- Mutation and imitation processes are stylized and may not capture all aspects of real-world learning or evolution.
