# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people
Authors: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M.
Journal: Nature Human Behaviour
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- interpersonal trust
- cooperation (amount sent/given in trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game)
- perceived dishonesty of third-party punisher
- expectations about punishment by TPP
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust and cooperation (amount sent/given), not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff. No explicit analysis of group efficiency or total earnings relative to a fully cooperative group is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect of TPP corruption on efficiency is not measured. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are played in dyads (2 players) for trust game, prisoner's dilemma, and dictator game, with a third-party punisher (TPP) as observer/punisher. So, 2 main players + 1 TPP per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All economic games are one-shot (single round) after the die-rolling phase.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1b includes a communication (chat/message) manipulation (present vs. absent) in a 2x2 design, but main results are reported for both conditions together; details in SI.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/cooperation choices are continuous (amount sent/given), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the trust game, each MU sent is tripled (MPCR = 3/2 = 1.5 for 2 players, but not a standard PGG). In dictator and prisoner's dilemma, standard payoffs apply. No explicit MPCR for PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. TPP can invest own endowment to reduce players' outcomes; cost structure is present but exact cost-to-impact ratio is not specified in main text.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. TPP can reduce others' payoffs by investing own endowment; details of punishment effectiveness per unit cost are not specified in main text.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single round) in Phase 2.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observe the behavior of the TPP in the die-rolling task (Phase 1), and receive feedback on outcomes. In Phase 2, no explicit mention of summary info about others' contributions/payoffs, but TPP observes and can punish.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the identity (role) of the TPP and whether the TPP is the same person as in Phase 1 (honest/corrupt) or a stranger (control).

## Important Limitations
- The studies do not report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes; only trust and cooperation (amount sent/given) are measured.
- There is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline; all games include a third-party punisher (TPP), so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
- The TPP is always present; the manipulation is on the perceived integrity (corrupt vs. honest) of the TPP, not on the presence/absence of punishment.
- The experimental games are not standard public goods games; they use trust games, prisoner's dilemma, and dictator games with TPP.
- All games are one-shot; repeated interactions and long-term effects are not studied.
- The operationalization of institutions as a TPP in a lab setting may not fully capture real-world institutional complexity.
