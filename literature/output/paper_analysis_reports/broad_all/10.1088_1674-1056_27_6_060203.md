Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the stochastic stable equilibrium (limit distribution) of group states in a finite-population public goods game with punishment. While expected payoffs are derived for each strategy, the main reported outcomes are the probabilities of the system being in certain states (e.g., all defect, coexistence of cooperation and punishment), not direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff. Efficiency is discussed conceptually but not directly quantified as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing first-order costly punishment into a finite-population public goods game can shift the system from an all-defection equilibrium to a coexistence of cooperation and punishment, provided that both the punishment intensity and the return on investment are large enough. This shift implies higher group welfare and efficiency, as the all-defection state is inefficient. Second-order punishment (punishing cooperators who do not punish defectors) does not dramatically change the evolutionary dynamics except in certain critical parameter combinations, and mainly increases the proportion of punishers among cooperators in coexistence states. The model provides a detailed mapping of parameter regions where cooperation is stochastically stable.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in finite-population public goods games, enabling costly punishment (with sufficient intensity and return on investment) can substantially increase the probability of the system being in cooperative or mixed cooperative-punisher states, which are more efficient than all-defection. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence and strength of punishment, as well as the return on investment (mpcr), are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. However, the paper does not report direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must be inferred from the stability of cooperative states. Second-order punishment adds little except in narrow parameter regions. The results are most applicable to settings matching the model's assumptions (finite, well-mixed populations, no communication, discrete strategies).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size M is a model parameter (e.g., M=20 in numerical examples); group size N=5 is also specified. The model allows arbitrary finite M and N.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: individuals choose cooperation, defection, or punishment (which includes cooperation).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Return on investment r (with 1 < r < N) is a key parameter; higher r increases the probability of cooperative/punishing states and thus efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit (gamma) is specified (e.g., gamma=0.2); lower cost (relative to penalty beta) makes punishment more effective at promoting cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (penalty per unit cost, beta/gamma) is a key parameter; higher beta (penalty) increases the probability of cooperative/punishing states.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency is not directly measured; outcomes are reported as probabilities of system states (e.g., all-defect vs. coexistence), which must be interpreted as proxies for efficiency.
- The model assumes well-mixed, finite populations with no communication, no reward, and binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with continuous contribution, communication, or more complex social structures.
- Second-order punishment effects are only substantial in narrow parameter regions.
