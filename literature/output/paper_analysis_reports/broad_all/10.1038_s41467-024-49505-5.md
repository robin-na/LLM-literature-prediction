## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper analytically demonstrates that in structured populations (regular graphs), the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer or pool) in public goods games can lead to the extinction of defection or to cyclic dominance among strategies, depending on the punishment type and strength. For peer punishment, if the punishment fine exceeds a critical threshold, defection is eliminated and only cooperators and punishers coexist, maximizing group efficiency. For pool punishment, strong punishment leads to cyclic dominance, preventing fixation of defection and maintaining higher group welfare. The paper provides explicit formulas for the critical punishment thresholds and shows that these effects are unique to structured populations and do not occur in well-mixed populations, where defection always retains a basin of attraction. The results are presented in terms of group payoff, mean payoff, and phase diagrams, all directly related to efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for predicting that, in public goods games on regular graphs, enabling punishment (peer or pool) will increase group efficiency and welfare, provided the punishment strength exceeds a critical threshold. The analytical results specify how the effect depends on the number of neighbors (player_count), the punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech), and the structure of the game (e.g., mpcr, payoff function). The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings with structured populations and multi-strategy dynamics. The explicit threshold formulas for punishment strength can be used to estimate when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Player count is determined by the number of neighbors (k) plus the focal individual (k+1). The effect of player count (group size) is explicitly included in the analytical formulas for thresholds and payoffs. Larger group size (higher k) affects the critical punishment threshold and the dynamics of cooperation and efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is modeled as a binary choice (cooperate or defect), with additional strategies for punishment/reward. The model allows for multi-strategy but not continuous contribution levels.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is determined by the synergy factor r and group size (k+1). The effect of mpcr is explicitly included in the payoff structure and threshold formulas. Higher mpcr (r/(k+1)) favors cooperation and lowers the threshold for effective punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is explicitly modeled for both peer and pool punishment. Higher punishment cost raises the threshold for effective punishment and can reduce the efficiency benefit of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per unit cost, beta/alpha) is explicitly modeled. Higher punishment effectiveness (higher beta for given alpha) increases the likelihood that punishment resolves the social dilemma and improves efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward mechanisms are mentioned as an extension and in supplementary material, but not analyzed in detail in the main text.

## Important Limitations
- The results are theoretical and based on evolutionary dynamics under weak selection; real-world dynamics may differ, especially under strong selection or finite populations.
- The model assumes infinite populations and regular graphs (structured populations with fixed degree), which may not capture all relevant features of real social networks.
- The analysis focuses on the absence of triangle motifs (no clustering) for pair approximation to be accurate; results may not generalize to networks with high clustering.
- No explicit time horizon or rounds; the model does not address endgame effects or finite repeated games.
- Reward mechanisms are only briefly mentioned and not analyzed in detail in the main text.
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are analytical or based on prior simulation studies.
