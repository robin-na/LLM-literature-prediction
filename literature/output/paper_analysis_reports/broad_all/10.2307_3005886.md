# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The maintenance of cooperation: Expectations of future interaction and the trigger of group punishment
Authors: Sell, J., & Wilson, R. K.
Journal: Social Forces
Year: 1999

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of cooperative responses
- first trial cooperation rate
- overall cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The dependent variable is the proportion of cooperative (Y) vs. defecting (Z) responses. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported, though the payoff structure is described and expected payoffs under different strategies are calculated in theory.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that requiring a grim trigger punishment rule (where defection locks a player into permanent defection) substantially increases cooperation rates in a repeated public goods game. The effect of the discount parameter (expected number of rounds) on cooperation is only significant when the grim trigger is imposed, and only between the highest and lower values. Without a mandated punishment phase, increasing the discount parameter does not increase cooperation. The main dependent variable is the proportion of cooperative choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that group-level punishment threats can coordinate expectations and sustain cooperation, but the value of future interaction alone is insufficient unless paired with credible punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the imposition of a group-level, long punishment phase (grim trigger) increases cooperation rates in repeated public goods games, especially in the early rounds. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a strong group punishment mechanism (even if not individual-targeted) will increase cooperation and likely improve efficiency, especially when players expect many rounds. The effect of expected future interaction (discount parameter) is only realized when punishment is credible and severe. For prediction tasks, the presence of a mandated, long punishment phase should be treated as a strong positive moderator of efficiency, but the absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative inference.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 4 players (each group had two men and two women).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds was stochastic, determined by a random draw with probabilities set to achieve expected values corresponding to discount parameters (.7, .8, .95). Higher expected rounds (higher discount parameter) increased cooperation only when grim trigger was imposed.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No face-to-face communication or chat was allowed; this is mentioned in the context of prior studies.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were binary: Y (cooperate, contribute) or Z (defect, do not contribute).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff matrix is provided. For 4 players: if all cooperate, each gets 65; if all defect, each gets 20. The marginal per-capita return can be calculated from the matrix.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a group-level trigger: if anyone defects, all must defect for the rest of the game (grim trigger). The cost is indirect: all lose future cooperation payoffs. No individual punishment cost per se.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: a single defection triggers permanent group defection (grim trigger). This is a strong, group-level punishment technology.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the exact number of rounds in advance; the game ended stochastically based on random draws, matching the discount parameter.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were shown the decisions of all group members, earnings per trial, and cumulative earnings.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff; no direct efficiency or welfare analysis is reported.
- Punishment is implemented as a group-level trigger (grim trigger), not as individual-targeted costly punishment; results may not generalize to individual punishment settings.
- The effect of the discount parameter is only significant in the presence of a mandated punishment phase, and only between the highest and lower values.
- The stochastic number of rounds and the use of expected continuation may differ from fixed-round or infinite-horizon designs.
- Sample is limited to university students in a lab setting; external validity may be limited.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward effects.
