## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- evolutionary stable strategies of government regulation, producer traceability system construction, and consumer satisfaction
- probabilities of choosing to regulate, build, or be satisfied
- conditions for stable cooperation or defection among stakeholders
Outcome notes:
The paper analyzes the strategic choices and evolutionary stable strategies of government, producers, and consumers in a regulatory context using evolutionary game theory. Outcomes are expressed as equilibrium strategies and probabilities, not as explicit group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare. Payoff parameters are discussed in the model, but the main results are about behavioral strategy selection.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that severe government penalties for non-compliance (punishment), sufficient incentives, and reduced costs for producers to build traceability systems are key to achieving stable cooperation among stakeholders. Depending on the relative size of these parameters, the system can stabilize in different equilibria: (Not regulate, Not build, Satisfied), (Regulate, Not build, Satisfied), or (Regulate, Build, Satisfied). The presence and strength of punishment (penalties) are critical in shifting the equilibrium toward higher compliance by producers. However, the outcomes are reported as stable strategy profiles, not as explicit efficiency or group payoff measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence and severity of punishment (penalties) can shift the equilibrium from non-compliance to compliance in a multi-actor regulatory game. However, because the model does not report group efficiency or explicit payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency changes due to punishment in public-goods-like games. The findings suggest that increasing punishment can increase compliance, but the translation to efficiency or group payoff is not quantified. The model structure and parameter dependencies may inform qualitative expectations about the effect of punishment in similar multi-actor regulatory environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Three players: government, agricultural producer, consumer. All are explicitly modeled as strategic actors.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each actor chooses between two discrete strategies: government (regulate/not), producer (build/not), consumer (satisfied/dissatisfied).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a penalty P imposed by the government on non-compliant producers. The effect of increasing P is directly analyzed in the model and simulations, showing that higher penalties increase compliance.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is represented by the penalty parameter P. The model shows that higher P (more effective punishment) increases the likelihood of compliance.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Government incentives (A) for compliance are explicitly modeled. Higher incentives increase compliance.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Outcomes are reported as stable strategy profiles (regulate/not, build/not, satisfied/dissatisfied), not as explicit group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The model is not a public goods game or direct variant; it is a regulatory compliance game with adjacent structure.
- No explicit mapping to marginal per-capita return or standard public goods parameters.
- No analysis of communication, repeated rounds, or feedback mechanisms.
- The model does not include third-party testing organizations or the public, which may be relevant in real-world regulatory environments.
- Authors note that the model may not fully capture real decision-making environments and plan to improve it in future work.
