## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in a two-player donation game with peer punishment, prospect theory's cognitive distortions (overweighting small probabilities and losses) make individuals more sensitive to the threat of punishment. As a result, cooperation can become globally stable in a wide range of parameter values where, under standard expected utility theory, only defection is stable. The model shows that even when punishment is rare (due to errors), the perceived risk is high enough to deter defection and promote cooperation, thus increasing group efficiency. The cooperative non-punishing strategy (CN) becomes globally stable as punishment strength increases, while the cooperative punishing strategy (CP) is always dominated. The effect is robust to parameter changes in the prospect theory functions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that the effectiveness of peer punishment in increasing efficiency depends not only on the objective game parameters (punishment cost, punishment strength, error rate) but also on the cognitive perception of risk and loss (as modeled by prospect theory). In environments where agents overweight the probability and severity of punishment, even infrequent or low-cost punishment can be sufficient to stabilize cooperation and increase efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that models assuming standard rationality may underestimate the efficiency gains from punishment, especially when cognitive biases are present. However, the model is limited to two-player donation games and does not directly address standard multi-player public goods games.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a two-player donation game, not a standard multi-player public goods game; results may not generalize directly to PGGs.
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is only allowed against defectors; antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is not modeled.
- The model assumes agents have prospect-theoretic cognitive distortions; real-world populations may vary in these parameters.
- The emergence of punishment (who punishes first) is assumed via error, not endogenously modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or social network structure is included.
