Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are firm and worker earnings (payoff), and the frequency of high effort (behavioral). The paper explicitly reports average payoffs for firms and workers by treatment, and analyzes how job rotation and punishment-like mechanisms affect these outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment tests whether job rotation can reduce the ratchet effect in a principal-agent setting. Three treatments are compared: no rotation (baseline), exogenous rotation (forced switch), and endogenous rotation (principal can pay to switch). In the baseline, workers often restrict effort to avoid revealing private information, resulting in lower efficiency. Exogenous rotation nearly eliminates the ratchet effect, with almost all workers providing high effort and firms earning significantly more. Endogenous rotation, where the principal can pay a small cost to rotate workers, also reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort, but the increase in firm earnings is not statistically significant. The findings support the idea that job rotation (especially exogenous) can improve efficiency by mitigating strategic output restriction, and that even a costly, non-credible threat of rotation (punishment) can have positive behavioral effects.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment option (here, job rotation) can increase efficiency by reducing strategic shirking, especially when the punishment is exogenously imposed. The effect is weaker but still present when the punishment is endogenous and costly. The experimental design is not a standard public goods game, but the principal-agent structure with costly punishment and efficiency outcomes is closely related. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling punishment (even at a cost) is likely to increase efficiency, with the effect size depending on whether the punishment is exogenous or endogenous and on the costliness of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 1 principal (firm) and 2 agents (workers) per round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 45 rounds, broken into 9 blocks of 5 rounds each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Direct communication between participants was strictly forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort/output choice is discrete: high or low output (not continuous).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the endogenous rotation treatment, the principal pays a cost of 1 point to rotate (punish) workers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as job rotation (switching workers between stands), which reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed of the number of rounds and block structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Firms see each stand's output and their own payoffs; workers see their own actions and payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Workers know if they have been switched (punished) by the firm.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental environment is a principal-agent game with job rotation as punishment, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Punishment is implemented as job rotation, which may differ in psychological and strategic impact from monetary punishment in PGGs.
- The cost of punishment (rotation) is low (1 point); effects may differ with higher costs.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- Communication between participants is not allowed; effects may differ if chat or communication is possible.
- The experiment uses a stated-effort/output design, not real-effort tasks, which may affect external validity.
