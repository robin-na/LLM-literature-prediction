The model's main dependent variable is the average fitness/payoff of different strategies in the population, which is analogous to group efficiency. Behavioral outcomes (e.g., frequency of monogamy, policing) are also tracked, but the model's core results are about the conditions for stable coexistence and the maintenance of high-payoff (monogamous) strategies.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a hidden-strategy game modeling human mating, the combination of punishment (social sanctions) and policing (gossip/observation), when stabilized by the possibility of blackmailing, leads to the stable coexistence of monogamy and other strategies, with monogamy maintained at high frequency. This results in higher average fitness (efficiency) for the population. If punishment is present but blackmailing is not possible, policing collapses due to second-order free-riding, and monogamy cannot be maintained. Thus, the introduction of punishment (with blackmailing) increases efficiency relative to the baseline with no punishment or with punishment alone. The model is robust to variations in the opportunity for blackmailing and to gender differences in payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (with sufficient severity and the possibility of side-payments/blackmailing) in a public-goods-like environment increases group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The model is not an exact PGG but is structurally analogous, with cooperation (monogamy) as the group-beneficial act and defection (EPM) as the selfish act. The results suggest that punishment alone may not be sufficient unless stabilized by additional incentives (e.g., blackmailing), but when these are present, efficiency is higher. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is costly and subject to second-order free-riding, and where additional mechanisms (like blackmailing) can offset these costs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested; predictions are based on mathematical analysis and simulation.
- The game modeled is analogous to, but not an exact, public goods game; results may not transfer directly to standard PGG laboratory settings.
- The model assumes large, well-mixed populations and does not address small group or finite population effects.
- Punishment is modeled as social sanctions and blackmailing, which may not map directly onto monetary punishment in experimental PGGs.
- The model assumes that gossip and blackmailing are possible; in environments where these are not feasible, the results may not hold.
