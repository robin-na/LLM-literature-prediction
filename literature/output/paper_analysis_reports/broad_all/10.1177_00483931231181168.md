## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proposes a refined theoretical model of social norms that distinguishes between norm subjects, enforcers, and audience, and analyzes their mutual influences and behavioral possibilities. It argues that the presence of enforcers (who punish norm violations) is crucial for sustaining cooperation, referencing public goods game experiments and the literature on punishment. The paper also discusses how group identity, audience effects, and the structure of enforcement (centralized vs. distributed) can affect cooperation and norm stability. It introduces the concepts of 'misplacement' (enforcers avoiding punishment duties) and 'identification avoidance' (subjects avoiding norm-triggering situations) as subtle forms of norm violation. However, the paper does not present new empirical data or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes; its contribution is conceptual and integrative.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual and mechanistic insights into how punishment, enforcement roles, and group structure may affect cooperation and norm stability in public goods game-like environments. It affirms, based on cited literature, that punishment can increase cooperation and that the structure of enforcement (centralized vs. distributed) can moderate these effects. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates for predicting efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions. Its value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of agent roles, group identity, and enforcement structure as potential moderators, but it does not supply empirical data for model training or calibration.
