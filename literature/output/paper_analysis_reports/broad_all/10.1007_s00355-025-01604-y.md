## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that community enforcement via grim punishment can sustain efficient cooperation in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with random matching, provided the cost of cooperation is not too low or too high. Specifically, if the cost-to-benefit ratio exceeds a threshold (less than 0.5), punishment is always credible and cooperation is an equilibrium for any population size and discount factor. For any environment, there exists a 'target cost of cooperation' that enables coordination on cooperation and makes punishment effective. When the cost of cooperation is endogenous, players can use it as a coordination device to achieve efficient outcomes. The results are entirely theoretical and provide explicit payoff-based conditions for when punishment increases group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with random matching and private monitoring, the introduction of credible punishment (grim trigger) can increase group efficiency to the cooperative level, provided the cost of cooperation is within a specific range. The key moderators are the cost-to-benefit ratio, population size, and discount factor. The results suggest that, for a wide range of parameter values, enabling punishment will move efficiency from the defection baseline to the cooperative optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the cost of cooperation (analogous to MPCR in PGGs), population size, and the presence of punishment. However, the model is not a literal public goods game but an adjacent repeated PD, so some caution is warranted in direct quantitative transfer.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or simulation results are provided.
- The model is an infinitely repeated, randomly matched Prisoner's Dilemma, not a literal public goods game, though it is closely related.
- Punishment is implemented as a grim trigger (permanent defection), which may not correspond to the more flexible or costly punishment options in experimental PGGs.
- The model assumes private monitoring and no communication, which may differ from some experimental or real-world settings.
- The results depend on infinite repetition; finite-horizon or noisy environments may yield different outcomes.
- The cost of cooperation is sometimes endogenous, but the model does not address heterogeneity in costs or benefits beyond symmetric cases.
