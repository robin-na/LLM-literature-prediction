# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Hierarchy and opportunism in teams
Authors: van der Heijden, E., Potters, J., & Sefton, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- team earnings
- team output
- average earnings per group
- proportion of maximum possible earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- team effort
- number of workers
- leader allocation strategies
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are team earnings (group payoff), team output, and effort levels. The paper directly reports efficiency as the ratio of actual to maximum possible earnings. Non-payoff outcomes include effort (contribution) and leader allocation behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares two institutions: equal revenue-sharing (control) and leader-determined shares (treatment, where one member can allocate output). In the control, free-riding leads to declining effort and efficiency, with teams achieving on average 61% of maximum possible earnings. In the leader treatment, most leaders reward contributors and exclude shirkers, resulting in average team earnings of 83% of maximum possible. The presence of a leader thus leads to a significant and substantial improvement in efficiency and group payoff. However, the benefit depends on the leader's behavior: teams with unfair or opportunistic leaders perform poorly. The results are explained by a reputation model where leaders may act fairly to sustain cooperation, especially in repeated games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a central authority with reward power (akin to a 'punishment' or sanctioning mechanism, though framed as reward) can substantially increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, provided the leader uses their power to reward contributors and punish shirkers. The effect is robust in a repeated, all-or-nothing contribution game with four players and perfect monitoring. For prediction, the presence of a leader with discretionary allocation power should be expected to increase efficiency relative to equal sharing, especially when the leader can observe and condition rewards on individual contributions. However, the effect depends on the leader's willingness to use their power to enforce cooperation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where the 'punishment' is implemented via exclusion from rewards rather than explicit costly punishment, but the mechanism is closely related.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consist of 4 players in both treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between subjects, except via decisions entered on their terminal.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: each player chooses to 'work' (contribute) or 'shirk' (not contribute).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is non-linear due to convex production function: Q = 60*(sum e_i)^2. For 4 contributors, total output is 960, so MPCR varies with number of contributors.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the leader-determined shares treatment, the leader can reward contributors by allocating them a share of output.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is not costly in the sense of reducing the leader's own endowment, but it does reduce the leader's share of output. The cost is opportunity cost of not keeping output.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward technology is direct: leader can allocate any share of output to any member, conditional on observed effort.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is repeated for 15 rounds (finite, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, subjects are informed of all group members' choices and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Leader's identity is fixed and known (subject 1), and all allocations are transparent to group members.

## Important Limitations
- The 'punishment' mechanism is implemented via reward exclusion (leader allocates zero to shirkers), not via explicit costly punishment as in standard PGG punishment games.
- The experiment uses a binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choice, not a continuous contribution scale.
- Groups are small (4 players) and fixed for 15 rounds; results may not generalize to larger or changing groups.
- Effort is perfectly and costlessly observable; real-world settings may involve imperfect monitoring.
- Leadership is exogenously assigned and not endogenous to group choice.
- The leader's power is absolute within the group; in other settings, leader authority may be constrained.
