## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews evidence that the availability of costly punishment in public goods games leads to much higher and more stable cooperation levels compared to games without punishment. Voluntary selection into punishment regimes further increases cooperation compared to exogenous assignment. The review also highlights that punishment is only effective at promoting cooperation when it is perceived as fair or socially justified. Neuroeconomic studies show that both cooperation and the act of punishing defectors activate reward-related brain circuits, suggesting a neural basis for strong reciprocity. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative results on efficiency or group payoff, instead focusing on behavioral outcomes and neural mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong qualitative support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation and contributions, especially when punishment is voluntary and perceived as justified. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. For prediction tasks, this review supports the expectation that punishment will increase efficiency when it increases cooperation, but the magnitude and conditions for this effect must be inferred from other sources or from the primary studies it cites.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review describes public goods games with arbitrary group sizes and notes that punishment effects are observed even in large groups. Specific player counts are not detailed for all studies, but the dimension is discussed as relevant.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Repeated and one-shot games are discussed. The review notes that cooperation decays over time in repeated games without punishment, and that punishment can stabilize cooperation even in long games (e.g., 30 rounds in Figure 2).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution choices are described as continuous (e.g., 'tokens that they can either contribute to a project... or keep for themselves'), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The marginal per-capita return is described in general terms (e.g., 'each player receives a lower individual profit from the tokens contributed to the public good than from the tokens kept'), but no specific values are given.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher in all referenced experiments. No specific cost values are given.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as reducing the target's payoff at a cost to the punisher. The effectiveness per unit cost is not quantified.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward mechanisms are discussed as an alternative to punishment. The review notes that reward regimes do not sustain cooperation as effectively as punishment regimes.
- reward cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewarding is described as costly to the rewarder, but no specific cost values are given.
- reward technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewarding increases the recipient's payoff at a cost to the rewarder, but effectiveness per unit cost is not quantified.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. In at least one referenced experiment (Figure 2), the number of rounds (30) is known to participants. The review does not systematically discuss this dimension.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players are described as being informed about others' contributions before punishment or reward decisions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a review and does not present new experimental data or quantitative results on efficiency or group payoff.
- Outcomes discussed are primarily behavioral (contribution, cooperation, punishment) and neural, not direct measures of efficiency or welfare.
- Specific parameter values for game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are not systematically reported.
- The review synthesizes findings from multiple studies, so the mapping to specific game design dimensions is often indirect.
