Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are norm strength (adherence and enforcement), cooperation/coordination rates, and strategy proportions. Payoff is modeled and used to determine evolutionary fitness, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported. Some figures (e.g., Fig. 7) show payoffs under different conditions, but the main focus is on behavioral outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in evolutionary simulations of the PGG with punishment, higher societal threat leads to higher rates of cooperation and a greater proportion of norm-enforcing punishers. Under low threat, cooperation and norm enforcement are weaker. The presence of punishment is necessary to achieve high cooperation under threat; spatial structure alone is insufficient. The model also shows that norm strength (adherence and enforcement) increases rapidly in response to increased threat and declines gradually when threat is removed. While payoffs are modeled, the main reported outcomes are behavioral, not efficiency or group payoff per se.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that punishment increases cooperation and norm enforcement in PGG-like environments, especially under higher threat. However, it does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment enabled/disabled, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes. The model parameters (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are specified, and the model is robust to parameter changes, but the main outcomes are behavioral. The paper supports the qualitative prediction that enabling punishment increases cooperation and norm enforcement, which is likely to increase efficiency, but does not provide direct efficiency data for prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG groups are formed from agents on a 50x50 grid, each agent plays 4 games per timestep with neighbors (von Neumann neighborhood). Group size is not fixed but determined by local structure; typical group size is 5 (agent plus 4 neighbors).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 5000 timesteps; each timestep includes a game and punishment phase. No explicit finite horizon; evolutionary steady state is focus.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: agents either contribute c=1 or defect (contribute nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Multiplication factor r=3; group size typically 5 (agent plus 4 neighbors), so MPCR = 0.6. Parameter r is varied in robustness checks.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is λ=0.5 per punishment action.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment fine to target is ρ=1.5 per punishment action. Effectiveness is 3x cost.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents know the punishment strategy (reputation) of their co-players, which influences opportunistic cooperation. Robustness checks include cases where this is probabilistic.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents know which co-players are punishers and their punishment strategy; this is essential for opportunistic agents' decision-making.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and highly abstract; results may not quantitatively generalize to real-world or laboratory PGGs.
- Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported; main outcomes are behavioral (norm adherence, punishment, cooperation).
- No direct comparison of group efficiency with punishment enabled vs. disabled; only qualitative statements about necessity of punishment for high cooperation.
- No reward mechanism is modeled; only punishment is considered.
- Agents have limited strategy space (binary contribution, fixed punishment strategies).
- No communication or chat is modeled.
- Model assumes spatial structure (grid), which may not match all experimental PGGs.
