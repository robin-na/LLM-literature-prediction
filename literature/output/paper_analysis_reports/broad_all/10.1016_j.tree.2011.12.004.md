The paper is a review and conceptual analysis, focusing on the behavioral effects of punishment (e.g., increased cooperation, reduced cheating) in both human and non-human animal studies. It discusses payoff concepts in a theoretical sense but does not report empirical efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment can promote cooperation in laboratory public goods games with humans, but evidence for similar effects in non-human animals is rare. It argues that punishment is most likely to evolve in symmetric n-player games or asymmetric two-player games, especially when cheating is a discrete event and repeated interactions are likely. The paper highlights cognitive and ecological constraints that limit the prevalence of punishment in nature. While it discusses the theoretical efficiency of punishment (e.g., when it is wasteful or beneficial), it does not provide empirical data on efficiency or group payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and conceptual guidance on when punishment is likely to increase cooperation in public goods-like settings, emphasizing the importance of player number, time horizon, and the nature of cheating. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions and control efficiency. Its main value is in highlighting moderators and mechanisms that may inform the interpretation of empirical studies.
