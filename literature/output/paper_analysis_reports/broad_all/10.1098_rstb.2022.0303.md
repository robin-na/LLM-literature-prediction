Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variables are group size (as a proxy for efficiency/welfare), fitness, and the share of resources allocated to punishment and technology. Cooperation rate and hierarchy are also tracked, but the focus is on how institutional rules and group structure affect group-level payoffs and the ability to sustain cooperation as group size increases.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that institutionally arranged punishment in public goods games allows groups to sustain high levels of cooperation and achieve much larger group sizes (higher efficiency/welfare) than groups without such institutions. As groups grow, the cost of forming consensus over rules increases, but the evolution of hierarchy (a skewed distribution of influence) allows large groups to maintain efficient cooperation-promoting institutions by reducing the cost of consensus formation. Groups that invest just enough in punishment to deter defection, while allocating the rest to technology (increasing carrying capacity), achieve the highest efficiency. If hierarchy cannot evolve, large groups cannot sustain cooperation and remain small and inefficient. The model also shows that if the political game (consensus formation) is too difficult, groups may fail to maintain cooperation-promoting institutions, leading to lower efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that institutional rules are set optimally (not over- or under-investing in punishment). The model also highlights that the cost of consensus formation (political game) can limit the efficiency gains from punishment in large groups unless hierarchy evolves to reduce these costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by group size, the cost of institutional rule formation, and the ability to evolve or implement hierarchy. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is institutionally arranged and the costs of monitoring/sanctioning are covered by the group, rather than being individually altruistic.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical data.
- Punishment is institutionally arranged and not individually costly, which may differ from classic peer-punishment public goods games.
- The model does not include reward mechanisms, only punishment.
- No explicit modeling of communication, chat, or repeated rounds as in laboratory experiments.
- The model assumes vertical cultural transmission and does not address other learning mechanisms in detail.
- The model abstracts the process of consensus formation and does not model specific real-world institutional processes.
- Economic inequality within groups is not explicitly modeled, though discussed as a possible extension.
