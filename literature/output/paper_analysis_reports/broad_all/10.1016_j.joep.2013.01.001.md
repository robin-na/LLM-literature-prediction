# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment
Authors: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group contribution
- average tokens earned (reported as KRW earnings)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned
- reward assigned
- contribution rate
- punishment/reward per unit deviation from norm
- punishment/reward per round
- retaliatory punishment/reward
- end-game effect
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is group contribution (tokens allocated to the group account). Payoff/efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio to the full-cooperation benchmark, but average earnings are reported in KRW. The paper does not explicitly calculate or compare efficiency or welfare, but group contribution is a direct proxy for group payoff in this linear PGG.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). In the stranger condition (random rematching), punishment sustains higher contributions (11.38) than reward (9.21). The effect of punishment on cooperation is robust to group reshuffling, while reward is much less effective when strategic reciprocity is not possible. The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 3.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each phase consists of 10 rounds; total of 30 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects allocate 0-20 tokens to the group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each token in public account gives 0.5 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token used for punishment costs 1 point to punisher and reduces target's earnings by 3 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each token spent reduces target's earnings by 3 points).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is a separate treatment arm; in reward sessions, subjects can assign positive tokens to increase others' earnings.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token used for reward costs 1 point to rewarder and increases target's earnings by 3 points.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (each token spent increases target's earnings by 3 points).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds in each phase (10 rounds per phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are shown other members' contributions and earnings (with anonymized IDs).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are not told who punished or rewarded them; IDs are randomized each round to prevent tracking.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum) is not directly reported; only group contribution and average earnings are provided.
- The study uses only 3-player groups and a specific MPCR (0.5), limiting generalizability to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or welfare; all main results are in terms of contribution, not payoff ratios.
- Punishment and reward are tested in separate sessions (between-subjects), not within the same groups.
- No communication is allowed, so results may not generalize to settings with chat or discussion.
