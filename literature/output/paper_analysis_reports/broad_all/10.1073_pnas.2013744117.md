# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Voluntary restrictions on self-reliance increase cooperation and mitigate wealth inequality
Authors: Gross, J., & Böhm, R.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- resource waste
- within-group inequality (Gini coefficient)
- public goods creation rate
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- frequency of self-reliance
- voting to restrict self-reliance
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings, resource waste (deviation from efficient investment), and within-group inequality. Behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rate and self-reliance frequency are also reported, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when individuals can solve a collective action problem either individually (self-reliance) or collectively (public good), the availability of self-reliance crowds out cooperation and increases inequality, especially when self-reliance is relatively cheap. However, when groups are given the opportunity to vote to abolish the individual solution, they overwhelmingly do so under medium and high interdependence, which leads to higher rates of public goods provision, lower resource waste, higher group earnings, and reduced inequality. The effect is particularly strong when there is a risk of miscoordination (medium interdependence) or when group members differ in their ability to be self-reliant (wealth heterogeneity). The study does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms in the standard sense, but self-reliance by richer group members can function as a coercive device, pressuring poorer members to cooperate.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that institutional design features that restrict individual opt-out/self-reliance options can substantially increase efficiency and group earnings in public-goods-like environments, especially when the efficiency gains from cooperation are significant (i.e., when self-reliance is costly relative to the public good). While the study does not manipulate punishment, it shows that endogenous institutional restrictions (analogous to mandatory participation or exclusion of exit options) can have large positive effects on efficiency, similar to or greater than those observed for punishment in standard PGGs. For prediction, the presence or absence of self-reliance/exit options, and the ability to restrict them, should be considered as key moderators of efficiency, alongside standard game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players; this was fixed and justified to avoid voting ties.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group played 3 blocks of 18 rounds (54 rounds total).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (participants could allocate any amount of their endowment to public, private, or keep), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly labeled, but the public solution requires 200 RP total (40 per person for 5 players), and the individual solution cost varies (40, 60, 80). The efficiency gain from cooperation is manipulated via the relative cost of self-reliance.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the block structure and number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants received feedback on how group members allocated resources, whether the public target was reached, which group members reached their individual target, and earnings for the round.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or reward mechanisms are present; the study focuses on self-reliance/exit options rather than sanctions.
- The public goods game is a variant with an individual solution (exit/self-reliance) option, not a standard PGG.
- All groups have 5 players; results may not generalize to other group sizes.
- The study is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- Wealth heterogeneity is manipulated only in the second study; most groups are homogeneous in endowment.
- Voting to restrict self-reliance is by simple majority and anonymous; real-world institutional change may involve different processes.
