# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strong reciprocity and team production: Theory and evidence
Authors: Carpenter, J., Bowles, S., Gintis, H., & Hwang, S. H.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency and amount
- individual-level altruism and reciprocity measures
- response to punishment (change in contribution)
- probability of being punished
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates, punishment assigned, and behavioral responses to punishment. The paper discusses the implications for group welfare and efficiency, but does not directly report group payoff, efficiency, or surplus as a primary outcome. Some calculations and discussion relate to the marginal costs and benefits of punishment and shirking, but these are not the main reported outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that allowing costly punishment in a public goods game prevents the typical decay in contributions over time, with average contributions rising or remaining stable across rounds. Punishment is widely used and is targeted at those who contribute less. The amount of punishment assigned increases with the degree of shirking and the harm imposed by shirking (i.e., higher MPCR), and decreases with group size. Individuals with higher reciprocity are more likely to punish, while those with higher unconditional altruism punish less. Punishment leads to increased contributions by shirkers, but the direct material return to the punisher is less than the cost incurred. The paper does not directly report group efficiency or total earnings, but the sustained high contributions suggest improved group welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly peer punishment in public goods games increases and sustains cooperation (contribution rates) compared to the standard game without punishment. The effect is robust across different group sizes (4 and 8) and MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), but the magnitude of punishment and contributions is sensitive to these parameters: punishment and contributions decrease as group size increases, and increase as MPCR increases. While the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, the sustained high contributions in the punishment condition imply higher efficiency than in the control. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in smaller groups and with higher MPCR, but the effect is likely attenuated as group size increases.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Two group sizes were used: 4 and 8. Punishment and contributions decrease as group size increases.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session lasted 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; decisions were made anonymously at computer terminals.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could contribute any fraction of their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR (q/n) was manipulated: 0.3 and 0.7. Higher MPCR led to more punishment and higher contributions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher: 1 EMU to impose a 2 EMU penalty on another player.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment technology: each EMU spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by 2 EMUs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the game lasted 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the total group contribution, their own gross earnings, and the contributions of other team members (in random order).

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total payoff, or surplus; main outcomes are contribution rates and punishment behavior.
- The experiment uses a 'strangers' protocol (random reassignment to groups each round), which may limit generalizability to fixed-group or repeated interaction settings.
- No direct comparison to a no-punishment control group within this experiment; efficiency gains are inferred from sustained contributions and comparison to prior literature.
- The laboratory setting and student sample may limit external validity.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so results pertain only to punishment, not to positive incentives.
