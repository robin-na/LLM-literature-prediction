## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. The review emphasizes that the presence of punishment alone is not sufficient for efficiency gains; the structure and normative context of punishment matter greatly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and integrative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff unless punishment is normatively constrained to reduce antisocial punishment and collateral costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the presence of normative or institutional constraints, the prevalence of antisocial punishment, and the cultural context. Game design dimensions that affect the cost, targeting, and normative context of punishment are likely to be critical moderators of efficiency outcomes. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes, but it cautions against assuming that increased cooperation from punishment always translates into higher efficiency.

- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Repeated games (e.g., 10 or 15 rounds) are discussed as standard; decay of cooperation over time is a key regularity.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Pre-play communication (chat) is discussed as strongly increasing cooperation rates.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both continuous and binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games are referenced, but most examples are continuous.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is referenced as a key parameter in public goods games, but not systematically varied or analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. High cost of punishment is repeatedly cited as the reason why increased cooperation does not always translate into higher efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is discussed in terms of its impact on cooperation and antisocial punishment, but not quantified.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Mutual reward opportunities are discussed as increasing cooperation rates.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Subjects are described as knowing the number of rounds in finitely repeated games.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players observe others' cooperation levels and payoffs in standard public goods games.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new experimental or quantitative efficiency data.
- Efficiency and welfare outcomes are discussed in general terms, not with direct measurements or effect sizes.
- The review highlights the importance of normative constraints and institutional context, but does not systematically analyze how specific game design dimensions quantitatively moderate efficiency effects.
- Cultural variation and the prevalence of antisocial punishment are discussed, but not linked to specific design parameters in a predictive way.
- No direct analysis of the effect of control (no-punishment) efficiency on treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency.
