## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that even weak social sanctioning (punishment) between communities can prevent the collapse of cooperation that would otherwise occur due to biophysical coupling (resource sharing). When only biophysical coupling is present and communities do not communicate or sanction each other, increased resource access can undermine cooperation in one community. However, if communities can sanction defectors in both communities, or if they communicate when setting norms, cooperation is more robust. The model identifies parameter regions where cooperation or defection collapses, depending on the strength of biophysical and social coupling and on whether communities collaborate in norm setting. The findings highlight the importance of cross-community sanctioning and communication for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource systems.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (social sanctioning) in public-goods-like environments can prevent the collapse of cooperation that may occur due to resource sharing or biophysical coupling. However, the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so its relevance to predicting efficiency outcomes is indirect. The results suggest that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms, as well as opportunities for communication and norm setting, are important moderators of cooperation stability. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that punishment will have a more positive effect on cooperation (and likely efficiency) when social coupling is present, especially if communities can communicate and set norms collaboratively. However, quantitative predictions about efficiency cannot be extracted from this paper.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each community is modeled as a population of n agents (e.g., n=50 in Figure 2), but the model is mean-field and does not vary player count as a treatment.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication between communities during norm setting is explicitly modeled as either present (collaborative scenario) or absent (uncollaborative scenario). Communication enables more robust cooperation.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctioning (punishment) is modeled as costless to cooperators (punishers), following the TSL model. The strength of sanctioning is parameterized (e.g., s, g, T), but cost to the punisher is zero.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized (e.g., s, g, T, λ). Increased social coupling (λ) increases the effectiveness of punishment across communities, which increases cooperation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communities may share information about their resources during norm setting (collaborative scenario), which is analogous to showing summary information. This increases cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as primary outcomes.
- Sanctioning (punishment) is assumed to be costless to cooperators, which may not reflect real-world or experimental settings.
- The model uses mean-field replicator dynamics and does not capture stochastic or network effects present in real groups.
- Player count, resource parameters, and other features are fixed or only varied in robustness checks, not as primary treatments.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented; all results are from mathematical analysis and simulation.
- The model does not include reward mechanisms or positive incentives.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of cooperation rates and stability, not directly in terms of efficiency or welfare.
