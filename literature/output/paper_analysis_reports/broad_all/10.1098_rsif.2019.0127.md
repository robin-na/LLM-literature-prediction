## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, the ability to observe the presence of punishment institutions is crucial for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff. When institutions are not observable, anti-social punishment can invade and reduce cooperation and efficiency. When institutions are observable, a small minority of pro-social institution supporters can sustain high levels of cooperation and group payoff, even in the presence of anti-social punishment strategies. The model demonstrates that the evolutionary outcome is robust to the inclusion of all possible strategies, and that the coexistence of institution supporters and opportunists is stable. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore context-dependent: positive when observability and conditional strategies are present, negative or neutral when they are absent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on whether players can observe the presence of punishment institutions and condition their behavior accordingly. If the game design allows for observability and conditional strategies, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. If observability is absent, enabling punishment—especially if anti-social punishment is possible—may reduce efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of supporting institutions and group size affect the probability of institution implementation and thus the efficiency outcome. For prediction, the presence or absence of observability/conditionality, the possibility of anti-social punishment, and the cost structure are key moderators.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The evolutionary dynamics assume infinite or large populations and strong selection; real-world dynamics may differ.
- Implementation details of institutions (e.g., corruption, group heterogeneity) are not modeled.
- No explicit modeling of communication, repeated finite rounds, or reward mechanisms.
- The model assumes that once implemented, institutions function as intended, which may not hold in practice.
