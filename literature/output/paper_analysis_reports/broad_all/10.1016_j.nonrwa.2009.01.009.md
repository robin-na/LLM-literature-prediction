## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews how different sources of power—such as punishment, reward, asymmetric dependence, and information—are operationalized in economic games, including public goods games. It highlights that punishment power is often used to enforce cooperation, but can also provoke retaliation and cycles of revenge, potentially undermining group outcomes. Delegation of punishment power and the use of centralized or third-party punishment mechanisms can help sustain cooperation. The review also notes that reward can be as effective as punishment in some contexts, but is less well understood. The paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative findings about efficiency or group payoff, but it synthesizes behavioral mechanisms that are relevant to the prediction of such outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual and mechanistic context for how punishment and reward power affect cooperation in public goods games, but does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff outcomes. It is useful for understanding the potential pathways through which punishment may increase or decrease efficiency, such as by deterring free-riding or provoking retaliation. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be moderated by factors such as the structure of punishment (individual vs. centralized), the possibility of retaliation, and the availability of reward. However, it does not provide data that can be directly used for quantitative prediction.
