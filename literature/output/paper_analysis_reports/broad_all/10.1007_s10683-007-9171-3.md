## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study experimentally varies the effectiveness of punishment in a repeated linear public goods game with and without punishment. It finds that contributions increase monotonically with punishment effectiveness. However, welfare (group earnings) only improves relative to the no-punishment control when punishment effectiveness is high (at least 3 out of 4). For low effectiveness, the cost of punishment outweighs the gains from increased cooperation, resulting in lower welfare than the control. The results highlight that the design parameter of punishment effectiveness is crucial: only sufficiently strong punishment leads to both higher cooperation and higher efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games as a function of punishment effectiveness. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is known, the effect of enabling punishment depends strongly on the punishment technology: low effectiveness can reduce efficiency, while high effectiveness can increase it. The findings suggest that the marginal per-capita return, group size, and other design features held constant, the key moderator is punishment effectiveness. The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design, especially regarding the punishment technology parameter.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions use n=4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments last for 10 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0 ≤ c_i ≤ y.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher is 1 ECU per punishment point in all punishment treatments.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per point) is varied across treatments: e = 1, 2, 3, 4. Higher effectiveness leads to higher efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (10) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributions, participants are informed about group total and individual contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are assigned random IDs each period; punishment is anonymous within the group for each period.

## Important Limitations
- The results are specific to the parameterization used (n=4, mpcr=0.4, 10 rounds, no communication, fixed matching).
- The study only varies punishment effectiveness; other dimensions such as punishment cost, group size, or mpcr are not manipulated.
- The time horizon is relatively short (10 rounds); longer games may yield different welfare results.
- Punishment is always costly and decentralized; no central enforcement or reward mechanism is studied.
- Generalizability to field settings or other public goods environments may be limited.
