# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Does she compensate the victim while he punishes the perpetrator? No gender differences in anonymous economic games across 11 nations
Authors: Dorrough, A. R., Olsson, M. I. T., Froehlich, L., Glöckner, A., & Martiny, S. E.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral Decision Making
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Amount transferred as punishment (by third party)
- Amount transferred as compensation (by third party)
- Gender differences in punishment and compensation behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amounts of money (Talers) that third-party observers transfer to punish a perpetrator or compensate a victim after observing an unfair allocation. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. The study focuses on behavioral choices (punishment/compensation) rather than on group or collective payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two large studies (one multinational, one German student sample), there is no evidence for systematic gender differences in third-party punishment or compensation behavior in response to unfairness. Both men and women are more likely to compensate the victim than to punish the perpetrator. Increasing the effectiveness of punishment (punishment impact per unit cost) does not substantially change this pattern. The study does not analyze or report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff, and the game is not a repeated public goods game but a one-shot third-party punishment/compensation scenario.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the behavioral use of punishment and compensation options in a third-party context, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves norm enforcement and costly punishment, but is not a repeated or group-based contribution game. The main decision-relevant finding is that, even when punishment is made more effective, participants prefer compensation over punishment, suggesting that punishment may be underutilized in similar designs. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not measured, the paper does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The evidence is most relevant for understanding behavioral preferences for punishment versus compensation, not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three players: Player C (perpetrator), Player D (victim), and Player E (third-party observer who can punish or compensate).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main scenario is a one-shot (single-round) decision for each unfair event. In Study 2, participants make several decisions in separate rounds (punishment-only, compensation-only, both), but each is a one-shot scenario.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; all interactions are anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (punishment/compensation) is continuous: Player E can choose any amount (0 to 50 Talers) to punish or compensate.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: Player E pays 1 Taler to reduce Player C's payoff. In Study 1 and part of Study 2, each punishment Taler reduces the perpetrator's payoff by 3 (1:3 ratio); in another condition in Study 2, the ratio is 1:5.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated: 1:3 (Study 1 and part of Study 2) and 1:5 (Study 2 high efficiency condition).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation (rewarding the victim) is available as an option for Player E.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation is costly: Player E pays 1 Taler to increase Player D's payoff by 3 (Study 1 and part of Study 2); no mention of other ratios.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation effectiveness is 1:3 (1 Taler spent increases victim's payoff by 3). No other ratios are reported.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure: they are told about the number of scenarios/rounds and that the game is one-shot or has a fixed number of decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Interactions are anonymous; punished or compensated players do not know the identity of the punisher/compensator.

## Important Limitations
- The design is not a repeated public goods game but a one-shot third-party punishment/compensation game, limiting direct applicability to standard PGG environments.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported; only individual behavioral choices (punishment/compensation amounts) are measured.
- The use of the strategy method (hypothetical decisions for scenarios) rather than direct real-time interaction may affect emotional engagement and behavior.
- Potential demand effects due to gender salience, though authors argue this is unlikely to explain the null results.
- The context is highly controlled and abstract, possibly omitting real-world cues that could moderate punishment or compensation behavior.
