## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in evolutionary simulations of a one-shot trust game with partner choice and partial information, both trust (willingness to invest with unknown partners) and costly punishment (rejecting profitable but unfair offers) can co-evolve and are adaptive, maximizing average payoffs. The benefit of punishment for efficiency only arises when trust is also present, and only in environments where information about partners is partially but not fully available. When information is fully transparent or there is no partner choice, punishment does not increase payoffs. The effect is robust to changes in the number of partners, population size, investment multiplier, selection intensity, and mutation rates. The model predicts that as the value of investment increases, the advantage and prevalence of fairness norms and punishment also increase.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency benefit of punishment in trust-game-like environments depends critically on the presence of both partner choice and partial information. If a public-goods or trust game is designed with multiple potential partners and only partial information about others' behavior, enabling punishment (the ability to reject unfair offers) can increase group efficiency, but only if players are also willing to trust unknown partners. If either partner choice or trust is absent, punishment does not improve efficiency. The model also predicts that the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with the investment multiplier (analogous to MPCR in PGGs). For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by information structure, partner choice, and the value of cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size (number of investors and trustees) is a parameter in the model, varied in sensitivity analysis (main text: 500 each; Appendix B: 5000 each).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent plays 500 rounds per generation; the game is modeled as one-shot per round, but repeated for evolutionary selection.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The investment multiplier b (analogous to MPCR) is varied (b=2,3,4,5). Higher b increases the state space and benefit of punishment/trust for efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as rejecting profitable but unfair offers, which is costly in the sense of forgoing profit. The cost is endogenous (opportunity cost), not a fixed parameter.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the impact of rejection on trustee behavior (raising return rates). The model shows that punishment is only effective (increases efficiency) when trust is present and information is partial.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and evolutionary, not empirical; results are based on simulations, not human subjects.
- The game modeled is a one-shot trust game with partner choice and partial information, not a standard public goods game; transferability to PGGs may be limited.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of unfair offers (costly punishment), not as direct costly reduction of others' payoffs.
- No explicit reward mechanism is modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or repeated interactions are included; the model does not capture all social dynamics present in real-world or experimental PGGs.
- The cost of punishment is endogenous (opportunity cost), not a fixed parameter.
- The model assumes evolutionary adaptation; real human behavior may be more flexible and context-dependent.
