# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The economics of altruistic punishment and the maintenance of cooperation
Authors: Egas, M., & Riedl, A.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- individual earnings
- relative gains (earnings in punishment treatment minus earnings in no-punishment treatment, divided by earnings in no-punishment treatment)
- monetary effect of punishment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- punishment assigned
- threshold of free-riding that goes unpunished
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variables are group and individual earnings, and relative gains compared to the control. The paper also reports on contribution rates and punishment behavior, but the main focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment only maintains cooperation when it is relatively effective (low cost to punisher, high impact on punished). However, even when cooperation is sustained, group and individual payoffs are lower than in the control without punishment, because the costs of punishment exceed the benefits from increased cooperation. The threshold of free-riding that goes unpunished increases as punishment becomes less effective. The main determinant of punishment's effect is the cost-to-impact ratio. The results suggest that altruistic punishment alone is insufficient to maintain cooperation efficiently, as it reduces group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard PGG with anonymous, one-shot (perfect stranger) interactions, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency and can reduce it, especially when punishment is effective (low cost, high impact). The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost-to-impact ratio: only when punishment is cheap and effective does it sustain cooperation, but even then, group payoffs are lower than in the no-punishment control. For prediction, this suggests that unless punishment is both cheap and highly effective, enabling punishment will not improve and may reduce efficiency relative to control. The control efficiency (without punishment) is always higher than with punishment in this design. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions, especially punishment cost and effectiveness.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per public goods game; fixed across all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 6 rounds per session; groups reshuffled each round (perfect stranger design).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication between players during the game; only allowed to ask experimenters questions during instructions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject could contribute any amount between 0 and 20 EMU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each EMU contributed returns 0.5 EMU to each of 3 group members (total multiplier 1.5, MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per point varied: 1 or 3 EMU per point assigned, depending on treatment (T13, T11, T33, T31).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment impact per point varied: 1 or 3 EMU per point, depending on treatment. Effectiveness (impact-to-cost ratio) is a key manipulated variable.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told they would play 6 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, each group member was informed about the other group members' contributions and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants were only told how many punishment points they received, not from whom.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used a 'perfect stranger' design with full anonymity and no repeated interaction between the same individuals, which may limit generalizability to real-world settings with reputation or reciprocity.
- The time horizon was short (6 rounds), so long-term effects of punishment on efficiency and cooperation are not observed.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism was included, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
- The study does not address the effects of counter-punishment or retaliation, which may occur in less anonymous or repeated settings.
- The subject pool, while more heterogeneous than typical student samples, was still self-selected and may not represent all populations.
