# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Participation and punishment
Authors: Myers, C. D.
Journal: Journal of Theoretical Politics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average tokens earned per round
- net loss from being punished
Non-payoff outcomes:
- participation rate (turnout)
- punishment frequency
- punishment target (abstainers vs. participators)
- participation rate by social value orientation (SVO)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is participation (turnout) rate. Payoff-related outcomes are reported as descriptive statistics (average tokens per round, net loss from punishment), but the main analysis and treatment effects are on participation, not efficiency or group payoff. The game is constructed such that participation is efficiency-reducing, so increased participation does not increase group welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that when punishment is possible, participation (turnout) increases dramatically (from 60% to 88%). Punishment is overwhelmingly directed at abstainers, and abstainers incur a net loss from being punished. Egoist subjects are more responsive to punishment than pro-social subjects, leading to a more egoist composition among participants when punishment is available. However, because participation is costly and does not increase the value of the prize, increased participation does not improve group efficiency or welfare. The study does not report treatment effects on efficiency or group payoff, and the design makes clear that increased participation is not efficiency-enhancing.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment increases participation in a binary-choice, team-based contest game where participation is individually costly and does not increase group welfare. However, it does not provide evidence that punishment increases efficiency or group payoff; in fact, the design ensures that increased participation is efficiency-reducing. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment depends critically on the structure of the game: in games where participation is not efficiency-enhancing, punishment may increase costly participation without improving (and possibly reducing) efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding behavioral responses (participation/cooperation) to punishment, not for predicting efficiency outcomes in standard public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round consists of two teams of four (8 players per session, 4 per team).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment consists of 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed; only actions are observed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (participation) is binary: participate (vote) or abstain.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 token per punishment; punished loses 2 tokens per punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment costs 1 token to the punisher and reduces the punished's payoff by 2 tokens.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds (20 per treatment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the actions of their three teammates after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players are told how many times they were punished but not by whom.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; participation is efficiency-reducing, so findings do not generalize to efficiency effects in standard PGGs.
- The main outcome is participation (turnout), not efficiency or group payoff.
- Payoff-related outcomes are only reported as descriptive statistics, not as treatment effects.
- Punishment is only possible within teams (politically homogeneous groups); effects may differ in heterogeneous networks.
- No communication or coordination is allowed, which may limit generalizability to real-world settings.
- The experiment uses undergraduate subjects in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
