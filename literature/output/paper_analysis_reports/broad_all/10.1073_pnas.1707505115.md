# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment diminishes the benefits of network reciprocity in social dilemma experiments
Authors: Li, X. L., Jusup, M., Wang, Z., Li, H. J., Shi, L., Podobnik, B., Stanley, H. E., Havlin, S., & Boccaletti, S.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- payoff per round
- total payoff
- correlation between payoff and cooperation/defection
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- frequency of punishment
- assortment (clustering of cooperators)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is payoff per round, with additional analysis of action frequencies and assortment. Efficiency is not reported as a normalized ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but payoff per round and its distribution are the central payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compared three treatments: (1) a well-mixed repeated PD (control), (2) a static network PD with two neighbors (network reciprocity), and (3) a static network PD with punishment available. Network reciprocity increased both cooperation and payoff relative to the well-mixed control. However, adding punishment did not further increase cooperation and actually reduced the median payoff per round back to the control level. Punishment also reduced assortment (clustering of cooperators) and, in larger neighborhoods, erased the positive correlation between cooperation and payoff. Thus, punishment in this setting is ineffective or even counterproductive for group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated prisoner's dilemma games on static networks (with two or four neighbors), enabling costly punishment (with a 1:4 cost-to-impact ratio) does not improve and may even reduce group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The negative effect is especially clear in payoff-based outcomes: median payoff per round is lower with punishment than without, and the benefit of network reciprocity is erased. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar networked PD or adjacent PGG-like environments, adding punishment may not increase efficiency and could reduce it, especially as neighborhood size increases. The findings are most directly applicable to static network PDs with small groups, no communication, and fixed payoff matrices.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The key manipulated variable is neighborhood size (2 or 4).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consisted of 50 rounds. Participants were unaware of the exact number of rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants; they were isolated in computer cubicles.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are all-or-nothing: participants choose between cooperation, defection, or punishment (when available).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 1 unit to the punisher and inflicts a 4-unit loss on the target (1:4 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4 units lost per 1 unit spent (1:4). The paper speculates that higher effectiveness might change results.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is present in the relevant treatment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were unaware of the exact number of rounds to avoid endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could see the result of each action and the 'opponent number' after each round, but no mention of summary statistics or full history.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Opponents were anonymous except for an 'opponent number' to examine the result of each action. It is implied that players could identify which neighbor punished them in a given round, but not their real identity.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to PGGs with continuous contributions or different payoff structures.
- Only one punishment cost-to-impact ratio (1:4) is tested; higher or lower ratios might yield different results.
- No reward mechanism is included; the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- Neighborhood size is limited to 2 and 4; effects in larger or more complex networks are not explored.
- Efficiency is measured as payoff per round, not as a normalized ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
- Participants are undergraduate students from two Chinese universities; generalizability to other populations is uncertain.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or reputation are not tested.
- The number of players per session is not explicitly stated, though neighborhood size is controlled.
