## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper shows that in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games with costly punishment, the addition of a punishment option does not increase cooperation or group payoffs in evolutionary simulations. Even when Nash equilibrium analysis allows for cooperative equilibria using punishment, evolutionary selection in finite populations favors generous tit-for-tat strategies that do not use costly punishment. Costly punishment is rarely used, and when it is, it is associated with lower payoffs. The presence of costly punishment does not allow cooperation to evolve in parameter regions where it would not already be supported by direct reciprocity alone. These findings are robust to variations in game parameters and are supported by experimental data from Dreber et al. (2008).

## Prediction Guidance
For the prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling costly punishment in a repeated two-player PD-like environment does not increase efficiency or group payoff, regardless of the punishment cost or effectiveness. Evolutionary dynamics select against strategies that use costly punishment, and the most successful strategies are those that reciprocate cooperation and defect against defectors (generous tit-for-tat), not those that punish. Thus, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar repeated games, one should not expect an increase in efficiency or group payoff, and may even expect a negative association between punishment use and payoff. However, the results are specific to repeated two-player PDs and may not generalize to larger group public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All analysis and simulations are for two-player repeated games (repeated Prisoner's Dilemma).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are repeated with a continuation probability w, so the number of rounds is stochastic (geometric distribution). Simulations also consider the limit w=1 (infinite repetition) and w<1 (finite expected rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between discrete actions: cooperate, defect, or punish. No continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is a key parameter. Increasing alpha reduces the use of punishment and does not increase cooperation or payoff. Even when alpha is low, punishment is not favored.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (beta) is varied. Increasing beta increases the use of punishment relative to defection, but punishment is still not favored and does not increase efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the continuation probability w, which determines the expected number of rounds. In the experimental comparison, w=0.75 is used.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the repeated PD, each player knows the co-player's action (C, D, or P) in the previous round, so punishment is always attributable.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis and simulations are limited to two-player repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, not multi-player public goods games.
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio of realized to maximum possible group payoff; payoff analysis is at the level of expected or average payoff per player.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies), not direct measures of group efficiency.
- Findings may not generalize to larger groups or to public goods games with different structures.
- No reward mechanisms are considered.
- No communication or reputation effects are modeled.
