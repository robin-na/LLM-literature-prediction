# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Does dishonesty really invite third-party punishment? Results of a more stringent test
Authors: Konishi, N., & Ohtsubo, Y.
Journal: Biology Letters
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of third-party punishment
- frequency of third-party reward
- cost participants were willing to incur to punish or reward
- proportion of punishers, rewarders, and onlookers
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and cost of third-party punishment and reward. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. The cost variable is individual and not aggregated to group-level efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that, even after removing five key methodological confounds (including adding a reward option and ensuring anonymity), a significant minority of third-party observers are willing to incur a personal cost to punish a dishonest trustee, even when the trustee's allocation is fair. The rate of punishment is lower than in previous studies (27% vs. 53%), but still significantly higher in the dishonesty condition than in the honesty condition. Participants were more likely to punish than reward in response to dishonesty. The main outcomes are the frequency and cost of punishment/reward, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that third-party punishment of norm violations (specifically dishonesty) can occur even when methodological confounds are controlled. However, the design is a modified trust game with third-party punishment and reward, not a public goods game or direct group efficiency context. The outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward rates and costs), not efficiency or group payoff. Thus, while the paper supports the robustness of third-party punishment as a behavioral phenomenon, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. Its main value is in showing that punishment can persist even when alternative explanations are minimized, but it does not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three roles: trustor, trustee, and third-party observer (participant). The participant always acts as the third-party.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a one-shot (single round) interaction.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. The trustee sends a pre-play message to the trustor, but there is no chat or communication between the third-party and other players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward choices are continuous (participants choose how much to spend), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:2 (participant pays c, trustee loses 2c).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2:1 (each unit spent by the punisher reduces the trustee's payoff by 2 units).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could choose to reward the trustee at the same cost/benefit ratio as punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1:2 (participant pays c, trustee gains 2c).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 2:1 (each unit spent by the rewarder increases the trustee's payoff by 2 units).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a one-shot (single round) interaction.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observe the full transaction between trustor and trustee, including messages and allocations.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward decisions are fully anonymous; neither the trustor, trustee, nor experimenter interacting with the participant knows the decision.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a modified trust game with third-party punishment and reward, not a public goods game or direct group cooperation context.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual-level punishment/reward behavior is measured.
- The design is one-shot, not repeated or multi-round.
- The study focuses on punishment of dishonesty (not fairness) and does not address efficiency consequences.
- Sample is limited to Japanese undergraduates; generalizability may be limited.
