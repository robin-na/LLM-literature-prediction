# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Modeling reciprocation and cooperation in primates: evidence for a punishing strategy
Authors: Chen, M. K., & Hauser, M.
Journal: Journal of Theoretical Biology
Year: 2005

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- pulling rate (cooperation rate)
- model fit to behavioral strategies (e.g., tit-for-tat, punish-2, punish-3, stimulus-response, etc.)
- error rates of strategy adherence
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are behavioral: the frequency and pattern of cooperation (pulling) and the fit of various strategic models to observed behavior. No explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The analysis is focused on identifying which behavioral strategies best describe the monkeys' actions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that tamarins' cooperative behavior in repeated food-exchange games is best described by 'punish-2' and 'punish-3' strategies, which are more punishing than standard tit-for-tat. These strategies require two or more consecutive cooperative acts to restart cooperation after a breakdown, leading to stable but sometimes brittle cooperation. The analysis uses maximum-likelihood fitting of a suite of behavioral models, showing that these punishing strategies account for over 90% of the posterior model weight. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, and all results are based on behavioral patterns (pulling rates and model fits).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. However, it does provide indirect evidence that more punishing strategies (requiring multiple acts of cooperation to restore cooperation) can stabilize cooperation in repeated dyadic interactions. The findings may inform mechanistic expectations about how punishment-like strategies affect cooperation dynamics, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency changes due to punishment in PGGs. The game is not a standard PGG, and the outcomes are not payoff-based.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments are dyadic (2 players: one actor, one recipient per trial, alternating roles).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sessions typically consist of 24 trials (see Experiment 2), with multiple sessions per experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trial is a binary choice: pull (cooperate) or not pull (defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the main repeated food-exchange game, pulling gives 1 food to the other, 0 to self (actor incurs cost, recipient gets benefit). In some variants, payoffs differ (e.g., Experiment 3), but the main model is 1-to-1 transfer.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or a direct variant; it is a repeated dyadic food-exchange game.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are present; 'punishing' refers to behavioral strategies (withholding cooperation), not costly punishment.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates, model fits).
- The findings may not generalize to multi-player or standard PGG environments.
- Subjects are non-human primates, which may limit direct applicability to human economic games.
