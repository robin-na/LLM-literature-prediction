Outcomes are primarily behavioral (strategy composition, phase transitions), not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment driven by inequity aversion can promote the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when punishment costs are low and fines are high. Inequity-averse cooperators (IACs) can invade defectors and dominate the population under favorable parameter regimes, but their effectiveness is limited by internal conflict (punishing other cooperators or IACs) and the presence of non-punishing cooperators. Inequity-averse defectors (IADs) can also promote cooperation by forming protective barriers around cooperators, reducing the ability of defectors to invade. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the ratio of fine to cost, the synergy factor (MPCR), and the spatial structure. However, the paper does not report on group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency is not established.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment mechanisms—specifically, those based on inequity aversion—can increase the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games, particularly when punishment is not too costly and fines are substantial. The findings suggest that the design of punishment (who is targeted, how costs and fines scale) and the spatial structure of interactions are important moderators. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Instead, it supports the idea that punishment can change the behavioral composition of the group, which may be associated with higher efficiency in related models, but this link is not demonstrated here.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each local public goods game involves 5 players (focal individual plus 4 neighbors on a square lattice). The total population is L x L (L=200), but local group size is always 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10^8 to 8x10^8 generations (rounds) until stationary state is reached.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: cooperators contribute a fixed amount (c=1), defectors contribute nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy coefficient r is varied (e.g., r=3.5, r=4). MPCR is r/5 (group size 5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost coefficient (alpha) is varied; lower alpha (cost) increases the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment fine coefficient (beta) is varied; higher beta (fine) increases punishment intensity. The ratio beta/alpha is key for effectiveness.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players (agents) know their own and neighbors' payoffs, which is necessary for inequity aversion-based punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; all outcomes are based on the prevalence of strategies (behavioral, not payoff-based).
- Findings are based on agent-based evolutionary simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes spatial structure (square lattice) and local interactions, which may not generalize to well-mixed or real-world settings.
- Punishment is based on inequity aversion (payoff comparison), not on direct observation of contribution or defection, which differs from most experimental PGGs.
- No reward mechanism is considered; only punishment is modeled.
- The link between increased cooperation prevalence and actual group efficiency is assumed but not demonstrated.
