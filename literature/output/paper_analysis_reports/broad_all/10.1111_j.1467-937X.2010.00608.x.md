# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Choosing the Carrot or the Stick? Endogenous Institutional Choice in Social Dilemma Situations
Authors: Sutter, M., Haigner, S., & Kocher, M. G.
Journal: Review of Economic Studies
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group profit
- average group earnings
- efficiency (implied by profit relative to maximum possible)
- total coins generated
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment frequency
- reward frequency
- voting participation
- institutional choice
- punishment/reward assignment patterns
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment/reward use, voting) and payoff-based outcomes (average profits, efficiency implications). Efficiency is not always reported as a ratio, but average profits are compared across treatments and interpreted as efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that enabling groups to endogenously choose their institution (via voting) leads to higher contributions and higher group profits (efficiency) than exogenously imposed institutions, holding the institution constant. Both punishment and reward options increase contributions and profits compared to the standard VCM, but the effect is much stronger when the punishment/reward technology is highly effective (high leverage). However, punishment is rarely chosen when groups can choose, likely due to its costliness; groups prefer reward when it is effective. In exogenous treatments, high-leverage reward yields the highest profits, followed by high-leverage punishment, with the standard VCM lowest. The efficiency gains from punishment are offset by the cost of punishing, while reward can be efficiency-enhancing if its leverage is high. The positive effect of endogenous choice is not explained by selection of more cooperative types into certain institutions, but rather by the act of participation itself.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players and 10 rounds, enabling punishment (especially with high effectiveness) increases group efficiency (average profit) compared to the standard VCM, but the efficiency gain is limited by the cost of punishment. Reward, when highly effective, yields even higher efficiency than punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive but smaller than the effect on contributions, due to the costliness of punishment. Endogenous institutional choice (voting) further increases efficiency for any given institution. For prediction, the presence and effectiveness of punishment/reward, the cost/impact ratio, and whether the institution is chosen endogenously or exogenously are all important moderators. Control efficiency (standard VCM) is always lower than with effective punishment or reward, but the size of the efficiency gain depends on the punishment/reward technology and institutional choice procedure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games have 10 periods/rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0 ≤ c ≤ 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (γ) is 0.4 (multiplier per capita return).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is binary; cost to punisher is fixed per use. Cost is not numerically specified in the main text, but is described as costly and less than in Fehr & Gächter (2000). High-leverage punishment is more effective but also more costly in terms of group efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (leverage |L|) is manipulated: low (1) or high (3). Higher leverage increases contributions and profits, but high-leverage punishment is rarely chosen endogenously.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward option is available in some treatments; groups can choose between VCM, punishment, or reward.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is costly to the rewarder and beneficial to the recipient. Cost per use is fixed; high-leverage reward is more effective. High-leverage reward yields the highest group profits.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness (leverage |L|) is manipulated: low (1) or high (3). High-leverage reward increases both contributions and profits substantially.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 10 periods in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed about other members' contributions, and (if applicable) about punishment/reward decisions and resulting payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward decisions are not linked to player IDs; subjects cannot identify who punished or rewarded them. This is to avoid retaliation.

## Important Limitations
- Punishment and reward technologies are binary and relatively weak compared to some other studies; maximum impact is 15% (low) or 45% (high) of endowment per period.
- Punishment/reward assignment is not identifiable; no targeted retaliation or reputation effects.
- All groups are of fixed size (n=4) and 10 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or communication are not tested.
- Institutional choice is by unanimity rule; other group decision rules are not explored.
- Efficiency is measured via average profit, not always as a normalized efficiency ratio.
- The study is conducted in a lab with student subjects; external validity to field settings is not established.
