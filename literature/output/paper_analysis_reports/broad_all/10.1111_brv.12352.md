## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the effectiveness and function of punishment in promoting cooperation depend critically on the symmetry or asymmetry of power among individuals. In asymmetric power settings (e.g., dominance hierarchies), punishment is typically used by dominants to coerce subordinates, not to enforce reciprocity or group-beneficial cooperation. In symmetric power settings, punishment can be counteracted by retaliation, and cooperation may break down unless specific conditions are met (e.g., territoriality, exit options). The paper reviews evidence that punishment often reduces group payoffs in experimental public goods games, and that the cost-to-impact ratio of punishment is crucial for its effectiveness. However, it does not provide new quantitative findings on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and conceptual guidance for predicting the effects of punishment on efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially regarding the role of power asymmetries. It suggests that punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency in settings with strong power asymmetries, as it is used for coercion rather than cooperation enforcement. In symmetric power settings, punishment may also fail to improve efficiency due to retaliation and the tragedy of the commons. The paper highlights the importance of considering power relations, cost-to-impact ratios, and the possibility of retaliation when predicting the efficiency effects of punishment. However, it does not provide direct empirical estimates or quantitative predictions for efficiency outcomes, so its value is mainly in informing the interpretation of empirical results and the design of experiments.
