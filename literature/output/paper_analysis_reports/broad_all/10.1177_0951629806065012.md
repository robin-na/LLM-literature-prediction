## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The model is for n symmetric agents; comparative statics note that as the number of participants increases, each individual's effect on the group signal decreases, leading to more shirking and lower efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model assumes an indefinitely repeated game (infinite horizon or unknown endpoint), which is necessary for sustaining any compliance via punishment. If the game is finitely repeated with a known endpoint, compliance collapses.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The paper contrasts binary (all-or-nothing) action spaces with continuous action spaces. In continuous action spaces, full compliance is not generally sustainable; in binary spaces, full compliance can be sustained under folk theorem conditions.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly parameterized, but the model assumes that each agent's utility increases in others' compliance and that higher returns to cooperation make compliance more attractive. Comparative statics suggest that higher returns (i.e., higher mpcr) increase compliance and efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a group-level regime (withdrawal of cooperation for T periods), not as individual costly punishment. The cost is the loss of group utility during punishment. Increasing the severity (cost) of punishment increases compliance, but with diminishing returns.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the severity and duration of the punishment regime. More effective punishment increases compliance and efficiency, but cannot eliminate shirking unless extreme.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model assumes an indefinite horizon or unknown endpoint; if the number of rounds is known and finite, compliance cannot be sustained.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players observe a public, group-level signal (function of all contributions plus noise), which is used to trigger punishment. No individual-level feedback is available.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- The model assumes symmetric agents and does not address heterogeneity in preferences, monitoring, or punishment.
- Punishment is modeled as a group-level regime (withdrawal of cooperation), not as individual costly punishment or peer-to-peer sanctions.
- The analysis assumes a public, common signal with imperfect monitoring; results may differ with private monitoring or richer information structures.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or institutional innovations beyond primitive punishment regimes.
- Multiplicity of equilibria is acknowledged; the model predicts what cannot happen (full compliance), but not which partial compliance equilibrium will be selected.
