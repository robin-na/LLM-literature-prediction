Outcome notes:
The main reported outcomes are the survival/extinction of strategies (cooperators, punishers, defectors) and the fraction of altruistic strategies. Payoff is analyzed at the level of pairwise comparisons to determine dominance and phase boundaries, but aggregate group efficiency or total welfare is not directly reported. The focus is on the conditions for the persistence of cooperation and punishment, not on explicit group payoff ratios.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a spatial PGG, the introduction of altruistic punishment allows for the survival of cooperation in a wider range of parameters than in the classic PGG. For low punishment costs, clusters of punishers can survive and support cooperation. At intermediate punishment costs, both punishers and cooperators are vulnerable to defectors. At high punishment costs, a symbiotic spatial structure emerges where neither cooperators nor punishers can survive alone, but together they can outcompete defectors by forming clusters with punishers in the core and cooperators at the boundary. The effect of punishment on cooperation is thus highly dependent on the cost and the spatial structure. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff directly, but the persistence of altruistic strategies is used as a proxy for cooperative success.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in spatial PGGs is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the spatial structure of interactions. While it does not report efficiency directly, it shows that punishment can expand the parameter region where cooperation survives, especially when spatial clustering allows for symbiosis between cooperators and punishers. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that punishment is most effective at low cost, can be detrimental at intermediate cost, and can again be effective at high cost if spatial symbiosis is possible. The results are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 5 players (G=5), with the overall lattice size L=100 (10,000 players). The group size is fixed in the main simulations.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run up to t_max = 10^6 Monte Carlo steps, with results averaged over 100 samples. The number of rounds is very large, effectively infinite-horizon for equilibrium analysis.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: each cooperator or punisher contributes 1 unit, defectors contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. mpcr = r/G, where r is the synergy factor and G=5. r is varied in the simulations.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per punished defector is gamma (γ), which is varied. The effect of punishment cost is central: low cost is beneficial, intermediate cost is detrimental, high cost can again be beneficial via symbiosis.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is 1: γ=δ, so each unit cost to punisher imposes equal fine on defector. The effect of varying γ is analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and simulation-based; it does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured or reported; the main outcome is the survival/extinction of strategies.
- Findings are specific to spatially structured (lattice) populations and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.
- The model assumes all-or-nothing contributions and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are included, limiting generalizability to richer social environments.
