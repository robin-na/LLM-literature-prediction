Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are expected payoffs for different strategies under different social norms, and the evolutionary stability of cooperation, which is determined by payoff differences. Non-payoff outcomes such as cooperation frequency and image scores are also discussed, but payoff is central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the 'Staying' norm, which prescribes abstaining from moral assessment when a donor interacts with a recipient of bad reputation, is more effective at establishing and maintaining cooperation than the four most studied social norms in indirect reciprocity. Under Staying, discriminators (conditional cooperators) are more likely to invade populations of defectors, and the evolutionary dynamics favor higher average payoffs and more efficient group outcomes. The model demonstrates that the range of initial conditions leading to efficient, cooperative equilibria is wider under Staying, and that the strict application of moral judgment (as in other norms) is less effective for promoting efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in indirect reciprocity games (a close relative of public goods games), the design of the assessment/punishment norm can have a large effect on group efficiency. Specifically, conditional assessment (Staying) leads to higher efficiency than unconditional assessment (Scoring, etc.), especially when assessment errors are low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the details of the punishment/assessment mechanism (e.g., whether punishment is conditional or unconditional, and how errors are handled) are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the idea that more nuanced, context-sensitive punishment/assessment rules can yield higher efficiency than strict, always-on punishment rules.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model assumes an infinitely large population, with each round being a two-player donor-recipient game. There is no fixed small group size as in standard PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each individual is assumed to play the one-round giving game infinitely many times with different opponents (i.e., repeated random matching).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The giving game is binary: the donor chooses to help (give) or not help (refuse).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b/c, where b > c. The donor pays cost c, recipient gets benefit b.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All players are assumed to have perfect information about all personal images (reputations) in the population.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a two-player giving game, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to multi-player PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as refusal to help, not as a costly, explicit punishment action.
- Population is assumed to be infinitely large, with perfect information about reputations.
- No communication, group structure, or network effects are modeled.
- Assessment and implementation errors are included, but other real-world complexities are not.
