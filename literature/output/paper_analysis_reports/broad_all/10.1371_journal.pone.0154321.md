## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated four-person public goods game, embedding a reciprocal strategy as an institution (institutionalized reciprocity, IR) leads to significantly higher group efficiency (total profit) than a standard control game. The effect is strongest when the institution enforces a strict upper bound on contributions (S-IR), which enables reciprocators to coordinate against free riders and prevents excessive cooperation. In S-IR, group profits are significantly higher than in both the control and a less restrictive IR variant (NL-IR). The IR mechanism operates as a collective punishment system: if any player fails to meet the target, the group's future benefit is reduced for all. The study does not implement standard peer punishment, but the IR mechanism provides a collective sanction that is effective at increasing efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that institutionalized, rule-based collective punishment (via reduction of future group benefit after a deviation) can substantially increase efficiency in repeated public goods games, especially when the institution strictly limits contributions to a target. While the study does not test standard peer punishment, it shows that collective, institutionally enforced sanctions can outperform standard repeated games without punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a credible, enforced institutional sanction (even if not peer punishment) can raise efficiency well above control, particularly when the rules are clear and binding. The results are most directly applicable to games with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions (in increments), and full feedback on others' contributions and payoffs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experimental groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were repeated for 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were in increments of 10 from 0 to 100 (i.e., discrete, not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (contributions multiplied by 1.6 and split among 4 players).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game would end after 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the contributions of each group member and their own payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not implement or test standard peer punishment; the institutionalized reciprocity mechanism is a form of collective, rule-based sanction, not individual punishment.
- Findings are limited to four-person groups and may not generalize to larger or smaller groups.
- The IR mechanism's effectiveness depends on strict enforcement of contribution limits; without this, efficiency gains are not realized.
- No communication between players was allowed; results may differ if chat or negotiation is possible.
- The study does not directly compare IR to standard peer punishment in terms of efficiency or cooperation.
- The mechanism may be vulnerable to persistent antisocial behavior, as collective punishment reduces group benefit for all.
