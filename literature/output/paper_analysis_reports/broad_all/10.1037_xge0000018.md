# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Value of Vengeance and the Demand for Deterrence
Authors: Crockett, M. J., Özdemir, Y., & Fehr, E.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Psychology-general
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount spent on punishment
- likelihood of punishing
- self-reported motives for punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount spent on punishment and the likelihood of punishing, both in second- and third-party trust game settings. No direct analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs is reported. The study focuses on the motives for punishment (retribution vs. deterrence) and their behavioral expression, not on efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that both retributive and deterrence motives drive costly punishment: participants punish norm violators even when the punishment is hidden and cannot deter future violations, but punishment is stronger when it can be communicated. Second-party punishment (when the punisher is the direct victim) is stronger than third-party punishment (when the punisher is an observer). The findings suggest that retributive motives can lead to punishment that does not increase social welfare and may be inefficient. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be motivated by retribution alone, leading to costly punishment even when it cannot improve future cooperation or group outcomes. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment does not guarantee increased efficiency or group payoff, especially when punishment is driven by retributive motives rather than deterrence. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency changes due to punishment. The design is adjacent to public goods games, using a trust game with punishment, so findings may transfer in terms of understanding motives but not in terms of efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three players: a punisher (P), a bystander (B), and a trustee (T).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 54 one-shot games with different partners; each game is a single round (one-shot).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all interactions are anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (entrustment) is all-or-nothing: P and B can entrust their entire endowment or not; back-transfer choices are discrete (0%, 25%, 50%).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Entrusted endowments are multiplied by a multiplier m (integer between 2 and 6) before being transferred to T. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly calculated but can be inferred from the multiplier and player count.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each CHF 0.10 spent by P reduces T's payoff by CHF 0.20; cost to punisher is 0.10 per 0.20 punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed: 1 unit cost yields 2 units impact (0.10 CHF cost reduces 0.20 CHF from T).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know that each game is a one-shot interaction; the number of games (54) is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the back-transfer decisions relevant to their own punishment decision; no mention of broader summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the open punishment condition, T is informed whether P punished him and by how much; in the hidden condition, T is not informed. The identity of the punisher is not revealed, but the fact of punishment is communicated in the open condition.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; all dependent variables are behavioral (punishment amount, likelihood, self-reported motives).
- The experimental design is a trust game with punishment, not a standard public goods game; findings may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- No analysis of how punishment affects cooperation rates, group efficiency, or total earnings is provided.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present, so the effects of combined punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- The study uses one-shot games, so repeated interaction and learning effects are not captured.
