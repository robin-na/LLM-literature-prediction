# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: To what reference point do people calibrate cost-free, third-party punishment?
Authors: Koenig, B. L., & Riley, C. M.
Journal: Personality and Individual Differences
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- preferred punishment amount (in dollars) assigned by third parties to a perpetrator
- preferred compensation amount (in dollars) assigned by third parties to a victim
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of punishment (fine) or compensation that participants believe should be assigned, not actual payoffs or efficiency in a game. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare is measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three experiments, participants assigned preferred punishments (fines) to a perpetrator in a theft scenario, with victim loss and perpetrator gain manipulated independently. Both victim loss and perpetrator gain influenced punishment preferences, with participants tending to select the larger of the two as a reference point. On average, preferred punishments exceeded the sum of victim loss and perpetrator gain. A substantial minority of participants preferred punishments much larger than this outcome differential, while a small minority preferred no punishment. When asked about compensation for the victim (rather than punishment for the perpetrator), participants' preferred amounts were lower and rarely exceeded the outcome differential. The study demonstrates that third-party punishment preferences are not simply proportional to harm or gain, but often exceed both, and that there is considerable heterogeneity in punitive preferences.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. However, it is informative about the psychological calibration of punishment preferences in third-party contexts, showing that people often prefer punishments that more than offset both victim loss and perpetrator gain. This suggests that, in environments where third-party punishment is possible and costless, punishment levels may be set high, potentially deterring transgressions more strongly than would be predicted by models based solely on harm or gain. The findings are most relevant for understanding the psychological underpinnings of punishment, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency in PGG-like games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is cost-free for the third-party (participant); participants assign fines without incurring any cost themselves.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a fine; each dollar of punishment is a dollar loss for the perpetrator.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 3 asks about compensation (reward) for the victim, but not in the same context as punishment; participants could only assign either punishment or compensation, not both.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation (reward) is cost-free for the third-party; participants assign compensation amounts without cost to themselves.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation is implemented as a direct monetary transfer to the victim; each dollar of compensation is a dollar gain for the victim.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is assigned anonymously; the scenario specifies that the third-party is anonymous and there is no possibility of retaliation or reputation effects.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or any interactive group context; it uses hypothetical vignettes with a perpetrator, victim, and third-party observer.
- No actual payoffs, efficiency, or group welfare are measured; only preferred punishment or compensation amounts are elicited.
- Punishment and compensation are cost-free for the third-party, which may not generalize to contexts where punishment is costly.
- The scenarios are highly simplified and lack ecological validity; real-world factors such as group dynamics, repeated interactions, and strategic behavior are not present.
- Findings may not generalize to other forms of punishment (e.g., incarceration, social exclusion) or to situations where punishment is not monetary.
