## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds, at a theoretical level, that polycentric governance structures are more likely to support the emergence and stability of group-beneficial norms, including costly punishment, by satisfying four key conditions: (1) minimizing within-group variance, (2) reducing within-group selective pressures against prosocial traits (e.g., via effective punishment), (3) increasing between-group selective pressures, and (4) maximizing between-group variance. It argues that punishment can transform altruistic traits into individually rational behaviors when punishment is widespread and coordinated, and that polycentricity supports the self-sorting and coordination needed for this. However, the paper does not provide empirical data or direct predictions about efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment in public goods games or similar environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual and mechanistic support for the idea that punishment, when embedded in polycentric or self-sorting group structures, can enhance cooperation and group-beneficial outcomes. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency or welfare depends on the ability of groups to self-sort, coordinate, and monitor, and on the cost structure of punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence that can be directly used to predict efficiency changes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of group structure, self-sorting, and the scaling of punishment costs, rather than in providing parameterized estimates.
