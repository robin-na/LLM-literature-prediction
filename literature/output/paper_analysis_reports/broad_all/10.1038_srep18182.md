# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Stochasticity in economic losses increases the value of reputation in indirect reciprocity
Authors: dos Santos, M., Placì, S., & Wedekind, C.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- final earnings
- return on investment into reputation
- correlation between generosity and earnings
- group cooperativeness (mean group cooperation rate, but also group earnings)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- helping frequency
- reputation effects on helping
- discrimination between large and small losses
- effect of previous help received on helping
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are helping frequency (a behavioral outcome) and final earnings (a payoff outcome). The paper analyzes the relationship between generosity (helping) and final earnings, and how this relationship differs between stable and stochastic loss environments. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but group earnings and return on investment into reputation are analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that stochasticity in economic losses does not increase mean helping rates or mean group earnings, but it does increase the payoff advantage of having a good reputation. Under stochastic losses, generous players are helped more and selfish players are helped less, leading to a steeper positive relationship between generosity and final earnings. In stable environments, this relationship is absent. Thus, stochasticity increases the efficiency of reputation-based cooperation by making investment in reputation more beneficial, even though overall group efficiency does not increase.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that, in repeated helping games with indirect reciprocity, environmental stochasticity (randomness in losses) increases the payoff return to cooperative behavior (reputation), even if mean group efficiency does not change. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, reputation-based exclusion or helping) on efficiency may be more positive in environments with stochastic payoffs or risks, because the social system more effectively rewards cooperators and penalizes defectors. However, the game is not a standard public goods game and does not feature explicit costly punishment; rather, it is an indirect reciprocity helping game. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation or exclusion is the main sanctioning mechanism, and where environmental risk is present.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group had 9 players; 16 groups in total.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player played 24 times in each role (Unlucky and Passer-by), for a total of 48 interactions per player. The total number of rounds was not revealed to participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Helping decision is binary: Passer-by can either help (pay 1 CHF to reduce Unlucky's loss) or not help.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 3 CHF per 1 CHF cost to the Passer-by (helping reduces Unlucky's loss by 3 CHF at a cost of 1 CHF to the helper).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Helping is a form of costly reward (helper pays 1 CHF to reduce recipient's loss by 3 CHF).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (help) costs 1 CHF to the helper.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (help) reduces recipient's loss by 3 CHF per 1 CHF cost to the helper.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were not told the total number of rounds; display included extra history to obscure the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw the full history of the Unlucky's helping behavior (reputation) in the Passer-by role, graphically displayed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players could identify the reputation of the Unlucky (i.e., who had helped or not helped in the past), but not the identity of the specific punisher/rewarder in each case. Anonymity was maintained, but reputation was individual-specific.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated indirect reciprocity helping game with pairwise interactions and reputation, not group contributions.
- There is no explicit costly punishment mechanism; the only sanction is indirect exclusion via reputation (not being helped).
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, though group earnings and return on investment are analyzed.
- The findings may not generalize to standard PGGs with explicit punishment or to settings without reputation mechanisms.
- The number of rounds was not known to participants, which may affect end-game behavior differently than in games with known horizons.
