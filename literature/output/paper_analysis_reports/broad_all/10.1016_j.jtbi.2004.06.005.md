## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that only a small subset of reputation assignment rules—those that treat defection against bad individuals as good (i.e., as a form of justified punishment or sanction)—can sustain high efficiency (group payoff close to the maximum possible) in indirect reciprocity games. These 'leading eight' rules enable stable cooperation by allowing individuals to refuse help to bad actors without losing their own good reputation. The model shows that, under these rules, the average payoff in the population approaches the maximum possible (b-c), minus a small term due to errors. The results are robust to error rates and to the benefit-to-cost ratio, as long as b > c. The paper provides a formal evolutionary justification for the importance of sanctioning/punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency in reputation-based social dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms—specifically, the ability to sanction or refuse help to bad actors without reputational cost—are critical for sustaining high efficiency in indirect reciprocity environments. While the model is not a standard public goods game, it is closely related: it is a repeated, pairwise helping game with a social dilemma structure. The findings suggest that, in environments where individuals can sanction defectors (and where such sanctioning is not itself punished), group efficiency can approach the maximum possible. For prediction tasks, this implies that enabling punishment or justified sanctioning in a game design is likely to increase efficiency, provided the reputation system is designed so that justified punishment does not harm the punisher's reputation. The results are robust to errors and to variations in the benefit-to-cost ratio, as long as cooperation is individually costly but socially beneficial.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes an infinite population, with random pairwise interactions in each round. There is no fixed group size, but each game is between two players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player participates in many one-shot games with different partners; the model assumes an infinite sequence of interactions (effectively infinite rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to help (cooperate) or not (defect) in each interaction.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b/c, where b > c. The effect of varying b/c is directly analyzed: higher b/c increases the number of stable cooperative equilibria and efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as refusal to help bad actors, which is cost-saving for the punisher (they avoid the cost of helping). There is no explicit cost to punishing beyond the opportunity cost of not helping.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective if and only if the reputation system treats justified punishment (defection against bad actors) as good. The 'technology' is the reputation assignment rule.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players have access to public reputation (H-score) of others, which is common knowledge in the indirect observation model. This enables discrimination and supports high efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a repeated, pairwise helping game with binary reputation, not a standard multi-player public goods game.
- Punishment is modeled as refusal to help, not as an explicit costly punishment action.
- The model assumes an infinite population and infinite rounds, which may not match finite experimental settings.
- No explicit communication, group structure, or identification of punishers is modeled.
- The reputation system is assumed to be public and error-prone, but the model does not address strategic manipulation of reputation.
