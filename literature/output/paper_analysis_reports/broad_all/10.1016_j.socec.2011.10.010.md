It references studies that examine efficiency, payoff, and punishment, but does not itself report new outcome data.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that economic experiments can be classified by their underlying concept of institutions: (1) as exogenous structures (rules), (2) as embedded in agents (norms, beliefs), or (3) as evolving endogenously through agent interaction and feedback. It highlights that experiments with endogenous institutional choice (e.g., voting on punishment or reward mechanisms) are particularly relevant for understanding institutional change in social-ecological systems. The paper references empirical findings (e.g., Walker et al. 2000) that voting on rules can increase efficiency, but does not provide new empirical results. The main contribution is a conceptual framework for interpreting and designing experiments on institutional change, not direct evidence on punishment or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. However, it is useful for understanding the types of experimental designs and institutional features (such as endogenous vs. exogenous rule change, presence of sanctioning or reward mechanisms, and the role of voting or communication) that may moderate such effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that allowing endogenous choice of punishment institutions (e.g., via voting) may increase efficiency, as supported by referenced studies, but does not quantify these effects. The typology can help identify which experimental features are likely to be important moderators in empirical studies.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses experiments where reward mechanisms are available, especially in the context of endogenous institutional choice (e.g., Sutter et al. 2010). It references findings that allowing voting on reward and sanctioning institutions can increase efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not report new empirical results or effect sizes.
- It reviews and classifies existing experimental literature but does not provide detailed design parameters or quantitative findings for any specific experiment.
- Dimension-level evidence is indirect, based on discussion and citation of other studies, not on original data.
- The typology is useful for conceptual framing but not for direct prediction of efficiency or payoff outcomes in specific game designs.
