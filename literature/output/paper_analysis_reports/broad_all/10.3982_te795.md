Outcome notes:
The paper is entirely theoretical and focuses on the set of equilibrium payoffs (efficiency, welfare, surplus) that can be robustly sustained in repeated games, especially the repeated prisoners' dilemma. It does not report experimental or empirical data, but all main results are about payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated prisoners' dilemma games, the set of robustly sustainable equilibrium payoffs (i.e., efficiency) is sharply affected by concerns about incomplete information. Under complete information, grim-trigger strategies can sustain cooperation if the discounted value of future cooperation exceeds the temptation to defect. However, when robustness to incomplete information is required, the cost of being the 'sucker' (cooperating while the other defects) becomes critical, and the set of sustainable payoffs can shrink dramatically. Selective-punishment strategies, which punish only the deviator, are more robust than grim-trigger strategies, and can sustain cooperation under a wider range of parameters. The paper provides explicit characterizations of when efficient cooperation is robustly sustainable and when only inefficient outcomes (permanent defection) are robust. The main outcomes are theoretical characterizations of efficiency/welfare as a function of game parameters and strategy types.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated prisoners' dilemma–like games under incomplete information. It shows that the design of the punishment mechanism (grim-trigger vs. selective punishment), the cost of being punished, and the enforceability of asymmetric outcomes are critical moderators. If only symmetric cooperation (CC) is enforceable, robust efficiency collapses to permanent defection. If asymmetric outcomes (DC or CD) are enforceable, robust efficiency can be as high as in the complete information case. The results are directly about efficiency/welfare, not just contribution rates. For prediction, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment scheme and the game's payoff structure, especially under incomplete information. However, the results are for repeated prisoners' dilemma, not public goods games per se, so transfer to PGGs should be done with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main analysis is for the two-player repeated prisoners' dilemma. Some generalizations to n-player games are discussed in the theory sections.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main model is the infinitely repeated game (infinite horizon), with discount factor delta. Some discussion of finite-horizon games in related literature.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Action space is binary: Cooperate (C) or Defect (D), i.e., all-or-nothing choices.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The prisoners' dilemma payoffs are parameterized by b (benefit to defector) and c (cost to cooperator), which together determine the marginal per-capita return analog.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented via strategy (grim-trigger or selective punishment), not as a costly action per se. The cost to the punished (not the punisher) is central. The cost to the punisher is not parameterized as in PGGs with explicit costly punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is determined by the strategy (grim-trigger: permanent punishment; selective: targeted punishment). The paper discusses how selective punishment is more effective/robust in sustaining cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Infinite horizon with known discount factor; players know the game is infinitely repeated.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Perfect monitoring: players observe all actions and payoffs (or public signals thereof).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In selective-punishment strategies, the identity of the deviator is known and only the deviator is punished.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The main results are for the repeated prisoners' dilemma, not public goods games; transfer to PGGs should be done with caution.
- Punishment is implemented via equilibrium strategies (grim-trigger, selective punishment), not as explicit costly punishment actions as in many PGG experiments.
- The model assumes perfect monitoring and infinite horizon; results may not generalize to games with noise, limited rounds, or imperfect monitoring.
- No analysis of communication, reward mechanisms, or group sizes greater than two in detail.
