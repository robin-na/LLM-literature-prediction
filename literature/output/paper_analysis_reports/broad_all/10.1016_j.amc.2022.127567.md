## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Assumes infinite well-mixed populations and evolutionary dynamics, which may not capture finite group or real-world effects.
- Preference reversal is modeled probabilistically and may not capture all psychological or contextual factors influencing real human decisions.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms beyond punishment and preference reversal.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are considered.
