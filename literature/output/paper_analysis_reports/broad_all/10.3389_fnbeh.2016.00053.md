# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners
Authors: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D.
Journal: Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- average group payoff
- welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment assigned
- counter-punishment assigned
- messages sent (communication content)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings (payoff) and contribution levels. Punishment and counter-punishment behaviors are also analyzed, as well as the content and frequency of communication messages.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use fixed groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per group, divided into three blocks of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication is manipulated: some treatments allow normative messages at the punishment stage, some require a message at counter-punishment, some both, and one has no communication. Any communication increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses an integer between 0 and 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token contributed returns 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 unit and reduces the target's payoff by 3 units.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each unit spent reduces target by 3 units).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told there are three blocks of 10 rounds (30 rounds total), and are informed about the rules for each block at the start of that block.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all group members are informed about the contribution levels of each of the other three members. After punishment and counter-punishment, players are informed about who punished them.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each punishment and counter-punishment stage, punished group members are informed about the IDs of all group members who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- All participants are undergraduate students from a single university, which may limit generalizability.
- The experiment uses fixed groups and fixed identifiers, which may not reflect all real-world settings.
- Communication is structured and limited to specific stages/messages; results may differ with richer or less-structured communication.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The study does not vary punishment cost or effectiveness; only one punishment technology is used.
