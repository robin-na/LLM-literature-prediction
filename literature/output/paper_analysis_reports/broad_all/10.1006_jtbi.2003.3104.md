## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that internalized prosocial norms, including altruistic punishment, can become evolutionarily stable through gene-culture coevolution, especially when the fitness benefits of prosocial norms are high, extraparental socialization is strong, and assortative mating is present. Simulations show that groups with internalized altruistic punishment achieve high mean fitness and eliminate antisocial norms. Altruistic punishment is particularly effective at sustaining cooperation and group efficiency because its cost is lower and more concentrated than the cost of direct cooperation. The models predict that, in the presence of punishment, group efficiency and mean fitness are higher than in its absence, especially in large groups or when repeated interaction is infrequent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like environments increases group efficiency and mean payoff, especially when direct cooperation is not stable due to high costs. The models suggest that punishment is a robust mechanism for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff, even in large groups or with high migration, as long as the cost of punishment is low relative to the benefit of cooperation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where internalized norms and cultural transmission play a role, and less so to short-run laboratory games, but the core logic supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like dilemmas.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses that the cost of punishment is typically much lower than the cost of cooperation, and that low punishment cost is key to the stability of prosocial equilibria.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is described as effective at sustaining cooperation even at low cost, especially when many punish few. Effectiveness is discussed but not parameterized.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and feedback are not directly modeled or manipulated.
- The models assume constant payoffs and do not address frequency-dependent payoffs or strategic adaptation in repeated games.
- Findings are most applicable to long-run evolutionary or cultural contexts, not short-run laboratory experiments.
- No direct mapping to standard public goods game parameters; results are more general to social dilemmas and norm internalization.
