# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Incorporating local institutions in irrigation experiments: evidence from rural communities in Pakistan
Authors: Javaid, A., & Falk, T.
Journal: Ecology and Society
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- individual earnings

- resource wastage
Non-payoff outcomes:

- extraction levels
- punishment frequency
- distributional equity (Gini coefficients)
- treatment initiation/receipt
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (tokens), group welfare, and efficiency relative to the social optimum. The paper also reports on behavioral outcomes such as investment, extraction, and punishment, but these are secondary to the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study implemented a sequential common-pool resource (CPR) game with five players per group, modeling irrigation dilemmas with asymmetric access. In the baseline (no institutional intervention), groups achieved high efficiency (average group earnings at 74% of the social optimum, group investment at 98% of the optimum). When external punishment (costly, probabilistic fines) was introduced, group earnings and efficiency significantly decreased (regression coefficient for group earnings: -9.03, p<0.1). The punishment mechanism reduced over-extraction by head-users but did not increase tail-user earnings; instead, it led to more water being wasted and lower group welfare. Communication and traditional authority treatments did not significantly affect group earnings or investment. The high baseline efficiency is attributed to strong local norms and the public revelation of individual extraction decisions. The main effect of punishment was to create additional coordination problems, not to improve efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments with high baseline efficiency and strong social information (public feedback on individual actions), introducing an external punishment mechanism can reduce group efficiency and welfare. The punishment treatment in this design (costly, probabilistic, not directly linked to rule violations) led to more resource wastage and lower group earnings. For prediction tasks, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high and social information is available, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency. The effect is specific to the punishment design used here (costly, probabilistic, not directly rewarding the punisher). Other institutional interventions (communication, traditional authority) had no significant effect. The findings are most applicable to sequential CPR games with asymmetric access, public feedback, and close-knit communities.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Baseline phase: 7 rounds; treatment phase: 8-11 rounds (randomized, not revealed to players).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication (chat) was allowed only in the communication treatment, not in baseline or punishment treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and extraction choices were continuous (tokens and water minutes), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is nonlinear (sigmoidal production function); not a fixed linear MPCR. The group investment function is provided in tables and figures.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 token per punishment initiated (shared if multiple initiators).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact: 4-token fine with 2/3 probability (i.e., expected impact 2.67 tokens per punishment event).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did NOT know the total number of rounds in advance; number of rounds was randomized and not revealed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, all players were shown the investment and extraction decisions of all other players (full transparency).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew who initiated punishment against whom; punishment decisions were not anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The baseline (control) efficiency was unusually high due to strong local norms and public feedback on individual actions; results may not generalize to settings with lower baseline cooperation or less social information.
- The punishment mechanism was costly, probabilistic, and not directly linked to rule violations, which may differ from other punishment designs.
- No pure control group in the treatment phase (i.e., all groups received some institutional intervention after baseline).
- The game is a sequential CPR/irrigation game with asymmetric access, not a standard simultaneous PGG; results may not transfer to simultaneous or symmetric games.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism was tested.
- The study was conducted in close-knit rural communities with strong pre-existing norms; findings may not generalize to more anonymous or less cohesive groups.
