# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Escalation of negative social exchange: Reflexive punishment or deliberative deterrence?
Authors: Vandermeer, J., Hosey, C., Epley, N., & Keysar, B.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount taken or given in repeated dictator/taking games
- frequency of escalation (taking more than previous partner took)
- effect of deliberation vs. reflexive response on escalation
- effect of cognitive load on escalation
- effect of explicit goals (punish, deter, fairness) on escalation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: how much participants take or give in response to another's action, and whether they escalate (take more than was taken from them). The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, nor does it use a public goods game or a direct punishment mechanism. Outcomes are about escalation in negative reciprocity, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that negative escalation in repeated taking/giving games is driven by reflexive punishment rather than strategic deterrence. Deliberation reduces escalation, while reflexive or high cognitive load conditions increase it. Escalation occurs only in response to perceived negative actions (taking), not positive ones (giving). Explicitly instructing participants to focus on future consequences or to deter does not increase escalation; only a goal to punish does. The findings are robust across one-shot and repeated interactions, and across different manipulations of deliberation and cognitive resources. However, the study does not use public goods games, does not implement a punishment stage, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The experimental paradigm is a repeated dictator/taking game, not a public goods game, and the main outcomes are escalation and behavioral responses to harm, not group efficiency or payoff. There is no punishment stage where players can pay to reduce others' payoffs, and no analysis of group welfare or efficiency. The findings may be relevant for understanding the psychological drivers of punitive escalation, but do not inform the quantitative effect of punishment mechanisms on efficiency in PGG-like games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use two-player interactions (dyads) in repeated dictator/taking games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some experiments use one-shot (2 rounds: one per player), others use repeated games (e.g., 5 rounds per player, 10 total turns).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; explicit instructions to prevent communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/taking choices are continuous (any whole dollar amount or coin amount from 0 to 10 or 100). Not all-or-nothing.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In some experiments, participants are not told the number of rounds in advance (to prevent endgame effects); in others, the number of rounds is clear (e.g., 2 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the other player's action directly (how much was taken/given), but no summary statistics or group-level info.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the identity of the other player in the dyad (face-to-face or via explicit assignment), but no anonymity between dyads.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental paradigm is a repeated dictator/taking game, not a public goods game or a game with a punishment stage.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; only behavioral escalation is measured.
- No punishment or reward mechanism is present; escalation is via taking more, not via costly punishment.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player public goods games or to environments with explicit punishment options.
- The study does not address the effect of punishment on efficiency or group welfare, limiting its relevance for the prediction task.
