Outcome notes:
The paper is entirely theoretical and focuses on the strategic riskiness of cooperation equilibria in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or other payoff-based outcomes, but rather analyzes which punishment strategies are viable and how riskiness and forgiveness interact in equilibrium selection.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, the riskiness of cooperation equilibria can be precisely characterized by comparing the Nash-products of cooperation and defection equilibria. The least risky cooperation equilibrium is always the grim trigger strategy. There exists a critical discount factor (delta*) above which cooperation can be supported without strategic risk, and below which all cooperation equilibria are risky. The more patient the players (higher discount factor), the more forgiving the punishment strategies can be while still supporting cooperation. The paper also introduces the concept of 'risk perfection', requiring that not only the equilibrium path but also all subgames (punishment phases) are not risky. The results generalize to asymmetric games and to repeated Cournot duopoly (collusion) games, which are structurally similar to public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical guidance on the relationship between punishment harshness, forgiveness, and the viability of cooperation in repeated games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it shows that the ability of punishment to support cooperation depends on the discount factor and the riskiness of the equilibrium. In environments structurally similar to public goods games, the results suggest that harsher punishments (like grim trigger) are less risky and more likely to support cooperation, especially when players are less patient. As patience increases, more forgiving punishments become viable. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the strategic riskiness of the environment and the patience of players, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main analysis is for 2-player (dyadic) repeated Prisoner's Dilemma; generalization to n-player games is discussed only in passing.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated with discounting; the number of rounds is infinite.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The stage game is a binary choice (cooperate or defect), i.e., all-or-nothing contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is implicit in the payoff parameters of the Prisoner's Dilemma stage game (b, c, d, a).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: it is implemented as future defection (loss of cooperation), not as a costly action per se. The cost is the opportunity cost of lost cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the harshness of the punishment strategy (e.g., grim trigger vs. finite punishment). The paper analyzes how the severity of punishment affects riskiness and equilibrium selection.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated; players know the structure is infinite with discounting.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report any empirical or simulated efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- The analysis is limited to the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma and structurally similar games; direct application to standard public goods games may require additional assumptions.
- Punishment is modeled as future defection (loss of cooperation), not as a costly action or explicit sanctioning mechanism.
- No analysis of finite repeated games, communication, or group sizes greater than two except in passing.
- No consideration of reward mechanisms or mixed punishment/reward environments.
