The main reported outcomes are the stationary abundance of strategies (cooperators, defectors, pro-social punishers, anti-social punishers) in evolutionary dynamics. Payoff is modeled and used to determine evolutionary success, but group efficiency or total group payoff is not directly reported as a primary outcome. The analysis is theoretical and based on evolutionary game theory, not empirical data.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation depends critically on the pattern of punishment and the presence of anti-social punishment. Centralized (pool) pro-social punishment is more effective than decentralized (peer) punishment at promoting cooperation. When anti-social punishment is possible and both pro- and anti-social punishments are in the same pattern, the positive effect of punishment is largely neutralized. However, if pro-social punishment is centralized and anti-social punishment is decentralized, cooperation can still be promoted. The results are derived from theoretical evolutionary game models and focus on the stationary abundance of strategies, not direct efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative theoretical evidence about how punishment design (centralized vs decentralized, presence of anti-social punishment) can affect the evolutionary success of cooperation in social dilemmas. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, it shows that centralized punishment mechanisms are more robust to anti-social punishment and more likely to sustain cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that games with centralized punishment (pool) are more likely to see positive effects of punishment on efficiency, especially when anti-social punishment is possible. However, the results are based on evolutionary dynamics and strategy abundance, not direct efficiency measures, and the model is a prisoner's dilemma rather than a public goods game.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Population size N is a parameter in the model (e.g., N=100 in figures). Larger populations are more beneficial for the emergence of pool pro-social punishers.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is a key parameter. Lower punishment cost increases the abundance of pro-social punishers and can promote cooperation, but also affects anti-social punishers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per cost, beta/alpha) is manipulated. Higher punishment effectiveness (higher beta) promotes pro-social punishers and cooperation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses that peer punishment exposes the identity of punishers (leading to retaliation), while pool punishment keeps it confidential, which is argued to make pool punishment more effective.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not based on empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported; outcomes are based on evolutionary abundance of strategies.
- Anti-social punishment is modeled as a strategy, but the real-world prevalence and dynamics may differ.
- No consideration of repeated rounds, communication, or other social mechanisms beyond punishment patterns.
