Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the cumulative cost to the institution to achieve a target cooperation level, which is a payoff-based outcome from the institution's perspective. The paper also analyzes the minimal incentive required for cooperation to emerge, and the time evolution of cooperation. Group efficiency in the sense of total group payoff is not directly reported, but the cost to achieve high cooperation is closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both institutional punishment and reward can be tuned to achieve near-full cooperation in structured populations, with the optimal protocol being time-invariant and identical for both types of incentives under a given update rule. The minimal cumulative cost to the institution depends on the initial fraction of cooperators: punishment is more cost-effective when initial cooperation is high, while reward is more cost-effective when initial cooperation is low. The results are supported by analytical derivations and simulations across regular, random, small-world, and scale-free networks. The main outcome is the cumulative cost to the institution, which is a proxy for efficiency from the perspective of implementing cooperation-promoting interventions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how the cost-effectiveness of punishment (and reward) depends on game parameters such as network structure, update rule, and initial cooperation level. While it does not report group efficiency in the standard sense, its focus on the cumulative cost to achieve high cooperation is closely related and can inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar structured games. The results suggest that, with optimally tuned punishment, high cooperation (and thus high group efficiency) can be achieved at lower institutional cost when initial cooperation is already substantial. The findings are most directly applicable to structured Prisoner's Dilemma settings with institutional (not peer) punishment or reward, and may be less directly transferable to standard public goods games.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N is a parameter in all models and simulations (e.g., N=100 in figures).
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The process is iterated until a target cooperation level is reached; time is continuous or discrete, but the number of rounds is not fixed in advance.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose to cooperate (C) or defect (D); binary choice.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a fine (mu_P) imposed by an institution; the cost is the cumulative institutional expenditure to achieve cooperation. The cost to the punisher (institution) is explicitly modeled and minimized.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the size of the fine (mu_P) per defection; the impact per unit cost is analytically derived.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is modeled as an alternative to punishment; both are analyzed in parallel.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward cost is the institutional expenditure per reward (mu_R); cumulative cost is analytically derived and compared to punishment.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the size of the reward (mu_R) per cooperation; impact per unit cost is analytically derived.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Efficiency is measured as cumulative institutional cost to achieve cooperation, not as group payoff or surplus.
- Punishment and reward are institutional (centralized), not peer-administered.
- The analysis assumes weak selection and may not generalize to strong selection regimes.
- The number of rounds is not fixed; the process continues until a target cooperation level is reached.
- No consideration of communication, information feedback, or player-level heterogeneity.
