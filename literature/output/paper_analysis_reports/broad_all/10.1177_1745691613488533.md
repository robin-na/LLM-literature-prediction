# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Trust, Punishment, and Cooperation Across 18 Societies: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Balliet, D., & Van Lange, P. A. M.
Journal: Perspectives on Psychological Science
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public goods
- cooperation rate
- effect size (d) for cooperation between punishment and control conditions
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the standardized mean difference (d) in cooperation/contribution between punishment and control conditions. The paper does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, but focuses on behavioral cooperation/contribution as the main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Punishment opportunities increase cooperation in public goods games, with a moderate average effect (d=0.77, 95% CI [0.63, 0.91]), but with substantial cross-societal variation. The effect of punishment is significantly stronger in high-trust societies. Methodological moderators show that punishment is more effective in partner-matching protocols (fixed groups) and with more iterations (rounds), but not significantly affected by group size. Other societal variables (GDP, democracy, market competition, religious participation, norms of civic cooperation) have weaker or non-significant effects after controlling for trust. The outcome measured is behavioral cooperation/contribution, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in the standardized Fehr & Gächter public goods game, enabling peer punishment increases cooperation rates, especially in high-trust societies. However, the outcome is contribution/cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone. The findings suggest that, for lab PGGs with 3-7 players, 6-50 rounds, and standard costly peer punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MUs), punishment increases cooperation, with larger effects in partner-matching and longer games. The effect on efficiency is likely positive if higher cooperation translates to higher group payoffs, but this is not directly measured. The paper is highly informative for predicting behavioral cooperation under different game designs and societal contexts, but less so for direct efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size ranged from 3 to 7, with mode at 4. Group size was coded and analyzed as a moderator, but found not to significantly affect the punishment effect.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of iterations (rounds) ranged from 1 (one-shot) to 50, with mean ~10. More rounds increased the effectiveness of punishment on cooperation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions were continuous (0-10 MUs), not all-or-nothing. This is standardized across studies.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.375 (multiplier 1.5, group size 4) in the standard protocol. All studies used the Fehr & Gächter protocol with this or very similar MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 MU to reduce 3 MUs from the target. This is standardized across studies.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 MU cost reduces 3 MUs from target). Standardized across studies.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds in advance in the standard Fehr & Gächter protocol.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed of other group members' contributions before punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous but targeted; participants know who punished whom in the sense that they can assign punishment to specific group members, but identities are not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is behavioral cooperation/contribution, not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The effect on efficiency is not directly measured.
- All included studies use university student samples from large-scale, industrialized societies; generalizability to other populations is limited.
- All studies use the Fehr & Gächter protocol, so findings may not generalize to other public goods game variants or punishment mechanisms.
- Potential publication bias is detected, with missing null findings likely, though the main conclusions are robust to bias correction.
- Trust is measured at the societal level using survey data, which may not perfectly capture relevant trust for lab participants.
