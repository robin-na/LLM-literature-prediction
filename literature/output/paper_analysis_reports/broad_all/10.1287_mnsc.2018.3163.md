# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ride Your Luck! A Field Experiment on Lottery-Based Incentives for Compliance
Authors: Fabbri, M., Barbieri, P. N., & Bigoni, M.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- on-board ticket sales (revenue)
- off-board ticket sales (revenue)
- cost-effectiveness (net profit: revenue minus lottery payouts)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of sanctions imposed (fare-dodging detected)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are ticket sales (on-board and off-board), which are used as proxies for compliance and revenue. The paper also discusses cost-effectiveness (net profit) and briefly reports on sanctions imposed, but does not directly measure group efficiency as a ratio to a full-cooperation benchmark.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
A field experiment on city buses in Italy tested a lottery-based reward for buying tickets, layered on top of existing probabilistic sanctions for fare-dodging. Treated buses saw a significant increase in on-board ticket sales (18–30% increase per hour), with no overall reduction in off-board ticket sales, indicating a net increase in compliance and revenue. The increased revenue covered the cost of the prizes actually claimed, but not the total value of all prizes offered, so cost-effectiveness depended on unclaimed prizes. The effect was not persistent after the lottery ended. Suburban routes showed a substitution from off-board to on-board ticket purchases. The study provides evidence that combining probabilistic sanctions with lottery-based rewards can increase compliance and revenue in a real-world public good context, but the net efficiency gain is limited by the cost of rewards and the sustainability of the effect.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides field evidence that adding a lottery-based reward to an environment with probabilistic sanctions increases compliance and revenue (a proxy for efficiency), but the net efficiency gain depends on the cost of the reward and whether all prizes are claimed. The effect is not persistent after removal. For prediction, the presence of both probabilistic sanctions and a reward mechanism (lottery) increases efficiency relative to sanctions alone, but the magnitude of the gain may be modest and sensitive to the cost structure and salience of the reward. The design is not a standard PGG, but the compliance/public good context and outcome measures are closely related. The findings are most informative for settings where compliance is individually observable and can be incentivized with both sanctions and probabilistic rewards.

- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic (random ticket inspections); effectiveness is discussed in terms of compliance, but not directly manipulated.

- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is the value of lottery prizes (€500 per prize, six prizes total).

## Important Limitations
- The setting is not a standard public goods game; compliance is an individual act (ticket purchase) rather than a group contribution.
- Efficiency is proxied by revenue and cost-effectiveness, not by a formal efficiency ratio relative to full cooperation.
- The net efficiency gain depends on the number of unclaimed prizes; if all prizes were claimed, the scheme would not have been cost-effective.
- The effect of the lottery-based incentive did not persist after removal; no evidence on long-term or repeated effects.
- The mechanism behind increased compliance (attraction to gambling vs. perceived monitoring) cannot be disentangled with available data.
- No direct measurement of group size, marginal per-capita return, or other standard PGG parameters.
- Possible substitution effects (e.g., from off-board to on-board tickets, or from other gambling activities) are not fully explored.
