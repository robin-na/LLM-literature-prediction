The model explicitly tracks aggregate profits and individual payoffs as a function of strategy mix and spatial structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that local punishment mechanisms in a spatial CPR game allow for the emergence and persistence of cooperative and mixed-strategy equilibria, which are associated with higher aggregate payoffs and efficiency than the all-defector equilibrium. The frequency of efficient equilibria increases with higher sanction levels and larger population sizes, and spatial clustering of cooperators and enforcers is crucial for sustaining cooperation. The model demonstrates that spatial structure and local enforcement can overcome the limitations of non-spatial models, leading to more diverse and efficient outcomes. Sensitivity analysis shows that increasing the cost or effectiveness of sanctions, or increasing population size, generally increases the prevalence of efficient (cooperative or mixed) equilibria.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in spatially structured public-goods-like games increases group efficiency and aggregate payoff, especially when punishment is local and sufficiently strong. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, the introduction of local punishment will often lead to higher efficiency, particularly in larger groups and with higher sanction levels. The results also highlight the importance of spatial structure and local interactions, which may moderate the effect of punishment compared to non-spatial models. The paper's parameter sensitivity analysis can inform predictions about how changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and group size affect the efficiency gains from punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model assumes discrete strategy choices (cooperate, defect, enforce) and does not allow for continuous effort or punishment levels.
- The spatial structure (torus or lattice) may not fully capture real-world social networks or CPR settings.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are considered, limiting generalizability to settings with positive incentives.
- The model assumes synchronous updating and simple imitation learning, which may not reflect all real-world learning dynamics.
- Parameter values are chosen for tractability and may not map directly to empirical settings.
