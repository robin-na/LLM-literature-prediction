Journal: Scientific Reports

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are final payoffs (efficiency/welfare), average contributions, and sanction use. Social norm perceptions and attitudes are also measured via questionnaires.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; participants seated in individual booths.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0 to 20 points per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.2 (total benefit per unit contributed is 0.8, less than cost of 1).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 4 points to the punisher; target loses 12 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 12 points lost per 4 points spent (ratio 3:1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is available in R and PR treatments; its presence strongly increases efficiency.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward costs 4 points to the rewarder; target gains 12 points.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 12 points gained per 4 points spent (ratio 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of rounds (20).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each PGG, all group members see each other's contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants have fixed unique IDs, so sanctioning is identifiable.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use small groups (4 players); results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Participants are Japanese university students; cultural and demographic generalizability is limited.
- Sample sizes are moderate, which may limit power to detect small effects, especially for punishment.
- The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is fixed at 4:12; results may differ with other ratios.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or communication are not tested.
- The design uses fixed IDs and repeated interactions; results may differ with anonymous or one-shot settings.
