# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Indirect Reciprocity, Resource Sharing, and Environmental Risk: Evidence from Field Experiments in Siberia
Authors: Howe, E. L., Murphy, J. J., Gerkey, D., & West, C. T.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual earnings
- amount received from sharing
- amount allocated to group activity
Non-payoff outcomes:
- sharing behavior
- allocation to group activity (contribution)
- reputation effects
- response to environmental risk (shock)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are sharing behavior and allocation to the group activity (i.e., contribution). While individual earnings and sharing received are reported, group efficiency (total group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly analyzed. The study focuses on how sharing and reputation interact with cooperation, not on group-level efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment found that voluntary sharing was substantial and directed toward those in need, especially when reputations could be built. Sharing was conditioned on cooperative behavior when such information was available. However, the presence of sharing, rewards, and reputation mechanisms did not increase contributions to the public good (i.e., did not increase cooperation in the production domain). The expected returns from contributing to the group activity, even with the possibility of receiving sharing, were not high enough to incentivize greater cooperation. Thus, the mechanisms studied did not improve group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that, in environments where sharing is voluntary and not highly efficient, and where rewards/punishments are not strong or direct, the introduction of sharing and reputation mechanisms may not increase group efficiency or cooperation in public goods games. The findings imply that unless the payoff structure makes rewards or punishments highly effective, simply enabling sharing or reputation is unlikely to boost efficiency. For prediction, if the control game has low efficiency and the only change is the addition of a voluntary, non-committal sharing/reward mechanism (with no direct punishment and no high-impact reward), the efficiency in the treatment is unlikely to increase. The paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment/reward mechanisms depends on their efficiency and observability.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups were primarily 5 players, with some 4-player groups in a few sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 5 rounds of Baseline, then 8 rounds of one of four sharing treatments (13 rounds total per session).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (time allocation) is continuous: each player allocates 0-50 'hours' between individual and group activity.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each hour allocated to group activity yields 20 rubles, divided equally among 5 players: MPCR = 4/10 = 0.4.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward exists via voluntary sharing; sharing can be used to reward cooperators, but is not highly efficient and does not increase group cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to rewarder is 1:1 (each ruble shared costs the giver 1 ruble).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (each ruble given is received by the recipient). This low efficiency is discussed as a reason for lack of increased cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds in each stage; instructions and procedures were explained in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, allocation decisions and earnings of each group member were publicly revealed (by anonymous letter). In sharing treatments, only aggregate sharing outcomes were revealed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were identified by a letter, but the mapping changed each round except in the Reputation treatment. In sharing, only aggregate amounts were revealed, not who gave to whom.

## Important Limitations
- No direct punishment mechanism; only voluntary sharing (reward/withholding aid) is present.
- Sharing is not highly efficient (1:1 cost/benefit), which may limit its impact on cooperation.
- Group-level efficiency or total payoff is not directly analyzed; focus is on individual behavior and sharing.
- The field context and cultural norms may limit generalizability to other settings.
- Sample size per treatment is moderate (N=29-40), which may limit statistical power for some comparisons.
- The design does not include a treatment with direct, costly punishment or highly efficient reward.
