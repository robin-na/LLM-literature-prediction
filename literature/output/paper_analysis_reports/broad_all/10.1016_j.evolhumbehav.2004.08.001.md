## Outcomes Measured

Non-payoff outcomes:
- fractions of cooperation and defection strategies

Outcome notes:
The paper reports the prevalence of different strategies (cooperation, defection, corruption, fairness) as the main outcomes. While the text discusses 'overall social payoffs' and 'maximization of collective interests', there are no explicit calculations or reporting of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The main dependent variables are behavioral (strategy fractions), not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The bidirectional supervision mechanism, where players can check and avoid corrupt umpires, is effective at suppressing both corruption and defection in a spatial donation game with third-party punishment. The effectiveness of this mechanism depends on the punishment amount (must be within a certain range), the bribe amount (must not be too high), and the ratio of tax to checking cost (higher tax or lower checking cost is better). The benefit-to-cost ratio of cooperation has little effect within reasonable ranges. The main outcomes are the fractions of different strategies (cooperation, defection, corruption, fairness) in the population. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but argues that the mechanism is most beneficial to collective interests when parameters are tuned appropriately.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the design of punishment, bribe, tax, and checking cost parameters can strongly affect the prevalence of cooperation and corruption in a donation game with third-party punishment. However, because the main outcomes are behavioral (strategy fractions) and not efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used as qualitative or mechanistic guidance rather than quantitative prediction of efficiency. The results suggest that punishment is only effective at suppressing defection and corruption if its severity is within a certain range and if corruption incentives (bribes) are not too high. The presence of a mechanism for players to avoid corrupt punishers is critical. The findings are most relevant for adjacent game designs with third-party punishment and corruption, but less directly transferable to standard public goods games or to quantitative efficiency prediction.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each simulation uses a 500x500 lattice (250,000 players), each with four neighbors (Von Neumann neighborhood). The player count is fixed and very large; the effect of player count is not varied or analyzed.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 100,000 steps, with averages taken over the last 5,000 steps. The number of rounds is fixed and very large; not varied or analyzed for effect.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (donate) or defect (do not donate).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a donation game (a two-player prisoner's dilemma variant), not a standard PGG. The benefit-to-cost ratio b (analogous to MPCR) is varied, but found to have negligible effect within reasonable ranges.

Punishment is implemented as a fine (alpha) paid by defectors to fair umpires. The cost to the punisher (umpire) is not explicit, as umpires are paid via taxes. The effect of punishment amount (alpha) is systematically analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the fine amount (alpha). The effect of varying alpha is analyzed; effectiveness is maximized within a certain range.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players can check the type (fair or bribed) of their own and neighbors' umpires by paying a checking cost (theta). This is a core part of the bidirectional supervision mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- The game is a spatial donation game (prisoner's dilemma variant), not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- Outcomes are reported as behavioral strategy fractions, not as efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff; claims about maximizing collective interests are qualitative.
- The model assumes very large populations and spatial structure, which may not generalize to small groups or well-mixed populations.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included.
- Parameter ranges and effects are specific to the model; real-world applicability is not tested.
