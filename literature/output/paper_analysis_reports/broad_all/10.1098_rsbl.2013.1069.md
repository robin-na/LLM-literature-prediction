# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: An experimental study of strong reciprocity in bacteria
Authors: Inglis, R. F., West, S., & Buckling, A.
Journal: Biology Letters
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- invasion success of cooperative punishers
- extinction times of invading strains
- frequency dynamics of strains (punishing cooperator, non-punishing cooperator, cheats)
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the ability of different strains (cooperative punisher, non-punishing cooperator, and their mixture) to invade a population of cheats, and the extinction times of these strains. There is no direct measurement of group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare. Outcomes are population dynamics and survival/extinction, not explicit payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in bacterial populations, cooperative punishers (who both produce a public good and punish non-cooperators) can invade a population of cheats only when the traits for cooperation and punishment are tightly linked. When the linkage is broken (by mixing punishing and non-punishing cooperators), invasion fails. Non-punishing cooperators alone also cannot invade. The main outcome is the frequency and survival/extinction of the different strains, not group efficiency or total payoff. The results support theoretical predictions that linkage is necessary for the evolutionary success of strong reciprocity in this context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and by implication, group efficiency) depends critically on the linkage between cooperation and punishment. However, the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff directly, and the outcomes are evolutionary (strain invasion/extinction) rather than economic payoffs. The findings suggest that in systems where punishment and cooperation are not tightly linked, punishment may not improve group outcomes. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the importance of linkage or coupling between cooperation and punishment, but less so about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency in standard public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each subpopulation is started with ~10,000 cells, but the concept of 'player' is mapped to bacterial cells. The experiment is structured as metapopulations with subpopulations, not discrete human players.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. There are 9 transfers (rounds of selection), each representing a round of population mixing and selection.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Cooperation and punishment are fixed traits (on/off) in each strain, not continuous choices.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment (toxin production) is individually costly, but the exact cost is not quantified.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is effective only against sensitive cheats; effectiveness is functionally linked to the presence of the receptor.

## Important Limitations
- The study is conducted in bacteria, not humans, and the mapping to public goods games is indirect.
- Outcomes are evolutionary (strain invasion/extinction), not economic payoffs or group efficiency.
- No direct measurement of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.
- Punishment and cooperation are fixed genetic traits, not strategic choices.
- The concept of 'player' is mapped to bacterial cells or strains, which may not generalize to human or agent-based PGGs.
