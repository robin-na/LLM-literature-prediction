# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inducing efficient conditional cooperation patterns in public goods games, an experimental investigation
Authors: Guillen, P., Fatas, E., & Brañas-Garza, P.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average contribution
- individual payoff (formula provided)
- average payoff (reported as 19.71 EUR including show-up fee)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution
- polarization of contributions (all-or-nothing)
- conditional cooperation pattern
- decline in contributions over time
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution level. Payoff is described and the formula is given, but efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed or reported as a main outcome. The study focuses on the effect of a 'grim trigger' threat (belief manipulation) rather than actual punishment or sanctions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of a grim trigger strategy threat (via possible automata) leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (by 40% before a surprise restart and 50% after) compared to a standard repeated public goods game. Contributions in the threat treatment are highly polarized (either full or zero), and the pattern of conditional cooperation changes: in the threat treatment, the minimum contribution in the previous round predicts current contributions, consistent with grim trigger logic. However, contributions still decline over time, and the effect is driven by belief manipulation rather than actual punishment or sanctions. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the higher contributions imply higher group payoffs, net of the absence of punishment costs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that belief in the possibility of strict conditional cooperation (grim trigger) can substantially increase contributions in repeated public goods games, even without actual punishment. However, because there is no real punishment mechanism (no cost, no assignment of punishment, no reduction of others' payoffs), the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to enabling punishment. The results suggest that the mere threat or belief in sanctions can have large effects on cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency impact of actual punishment mechanisms, especially considering the cost of punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, but the absence of real punishment means the findings should be used as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per game, with a surprise restart for an additional 10 rounds (total 20 rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; any communication is forbidden and punished by expulsion.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (0-50), but in the threat treatment, behavior is polarized to all-or-nothing (≥45 or 0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5 (contributions multiplied by 2, divided among 4 players).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 10 rounds; after round 10, a surprise restart is announced for another 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects see the ranked vector of others' contributions (from highest to lowest), but not identities.

## Important Limitations
- No actual punishment or sanction mechanism is implemented; the 'threat' is a belief manipulation, not a real cost-imposing mechanism.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed or reported as a main outcome; only contributions are analyzed in detail.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with real punishment, as the cost and impact of punishment are not present.
- The effect is driven by uncertainty and belief in conditional cooperation, not by actual enforcement or sanctioning.
- The study does not report efficiency ratios or total group payoffs relative to the social optimum.
