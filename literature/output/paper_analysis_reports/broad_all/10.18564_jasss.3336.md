Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate of CC outcomes in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, which is interpreted as a proxy for cooperation. Payoff is not directly reported as group efficiency or total earnings, but the model is evolutionary and fitness-based, so higher CC rates are associated with higher group-level cooperation. The models do not use a public goods game but a repeated PD with post-game punishment/fighting.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in agent-based models of repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with post-game fighting (punishment), aggressive strategies (harassers) become evolutionarily stable and promote high rates of mutual cooperation (CC) in small groups with strong deterrence. The mechanism is that agents develop reputations for toughness, deterring defection and reducing the need for actual fighting. In large groups, anonymity undermines this effect, and cooperation rates fall. When third-party (moralized) punishment is allowed, coalitions can enforce cooperation, but only under certain conditions (small groups, justified defection rules). The models suggest that punishment can increase group-level cooperation and, by proxy, efficiency, but only under specific structural conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence that enabling punishment in repeated PD-like environments can increase cooperation rates (and thus efficiency) compared to control, especially in small groups with strong deterrence and information spread. The effect is weaker or absent in large, anonymous groups. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is reputation-based and where agents can observe and remember others' aggressive acts. The results are not from public goods games but from repeated PDs with post-game punishment, so transfer to PGGs should be cautious but is likely informative for adjacent settings. The paper does not report efficiency as a direct ratio of group payoff to maximum, but CC rates can be used as a proxy for high group welfare.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Population size (player count per simulation) is a key manipulated variable: 50, 100, or 500. Small groups (50, 100) show much higher cooperation (CC) rates with punishment enabled than large groups (500), where the effect is weak or absent.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Each generation consists of 200 interaction rounds, but the number of rounds is not varied or analyzed as a moderator.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: cooperate or defect in the PD game (all-or-nothing choice).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost (pun_cost) is varied: 3, 6, 9. However, the effect of punishment cost on cooperation is found to be negligible in these models.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact (punishment parameter) is set at 9; effectiveness is determined by fight outcome and reputation. The deterrence parameter (1, 3, 6) modulates the perceived strength of aggressive agents, which is a key mechanism for punishment effectiveness.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Agents observe fights and outcomes, and information about aggressive acts is spread via gossip (parameter: gossip = 5 or 20). However, gossiping had negligible effect on cooperation rates.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Agents can identify and remember who punished (fought) whom via the fear_list and observation. This is central to the deterrence mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- The models use repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not public goods games; transfer to PGGs is indirect.
- Efficiency is not reported as a direct ratio of group payoff to maximum, but inferred from CC rates.
- Agent-based models are theoretical and may not capture all real-world complexities.
- The positive effect of punishment on cooperation is limited to small groups; in large groups, the effect disappears.
- No empirical or experimental data; results are simulation-based.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are modeled.
- No communication or chat between agents is modeled.
- Well-mixed populations are assumed; local interaction structures are not explored.
