# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reward and punishment in a team contest
Authors: Heine, F., & Strobel, M.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean income per participant
- total tokens earned
- overspending compared to Nash equilibrium
- rent dissipation
- group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to group account
- response-giving (reward/punishment) frequency and amount
- who receives response
- dynamics of contribution and response
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables include both payoff-based outcomes (mean income, total tokens, overspending, group payoff) and behavioral outcomes (contribution, response-giving). Efficiency is explicitly discussed in terms of realized payoffs versus Nash equilibrium and social optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares reward and punishment mechanisms in a team contest game (with and without between-group competition). Groups of four play 15 rounds, deciding how much to contribute to a group lottery (contest) and then can reward or punish teammates at a cost. The main findings are: (1) Both punishment and reward mechanisms lead to over-contribution relative to Nash equilibrium, causing significant efficiency losses (rent dissipation). (2) Punishment does not increase efficiency; it reduces it, as punishing is costly and does not significantly increase contributions. (3) Rewarding increases contributions more than punishment, but is welfare-neutral (cost to giver equals benefit to receiver). (4) The contest environment (with an opponent group) leads to even greater inefficiency and lower average payoffs than the non-contest environment. (5) The R&P treatment (both reward and punishment available) in the contest environment is the least efficient, with the highest overspending and lowest mean income. (6) Receiving reward increases subsequent contributions, but punishment does not. (7) Overall, the presence of punishment does not solve the efficiency problem in this environment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a team contest environment with public-good-like features, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency and may worsen it, especially when both reward and punishment are available. The effect of punishment is less positive (more negative) on efficiency compared to reward or baseline. The contest structure (between-group competition) amplifies inefficiency. For prediction, if the control game (no punishment) is already inefficient due to over-contribution, enabling punishment is likely to further reduce efficiency, not improve it. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, known MPCR, punishment cost and tech, no identification of punishers, no communication) are all specified and match standard lab contest/PGG designs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled in similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players; contest is between two groups of 4 (8 players per contest).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds per session, known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how many tokens (0-100) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is implicit in the contest success function: each token buys a lottery ticket for the group; winning probability is proportional to group tickets. Prize is 100 tokens per group member if group wins.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:1 (cost to punisher = deduction from target).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (1 token spent removes 1 token from target).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available in some treatments (reward, R&P).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1:1 (cost to rewarder = benefit to recipient).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (1 token spent gives 1 token to target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds (15) is disclosed and commonly known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see how much each of their group members contributed after each round; also see total tickets bought by opponent group (if applicable).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are anonymous; players do not know who punished or rewarded them.

## Important Limitations
- Comparison between contest and non-contest environments may be affected by the use of a fixed number of blank tickets in the non-contest, which could create a level effect.
- The contest environment uses a dynamic opponent group, while the non-contest uses static blank tickets, potentially confounding strategic uncertainty effects.
- Rewarding is welfare-neutral (cost to giver = benefit to receiver), while punishment is welfare-reducing; this difference may affect efficiency comparisons.
- No communication or reputation mechanisms are allowed, which may limit generalizability to real-world group settings.
- The Nash equilibrium and social optimum differ between contest and non-contest environments, complicating direct efficiency comparisons.
