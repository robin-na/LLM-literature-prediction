## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group achievement (fraction of groups that successfully produce or maintain the public good)
- stationary distribution of strategies (C, D, P)
- prevalence of sanctioning institutions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- prevalence of punishers
- prevalence of defectors
- institutional stability
Outcome notes:
The main outcome is 'group achievement' (η_G), defined as the average fraction of groups that reach the public good threshold. This is not a direct measure of efficiency or total group payoff, but is closely related to successful collective action. The model tracks the stationary distribution of strategies and the prevalence of institutions, but does not report total payoffs, welfare, or efficiency ratios.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that local, group-level sanctioning institutions are much more effective than global, population-level institutions at promoting successful collective action in a threshold public goods game with risk of collective loss. Local institutions increase the fraction of groups that reach the public good threshold (group achievement), especially when the perceived risk of failure is low. Global institutions are unstable and provide only marginal improvements. The effect is stronger in smaller groups. The model does not report efficiency or total payoff, but the results strongly suggest that local punishment institutions increase the likelihood of group success, which is closely related to efficiency in this context.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling local, group-level punishment institutions in threshold public goods games (with risk of collective loss) will increase the rate of successful group outcomes compared to no punishment or global punishment. While the model does not report efficiency or total payoff, the 'group achievement' outcome is a close proxy for efficiency in threshold settings. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is local, groups are small, and risk perception is low. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so its value is mainly in qualitative prediction and mechanism understanding.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Population size Z and group size N are explicit model parameters. Main results use Z=100, N=4. Smaller groups (lower N) are associated with stronger positive effects of local punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and tracks stationary distributions over time, not discrete rounds. The game is repeated indefinitely in a Markov process.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a fixed fraction c of the endowment; players choose to contribute or not (C, P, or D). The public good is provided only if a threshold number of contributors is reached (all-or-nothing threshold).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly named, but the model specifies the cost c and benefit b per player, and the public good is provided if the threshold is met. In main results, c/b=0.1.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishers pay a tax π_t (e.g., π_t=0.03) to fund the institution. The cost is explicit and varied in the model.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is set by the fine π_f (e.g., π_f=0.3) applied to defectors if the institution is funded. The model varies this parameter.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency, total payoff, or welfare directly; the main outcome is the fraction of groups achieving the public good (group achievement), which is a proxy for efficiency but not equivalent.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented; results are based on evolutionary game theory simulations.
- The model assumes fixed group size and does not explore heterogeneity in group composition or network structure beyond local/global institution distinction.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are considered.
- The model does not include communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms that may affect cooperation in real-world settings.
