## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review argues that mechanisms such as punishment, reputation, and signaling are crucial for sustaining cooperation in large-scale human societies, especially where kinship and direct reciprocity are insufficient. It highlights that the willingness to punish norm violations (including in public goods and related games) varies dramatically across societies and is associated with community size and institutional context. The review synthesizes evidence that punishment can sustain cooperation, but also that the form and prevalence of punishment are culturally variable. It also notes that mechanisms supporting cooperation can stabilize both prosocial and maladaptive costly norms. However, the review does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment mechanisms can sustain cooperation in public goods game-like environments, especially in large, anonymous societies. It also highlights that the effectiveness and prevalence of punishment depend on cultural, institutional, and demographic factors such as community size. However, because the review does not report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions and control efficiency. Its main value is in identifying which dimensions (e.g., community size, presence of punishment, cultural context) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation, and in providing context for interpreting empirical findings from other studies.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Public goods games are described as involving n players; the review discusses how group size and community size affect the prevalence and effectiveness of punishment, but does not provide quantitative analysis of player count effects on efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review describes both binary (all-or-nothing) and continuous contribution games (e.g., binary dictator game, public goods game with any portion of endowment).
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is described in the definition of the public goods game, but not analyzed as a moderator.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher; the cost structure is discussed in general terms, but not manipulated or analyzed for efficiency effects.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment effectiveness (e.g., multiplier in third-party punishment game) is mentioned, but not analyzed as a moderator.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Some games and mechanisms discussed involve knowledge of others' actions (e.g., reputation, summary information), but not systematically analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or meta-analyze efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
- Outcomes discussed are primarily behavioral (contribution, punishment, norm compliance) rather than efficiency or welfare.
- Game design dimensions are discussed in general terms, but not systematically analyzed for their quantitative effect on efficiency.
- Cultural, institutional, and demographic moderators are highlighted, but not operationalized for predictive modeling.
- The review draws on a wide range of studies, but does not provide effect sizes or direct estimates relevant to the prediction of treatment efficiency.
