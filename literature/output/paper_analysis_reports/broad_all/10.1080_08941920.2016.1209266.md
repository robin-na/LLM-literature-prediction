# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Environmental Policies Can Buttress Conservation Norms
Authors: Nolan, J. M.
Journal: Society & Natural Resources
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution decisions
- internal sanctions (guilt/shame)
- indirect social sanctions (disapproval)
- direct social sanctions (messages, emoticons, point deductions)
- expectations of cooperation
- decision frame (ethical/economic)
- perceived choice (intrinsic motivation)
- attributions for others' behavior (trust)
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variables are informal sanctioning measures (internal, indirect, direct), contribution rates, and psychological variables. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. Contribution is measured, but not translated into efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab public goods game, introducing formal sanctions (either market-based or regulatory) increases contributions in the round where sanctions are present, and increases informal sanctioning (internal, indirect, and some direct measures) toward defectors. However, the effect on contribution does not persist after sanctions are removed. The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. The field study supports the finding that formal sanctioning systems are associated with stronger informal sanctioning norms, but again does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that introducing punishment (formal sanctions) in a standard linear public goods game increases contributions while the sanction is present, and increases informal sanctioning. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are partially specified and can be inferred for the lab experiment, but the main outcomes are non-payoff behavioral measures. The paper is useful for understanding the psychological and social norm effects of punishment, but not for predicting efficiency changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Lab experiment groups were presented as 8 players (though only 3-5 real participants per session, with deception).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Five rounds; critical manipulation in Rounds 2 and 3.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Participants could send messages and emoticons after feedback, but not general chat before or during contribution decisions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: participants choose how many of 10 points to contribute (0-10).
- default contribution framing: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Experimenter recommended contributing at least 7 points, but no default enforced.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Points contributed to the group account are doubled and distributed equally among 8 players. MPCR = 2/8 = 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Formal sanction: 1 point deducted from private account for under-contribution. In direct social sanctioning, participants could pay 0.25 points to add or subtract 1 point from another's account.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Formal sanction: 1 point penalty for under-contribution. Direct social sanction: 0.25 cost for 1 point impact (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Participants could add points to others' accounts at a cost (reward), but the main focus is on punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 0.25 points per 1 point added to another's account.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1 point per 0.25 cost (effectiveness = 4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Participants were told the experiment would last five rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Participants received feedback about other players' contributions (preprogrammed).

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are reported; only contribution and psychological/behavioral outcomes are measured.
- The lab experiment uses deception and simulated group members, which may affect external validity.
- The field study is quasi-experimental and cannot establish causality.
- The main outcomes are informal sanctioning and contribution, not efficiency or welfare.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly inferred from the reported results.
