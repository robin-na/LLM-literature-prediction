Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the final level of public good produced, which is a direct function of the number of contributors and thus equivalent to group efficiency in this context. Payoff is explicitly modeled for each agent. Non-payoff outcomes include network structure and behavioral adaptation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games on networks, the ability of agents to change their social ties in response to social comparison (i.e., to seek out like-minded cooperators and avoid defectors) can sustain or increase cooperation and thus the production of public goods. Low network density and low costs of network change (low cohesion) allow cooperators to persist by forming supportive subgroups, leading to higher efficiency. High initial cooperation and low segregation also promote full cooperation. When network change is costly (high cohesion), cooperation collapses and efficiency is low. The model shows that both forward-looking and backward-looking (adaptive) agents can achieve high efficiency under favorable network conditions, but backward-looking agents require even more favorable starting conditions. Explicit costly punishment is not modeled; instead, social comparison provides a form of endogenous, non-costly social pressure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that endogenous network change and social comparison can substitute for explicit costly punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that game designs allowing for dynamic partner selection or network rewiring (low cohesion/costs) will see higher efficiency, especially when initial cooperation is high and the network is not highly segregated. However, the absence of explicit costly punishment means the results are most applicable to settings where social comparison or endogenous social pressure is the main incentive, not where formal punishment is enabled. The results are directly informative for predicting efficiency in networked PGGs with endogenous tie change, but less so for classic PGGs with explicit punishment options.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use n=50 agents; player count is fixed in all reported results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are iterated until equilibrium or for a fixed number of iterations (e.g., 4000 for backward-looking agents). The game is recurring/repeated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: agents either contribute one unit or nothing (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is defined by c1 (cost of contribution) and n (group size); c1=8, n=50 in simulations. The game is set up as an n-person Prisoner's Dilemma.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Agents observe the actions of their neighbors (social comparison), but there is no explicit summary information shown as in lab experiments.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism is modeled; only endogenous social comparison (felt reward/punishment) is present.
- The payoff function assumes that one unit of public good is as valuable as one unit of behavioral confirmation from a friend, which is a strong and potentially unrealistic assumption.
- All social ties are of equal strength; heterogeneity in tie strength is not modeled.
- Simulations use fixed group size (n=50) and do not explore variation in player count.
- Results are theoretical/simulation-based and not empirically validated.
- Backward-looking (adaptive) agent model is relatively simple and may not capture more sophisticated learning or adaptation processes.
- Findings may not generalize to classic PGGs with explicit punishment or to settings without endogenous network change.
