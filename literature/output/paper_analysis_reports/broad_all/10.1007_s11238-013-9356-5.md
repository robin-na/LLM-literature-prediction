# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Different carrots and different sticks: do we reward and punish differently than we approve and disapprove?
Authors: Leibbrandt, A., & López-Pérez, R.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of punishment and reward (monetary and non-monetary)
- intensity/strength of punishment and reward
- determinants of punishment/reward/approval/disapproval (e.g., inequity aversion, reciprocity)
- A-player allocation choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and strength of punishment/reward (monetary and non-monetary) and their determinants. The study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare in a repeated public goods context. Some references to average payoffs exist, but the focus is on behavioral responses, not efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that monetary punishment is used more often and more intensely than non-monetary disapproval, but monetary reward and non-monetary approval are used at similar rates. The determinants of monetary punishment/reward are primarily payoff-based (inequity aversion), while non-monetary approval/disapproval is more strongly driven by reciprocity and norm-based considerations. Strict payoff equality reduces disapproval and increases approval. The experiment is not a repeated public goods game but a set of one-shot, two-player allocation games, so efficiency or group payoff outcomes are not reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment and reward, distinguishing between monetary and non-monetary forms. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes in a repeated public goods context, nor does it manipulate or report most of the game design dimensions relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. Its main value is in clarifying that the impact of punishment/reward on behavior may depend on whether the mechanism is monetary or non-monetary, and that motivations differ. For direct prediction of efficiency effects in PGGs, this paper is only indirectly informative.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (A and B) allocation games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays four one-shot games; only one is randomly selected for payment. No repeated interaction or feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. A chooses between two discrete allocations; not a continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (B) pays a fixed cost of 5 points to assign punishment/reward (monetary or non-monetary).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In monetary treatment, B can increase or decrease A's payoff by up to 100 points (per 5-point cost). In non-monetary, B can assign approval/disapproval score (no effect on A's payoff).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is possible in both treatments (monetary and non-monetary).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder (B) pays a fixed cost of 5 points to assign reward (monetary or non-monetary).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In monetary treatment, B can increase A's payoff by up to 100 points (per 5-point cost). In non-monetary, B can assign approval score (no effect on A's payoff).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are four games and that one will be randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No feedback about the other player's actual choices is given before decisions are made; no summary information is provided during the experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is anonymous; A is informed of the score assigned by B at payment, but not B's identity.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a repeated public goods game; it uses one-shot, two-player allocation games, so findings may not generalize to standard PGG settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (punishment/reward/approval/disapproval frequencies and intensities) are analyzed.
- Most game design dimensions relevant for PGG efficiency prediction (e.g., mpcr, group size, repeated interaction) are not present or manipulated.
- The study focuses on the motivations for punishment/reward rather than their effects on cooperation or efficiency.
