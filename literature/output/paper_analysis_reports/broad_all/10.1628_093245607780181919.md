# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ex interim voting:: An experimental study of referendums for public-good provision
Authors: Fischer, S., & Nicklisch, A.
Journal: Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group profits
- efficiency (ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff)
- average profits when public good is provided
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- voting behavior
- frequency of public good provision
- yes vote rates
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group profits (efficiency) and contribution rates. Voting behavior and provision frequency are also analyzed. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the focus is on voting mechanisms as a safeguard against exploitation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Unanimity voting increases contributions but leads to frequent rejections, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to majority voting or automatic provision. Majority voting does not significantly differ from automatic provision in terms of efficiency or profits. The mechanism (voting rule) is the primary driver of these outcomes, not individual voting behavior. The study provides direct evidence on how institutional design (voting rules) affects efficiency in public good games, but does not test punishment or sanctioning per se.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is directly informative for predicting efficiency in public good games with voting mechanisms but does not provide evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that mechanisms that safeguard against exploitation (like unanimity voting) can increase contributions but may reduce efficiency due to coordination failures or excessive vetoes. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 5 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group played 10 rounds, then were rematched for another 10 rounds (20 rounds total per subject).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (integer values from 0 to 20), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) was 0.4 in all treatments.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the number of rounds in advance (10 rounds per matching group, then rematched for another 10).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, players were informed about the contributions of each group member and about each subject's payoff in both provision and reimbursement cases.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on voting as a safeguard, not on punishment.
- Findings on efficiency and contributions may not generalize to settings with punishment or reward mechanisms.
- Only one value of mpcr (0.4) and group size (5) are tested; results may differ with other parameters.
- No communication between players; effects of chat or negotiation are not tested.
- The study uses a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
