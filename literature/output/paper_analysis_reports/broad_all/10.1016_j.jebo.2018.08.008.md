Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2018

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is joint/pair profit (total earnings of the pair), which is directly analogous to efficiency. The paper also reports behavioral outcomes such as entry rates, cooperation, and strategy use, but these are secondary to the payoff-based analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three treatments of a two-player entry game: simultaneous moves (Now), endogenous timing (Wait), and exogenous sequential moves (Seq). In all treatments, players have private values and must decide whether to enter or exit, with payoffs depending on both players' choices. The main finding is that the Seq treatment yields the highest average pair profits (mean 170.2), followed by Wait (167.9), and Now (163.2). The difference between Seq and Now is statistically significant. The two-stage games allow cooperative pairs to coordinate more efficiently and achieve higher profits, but also allow uncooperative pairs to do worse, leading to a wider distribution of outcomes. The Wait and Seq treatments reduce double exit and increase efficient cooperation compared to Now, but also see a modest increase in double entry. The paper does not study punishment as a formal mechanism, but discusses how uncooperative behavior in the two-stage games can be met with retaliatory entry (a form of endogenous punishment).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that changing the timing structure of a two-player entry game (from simultaneous to sequential or endogenous timing) can increase average efficiency, but with substantial heterogeneity: cooperative pairs benefit, while uncooperative pairs may do worse. The results are highly relevant for predicting efficiency in games where the main design dimension is the timing of moves, but less so for games where formal punishment mechanisms (with explicit costs and impacts) are present. The findings suggest that enabling sequential or observable timing can improve efficiency, especially when players can coordinate, but may also increase the risk of low efficiency if players are uncooperative. The paper does not manipulate or analyze formal punishment costs or technologies, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled public goods games is limited, but it is informative about the role of timing and endogenous retaliation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use fixed pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair plays 60 rounds (after 5 practice rounds).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/entry decision is binary: enter or exit.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If both enter, each receives 1/3 of their value; if only one enters, that player receives their full value. This is analogous to an MPCR of 1/3 for joint entry.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is 60 rounds; the number of rounds is fixed and known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects observe their partner's decision and value.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game, but a two-player entry game with congestion and private values. Results may not generalize to multi-player or standard PGG settings.
- No formal punishment or reward mechanism is present; only endogenous retaliation via entry is possible.
- All groups are fixed pairs; no random rematching, which may affect cooperation dynamics.
- The study only examines two-player games; effects in larger groups are unknown.
- The findings on efficiency are conditional on the specific payoff structure (private values, 1/3 payoff for double entry).
- The paper does not manipulate or analyze punishment cost or technology, so direct implications for punishment-enabled PGGs are limited.
