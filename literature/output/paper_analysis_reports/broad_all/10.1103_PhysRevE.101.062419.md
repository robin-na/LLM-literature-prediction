The fraction of cooperators and the basin of attraction for cooperation are also discussed, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both autonomous and consensual punishment can promote cooperation and increase average payoffs in public goods games. Consensual punishment (where a group vote determines whether defectors are punished) can be more effective than autonomous punishment when the consensus threshold is low and the willingness to punish is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation dominates defection in terms of average payoffs. The effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the group size, the marginal per-capita return, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the consensus threshold for punishment. The paper also shows that increasing the cost of punishment or the group size can make autonomous punishment more effective relative to consensual punishment, while increasing the fine or the marginal return favors consensual punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given game design parameters such as group size, number of rounds (implicitly infinite in the model), marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the decision rule for punishment (autonomous vs. consensual, and the consensus threshold). The explicit payoff formulas and comparative statics allow for quantitative or qualitative prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment (especially with low consensus thresholds and high willingness to punish) will increase efficiency, but the effect is moderated by punishment cost, group size, and the consensus rule.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size N is a key parameter in the model, with explicit comparative statics (e.g., Table II shows how increasing N affects the critical willingness to punish for consensual vs. autonomous punishment). Larger groups can make autonomous punishment more effective relative to consensual punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes an infinite and well-mixed population, corresponding to an infinite or indefinite number of rounds (replicator dynamics). No explicit finite-round analysis.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The enforcement factor r (1 < r < N) is the multiplier; MPCR = r/N. Higher r (and thus higher MPCR) increases the basin of attraction for cooperation and makes consensual punishment more effective.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit (alpha) is explicitly modeled and varied. Higher punishment cost reduces the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation and efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per unit cost, beta/alpha) is explicitly modeled. Higher punishment effectiveness (higher beta) increases the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Assumes infinite, well-mixed populations and replicator dynamics, which may not capture finite group or repeated-game effects.
- No explicit modeling of communication, information feedback, or psychological factors.
- No analysis of reward mechanisms or combined reward and punishment.
- No explicit treatment of finite rounds or end-game effects.
- The model assumes binary strategies (cooperate/defect) and fixed contribution amounts.
