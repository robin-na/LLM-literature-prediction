## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in structured populations, the introduction of both antisocial and prosocial pool rewarding does not deter the evolution of cooperation or reduce group efficiency. Instead, prosocial rewarding continues to promote cooperation, and the aggregation required for rewards to be effective leads to higher group payoff and efficiency. The critical threshold for cooperation to dominate is lowered as the effectiveness of rewarding increases, even when defectors can also reward each other. In well-mixed populations, antisocial rewarding can prevent cooperation unless cooperators are initially predominant, but in structured populations, local aggregation allows cooperation to thrive. The results are robust to increased randomness in the interaction network.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games with pool rewarding, the presence of antisocial rewarding does not reduce the efficiency gains from prosocial rewarding in structured populations. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling reward mechanisms (even if both cooperators and defectors can use them) will generally increase efficiency compared to control, provided the population structure allows for aggregation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings with spatial or network structure, group size of 5, all-or-nothing contributions, and no punishment. The results are less applicable to well-mixed populations or to games with punishment rather than reward.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no experimental or observational data are presented.
- The model assumes all-or-nothing contributions and fixed group size (G=5), which may limit generalizability to other settings.
- The results are most relevant to structured populations; in well-mixed populations, antisocial rewarding can undermine cooperation.
- No punishment mechanism is modeled, so findings may not generalize to settings with punishment.
- The evolutionary model does not use discrete rounds or human decision-making processes, which may differ from laboratory or field experiments.
