# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Conditional cooperation and the effect of punishment
Authors: Kirchkamp, O., & Mill, W.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- conditional cooperation slope
- probability of being a conditional cooperator
- punishment assigned
- classification of player types (conditional cooperator, free rider)
- punishment sensitivity to others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: the slope of conditional cooperation, the probability of being a conditional cooperator, and punishment behavior. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes. Payoff calculations are described in the methods, but efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three treatments: a baseline public goods game (no punishment), a treatment where the conditional decision maker (CDM) can be punished by an unconditional decision maker (UCPun), and a treatment where the CDM can punish an unconditional decision maker (CPun). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (i.e., more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), and actually increases the slope of conditional cooperation due to a responsibility effect. There is evidence for substitution between punishment and cooperation in punishment behavior, but not in contribution behavior. The overall effect of punishment is to increase conditional cooperation, but with some increase in free riding. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency is not measured.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a one-shot public goods game increases the slope of conditional cooperation, but also increases the number of free riders. The net effect on cooperation is positive, but the effect on efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that punishment is likely to increase cooperation rates, but may have ambiguous effects on efficiency if the increase in free riding offsets the gains from stronger conditional cooperation. The design features—such as costless punishment, no communication, and a one-shot game—should be considered when generalizing to other settings. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff analysis limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single period).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants allocate 0-20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token in the public account returns 0.4 to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher (zero cost per unit).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's payoff by 1 point; maximum 10 points per target.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single period).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the game, participants are told the overall contribution of their group and their own payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes; only behavioral outcomes are analyzed.
- Punishment is costless, which differs from most standard public goods experiments where punishment is costly.
- The experiment is one-shot (not repeated), limiting generalizability to repeated games.
- No communication is allowed, which may affect the external validity for settings with communication.
- The sample is limited to non-economics students from a single German university.
- The effect of punishment on actual group efficiency or total earnings is not measured, so the impact on efficiency is inferred only indirectly from behavioral changes.
