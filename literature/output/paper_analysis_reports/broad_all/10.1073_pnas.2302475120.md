# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How reputation does (and does not) drive people to punish without looking
Authors: Jordan, J. J., & Kteily, N. S.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount of money shared with Actors in a Dictator Game (as a reputational reward for punishment/looking behavior)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment behavior (signing punitive petitions)
- looking behavior (considering opposing perspectives before punishing)
- punishment without looking (punishing without considering opposing perspectives)
- evaluations of Actors (positivity, fairness, competence, loyalty)
- influence of reputational observability on punishment and looking
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (punishment, looking, punishment without looking) and reputational evaluations. The only payoff-related outcome is the amount of money shared in a Dictator Game, which is used as a reputational reward, not as a group efficiency or welfare measure. There is no group public goods game, and no group efficiency or surplus is measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that reputational incentives can increase the likelihood that individuals engage in punishment (signing punitive petitions), and as a byproduct, some of this punishment occurs without considering opposing perspectives. However, when the process of 'looking' is made observable, individuals are more likely to consider opposing perspectives, and punishment without looking does not increase. Evaluators (the audience) reward punishment in general, but do not reward punishment without looking; in fact, they see it as less fair and competent. The effect is stronger with more ideological audiences. The study does not use a public goods game or measure group efficiency, but rather focuses on reputational and behavioral outcomes in a Dictator Game context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or social dilemmas. Its design is adjacent: it uses a reputational Dictator Game with real-world punitive actions (signing petitions) and manipulates observability of punishment and information-gathering. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment, looking) and reputational (evaluations, money shared as a reward), not group efficiency or welfare. The findings suggest that making punishment observable increases punishment (and punishment without looking), but making deliberation observable increases deliberation. For prediction tasks about efficiency in PGGs, this paper provides only indirect, contextual evidence about how reputational incentives can increase punishment rates, but not about their effect on group efficiency or welfare.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main experimental units are dyads: one Actor and one Evaluator. There is no group interaction as in a PGG; each Actor is matched with a single Evaluator for reputational feedback.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes a single decision per study (whether to punish, whether to look). There is no repeated or multi-round structure.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as a binary choice: sign the petition (punish) or not. Looking is also binary: click at least one link to an opposing perspective or not.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment (signing a petition) is a real action with potential real-world consequences, but no explicit monetary cost to the punisher is described. The cost is more reputational or effort-based.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is collective and indirect (signing a petition that could, with enough support, lead to a costly outcome for the target). The impact per unit action is not quantified.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Evaluators can reward Actors by sharing money in a Dictator Game, based on the Actor's punishment and looking behavior.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the rewarder (Evaluator) is the amount of their endowment they choose to share with the Actor (from $0 to $0.50).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is direct: every cent shared is transferred from Evaluator to Actor.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure: a single decision per study, not repeated.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Manipulated: in some conditions, Evaluators observe whether the Actor punished, and/or whether they looked. In other conditions, nothing is observable.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental paradigm is not a public goods game or social dilemma; it uses a reputational Dictator Game with real-world punitive actions (signing petitions).
- No group efficiency, welfare, or surplus is measured; the only payoff-related outcome is the amount of money shared as a reputational reward.
- Punishment is not costly in the monetary sense for the punisher; costs are reputational or effort-based.
- Findings may not generalize to economic games with explicit group payoffs or to settings where punishment directly affects group efficiency.
- The audience (Evaluator) is always a single individual, not a group, and the reputational context is dyadic.
- The study focuses on collective, indirect punishment (petitions), not direct costly punishment as in PGGs.
- The sample is from online platforms (MTurk, Prolific) and may not generalize to other populations or real-world settings.
