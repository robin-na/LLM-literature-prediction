# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: TAX AUDITING AS A PUBLIC GOOD GAME: AN EXPERIMENTAL STUDY ON PUNISHMENT AND COMPLIANCE
Authors: Hsu, L. C.
Journal: Pacific Economic Review
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- average taxes reported and discovered
- average taxes reported and discovered plus fines
- average reported tax per audit
- compliance rate/audit probability
- compliance rate/monitoring costs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- compliance rate
- percentage of subjects declaring zero income
- percentage of subjects fully complying
- average audit probability
- average monitoring points
- percentage of subjects investing no monitoring points
- percentage of subjects investing all taxes to the group monitoring fund
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is compliance rate (reported income / true income), which is a behavioral outcome. Some payoff-related outcomes (average earnings, audit productivity indices) are reported, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed. Average earnings are reported, but the focus is on compliance and audit productivity.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The endogenous-audit (collective punishment) treatment produces much higher compliance rates than both the no-audit and random-audit treatments. In the final 10 rounds, compliance rates are 0.297 (no audit), 0.758 (random audit), and 0.921 (endogenous audit). However, average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment (85.2 points) than in the random-audit treatment (92.1 points), due to the distribution of fines and discovered taxes. Audit productivity (e.g., reported tax per audit) is higher in the endogenous-audit treatment in the latter half of the game. The main effect of punishment is to increase compliance, but the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous due to the cost of monitoring and the way fines are pooled.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a collective punishment mechanism (endogenous audit) in a public-goods-like tax game increases compliance rates compared to both no-punishment and exogenous punishment (random audit) conditions. However, the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous, as average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment than in the random-audit treatment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment can increase compliance but may not always increase efficiency, depending on the cost structure and how punishment/fines are redistributed. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit cost structure, etc.) are well specified and can be mapped to the prediction task, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per round; 12 subjects per session, randomly rematched into groups of 4 each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; subjects are randomly and anonymously grouped each round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (reported income) is continuous between 0 and true income.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (m) is 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In endogenous-audit, punishment is funded by transferring part of one's tax payment to the monitoring fund; no extra out-of-pocket cost beyond the tax paid. The group monitoring fund has a quadratic cost structure for auditing (c(s) = a s^2, a=2).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic: the group monitoring fund determines how many group members are audited. If audited and caught, the penalty is 3x the evaded tax.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. No explicit reward mechanism; only punishment (auditing and fines). The public good is funded by taxes and fines, but there is no direct reward for compliance.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds (20).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are shown a result report with the number of points declared and invested in the public good by each group member and the group total, as well as their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is collective and anonymous; subjects do not know who contributed to the monitoring fund for auditing, only the group total and whether they were audited.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed; only average earnings and audit productivity indices are reported.
- The punishment mechanism is collective and indirect (via audit probability), not direct peer-to-peer punishment.
- Average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit (punishment) treatment than in the random-audit treatment, which may not generalize to all public goods games.
- The mapping to standard PGGs is close but not exact due to the tax compliance framing and the two-stage structure.
- The sample is limited to undergraduate students in Taiwan, which may affect generalizability.
