# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Curbing coca cultivation in Colombia - A framed field experiment
Authors: Ibanez, M., & Martinsson, P.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- expected income
- willingness to accept compensation to stop cultivating coca
- cost-effectiveness of carrot and stick policies
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment in coca (amount and probability)
- heterogeneity in moral cost and risk aversion
- responsiveness to carrot and stick policies
- classification of producer types
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: investment in coca (extensive and intensive margin), and willingness to accept compensation to stop cultivating. Payoff-related outcomes are discussed in terms of expected income and policy cost-effectiveness, but group efficiency (as in PGG) is not directly measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that both increased risk of punishment (eradication) and increased legal profits (subsidies) reduce investment in illegal coca cultivation, but the effect is highly heterogeneous. About one third of participants have high moral costs and do not need external incentives, while two fifths are very hard to deter. Only about a quarter are responsive to policy levers. The supply of coca is inelastic to policy changes unless eradication risk is high (>30%). Carrots (subsidies) are more effective in areas with better market integration, while sticks (punishment) are more effective in depressed areas. The study provides cost estimates for both policies and suggests that optimal policy should mix both, tailored to local conditions. However, the main outcomes are behavioral (investment choices), not group efficiency or total welfare as in standard PGGs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence on how exogenous punishment (risk of eradication) and reward (subsidy) policies affect individual investment in a socially harmful activity, with a design adjacent to public goods games. The findings support that punishment can reduce undesirable behavior, but the effect is highly dependent on individual heterogeneity. The results are most useful for predicting how changes in punishment and reward parameters might affect behavioral outcomes (e.g., investment in the public bad), but less directly for predicting group efficiency as defined in PGGs. The cost-effectiveness analysis and elasticity estimates can inform models that link punishment/reward design to behavioral and, indirectly, payoff outcomes, especially in field-like or framed settings with real-world stakes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 participants were used in the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant made 9 decisions (one per treatment), but these are not repeated rounds in a dynamic game; rather, they are separate one-shot decisions under different policy conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) is continuous: participants allocate 0-10 points between coca and cattle.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal return to legal alternative (a) is manipulated (0.2, 0.44, 0.68); the marginal return to coca is 1. The negative externality per unit of coca is 0.17 per group member.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous: risk of eradication (p) is manipulated (0%, 10%, 30%). If eradication occurs, the loss is 1.2 points per point invested in coca. The cost to the punisher (the state) is discussed in policy cost analysis.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the probability of eradication and the loss per unit of coca. The technical rate of substitution and cost-effectiveness are analyzed.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (carrot) is present: the relative profit of the legal alternative is manipulated as a proxy for subsidy.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost of reward (subsidy) is analyzed in policy cost-effectiveness calculations.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is analyzed via elasticity of investment to changes in legal profit/subsidy.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were 9 decisions (treatments) to make; all were presented in advance.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a framed field experiment with a specific population (Colombian coca farmers), limiting generalizability to standard PGG settings.
- Main outcomes are behavioral (investment in coca), not group efficiency or total welfare as in PGGs.
- Punishment and reward are exogenously imposed by the experimenter, not endogenously chosen by peers.
- No direct measurement of group payoff or efficiency; only individual expected income and policy cost-effectiveness are analyzed.
- No communication or feedback between participants; social dynamics may differ from standard PGGs.
- Heterogeneity in moral cost and risk aversion is central, which may not map directly to standard PGG participant pools.
- Assumptions in policy cost calculations (e.g., perfect monitoring, effectiveness of eradication) may not hold in practice.
