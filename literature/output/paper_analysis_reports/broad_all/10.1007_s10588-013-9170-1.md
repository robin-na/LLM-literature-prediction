# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Evolving intergroup cooperation
Authors: Bausch, A. W.
Journal: Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory
Year: 2014

theory; empirical subtype=N/A; experimental subtype=N/A

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- percentage of CC (cooperation-cooperation) outcomes in across-group interactions
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of punishment (in-group policing, collective sanctions)
- strategy composition of agent population
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of CC outcomes in across-group interactions, which is a behavioral measure of cooperation, not a direct measure of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. The model is based on a Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game, and does not report group payoffs or efficiency ratios.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in-group policing is highly effective at promoting inter-group cooperation in an evolutionary Prisoner's Dilemma model, with high levels of CC outcomes (over 80% in many runs). In contrast, collective sanctions alone do not increase cooperation above baseline and can even reduce it when combined with in-group policing, especially when the sanction period is long. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the behavioral results suggest that agent-level punishment is more effective than group-level punishment for sustaining cooperation. The findings are robust across a range of parameterizations, but are limited to behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates) rather than direct payoff or efficiency measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments structurally similar to a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with group structure, agent-level punishment (in-group policing) is much more effective at sustaining cooperation than group-level punishment (collective sanctions). However, because the model does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and is not a public goods game, its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments is limited. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can promote or hinder cooperation, and for anticipating that group-level punishment may not translate into higher efficiency or payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the presence of in-group policing (individualized punishment) is likely to have a more positive effect on cooperation than collective sanctions, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Population size is fixed at 2601 agents in the main simulations (Appendix A). Agents are divided into two groups. The model is not a small-n repeated game but a large agent-based simulation.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model is run for thousands of time steps until a stochastic steady state is reached (burn-in 3000 steps), but there is no fixed number of rounds as in a standard repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a binary Prisoner's Dilemma: agents choose to cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each interaction.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Agents have access to reputational tags (cheater status) of in-group members and, for out-group, only group-level tags. No explicit summary statistics are shown, but agents use available information for strategy.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In-group members can identify which of their own group cheated on the out-group (for in-group policing), but out-group members cannot identify individual cheaters (collective sanctions).

## Important Limitations
- The model is based on a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game, so results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates) are analyzed.
- Punishment is not modeled as a costly action; there is no explicit punishment cost or effectiveness parameter.
- The model assumes two groups; results may differ with more groups or different group structures.
- No communication, leadership, or coordination mechanisms are modeled.
- Norms for punishment are exogenous and not endogenized.
- Parameter values (e.g., population size, mutation rate) are fixed and may affect generalizability.
