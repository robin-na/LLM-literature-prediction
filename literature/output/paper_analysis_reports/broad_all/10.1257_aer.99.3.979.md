# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation among Strangers under the Shadow of the Future
Authors: Camera, G., & Casari, M.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- realized efficiency (ratio of actual group payoff to maximum possible group payoff)
- average cooperation rate (as a proxy for efficiency, but efficiency is directly reported)
- average earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual cooperation/defection choices
- punishment behavior (frequency, targeting)
- strategy use (reactive, global, targeted, personal punishment)
- transitional matrices of cooperation
- probit regressions on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are realized efficiency and cooperation rate. Punishment behavior and strategy use are also analyzed in detail. Efficiency is explicitly defined and reported for each treatment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In small, randomly matched groups playing indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma games, enabling costly personal punishment (where a player can pay 5 points to reduce the opponent's payoff by 10) in an otherwise anonymous, privately monitored environment increases both cooperation rates and realized efficiency. Specifically, average realized efficiency rises from 0.595 (private monitoring, no punishment) to 0.652 (private monitoring with punishment). The effect is comparable to that of making individual histories public (efficiency 0.815), though the efficiency gain from punishment is partially offset by the cost of punishment itself. The paper also finds that simply making aggregate histories public (without identities) does not improve efficiency. Subjects tend to use selective punishment strategies, and personal punishment is often used as a substitute for informal punishment (defecting in future rounds). Larger group sizes (14 vs. 4) lead to much lower cooperation and efficiency, suggesting strong group size effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for the effect of enabling costly personal punishment in indefinitely repeated, small-group, random-matching prisoner's dilemma environments. The design is adjacent to public goods games but not identical (pairwise PD rather than n-player PGG), so results should be transferred with some caution. The key finding is that, holding all else constant, enabling punishment increases realized efficiency substantially (from 0.595 to 0.652), though not as much as making individual histories public (0.815). The cost of punishment reduces the efficiency gain relative to the increase in cooperation. The paper also shows that group size is a strong moderator: larger groups have much lower efficiency. For prediction, the paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency in small, anonymous, privately monitored repeated games, with the caveat that the effect is partly offset by the costliness of punishment. The results are most applicable to settings with 4 players, random matching, no chat, continuous contribution/defection, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 5/10.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a pairwise prisoner's dilemma rather than a standard n-player public goods game, so results are adjacent but not exact for PGG environments.
- Group size is small (4 players); results may not generalize to larger groups, as shown by much lower cooperation/efficiency in 14-player sessions.
- Punishment is costly and only available as a personal, direct sanction; no institutional or third-party punishment is studied.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit generalizability to environments where communication is possible.
- The effect of punishment is partly offset by its cost, so efficiency gains are less than the increase in cooperation.
- The indefinite horizon is implemented via a random continuation rule, which may differ from fixed or known-horizon settings.
