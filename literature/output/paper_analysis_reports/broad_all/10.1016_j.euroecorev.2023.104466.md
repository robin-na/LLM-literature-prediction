# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Civic engagement, the leverage effect and the accountable state
Authors: Kamei, K., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- group payoff as percent of social optimum
- likelihood of achieving sanctioning institution
- main stage allocations to public sector as percent of optimum
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public sector (main stage)
- number of civic tasks completed (pre-stage, 2nd-order cooperation)
- feedback and approval ratings in social circles
- individual heterogeneity in civic engagement
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings and allocations to the public sector (as a percent of the social optimum), both with and without the sanctioning institution. The number of civic tasks completed is a key behavioral outcome, but the primary focus is on efficiency and group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a large-group lab experiment, participants first face a public goods allocation problem (main stage) and can only enable a central punishment institution (which enforces optimal contributions) if enough group members voluntarily complete costly 'civic tasks' in a pre-stage (second-order dilemma). When the punishment institution is absent, group efficiency is low (public sector allocations rapidly decline to ~13% of the optimum). When the institution is present, allocations and group earnings approach the social optimum. The probability of achieving the institution is much higher when the cost of civic engagement is low and when social interaction is allowed. The study demonstrates that the presence of a punishment institution, conditional on sufficient civic engagement, robustly increases efficiency in public goods provision. The effect is strongest when the cost of civic engagement is low and when social feedback is available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a central punishment institution in a public-goods-like environment dramatically increases efficiency, moving group payoffs close to the social optimum. The effect is conditional on the institution being present, which depends on a prior (second-order) cooperation problem. The efficiency gain from punishment is robust across treatments, but the likelihood of achieving the institution (and thus the efficiency gain) is higher when the cost of enabling punishment is low and when social interaction is present. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, given a design where punishment is enabled (i.e., the institution is present), efficiency will be much higher than in the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect is moderated by the cost of enabling punishment and by the presence of social interaction, but the direction is consistently positive for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 24 participants, a large group size chosen to foster a sense of 'society'.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Part 1: 3 rounds without sanctions; Part 2: 15 rounds with possibility of sanctions (total 18 rounds per session).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution to the public sector is continuous (0-20 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is not linear; public sector allocation has both direct (logistic) and indirect (piecewise linear) benefits. Social optimum is at 8 tokens per person (P*=192 for 24 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized: when the scheme is present, non-contributors pay a fine of 18 points per token below 8. The cost to the punisher is not directly specified, as the institution is collective and not peer-to-peer.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: 18-point fine per token below target, sufficient to make optimal contribution privately optimal when the scheme is present.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No material reward mechanism; only non-monetary approval/disapproval feedback in social circles.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of periods in advance (finite repetition, 18 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In social interaction treatments, subjects see the number of civic tasks completed by social circle members and receive feedback; in all treatments, summary information about group allocations is available.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment institution is only present if enough participants engage in costly civic tasks; thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on solving a second-order cooperation problem.
- The experiment uses a large group size (24), which may not generalize to smaller groups or real-world settings with different social structures.
- The punishment mechanism is centralized and not peer-to-peer, so results may not generalize to decentralized punishment settings.
- The cost of enabling punishment (civic engagement) is experimentally manipulated and may not reflect real-world costs or motivations.
- No monetary reward mechanism is present; only non-monetary social feedback is available.
- The public goods game is not a standard linear PGG; the payoff structure includes both direct and indirect (productivity) effects, which may affect generalizability.
- The study does not explore partial or imperfect punishment institutions, only the polar cases of full absence or presence.
