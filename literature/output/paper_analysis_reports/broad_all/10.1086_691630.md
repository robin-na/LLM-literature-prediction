# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: You Are in Charge: Experimentally Testing the Motivating Power of Holding a Judicial Office
Authors: Engel, C., & Zhurakhovska, L.
Journal: Journal of Legal Studies
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean earnings of active participants
- mean earnings of authorities
- profit of active participants
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- frequency and severity of punishment
- probability of being punished
- punishment assigned
- sensitivity of punishment to contributions
- effect of authority's social value orientation (SVO) on punishment
- effect of framing (public official/judge) on punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contribution rates, punishment frequency/severity, and how these are moderated by authority characteristics and framing. Payoff-related outcomes (mean earnings, profit) are reported but not the primary focus, and efficiency per se is not directly calculated or compared between punishment and no-punishment conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Authorities (randomly assigned, with no personal stake in group outcomes) are willing to incur costs to punish free riders in a repeated linear public goods game. This punishment leads to higher contributions and higher payoffs for active participants than the Nash equilibrium (no contribution, no punishment). The effect is robust to framing the authority as a public official or judge, which further reduces the influence of personal preferences on punishment. Announcing punishment policies, election of judges, and experience all modulate the targeting and sensitivity of punishment, but the basic effect—punishment increases cooperation and group payoffs—remains. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), but group profit is reported and is higher than in the absence of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized punishment (by a non-beneficiary authority) in a repeated linear public goods game increases group payoffs and efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across variations in authority framing, policy announcement, election, and experience. The design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, full information on others' actions and punishment) are all specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the social optimum, so the effect must be inferred from reported mean earnings and profit.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 active participants and 1 authority (5 total per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment consists of 10 periods (rounds); the experience treatment has 20 periods for the authority (two groups of 10).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; the experiment is fully anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each active participant can contribute any number of ECUs from 0 to 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each ECU contributed is multiplied by 0.4 and distributed to all 4 active participants).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs the authority 1 ECU per punished active player per period, regardless of severity.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Authority can reduce any active participant's income by up to 80 ECUs per period, with a total punishment budget of 80 ECUs per period for the group. Cost to authority is fixed per punished player, not per unit of punishment.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of periods in advance; the game is repeated for a fixed number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Active participants are shown the individual contributions, punishment assigned, and income of all group members each period.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always assigned by the single authority, whose identity is known only as 'the authority' (not by personal ID); the role is known but not the individual.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio to the social optimum; only mean earnings and profit are provided.
- The authority is a centralized third party with no personal stake, which differs from standard decentralized punishment in PGGs.
- The experiment uses student participants, not real judges or officials.
- The authority's punishment cost is fixed per punished player, not per unit of punishment, which may affect generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), with only secondary reporting of payoff outcomes.
