# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Meeting a national emission reduction target in an experimental setting
Authors: Brick, K., & Visser, M.
Journal: Climate Policy
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings by player type
- percentage of groups meeting the emission reduction target
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution to public good
- frequency distribution of contribution levels
- compliance with minimum contribution (tax) requirements
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels and compliance with targets. Group earnings and the percentage of groups meeting the target are reported, but efficiency (as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff) is not directly calculated or discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Voluntary cooperation alone is insufficient to meet a group emission reduction target in a public good game with heterogeneous players. Allowing communication increases average contributions and the proportion of groups meeting the target (from 35% to 50%), but also increases polarization between full cooperation and free-riding. Introducing a tax-based sanction (automatic fine for under-contribution) leads to near-universal compliance with the target (88–91% of groups meet the target), but also crowds out voluntary contributions above the minimum required. The effect of taxation on contributions above the minimum differs by player type: for capital, taxation reduces excess contributions; for labour, taxation can encourage excess contributions relative to baseline. The study does not directly analyze efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the compliance rates and average earnings suggest that tax-based sanctions are highly effective at achieving the group target.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that automatic, rule-based sanctions (taxes/fines for under-contribution) are highly effective at increasing compliance with group targets in public good games, even with heterogeneous players. Communication alone improves cooperation but is less reliable than sanctions. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of institutional (not peer) punishment on group compliance and contributions, especially in settings with asymmetric player types and marginal returns. However, because the study does not report efficiency in the standard sense (group payoff as a share of maximum possible), its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. Still, the high compliance rates under tax-based sanctions suggest that efficiency would be much higher with punishment enabled than in the voluntary or communication-only conditions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 4 players (2 capital, 2 labour) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Each sequence consisted of four repetitions of the baseline treatment; the number of rounds per treatment is not clearly specified for all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) was allowed in the COMM treatment and significantly increased average contributions and target compliance.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could allocate any number of their 10 tokens to the public account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.42 for all players, despite asymmetric returns to private and public accounts for capital and labour.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In tax treatments, the punishment cost is 10 tokens per token below the minimum required contribution (automatic fine).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is automatic and proportional: for each token below the minimum, a 10-token fine is imposed.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Participants were informed about the structure and sequence of treatments; unclear if the exact number of rounds was always known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told the player-types or individual contributions of other group members, but had access to summary sheets and payoff tables.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, limiting its direct applicability to efficiency prediction tasks.
- Punishment is implemented as an automatic, institutional sanction (tax/fine), not as peer punishment; results may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- The public good game uses heterogeneous players (capital and labour) with different marginal returns, which may limit comparability to standard symmetric PGGs.
- Contribution and compliance outcomes are emphasized over direct payoff or welfare outcomes.
- The number of rounds per treatment is not always clearly specified, and repeated play effects are not the main focus.
