The main dependent variable for the prediction task is group efficiency (average group payoff, including both A-types and B-type, accounting for punishment costs). The paper also reports on contribution rates, punishment levels, and beliefs, but these are secondary to the efficiency analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public good game with binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, groups could vote to introduce a third-party punishment institution. When punishment was enabled, a B-type player could assign costly deduction points to defectors (non-contributors), with each deduction point costing the punisher 1 point and reducing the defector's income by 3 points. The main experimental manipulation was whether the punishment institution was implemented endogenously (by majority vote) or exogenously (by random assignment, sometimes overruling the vote). The study finds that: (1) Punishment is significantly milder when the institution is endogenously chosen, but is more effective in inducing cooperation per unit of punishment; (2) Exogenous punishment is harsher, leading to similar or slightly higher cooperation in the long run, but at higher punishment cost; (3) Overall, group efficiency (total payoffs) is not significantly different between endogenous and exogenous punishment conditions; (4) The presence of punishment (vs. no punishment) increases cooperation and efficiency, but the method of institution selection does not further increase efficiency. The study provides direct evidence on efficiency, group payoff, and the moderating role of institutional endogeneity.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a public good game increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the way the punishment institution is implemented (endogenous vs. exogenous) does not significantly affect overall efficiency, even though it affects punishment severity and effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is a key driver of efficiency gains, but whether the institution is chosen by vote or imposed does not further increase group efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, binary contribution, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for mapping design to efficiency outcomes. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is lower than with punishment enabled, and the efficiency gain from punishment is robust to the method of institution selection.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players: 3 A-types (contributors) and 1 B-type (third-party punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per part (two parts: one baseline, one treatment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: each A-type can contribute all (70 points) or nothing each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.6 (each 70-point contribution increases each A-type's payoff by 42 points).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point costs the B-type 1 point and reduces the defector's income by 3 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point spent by B-type reduces defector's income by 3 points).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward mechanisms are discussed in literature review but not implemented in this experiment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 20 periods in each part; instructions specify this.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, A-types learn the number of contributors and their own income; no detailed breakdown of others' payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is administered by a single, known B-type (third-party) in each group; A-types know the B-type's role but not their identity outside the experiment.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a binary (all-or-nothing) contribution structure, which may limit generalizability to continuous-contribution PGGs.
- Punishment is only possible against defectors (no anti-social punishment), and the maximum punishment is non-deterrent (cannot fully offset the gain from defection).
- The third-party punisher (B-type) has no material stake in the public good, which may differ from real-world settings where enforcers are not fully impartial.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- The effect of punishment is only measured for a specific MPCR (0.6), group size (4), and punishment cost/tech parameters.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
