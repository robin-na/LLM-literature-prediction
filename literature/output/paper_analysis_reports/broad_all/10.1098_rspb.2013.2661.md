The main reported outcomes are the evolutionary prevalence of responsible third-party punishment and cooperation rates. Payoff is discussed in terms of local and relative pay-off advantage, but not as explicit group efficiency or total welfare. The model tracks evolutionary success (strategy spread) as a function of pay-off, but does not report group efficiency as a ratio to full cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that responsible third-party punishment (3PP) can evolve and sustain cooperation in structured populations when social-structural constraints are high (i.e., high strength-of-ties and low mobility). Under these conditions, clusters of responsible punishers can induce cooperation in their local neighborhoods, leading to higher local pay-offs and the spread of both cooperation and punishment. In contrast, in well-mixed or highly mobile populations, responsible 3PP does not evolve, and cooperation fails to emerge. The model shows that 3PP is pivotal for the emergence of cooperation and increased pay-off, as neither population structure alone nor direct punishment alone suffice. The findings are robust to variations in network structure and to the presence of antisocial punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation and increasing pay-off (and thus efficiency) depends critically on the social structure of the population. Specifically, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency in games with high strength-of-ties (low average degree, i.e., players interact repeatedly with a small set of neighbors) and low mobility (players do not frequently change partners). In well-mixed or highly mobile settings, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with fixed, repeated local interactions and low partner switching will see greater efficiency gains from punishment mechanisms, while designs with random matching or high mobility will not. The model is adjacent to public goods games (it uses a two-player cooperation game with third-party punishment), so the transfer to standard PGGs is not exact but likely informative.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and evolutionary, not empirical or experimental.
- The game is a two-player cooperation game with third-party punishment, not a standard public goods game; transfer to PGGs is plausible but not exact.
- Efficiency is not reported as a group payoff ratio to full cooperation, but as local pay-off advantage and prevalence of cooperation.
- The model assumes agents know the punishment strategies of their neighbors (perfect punishment reputation), which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are modeled.
- The model does not vary or analyze the effect of player count or number of rounds as moderators.
