# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ex-ante Evaluation of Policy Measures: Effects of Reward and Punishment for Fertiliser Reduction in Palm Oil Production
Authors: Moser, S., & Musshoff, O.
Journal: Journal of Agricultural Economics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- profit difference (efficiency measure)
- total profit per round
- costs of reducing fertiliser use
Non-payoff outcomes:
- fertiliser use (as a proxy for compliance/behavioral response)
- sustainability (persistency of behavioral change)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is profit (efficiency), with behavioral outcomes (fertiliser use) as mediators. Efficiency is defined as the summarized costs and benefits for all affected stakeholders, operationalized as profit difference between compliant and non-compliant behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that, holding expected income effects constant, the design of incentives (reward vs. punishment, certain vs. uncertain) significantly affects both behavioral response and efficiency. Certain rewards are the most efficient (i.e., minimize profit loss for a given reduction in fertiliser use), while uncertain punishments are the least efficient. Certain punishments and uncertain rewards are intermediate. Punishment is less efficient than reward, and uncertainty reduces efficiency. The findings suggest that policymakers aiming for efficient reduction of negative externalities should prefer certain rewards over punishments or uncertain incentives.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a public-good-like field context, the efficiency impact of punishment (vs. reward) depends strongly on the design: punishment is less efficient than reward, and uncertainty in the incentive (probabilistic punishment or reward) further reduces efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment (especially uncertain punishment) will not increase efficiency as much as enabling certain rewards, even if the expected value is held constant. The results are most applicable to field settings with individual decision-making and externalities, but less so to classic multi-player PGGs with peer punishment. The efficiency outcome is measured as profit difference, directly supporting payoff-based prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session involved groups of 10-18 participants, but the game was played individually (each participant managed their own simulated farm). No direct group interaction or multiplayer public goods structure.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per participant (5 pre-treatment, 5 post-treatment).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a deduction from participant profit; there is no explicit cost to a punisher (since punishment is imposed by the experimenter/policy, not by peers).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment magnitude is either 10,000 Rp (certain) or 100,000 Rp with 10% probability (uncertain), both designed to have the same expected value.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward treatments are directly compared to punishment treatments.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is implemented as a bonus to participant profit; no explicit cost to a rewarder (reward is imposed by the experimenter/policy, not by peers).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward magnitude is either 10,000 Rp (certain) or 100,000 Rp with 10% probability (uncertain), both designed to have the same expected value.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were 10 rounds, but were not told when the policy change would occur (it always occurred after round 5).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a classic public goods game: decisions are individual, and payoffs are not directly interdependent except via the externality framing.
- Punishment and reward are imposed by the experimenter/policy, not by peers; there is no cost to the punisher or rewarder.
- The context is specific to Indonesian palm oil farmers and fertiliser use; generalizability to other settings or classic PGGs is uncertain.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (mpcr) or group payoff structure as in standard PGGs.
- No group interaction, communication, or peer monitoring.
- Efficiency is measured as profit difference, not as group surplus relative to a social optimum.
- Uncertainty in randomization and price assignment may have affected comparability across treatments.
