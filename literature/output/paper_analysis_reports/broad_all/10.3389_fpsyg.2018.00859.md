## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews neuroscience evidence showing that costly punishment, cooperation, trust, and fairness are associated with activation in specific brain regions, such as the prefrontal cortex, dorsal striatum, anterior insula, and amygdala. It discusses how these neural mechanisms underlie pro-social behaviors observed in economic games, including the willingness to punish norm violators at a personal cost. However, the review does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, nor does it provide quantitative or qualitative evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in public goods or related games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence to inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it reviews behavioral and neural evidence on costly punishment and other pro-social behaviors, the outcomes discussed are primarily neural activation and behavioral propensities (e.g., willingness to punish, cooperate, or trust), not efficiency or group payoff. The review may be useful for understanding the psychological and neural mechanisms underlying punishment, but it does not support quantitative or qualitative prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review discusses costly punishment (punishment at a personal cost) as a central concept, but does not analyze variation in punishment cost or its effect on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical and neuroscience review, not an empirical study of efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- No direct evidence is provided on the effect of punishment on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare in public goods or similar games.
- Game design dimensions relevant to efficiency prediction (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds) are not analyzed.
- Outcomes discussed are primarily neural activation and behavioral propensities, not efficiency or group payoff.
- The review synthesizes findings from diverse experimental paradigms, making it difficult to extract game-specific design evidence.
