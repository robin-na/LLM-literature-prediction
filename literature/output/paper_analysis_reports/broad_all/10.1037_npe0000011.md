# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social Rewards and Sanctions: A Voluntary Contribution Mechanism Experiment
Authors: Kumakawa, T.
Journal: Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution
- change in contribution after receiving reward or sanction
- frequency of reward and sanction use
- message valence (positive/neutral/negative)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is individual contribution (cooperation), not group payoff or efficiency. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, nor does it provide enough data to reconstruct these outcomes. The analysis focuses on how social and monetary rewards/sanctions affect subsequent contributions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that in a two-player public goods game, the opportunity to send social (free-form) or monetary rewards/sanctions does not increase first-round contributions compared to a baseline with no such opportunity. However, when participants actually experience social sanctions (i.e., receive a negative message from their partner), their contributions in the next round increase significantly. Social rewards (positive messages) do not have a significant effect. Monetary sanctions and rewards are rarely used and do not significantly affect subsequent contributions. The study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player public goods game with a game-theoretic payoff table and no prior knowledge of a second round, social sanctions (blame via free-form messages) can increase subsequent contributions, but only after they are actually experienced. The mere opportunity for sanctions or rewards does not affect cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (contribution) responses to social sanctions, not efficiency outcomes. The design (two players, two rounds, no chat except for the sanction/reward stage, full information about partner's contribution, and a linear MPCR of 0.7) should be considered when generalizing to other settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment consists of two rounds. Subjects are not told about the second round until the first is complete.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication is allowed except for the written message in the social reward/sanction stage (SRS treatment).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose an integer investment from 0 to 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.7.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the monetary reward/sanction treatment (MRS), cost to punisher is 1 per point assigned (1-to-1 cost/impact). In the social reward/sanction treatment (SRS), there is no monetary cost to sending a message.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In MRS, each point reduces the target's payoff by 1 and costs the punisher 1 (1-to-1). In SRS, the impact is non-monetary (social disapproval).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both social and monetary reward options are present in SRS and MRS treatments, respectively.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In MRS, cost to rewarder is 1 per point given (1-to-1). In SRS, no monetary cost to sending a positive message.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In MRS, each point increases the target's payoff by 1 and costs the rewarder 1 (1-to-1). In SRS, the impact is non-monetary (social approval).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are not told about the second round until the first is complete; thus, they do not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see their own investment, their partner's investment, and their own payoff. They do not see the partner's payoff or information about other pairs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is always from the known partner in the pair; identity is known within the pair.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The experiment uses only two players per group, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- The number of rounds is only two, and subjects do not know about the second round in advance, limiting strategic behavior.
- The use of a game-theoretic payoff table may affect the salience and use of punishment/reward mechanisms compared to standard payoff presentations.
- The main outcome is contribution (cooperation), not efficiency or group payoff.
