# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Powering up with indirect reciprocity in a large-scale field experiment
Authors: Yoeli, E., Hoffman, M., Rand, D. G., & Nowak, M. A.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- participation rate in demand response program
- effect of observability on participation
- effect of monetary incentive on participation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the fraction of residents who sign up for a voluntary demand response program (i.e., participation rate). No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus is reported. The study focuses on behavioral participation as a proxy for cooperation in a public goods context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that making participation in a voluntary demand response program observable to neighbors nearly triples participation rates compared to an anonymous condition. The effect of observability is much larger than that of a $25 monetary incentive. The effect is strongest in contexts where reputational concerns are likely to matter more (e.g., apartment buildings, homeowners vs. renters, and when the program is framed as a public good). The results support the theory that indirect reciprocity and reputational incentives can drive large-scale cooperation in real-world public goods dilemmas. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that increasing observability (reputational incentives) can dramatically increase participation in a real-world public goods context, especially when social ties and public good framing are salient. However, because the outcome is participation rate (not efficiency or group payoff), the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or payoff-based efficiency, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of punishment mechanisms. It does, however, suggest that non-monetary, reputational incentives can be more effective than small monetary incentives in increasing cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The intervention is at the level of residential HOAs and apartment complexes, with group sizes ranging from small to large (total N=2413). The exact group size per game is not specified, but the context is a large-scale, real-world public goods dilemma.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The decision is a one-shot participation choice (sign up or not) during a 3-10 day window; not a repeated or multi-round game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participation is an all-or-nothing decision: residents either sign up for the program or not.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. A $25 monetary incentive (reward) is experimentally manipulated in some conditions.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the rewarder (utility company) is $25 per participant in the incentive condition.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is measured by the increase in participation rate due to the $25 incentive.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the decision is a one-time sign-up opportunity; the duration of the sign-up window is known (3-10 days).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the observable condition, participants can see the names and unit numbers of those who have signed up (i.e., summary of others' participation). In the anonymous condition, this information is hidden.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is participation rate (a behavioral proxy for cooperation), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or analyzed; the study focuses on observability and reputational incentives.
- The public goods context is real-world and large-scale, but the mapping to standard laboratory PGG parameters (e.g., MPCR, group size) is indirect.
- No direct measurement of the marginal per-capita return or the efficiency of the intervention is provided.
- Findings may not generalize to settings where punishment, rather than observability, is the primary intervention.
