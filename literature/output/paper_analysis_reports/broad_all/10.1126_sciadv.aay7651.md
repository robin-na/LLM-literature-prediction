# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Procedural fairness and nepotism among local traditional and democratic leaders in rural Namibia
Authors: Vollan, B., Blanco, E., Steimanis, I., Petutschnig, F., & Prediger, S.
Journal: Science Advances
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- monetary consequences of antisocial leader behavior (amount destroyed)
- distributional choices in procedural fairness task (group and individual payoffs, but not efficiency per se)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- procedural fairness (choice of democratic, pseudo-democratic, or dictator rule)
- nepotism (leniency or vengefulness in punishment toward relatives)
- punishment behavior (altruistic punishment in trust game)
- social preferences (egalitarian, generous, spiteful types)
- personality traits (Big Five)
- villager perceptions of leader performance
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are leaders' choices of decision-making procedure (democratic/autocratic) and nepotistic punishment behavior. Some monetary outcomes are reported (e.g., amount destroyed by antisocial decisions), but efficiency or group payoff is not the primary focus. The punishment task is a trust game with third-party punishment, not a standard public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both traditional chiefs and democratically elected leaders in rural Namibia are similarly likely to implement democratic decision-making procedures and are similarly unlikely to engage in nepotistic punishment. Chiefs may be slightly more likely to choose democratic procedures and less likely to be lenient toward relatives in punishment, but these differences are not statistically robust. The main outcomes are behavioral (procedural choice, nepotism in punishment), not efficiency or group payoff. The punishment task is a trust game with third-party punishment, not a public goods game. The study suggests that, in this context of legal pluralism, traditional leaders are not more autocratic or nepotistic than elected leaders.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence on punishment behavior (altruistic punishment) and procedural fairness in a trust game with third-party punishment, not in a public goods game. It does not report efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it does show that, in a real-world setting, leaders' willingness to punish norm violations is not strongly affected by whether they are democratically elected or traditional chiefs, and that nepotism in punishment is low for both. The design and context differ from standard PGGs, so the transfer value for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGGs is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Procedural fairness task: 1 leader + 6 villagers per group; Nepotism task: leader observes/punishes trust game between 2 villagers (multiple pairs).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Procedural fairness task: 4 decision situations per leader; Nepotism task: each leader makes punishment decisions for 3 pairs (each with 2 possible actions).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the nepotism task, leaders spend 1 N$ to reduce the punished player's payoff by 3 N$ (punishment cost = 1 per unit).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 N$ spent by leader reduces punished player's payoff by 3 N$.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of decision situations is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Leaders are informed of villagers' choices in the trust game; villagers are informed of the decision rule chosen by the leader (democratic/pseudo-democratic/dictator).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment decisions by leaders are anonymous; villagers cannot trace back who punished.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental tasks are not public goods games; the main punishment task is a trust game with third-party punishment.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not the primary outcome; main outcomes are behavioral (procedural choice, nepotism in punishment).
- The context is local-level leadership in rural Namibia with legal pluralism; results may not generalize to other settings or to standard PGGs.
- Monetary stakes, while substantial locally, are much lower than real-life temptations for abuse of office.
- No direct manipulation of punishment/no-punishment conditions; all leaders have the option to punish in the nepotism task.
- Sample size for leaders is moderate (n=64), limiting power to detect small effects.
- No analysis of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in a repeated or multi-round context.
