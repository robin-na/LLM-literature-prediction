However, it does not report group efficiency or total group payoff as a primary outcome, but rather focuses on the conditions for strategy dominance and the required frequency of punishers for punishment to be effective.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that altruistic punishment in a public goods game is only effective at promoting cooperation (and thus improving group outcomes) if the frequency of punishers exceeds a threshold determined by the cost of contribution and the punishment level. If this threshold is not met, defection remains the dominant strategy and group efficiency does not improve. The analysis provides explicit formulas for the returns to each strategy and the minimum frequency of punishers required for punishment to be effective. The findings imply that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to improve efficiency; the population composition and punishment parameters are critical.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public goods games. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional: punishment only increases efficiency if the proportion of punishers is above a threshold that depends on the cost of contribution and the punishment level. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on the game parameters (contribution cost, punishment cost/impact) and the frequency of punishers. The explicit formulas can be used to estimate the required conditions for punishment to be effective, but the paper does not provide empirical efficiency values or group payoff data.

- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: players choose whether to contribute c or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N, where r is the multiplier and N is the number of players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (eta) is set equal to the penalty (beta); the effectiveness of punishment depends on the value of beta relative to contribution cost c.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is 1 (cost equals penalty); effectiveness depends on beta and the frequency of punishers.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff.
- Findings are based on mathematical models and evolutionary dynamics, not on observed human behavior.
- The model assumes fully committed punishers and does not address how such a majority would arise in practice.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency or total payoff; only strategy dominance and payoff formulas are provided.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or other institutional features that may affect real-world outcomes.
