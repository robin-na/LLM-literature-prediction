# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Investigating the effects of social information on spite in an online game
Authors: Watson, R., Morgan, T. J. H., Kendal, R. L., van de Vyver, J., & Kendal, J.
Journal: Evolutionary Human Sciences
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- social behaviour (slider: costly spite to costly altruism)
- frequency of spiteful, neutral, and altruistic choices
- influence of social information on social behaviour
- correlation between prior earnings and social behaviour
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is a behavioral slider from costly spite to costly altruism. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff is reported. The only monetary/point outcome is individual-level, not group-level, and is not analyzed as efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Participants overwhelmingly chose altruism over spite when given a costly choice to increase or decrease an anonymous partner's payoff. Exposure to social information indicating others had behaved spitefully (vs. altruistically) reduced the degree of altruism, but did not substantially increase spiteful acts. The source of social information (majority vs. most successful) had no effect. The study does not report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff, and the design is a one-shot, two-player, non-iterated game with a continuous choice from costly spite to costly altruism.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar repeated group games. The design is a one-shot, two-player, continuous-choice game where participants can pay to harm or help an anonymous partner, and the main outcome is a behavioral slider, not group efficiency or payoff. There is no repeated interaction, no group context, and no explicit public goods structure. The findings suggest that when both costly spite and costly altruism are available, spite is rare and social information can reduce altruism, but this is not directly transferable to the efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always two players: participant and (simulated) partner.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main decision is one-shot (single round) in part two. Part one is a five-round earning phase, but the social behavior decision is one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/harm/help choice is continuous via a slider, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher is 1 point per 3 points deducted from partner (3:1 ratio), up to 10 points cost for 30 points change.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 points lost by partner per 1 point cost to punisher (3:1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants can pay to increase partner's score (costly reward/altruism) as well as to decrease it (costly spite/punishment).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to rewarder is 1 point per 3 points given to partner (same as punishment).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3 points gained by partner per 1 point cost to rewarder (3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the decision is one-shot; instructions clarify the structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Social information about others' behavior (altruistic, neutral, or spiteful) is experimentally manipulated and shown to participants in some conditions.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or repeated group interaction; it is a one-shot, two-player, continuous-choice game.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- The main outcome is a behavioral slider (costly help/harm), not efficiency or group payoff.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods contexts.
- Social information is experimentally manipulated and fictitious, not based on real group behavior.
- Participants are MTurk workers; generalizability to other populations is limited.
- Potential framing effects may have promoted altruism.
- Use of deception (simulated partner, fictitious social information) may affect external validity.
