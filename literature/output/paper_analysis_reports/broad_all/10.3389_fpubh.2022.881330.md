## Outcomes Measured

Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of self-discipline behavior (analogous to cooperation rate)
- proportion of positive supervision by local governments
- evolutionary stability of strategies under different reward and punishment mechanisms
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the proportions of actors choosing 'self-discipline' (for PHSIs) and 'positive supervision' (for governments), which are behavioral strategy rates, not direct payoffs or efficiency. The model is payoff-based, but the reported results focus on strategy frequencies, not group welfare or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a principal-agent setting with information asymmetry, static punishment or reward mechanisms are insufficient to ensure full compliance (self-discipline) by service providers. Dynamic mechanisms, where the intensity of punishment or reward depends on the observed behavior, can stabilize higher levels of self-discipline. Specifically, higher punishment intensity increases the proportion of self-discipline, while higher reward intensity can decrease it due to moral hazard and adverse selection. The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the results suggest that dynamic sanctioning mechanisms are more effective at promoting compliance than static ones.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (or self-discipline) depends on the design of the sanctioning mechanism, particularly whether it is static or dynamic. While the model is not a public goods game per se, it is structurally similar in that it models a social dilemma with potential for free-riding and the use of sanctions. The findings suggest that, in analogous PGG-like environments, dynamic punishment mechanisms are likely to be more effective at increasing cooperation than static ones, and that increasing punishment intensity generally increases compliance. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. It is most useful for understanding the qualitative effects of punishment design and intensity on compliance behavior.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes two populations: local governments and PHSIs, each treated as large/infinite groups. The game is not a fixed small-n PGG but a population-level evolutionary game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is continuous-time evolutionary (replicator dynamics), not a finite-round repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent chooses between two discrete strategies: self-discipline or fraud (for PHSIs), positive or passive supervision (for governments).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost of punishment to the government is modeled as 'I' (incremental effort cost of positive supervision), and the penalty 'P' is imposed on PHSIs. The effect of increasing P is directly analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the penalty 'P' imposed on PHSIs. Higher P increases self-discipline.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward mechanisms (S) are modeled and analyzed, both as static and dynamic. Increasing S can decrease self-discipline due to adverse selection.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward cost is modeled as S (subsidy to PHSIs). Higher S can reduce self-discipline due to moral hazard.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is modeled as S. Higher S does not always increase self-discipline; can have negative effects.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- Outcomes are reported as behavioral strategy frequencies, not as group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The setting is a principal-agent supervision game, not a standard public goods game; transferability to PGGs is indirect.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes.
- Assumes infinite populations and continuous-time dynamics, which may not match experimental or real-world PGG settings.
