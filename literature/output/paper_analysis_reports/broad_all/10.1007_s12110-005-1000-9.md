# Analysis Report

Title: Norm enforcement among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen - A case of strong reciprocity?
Authors: Wiessner, P.
Journal: Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective
Year: 2005

empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

Outcome notes:
The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or any direct measure of economic or material welfare. Outcomes are primarily behavioral and social, such as norm enforcement, punishment frequency, and social consequences.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment is a frequent and central mechanism for enforcing social norms among the Ju/'hoansi, with most norm enforcement occurring through criticism, mockery, or coalition-based verbal sanctions rather than material or violent means. The costs of punishment are generally low due to cultural mechanisms that distribute costs, allow restraint, and minimize escalation, but are not negligible. Punishment is more often used than reward, and is primarily aimed at maintaining group harmony and curbing disruptive behavior rather than strictly deterring free-riding. The study finds some support for the strong reciprocity hypothesis, as individuals sometimes incur costs to punish norm violators even when not directly benefiting. However, the outcomes of punishment are mixed, with only a minority of cases resulting in visible reform. The study does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or material payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar experimental settings. Its relevance is primarily in providing ethnographic context and qualitative insight into how punishment operates in a real-world cooperative society, including the social costs, coalition dynamics, and norm enforcement mechanisms. It does not report or allow estimation of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus, and does not manipulate or systematically vary game design dimensions. Therefore, it should not be used as a quantitative datapoint for predicting efficiency outcomes in experimental public goods games, but may inform qualitative understanding of punishment's social functions and costs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Group size in conversations with criticism is reported (mean 7.2 adults, village size 20-40), but not in a game-theoretic or experimental context.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. All punishment is delivered through conversation, criticism, and verbal interaction.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Costs of punishment are discussed in social, reputational, and group cohesion terms, not as explicit material costs per unit.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is discussed in terms of social outcomes (e.g., bringing transgressors in line, ostracism), not as a numeric impact per cost.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (praise) is present but rare; only 7% of conversations included praise.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Social costs of reward (praise) are discussed, especially the risk of creating social debts or inequality.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Effectiveness of reward is discussed in terms of its rarity and potential to threaten egalitarian relations.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Social information about others' behavior is widely available through conversation and observation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is public and the identity of punishers is known.

## Important Limitations
- The study is ethnographic and observational, not experimental or game-theoretic.
- No formal public goods game or equivalent economic game is used; no control or treatment conditions.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or material welfare outcomes are reported or estimated.
- All outcomes are behavioral or social (e.g., norm enforcement, punishment frequency, coalition formation).
- Game design dimensions are not systematically manipulated or analyzed.
- Findings may not generalize to larger-scale, less mobile, or more anonymous societies.
