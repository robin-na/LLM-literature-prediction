## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that cooperative strategies in public goods games and related social dilemmas are governed by a common conditional rule: cooperate with those of good reputation and enforce (by withholding benefits or costly punishment) against those of bad reputation. The choice between withholding benefits and costly punishment depends on the structure of the game—costly punishment is more relevant in public goods games where defectors cannot be targeted directly. The paper reviews experimental evidence showing that combining costly punishment with indirect reciprocity can increase cooperation at lower cost than punishment alone, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes. Theoretical and experimental evidence suggests that the same reputation rules (e.g., SCORING, SIMPLE STANDING, STERN JUDGING) operate across frameworks.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing cooperation (and potentially efficiency) in public goods games depends on whether withholding benefits is a viable enforcement mechanism. It suggests that costly punishment is most effective and necessary in public goods games where direct targeting of defectors is not possible. However, the paper does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. It is most useful for understanding the conditions under which punishment is likely to be effective as an enforcement mechanism, and for mapping the conceptual relationships between different enforcement strategies.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and does not report new empirical data or quantitative efficiency/payoff outcomes.
- References to experiments are used to illustrate theoretical points, not to provide systematic evidence for efficiency effects.
- No direct analysis of how game design dimensions quantitatively moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Does not provide parameter values (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR) or experimental details needed for predictive modeling.
- Payoff and efficiency outcomes are discussed only qualitatively or indirectly.
