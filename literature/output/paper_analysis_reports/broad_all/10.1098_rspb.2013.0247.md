# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Individual heterogeneity and costly punishment: a volunteer's dilemma
Authors: Przepiorka, W., & Diekmann, A.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- stealing rate (defection rate by person X)
- reclaim rate (punishment rate by group members)
- rate of efficient punishment (punishment by only one group member)
- rate of punishment by strong vs. weak group members
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: rates of stealing (defection), rates of punishment (reclaiming), and coordination in punishment. The paper discusses efficiency and effectiveness in a qualitative sense but does not report group payoff, welfare, or efficiency ratios as explicit outcome variables.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that costly punishment, when implemented as a volunteer's dilemma, is effective in deterring defection (stealing) in groups. When only one group member needs to punish and punishment costs are heterogeneous, punishment is more efficiently coordinated (mainly by the lowest-cost punisher), and deterrence of defection is as strong as with much higher penalties in a symmetric-cost setting. The introduction of punishment reduces the stealing rate by about 50 percentage points overall, with greater reductions in conditions with high penalty or with cost heterogeneity. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral results suggest that punishment can sustain cooperation and that heterogeneity in punishment costs facilitates more effective and efficient norm enforcement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong behavioral evidence that introducing punishment in a volunteer's dilemma setting (where only one punisher is needed) greatly reduces defection rates, especially when punishment costs are heterogeneous. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that punishment will likely increase efficiency (by reducing defection) in similar settings, and that cost heterogeneity among punishers can substitute for higher penalty size in deterring defection. However, because the paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff outcomes, the quantitative effect on efficiency must be inferred indirectly from the large reductions in defection rates. The findings are most applicable to step-level public goods games with punishment opportunities and cost heterogeneity among punishers.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per round (1 potential defector, 3 potential punishers).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session, with treatments changing at round 16.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (cooperation) and punishment choices are binary: steal/not steal, punish/not punish.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per punisher is 30 MU (symmetric) or 30/40 MU (asymmetric). Lower punishment cost for one player increases efficient punishment and deterrence.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (penalty to defector) is manipulated: 0, 40, or 120 MU. Higher penalty increases deterrence of defection.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects were told the experiment lasted 30 rounds, with a change in condition at round 16.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes directly; all efficiency claims are inferred from behavioral outcomes.
- The game is a volunteer's dilemma (step-level public good), not a standard linear PGG, so results may not generalize to all PGG settings.
- The experiment uses artificial laboratory conditions with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- No analysis of long-term or repeated effects beyond 30 rounds.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are studied.
