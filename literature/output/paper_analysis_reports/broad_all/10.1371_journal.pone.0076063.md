## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that increasing punishment or inspection does not always reduce crime; instead, the system exhibits complex, sometimes counterintuitive dynamics, including cyclic dominance and abrupt phase transitions between different equilibria (e.g., dominance of criminals, coexistence of criminals and inspectors, or cyclic coexistence of all three strategies). The effectiveness of punishment depends critically on the temptation to commit crime, the cost and incentive for inspection, and the structure of social interactions. The model demonstrates that crime can be recurrent and robust to increased punishment, especially when spatial structure and local interactions are considered. However, the paper does not report on efficiency or group payoff, so its findings are not directly translatable to predictions about group welfare or efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on crime (and by analogy, on cooperation in social dilemmas) is highly sensitive to the interplay of temptation, punishment cost, and network structure, and that simple linear predictions (e.g., more punishment always increases efficiency) are likely to fail. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main value is in highlighting the potential for non-monotonic, phase-transition-like effects and the importance of considering spatial and network structure. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests caution in assuming monotonic or simple effects of punishment, especially in spatially structured or networked environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run on a square lattice of size L x L, with L varied between 200 and 1600. Each player interacts with k=4 nearest neighbors. The effective group size is determined by the local neighborhood, not a fixed group as in standard PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 10^6 full rounds to reach stationary distributions. The number of rounds is very large and chosen for equilibration, not as an experimental variable.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment (inspection) cost is a key parameter (alpha = c/f), varied systematically. Increasing inspection cost can lead to abrupt transitions between phases (e.g., from cyclic coexistence to dominance of criminals or inspectors), with no simple monotonic effect on crime rate.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is captured by the fine (f) and the inspection incentive (gamma = r/f). Varying these parameters changes the phase structure, but effects are non-monotonic and context-dependent.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of strategy frequencies and crime rates, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The inspection game is structurally different from standard public goods games; results may not transfer directly.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or identification of punishers is included.
- The model assumes infinite or very large numbers of rounds and large populations, which may not match experimental or real-world settings.
- Findings are qualitative and phase-based, not quantitative predictions of efficiency.
