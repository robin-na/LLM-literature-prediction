The main reported outcomes are frequencies of strategies (cooperation, defection, punishment) and the size of the cooperative basin of attraction. Payoff is modeled and calculated for each strategy, and average population payoff is defined, but the main focus is on cooperation rates and evolutionary stability, not directly on efficiency as a ratio of realized to maximum possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size G=5 is used throughout the main model and simulations. Population size N=100 in finite well-mixed simulations; L=100 (10,000) in structured populations.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical model is evolutionary and does not use discrete rounds in the experimental sense, but simulations run for a large number of time steps (e.g., 2x10^5, 10^9).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a fixed amount c=1 for cooperators and punishers; defectors contribute nothing. No continuous or partial contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=2.0, r=3.0), group size G=5, so MPCR = r/G (e.g., 0.4 or 0.6).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per punished defector is beta, varied in the model (e.g., beta=0.7, 0.15, 0.8). Cost is shared among punishers. Observation cost gamma for conditional punishers is also modeled.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment impact per punished defector is alpha, varied in the model (e.g., alpha=0.3, 1.0).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Conditional punishers observe the number of unconditional punishers in their group (at a cost gamma).

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main outcomes are strategy frequencies and basins of attraction, not direct measures of efficiency as a ratio of realized to maximum possible group payoff.
- The model assumes fixed group size and does not explore variation in group size or population size as a moderator.
- Conditional punishment is modeled as a threshold function; real-world conditionality may be more complex.
- No reward mechanisms or anti-social punishment are included in the main model (though anti-social punishment is discussed in the Supplementary Information).
- No communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms are modeled.
