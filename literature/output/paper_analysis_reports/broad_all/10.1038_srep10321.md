# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punitive preferences, monetary incentives and tacit coordination in the punishment of defectors promote cooperation in humans
Authors: Diekmann, A., & Przepiorka, W.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment rate
- defection rate (stealing)
- rate of efficient punishment (punishment by one group member only)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment rates, defection rates, and the efficiency of punishment (i.e., whether punishment is carried out by only one group member). The paper discusses efficiency in the sense of 'efficient provision of a cooperative environment' and 'efficient punishment', but does not report group payoff, total earnings, or explicit efficiency ratios as defined by total group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when only one group member is needed to punish a defector (step-level public good), groups with punishment cost heterogeneity (one strong, low-cost punisher) achieve higher rates of punishment and lower rates of defection than symmetric groups. Tacit coordination on the strong punisher leads to more efficient punishment (i.e., punishment is more often carried out by only one group member). The presence of a penalty for defectors (i.e., punishment that also harms the defector) is necessary to reduce defection rates. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the behavioral results suggest that punishment cost heterogeneity can improve the effectiveness and efficiency of norm enforcement in such games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment cost heterogeneity (i.e., having a designated or focal punisher with lower costs) increases the effectiveness and efficiency of punishment in step-level public good games, as measured by behavioral outcomes (punishment and defection rates). While it does not report group efficiency or total payoff, the findings suggest that game designs enabling tacit coordination on a single, low-cost punisher are likely to yield higher efficiency when punishment is enabled, especially when a penalty for defectors is present. The results are most directly relevant for step-level or volunteer's dilemma variants of public goods games, rather than standard linear PGGs. The absence of explicit efficiency or payoff data limits direct quantitative prediction, but the qualitative pattern is clear: punishment is more effective and less wasteful when cost heterogeneity allows for coordination on a single punisher.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session; 15 rounds without penalty, 15 rounds with penalty.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (punishment) is all-or-nothing: each eligible group member chooses whether to punish (reclaim) or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard linear PGG; the marginal return is step-level: if at least one punisher, the group benefit is restored. In VOD, U_i = 50 MU, K_i = 25 or 35 MU (asymmetric), so benefit > cost for the punisher.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (K_i) is varied: symmetric (all equal), asymmetric (one lower-cost punisher). Lower punishment cost enables more efficient and more frequent punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is effective if at least one group member chooses to punish; in part 2, punishment also imposes a penalty (P=60 MU) on the defector.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds (30) and the structure (first 15 without penalty, next 15 with penalty).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all group members receive full information feedback about the outcome and learn how every group member decided.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players learn who punished whom after each round (full feedback on individual decisions).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or explicit payoff-based efficiency outcomes; all main results are based on behavioral measures (punishment and defection rates).
- The game is a step-level public good (volunteer's dilemma or missing hero dilemma), not a standard linear PGG, so findings may not generalize to linear PGGs.
- The experiment uses all-or-nothing punishment and does not allow for continuous contribution or punishment levels.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- Groups are re-randomized every round, so repeated interaction and reputation effects are minimized.
- The sample is limited to university students in a laboratory setting, which may limit external validity.
