Outcomes are modeled as equilibrium payoffs and sustainability of cooperation, not as empirical measurements.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proves that in a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma, the introduction of either collective punishment (Nash reversion by all after any defection) or reward mechanisms (transfers contingent on cooperation) can sustain full cooperation as a subgame perfect equilibrium, even when bilateral incentive compatibility constraints are violated. This means that group efficiency (welfare) can be maintained at the cooperative level under broader conditions than in the absence of such mechanisms. The results are general and apply to settings where players interact repeatedly and can observe each other's actions, and where the game is either infinite or has an unknown end point.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency by sustaining cooperation, even when cooperation would not be sustainable without such mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially collective punishment with Nash reversion) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment, provided players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor) and can observe actions. The model is adjacent to public goods games (it is a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma), so the results are most directly applicable to small-group, repeated, fully observable settings. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it does establish that the direction of effect is positive for efficiency when punishment is enabled.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical evidence or effect sizes.
- The model is a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results are adjacent but not directly parameterized for PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as Nash reversion (loss of future cooperation), not as a costly, targeted action.
- Assumes perfect observability of actions and infinite or indeterminate horizon, which may not hold in many experimental or field settings.
- Does not address heterogeneity in player preferences, errors, or bounded rationality.
