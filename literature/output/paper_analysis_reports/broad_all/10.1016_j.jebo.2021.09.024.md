# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When no bad deed goes punished: Relational contracting in Ghana and the UK
Authors: Davies, E., & Fafchamps, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- employer payoff
- worker payoff
- joint surplus
- average earnings
- efficiency
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/effort level
- compliance with requested effort
- punishment/reward behavior (wage adjustment)
- acceptance of contract offers
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are employer and worker payoffs, joint surplus, and compliance with requested effort. The paper also analyzes behavioral outcomes such as effort, compliance, and wage adjustment as punishment/reward.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated gift-exchange games, UK subjects use wage reductions as punishment for low effort and wage maintenance/increases as reward for high effort, resulting in higher effort, higher employer payoffs, and higher efficiency. Ghanaian subjects do not systematically punish low effort or reward high effort, and punishment (wage reduction) does not deter shirking—in fact, it may increase it. As a result, efficiency and employer payoffs are much lower in Ghana. Competition and reputation mechanisms do not close this gap. The findings show that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is context-dependent and not universal.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated principal-agent or gift-exchange games is highly context-dependent. In environments where punishment is used and anticipated (as in the UK), enabling punishment increases efficiency and employer payoffs. In environments where punishment is not used or is counterproductive (as in Ghana), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency and may even worsen outcomes. For prediction, the paper suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism alone is not sufficient to guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on whether participants use and respond to punishment as intended. Game design dimensions such as the ability to punish (via wage adjustment), the number of players, and the presence of reputation or competition do not guarantee efficiency gains from punishment unless the behavioral norms support its use.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are played in 1-on-1 (2 players: 1 employer, 1 worker) and 3-on-3 (6 players: 3 employers, 3 workers) formats.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consists of 5 periods (rounds), and each participant plays four such games (20 rounds total).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort is discrete with three levels (low, medium, high), not continuous, but not strictly all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by the benefit to employer per effort level: high effort gives 40 points to employer at a cost of 6 to worker; medium effort 20/2; low effort 5/0.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via wage reduction; the cost to the punisher (employer) is opportunity cost of not inducing high effort, not a direct cost per se.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the reduction in wage offer after low effort; the impact is the difference in future wage and thus future incentives.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is implemented via wage maintenance or increase after high effort.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is the higher wage paid by the employer after high effort.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is the increase in wage after high effort; the impact is the incentive for future high effort.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of periods (5 per game) is known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects are provided with a summary of actions and payoffs. In some treatments, information about past actions is shared among all employers (reputation treatment).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the 3-on-3 treatments, workers are identified by number and employers can see which worker they are interacting with; in the reputation treatment, employers see past actions of each worker.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a repeated gift-exchange (principal-agent) game, not a standard public goods game, though it shares some features (social dilemma, repeated interaction, punishment/reward).
- Punishment is implemented via wage reduction, not via a direct costly punishment mechanism as in standard PGG punishment treatments.
- Cultural/contextual differences (Ghana vs UK) are central; results may not generalize to other populations.
- The game uses discrete effort levels (not continuous contribution), and the mapping to standard PGGs is not exact.
- No direct manipulation of punishment cost or effectiveness beyond wage adjustment.
- No chat or free-form communication between players.
- The number of rounds is relatively small (5 per game), which may affect the dynamics of learning and punishment.
- The study does not include a standard PGG control for direct comparison.
