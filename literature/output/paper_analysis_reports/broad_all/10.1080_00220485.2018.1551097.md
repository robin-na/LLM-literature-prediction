# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: A dynamic semester-long social dilemma game for economic and interdisciplinary courses
Authors: Secchi, S., & Banerjee, S.
Journal: Journal of Economic Education
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- harvest behavior
- cooperation
- cheating
- poaching
- institutional innovation
- peer punishment (when implemented by students)
- group and individual strategies
Outcome notes:
The paper describes behavioral outcomes such as harvest levels, cheating, poaching, and the emergence of group-devised solutions (including punishment), but does not report quantitative payoff or efficiency outcomes. The main focus is on learning, engagement, and the evolution of strategies, not on formal measurement of efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that students, when given the opportunity, may devise and implement their own institutional solutions to the common-pool resource dilemma, including costly peer punishment. The game is effective for teaching about the dynamics of cooperation, cheating, and institutional innovation in resource management. However, the paper does not provide quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it systematically compare outcomes with and without punishment. The main findings are qualitative and pedagogical, emphasizing the diversity of student-devised solutions and the context-dependence of group behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides limited direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. While it confirms that peer punishment can emerge and is sometimes used by participants to address over-extraction, it does not provide quantitative or systematic evidence on its impact on efficiency or group payoff. The game design dimensions are described in detail, but the effect of punishment is only discussed anecdotally. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that allowing for endogenous punishment mechanisms can lead to creative solutions and potentially improved group outcomes, but does not provide data to calibrate the size or direction of the effect on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups are typically 5-10 students; class sizes can be much larger, with multiple groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played over a semester, with one round per class period; the exact number of rounds is not known to participants in advance and is determined stochastically at the end.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Within-group communication is allowed and encouraged; students discuss harvest decisions before submitting them privately.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvest) is a continuous choice from 0 up to a maximum (10 fish per round per student).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal return to leaving fish in the sea is 2x per capita for the next round, up to a cap; this is a dynamic, nonstandard MPCR but functionally similar.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment, when implemented, is costly (e.g., students sacrificed fish to create a coast guard who could penalize poachers). However, this is not a standard, systematically applied punishment cost; it is emergent and varies by student proposal.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the student-devised system (e.g., coast guard could prevent poachers from playing for several rounds). Not standardized or systematically measured.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. There are monetary rewards for both the highest group and highest individual harvesters.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants do not know the total number of rounds in advance; the endpoint is determined stochastically (coin toss) to prevent backward induction.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, students are provided with feedback about total catch of each fishery, fish stock for next round, and a ranking of individual catch values (by ID).

## Important Limitations
- No formal measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or related quantitative outcomes.
- Punishment is not systematically manipulated or analyzed; it emerges endogenously and is described anecdotally.
- No control or counterfactual conditions for the presence or absence of punishment.
- Findings are qualitative and pedagogical, not statistical or causal.
- Game is designed for teaching, not for research on punishment effects.
