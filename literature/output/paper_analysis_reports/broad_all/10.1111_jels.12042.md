# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment
Authors: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C.
Journal: Journal of Empirical Legal Studies
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean group profit
- mean individual profit
- mean group contribution (as a proxy for group welfare)
- stability of contributions over time
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- frequency and amount of damages imposed
- probability of being punished
- use of forfeiture option
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good, but group and individual profit are also reported. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but group profit and contribution stability are closely related. The paper focuses on how different punishment/damages regimes affect the decay or stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players; fixed throughout the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods (rounds) per session, announced in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 20 ECUs; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (each ECU contributed returns 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (damages) is costless to the punisher; the active player retains the amount taken from others (except in forfeiture treatments, where the option to forfeit exists).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness varies by treatment: compensatory (up to individual harm), class action (up to group harm), treble damages (up to 3x individual harm). Higher punishment effectiveness (severity) leads to more stable cooperation and higher group payoffs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 30 periods; number of rounds is announced.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants receive feedback about each participant's contribution after each period.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group members are randomly assigned numbers each period; players do not know who punished whom across periods.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but group profit and contribution stability are closely related proxies.
- The punishment mechanism is not costly to the punisher (except in the forfeiture option, which is rarely used), differing from standard costly punishment in some PGG experiments.
- The design uses random assignment of the right to punish (active player), which may understate deterrence compared to real-world settings where all harmed parties can act.
- The class action treatment allows the active player to retain all damages, unlike real class actions where damages are distributed among class members.
- The experiment does not model precautionary behavior or declining marginal returns to cooperation, which may limit generalizability to some real-world settings.
- External validity is limited by the artificiality of the lab setting and the stylized nature of the damages rules.
