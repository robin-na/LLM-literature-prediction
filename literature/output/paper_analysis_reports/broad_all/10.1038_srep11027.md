Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the prevalence of different strategies (criminals, punishers, ordinary people) and the effects of punishment heterogeneity on these fractions. While payoffs are used to determine evolutionary success, the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies), not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing heterogeneous punishment (punishers who invest different amounts in sanctioning) can reduce the prevalence of second-order free-riders (ordinary people) compared to uniform punishment, but this does not always translate into lower crime rates. In some parameter regions, uniform punishment is as effective or more effective at reducing crime. The system exhibits complex phase transitions and cyclic dominance, which maintain the coexistence of criminals, punishers, and ordinary people. The main obstacle to eliminating crime is the ability of ordinary people to free-ride on the efforts of punishers. The results are presented as fractions of strategies in the population, not as efficiency or total group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction of punishment effects in public-goods-like environments, especially regarding the role of punishment heterogeneity and second-order free-riding. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict changes in efficiency when punishment is enabled. The findings suggest that the presence of second-order free-riders and the structure of punishment (uniform vs. heterogeneous) can have complex, non-monotonic effects on cooperation and norm enforcement, which may inform qualitative expectations about efficiency in related games. The results highlight the importance of considering strategy diversity and cyclic dominance in predicting the effects of punishment, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require additional evidence.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations are run on a square lattice with typically 600x600 players (sometimes up to 9000x9000 for stability), but the effect of player count is not systematically analyzed.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations run until stationary state is reached (up to 10^5 Monte Carlo steps), but the effect of the number of rounds is not varied or analyzed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Strategy choices are discrete (criminal, punisher, ordinary, and punisher subtypes), not continuous contributions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is a key parameter; in the heterogeneous model, punishers bear different fractions of the cost. Effects are complex: lower costs can help punishers survive but may not always reduce crime.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is tied to the fine imposed (normalized to 1) and the cost borne by punishers. Heterogeneous punishment allows for different effectiveness levels. The impact on crime and free-riding is non-monotonic.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are reported as fractions of strategies, not as group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The game is an inspection game with pairwise payoffs, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes is provided.
- No human subjects or real-world data are used; all results are from simulations.
- No communication, reward, or information-sharing mechanisms are modeled.
