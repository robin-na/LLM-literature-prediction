# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Hunger Affects Social Decisions in a Multi-Round Public Goods Game but Not a Single-Shot Ultimatum Game
Authors: Fraser, S., & Nettle, D.
Journal: Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- punishment frequency
- decision latency
- response to punishment
- influence of others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contribution levels, punishment behavior, and decision latency. Group payoff, efficiency, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed. Some analyses (e.g., effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions) are related to efficiency but are not direct measures of group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a 20-round PGG (10 rounds without punishment, 10 with punishment), groups of four played with either all members having eaten breakfast or all having skipped breakfast. In the no-punishment phase, hungry participants contributed more initially and were more likely to match their contributions to those of their groupmates in subsequent rounds. In the punishment phase, hungry participants punished less, especially when group contributions were high, making punishment less effective at boosting contributions. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the reported results.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative behavioral evidence about how punishment and hunger interact to affect contribution and punishment behavior in a standard PGG. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment is less effective at increasing contributions when participants are hungry, which may imply a smaller efficiency gain from punishment in such contexts, but this is not quantified in payoff terms. The game design dimensions are well specified and match standard PGG with punishment, so the behavioral patterns may inform models that link contribution and punishment behavior to efficiency, but direct efficiency data are lacking.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of four players per PGG session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds total: 10 rounds without punishment, 10 rounds with punishment. Order counterbalanced.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous from 0 to 20 credits per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.4 (multiplier) / 4 (players) = 0.35 per unit contributed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each credit spent on punishment reduced the target's income by 10% for the round (minimum zero). The cost to the punisher is 1 credit per punishment action.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 credit spent reduces target's round income by 10%.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the number of rounds in advance (20 rounds: 10 with, 10 without punishment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were shown the contributions of all group members.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
- All group members were assigned to the same hunger condition within a session, so there is no within-group variation in hunger.
- The findings are based on behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) rather than direct payoff or efficiency measures.
- The punishment mechanism is limited to reducing others' payoffs; no reward mechanism is present.
- The study is conducted in a student sample at a single university, which may limit generalizability.
