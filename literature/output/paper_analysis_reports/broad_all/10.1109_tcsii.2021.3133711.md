The main dependent variables are population-mean payoff (efficiency/welfare) and the frequency of prosocial strategies. The paper explicitly analyzes how institutional punishment and network structure affect both the evolution of cooperation and the resulting group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional punishment and network structure can interact synergistically to promote prosocial behavior and increase group efficiency (mean payoff) in a trust game. Specifically, the combination of even modest punishment and network structure can achieve full cooperation and maximal efficiency in parameter regions where neither mechanism alone would suffice. The required punishment level to achieve full cooperation is lower in structured populations than in well-mixed populations. The mean payoff is maximized at the threshold punishment level that induces full cooperation; increasing punishment beyond this point does not further increase efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of maintaining the incentive system. The paper provides explicit analytical boundaries for these effects as a function of the trust return (r), network degree (k), and punishment size (p).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a networked trust game (and by extension, in adjacent public-goods-like games) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is not too costly and the network structure is not too sparse. The results suggest that the efficiency gain from punishment is greater in structured populations than in well-mixed ones, and that the combination of network structure and punishment can achieve high efficiency at lower punishment levels than punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the network structure (degree), the effectiveness and cost of punishment, and the underlying return to cooperation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games but are likely to transfer to public goods games with similar strategic structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is for a large population of agents, with pairwise interactions on a regular graph of degree k. The effective group size per interaction is 2 (investor and trustee), but the population is large.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and continuous-time (replicator dynamics), not round-based, but implicitly assumes repeated/ongoing interactions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment is binary: invest or not; trustee is trustworthy or not. All-or-nothing choices.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The return to trust (r) is analogous to MPCR; higher r increases the range for synergy and makes cooperation easier to sustain.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a fine p imposed on untrustworthy agents, with a tax f paid by all to maintain the institution (often f = p in examples). Higher punishment increases cooperation up to a point, but excessive punishment reduces mean payoff due to cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is captured by the size of the fine p; higher p increases the deterrent effect, but the optimal p is at the threshold for full cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and based on evolutionary game dynamics; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The game studied is a symmetric binary trust game, not a public goods game, though it is adjacent in strategic structure.
- Only institutional punishment (not peer punishment or reward) is modeled.
- The model assumes large populations and regular network structure; results may not generalize to small groups or irregular networks.
- The cost of punishment is modeled as a tax paid by all agents, which may differ from real-world or experimental implementations.
