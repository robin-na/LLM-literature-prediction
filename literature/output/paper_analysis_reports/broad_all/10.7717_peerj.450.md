# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Local norms of cheating and the cultural evolution of crime and punishment: a study of two urban neighborhoods
Authors: Schroeder, K. B., Pepper, G. V., & Nettle, D.
Journal: Peerj
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean payoff from the game (reported for each neighborhood)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- theft (amount Player 1 took from Player 2)
- punitiveness (number of thefts Player 3 would punish)
- expectation of third-party punishment (expect 3PP)
- perceived cheating (perception of local norm violation)
- trust in neighbors
- condoned cheating (injunctive norm)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (theft, punishment, expectation of punishment, perceived cheating, trust). Mean payoffs are reported but not analyzed as a primary outcome, and efficiency is not directly calculated or discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a 3PP game, residents of a high-crime, low-trust neighborhood (B) are more likely to steal and less likely to punish theft than those in a low-crime, high-trust neighborhood (A). Perceived cheating (descriptive norm violation) is higher in B and predicts both more theft and less expectation of punishment. Experimentally manipulating perceived norms in B increases expectation of punishment. The cost of punishment (subjective value of £2) and trust both moderate willingness to punish. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but mean payoffs are similar across neighborhoods. The results support the idea that local norms and trust shape antisocial behavior and punishment, with potential feedback loops.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. While it does not use a standard public goods game or directly analyze efficiency, it shows that punishment is less likely to be used in low-trust, high-norm-violation environments, and that expectation of punishment can be increased by manipulating perceived norms. The cost of punishment and trust are important moderators. For prediction, this suggests that punishment may be less effective at increasing efficiency in environments with low trust and high perceived norm violation, and that the subjective cost of punishment matters. However, because efficiency is not directly measured, the evidence is indirect and should be used with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves 3 players: Player 1 (potential thief), Player 2 (potential victim), Player 3 (potential punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a one-shot (single round) interaction for each participant.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player 1 chooses an integer amount (0-10) to steal; not strictly all-or-nothing, but discrete choices.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (Player 3) pays £2 to fine Player 1 £6. Subjective value of £2 is measured and analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed: £2 cost to punisher, £6 loss to punished.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a single round (one-shot).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; Player 3 can punish without being identified.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a third-party punishment game focused on theft, so results may not generalize directly to PGG environments.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed; only mean payoffs are reported descriptively.
- The study is conducted in real neighborhoods with possible selection bias (registered voters, research participants).
- Order of questionnaire and game components could not be controlled, possibly affecting responses.
- Punishment is anonymous and does not capture real-world risks of retribution.
- Sample sizes are modest, and not all triads are fully independent.
- Manipulation of perceived norms is only tested for expectation of punishment, not for actual behavior or efficiency.
