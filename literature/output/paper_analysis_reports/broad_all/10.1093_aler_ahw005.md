# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Punishment Regimes for Collusive Bribery
Authors: Engel, C., Goerg, S. J., & Yu, G. N.
Journal: American Law and Economics Review
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of bribe offers
- frequency of reporting to authorities (punishment)
- frequency of favors granted
- frequency of exploitation (accepting bribe but not granting favor)
- frequency of bribe rejection
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: bribe offers, reporting (punishment), granting favors, and exploitation. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Payoff consequences are discussed in mechanism terms but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that when punishment is asymmetric (bribers punished less than recipients), bribers are much more likely to report to authorities if recipients accept the bribe but do not grant the favor. This threat is anticipated by recipients, who are then more likely to grant the favor, and bribers are more likely to offer bribes. These effects are robust across countries (Germany and China) and to framing (neutral vs. corruption). The main outcomes are behavioral (bribe offers, reporting, granting favors), not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that asymmetric punishment, while intended to deter corruption, may actually facilitate collusive bribery by making enforcement of corrupt deals easier.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that lowering the cost of punishment (making it cheaper for one party to punish another) increases the use of punishment and, in this context, increases the frequency of cooperation in corrupt deals. However, because the game is not a public goods game or direct variant, and because efficiency or group payoff is not reported, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. The main implication is that punishment cost and effectiveness are critical moderators of behavioral outcomes, but the translation to efficiency is not directly tested here.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) interactions: one briber, one recipient.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session includes a one-shot round followed by 10 repeated rounds (total 11 rounds per pair).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (bribe) is a fixed amount (40 taler); no continuous or partial contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to briber is 10 taler (asymmetric) or 50 taler (symmetric). Lower cost (asymmetric) leads to much more frequent punishment (reporting).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: reporting by briber leads to 50 taler fine for recipient, regardless of regime. Asymmetric regime makes punishment cheaper for briber, increasing its use.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed after round 1 that there will be 10 more rounds (total 11), so the number of rounds is known.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (reporting) is always attributable to the briber; the recipient knows who reported.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or direct variant; it is a two-player bribery game with punishment, so generalizability to PGG-like environments is limited.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (bribe offers, reporting, granting favors, exploitation) are analyzed.
- The punishment mechanism is not peer-to-peer as in standard PGGs, but is implemented as reporting to authorities.
- The study does not analyze the effect of punishment on group efficiency or surplus, so direct prediction of efficiency effects is not possible.
- All interactions are dyadic; findings may not generalize to larger groups.
