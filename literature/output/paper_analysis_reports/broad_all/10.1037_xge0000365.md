# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Role of a Common Is Moral Heuristic in the Stability and Change of Moral Norms
Authors: Lindström, B., Jangard, S., Selbing, I., & Olsson, A.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Psychology-general
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- moral judgment ratings
- punishment judgment ratings
- costly punishment behavior (amount spent on punishment)
- reaction times for judgments
- frequency of cooperation/selfishness
- agent-based simulation of norm change
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are moral and punishment judgments (on 9-point scales), and in one experiment, actual costly punishment behavior. There is no direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The agent-based model simulates norm dynamics, not payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across nine experiments, participants observing one-shot PGGs judged common behaviors (whether altruistic or selfish) as more moral and less deserving of punishment than rare behaviors. This effect was robust across lab and online samples, and held for both preexisting moral norms (cooperation/selfishness) and arbitrary behaviors. In one experiment, participants could spend real money to punish, and commonness reduced costly punishment of selfishness. The effect of commonness was explained by a formal model of social influence (Social Impact Theory). Agent-based simulations showed that the CIM heuristic can generate both stability and sudden change in group-level moral norms. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived commonness of behaviors in PGGs shapes moral and punishment judgments, and that common selfishness is punished less than rare selfishness. It also shows that costly punishment is reduced when selfishness is common. However, because the experiments are one-shot and do not measure group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, the findings do not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled. The results are most useful for understanding psychological and behavioral responses to norm violation and punishment, rather than for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions in repeated or strategic PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments used groups of 4 or 8 players in the observed PGGs; this was manipulated to test whether absolute or relative frequency drives the CIM effect.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All PGGs were one-shot; participants observed sequences of one-shot games, but each game was not repeated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was binary: keep all (selfish) or invest all (altruistic).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Experiment 9, each deduction point cost the punisher real money (from a $2 endowment); 1 deduction point cost the punisher and reduced the target's payoff by 5 times that amount.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each deduction point cost the punisher and reduced the target's payoff by 5 times the cost.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observed a fixed number of one-shot games (e.g., 40), and this was known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observed the behavior (altruistic/selfish) of all players in each PGG; the frequency of each behavior was made explicit.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus; all outcomes are judgments or costly punishment behavior.
- All PGGs are one-shot; there is no repeated interaction or strategic adaptation.
- Participants are third-party observers, not direct participants in the PGGs.
- The agent-based simulation abstracts away from real payoff structures and is not calibrated to empirical efficiency data.
- No analysis of how punishment affects group efficiency or cooperation rates over time.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present.
