The main dependent variables in the public goods game sections are group earnings and cooperation rates, with explicit discussion of how punishment affects group payoff and efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games, cooperation and group efficiency decline over time when punishment is not available, as self-regarding individuals free ride and conditional cooperators reduce their contributions in response. When a costly punishment mechanism is introduced, cooperation and group efficiency increase dramatically, approaching full cooperation and maximum group earnings. This effect is robust even when punishment is costly and when group composition changes to prevent repeated interactions. The review also highlights that a minority of strong reciprocators can sustain high efficiency when punishment is possible, but even a majority cannot do so without punishment. The findings are supported by experimental evidence from Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and others.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games substantially increases group efficiency, even when punishment is costly to the punisher and when group composition is randomized. The effect is robust across different group sizes and settings. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency from low or declining levels (in the control) to near-maximal levels (in the treatment), provided that punishment is sufficiently effective and visible. The review also suggests that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are important moderators, but the main qualitative result is that punishment enables high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The review describes public goods games with N >= 2, and provides specific examples with N=4 and N=10. The main qualitative results are robust across these group sizes.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments are described with 10 and 20 rounds (periods). The decline in cooperation without punishment and the increase with punishment are shown over these rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player can contribute any amount up to their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. mpcr is described as M/N, with examples such as M=1.6, N=4 (mpcr=0.4), and M=2, N=10 (mpcr=0.2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs the punisher 1 unit per point assigned; each point reduces the target's payoff by 3 units (10%). The cost is substantial but still leads to high efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective: 1 unit cost to punisher reduces target's payoff by 3 units. This high leverage is key to the observed efficiency gains.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the main experiments, subjects know the total number of rounds (e.g., 20). Some referenced studies use unknown horizon, but the main results are for known n.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, individual contributions are revealed to the group, enabling targeted punishment.

## Important Limitations
- This is a review and synthesis paper, not a primary experimental report; it summarizes results from multiple studies, especially Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002).
- The review focuses on laboratory public goods games with specific parameterizations; generalizability to all real-world settings may be limited.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is demonstrated for specific punishment cost/impact ratios; results may differ with less effective or more costly punishment.
- No direct analysis of the effect of communication, reward mechanisms, or default framing is provided.
- Some referenced studies use different group sizes, round numbers, or matching protocols, but the main qualitative findings are robust across these variations.
