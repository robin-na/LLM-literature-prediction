# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Endogenous Authority and Enforcement in Public Goods Games
Authors: Lim, W., & Zhang, J. P.
Journal: B E Journal of Theoretical Economics
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency gain (percentage of maximum possible group payoff)
- average earnings
- social cost (percentage of group resources lost to contest and non-contribution)
- total public goods provision
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual and group contributions
- contest spending
- punishment frequency and severity
- authority selection and behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency gain (group payoff as a percentage of maximum), social cost, and average earnings. Contribution rates, contest spending, and punishment behavior are also reported but are not the primary outcome for the prediction task.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Introducing an endogenous authority with punishment power in a linear public goods game significantly increases group efficiency and average earnings compared to a no-authority baseline. In the experiment, with N=4, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical prediction) or 12.6% (empirical, due to positive baseline contributions) to 40.9% with authority; with N=8, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical) or 16.6% (empirical) to 47.7%. The social cost of contesting for authority is lower in larger groups (18.95% for N=8 vs 32.2% for N=4). Authorities tend to punish the lowest contributor and exercise most of their punishment power. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across treatment orders. Authorities contribute less than others but still contribute positively. The mechanism is effective at raising efficiency, especially as group size increases.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a punishment-enabled authority in a linear public goods game. The design dimensions (player_count, mpcr, punishment_tech, etc.) are clearly specified and manipulated. The efficiency gains from punishment are substantial and increase with group size, while the social cost of authority selection decreases with group size. The control (no authority) efficiency is empirically measured, and the treatment (authority/punishment enabled) efficiency is reported, allowing for direct mapping to the prediction task. The findings suggest that, for similar game parameters, enabling punishment via an endogenous authority will significantly increase efficiency, with the effect size moderated by group size and the cost structure of authority selection.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Two group sizes were directly manipulated: N=4 and N=8. Efficiency gains and social costs were compared across these sizes. Larger groups had higher efficiency gains and lower social costs.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 30 rounds (15 with and 15 without authority/punishment). The number of rounds is fixed and does not vary as a treatment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions (RED investment) were continuous from 0 to 50 tokens, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 1.5/8 = 0.1875 for N=8 and 1.5/4 = 0.375 for N=4. Each token in RED investment yields 1.5 points to each group member.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not individually costly to the authority; the cost is the contest spending to become authority, which is shared. The punishment itself is a deduction from the punished player's payoff and does not transfer to the authority.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is capped at 3 times the total contest spending (Administrator Fund) per round. The authority can impose a deduction up to this cap on a single group member.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed that there would be 30 rounds (15 per condition).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, participants received a summary of their own and group outcomes, including AF spending, BLUE investment, group total RED investment, punishment received, and final income. The authority saw all group members' RED investments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. The punished individual was informed of the deduction and that it was imposed by the administrator, but the identity of the administrator was not revealed to the group. The authority knew the identities of group members for punishment purposes.

## Important Limitations
- The authority can only punish one group member per round, which may limit the generalizability to settings with broader punishment options.
- The contest to become authority introduces a social cost (rent-seeking) that is not present in all real-world or experimental settings.
- The mechanism does not allow for rewards or positive reinforcement, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The authority is selected via a costly lottery contest, not by voting or other democratic means, which may affect external validity.
- The experiment prohibits communication, so results may differ in settings where chat or discussion is allowed.
- The authority's identity is not revealed to the group, which may affect deterrence and compliance compared to settings with transparent punishment.
