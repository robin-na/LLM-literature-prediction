## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that contagious punishment strategies can sustain cooperation and near-efficient payoffs in large, anonymous, randomly-matched repeated prisoner's dilemma games. The equilibrium is robust to small amounts of noise (players making mistakes) and does not require excessive patience except in very large populations or when the gain from defection is very small. The efficiency loss due to noise can be made arbitrarily small as the noise probability decreases. The results hold even without public randomization devices, though the required patience may increase in some parameter regimes. The analysis provides explicit conditions (in terms of discount factor, population size, and gain from defection) under which efficient cooperation is possible.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that punishment mechanisms (here, contagious punishment) can enable high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas, even under severe informational constraints (anonymity, random matching, no identification, no communication). The results are most directly applicable to repeated prisoner's dilemma games, which are adjacent to public goods games. The findings suggest that, for games with similar structure and sufficient patience, enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency relative to the baseline (no punishment) case. The paper also provides explicit formulas and numerical examples for the minimum patience (discount factor) required for cooperation at different population sizes and temptation levels. For prediction tasks, the main implication is that punishment can enable near-full efficiency in repeated, anonymous, randomly-matched games, provided the population is not too large, the gain from defection is not too high, and players are sufficiently patient. The results are robust to small noise and do not require public randomization, though the patience requirement may increase in those cases.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The results apply to repeated prisoner's dilemma games, which are adjacent but not identical to public goods games.
- The cooperative equilibrium is only one of many possible equilibria; the model does not predict which equilibrium will be selected.
- The results require all players to be rational and to know the equilibrium strategies; a single 'crazy' or always-defecting player can undermine cooperation.
- The patience (discount factor) required for cooperation increases with population size and the gain from defection; in very large populations or with frequent mistakes, cooperation may not be sustainable.
- Punishment is implemented via endogenous breakdown of cooperation, not via explicit costly punishment actions as in some experimental designs.
- No reward mechanisms are considered.
- The model assumes no communication, identification, or observation of others' outcomes beyond one's own match.
