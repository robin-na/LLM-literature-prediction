## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods and prisoners' dilemma games, voluntary cooperation and self-enforcing treaties generally fail to achieve efficient outcomes, especially as the number of players increases. Costly punishment can increase contributions but does not necessarily improve group efficiency or payoff, and may even reduce it if punishment is inefficient or leads to counter-punishment. Coordination mechanisms, such as trade restrictions or weakest-link structures, are more effective at achieving efficient outcomes. The paper also notes that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) affects cooperation rates in experiments, even though theory predicts it should not. Theoretical models are supported by references to experimental findings from other studies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment. If punishment is costly and not highly effective, it may increase contributions but reduce or fail to improve efficiency due to the cost imposed on both punishers and punished. Coordination mechanisms or institutional changes that alter incentives (e.g., trade bans, weakest-link structures) are more likely to improve efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to increase efficiency unless punishment is highly effective and not subject to costly retaliation. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) and group size are also important moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Theoretical models explicitly analyze the effect of increasing N (number of players) on efficiency and the effectiveness of punishment and treaties. As N increases, the efficiency gains from treaties and punishment diminish.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses both one-shot and repeated games. In repeated games, efficient outcomes can be supported in theory, but enforcement and equilibrium selection are problematic in practice.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both binary (all-or-nothing) and continuous contribution choices are modeled. The main results hold for both, but the binary case is used for clarity in some sections.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return (b) is a key parameter in the models. Higher mpcr increases cooperation in experiments, even though theory predicts it should not affect equilibrium behavior.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as costly to the punisher. High punishment cost reduces the likelihood that punishment will be used and reduces its effectiveness at improving efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is more likely to improve efficiency if it is highly effective (i.e., high impact per unit cost). If punishment is inefficient, it is rarely used and does not improve group payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report new empirical or experimental data.
- Empirical claims are based on references to other studies, not on new analysis.
- The models assume rational actors and may not capture all relevant psychological or social factors.
- The analysis of punishment is based on stylized models and may not generalize to all real-world or experimental settings.
- No direct analysis of communication, reward, or information feedback mechanisms.
