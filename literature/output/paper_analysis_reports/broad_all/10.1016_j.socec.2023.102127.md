# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How altruistic is indirect reciprocity? - Evidence from gift-exchange games in the lab
Authors: Hopp, D., & Süss, K.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- abuse of power (punishers punishing higher contributors than themselves)
- imposed contribution norm
- contribution behavior (punisher and non-punisher)
- frequency and amount of punishment
- effect of transparency and power on abuse
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: abuse of power (punishers punishing above their own contribution), imposed norm, and contribution levels. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Some discussion of group contribution rates and the ability of punishment to increase contributions, but not in terms of efficiency or total group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when a single group member is given punishment power in a repeated public goods game, most punishers abuse their power by punishing others for contributing more than themselves. Abuse is highest when punishment power is high and transparency is low. High transparency reduces abuse only in the high-power condition. Contribution rates of non-punishers increase over time, especially when transparency is high, but punishers consistently contribute less than non-punishers except in the high-power, high-transparency condition. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about how centralized punishment power and transparency affect behavioral outcomes like abuse of power and contribution norms in public goods games. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that centralized punishment can lead to norm enforcement but also to widespread abuse, especially when power is high and transparency is low. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it shows that punishment can increase contributions (especially among non-punishers), but also that punishers may undercontribute, potentially reducing group efficiency. The interaction between power and transparency is important for behavioral outcomes, but the net effect on efficiency remains unreported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players (1 punisher, 3 non-punishers), fixed for the duration of the experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates 0-20 tokens per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each token contributed returns 0.5 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless for the punisher: each punishment token deducts 1 from the target, costs 0 to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token reduces the target's payoff by 1; punisher has either 30 (low power) or 120 (high power) tokens per round.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game has 30 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In low-transparency, only group total contribution is shown to non-punishers; in high-transparency, all individual contributions are shown to all.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always assigned by the single punisher, whose identity (role D) is fixed and known; non-punishers know who the punisher is, but do not see who punished whom each round (since only the punisher can punish).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; all main outcomes are behavioral (abuse, contribution, imposed norm).
- Punishment is centralized and costless for the punisher, which differs from standard peer punishment designs.
- No accountability or counter-punishment is possible; only one player can punish, and their role is fixed.
- External validity is limited: real-world power structures often involve selection, accountability, or rotation, which are absent here.
- Transparency manipulation changes more than one informational aspect, and the psychological mechanisms are not disentangled.
