# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The 'spiteful' origins of human cooperation
Authors: Marlowe, F. W., Berbesque, J. C., Barrett, C., Bolyanatz, A., Gurven, M., & Tracer, D.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- minimum acceptable offer (MAO) in Ultimatum Game (2PP)
- minimum acceptable offer (MAO) in Third-Party Punishment Game (3PP)
- mean offer in Dictator Game (DG)
- correlations between offers in DG, UG, and 3PPG
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes such as generosity (DG offers), willingness to punish (MAO in UG and 3PPG), and their variation across societies. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that: (1) Small-scale societies are less generous in the Dictator Game and less likely to engage in third-party punishment, but are equally likely to engage in second-party punishment (Ultimatum Game) as large societies. (2) In large societies, third-party punishment is more common and even exceeds second-party punishment, suggesting a greater role for norm enforcement. (3) Generosity and punishment behaviors are correlated across games and societies. The findings challenge the view that strong reciprocity (including third-party punishment) is universal, suggesting that 'spiteful' second-party punishment is more fundamental in small-scale societies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment, as it does not use a PGG or report efficiency-related outcomes. However, it provides informative context about the prevalence and type of punishment (second- vs third-party) across societies, which may be relevant for understanding the social and cultural moderators of punishment effects in related game environments. The results suggest that the willingness to punish (especially second-party) is widespread, but the emergence of third-party punishment and higher generosity is more characteristic of large, complex societies. For prediction tasks, this implies that the social context (e.g., group size, societal complexity) may moderate the type and frequency of punishment, but the direct impact on efficiency in PGG-like games is not addressed.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two- or three-player: Dictator Game (2 players), Ultimatum Game (2 players), Third-Party Punishment Game (3 players).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; games are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/offers are continuous (0-100% of stake), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In 3PPG, punisher (P3) pays 20% of their allotment to reduce P1's payoff by 3x that amount. In UG, punishment is the cost of rejecting an offer (P2 gets zero instead of a low offer).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In 3PPG, each unit of cost to P3 removes 3 units from P1 (punishment effectiveness = 3). In UG, punishment is all-or-nothing (rejection leads to zero for both).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are explicitly one-shot; participants know there is only one round.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use public goods games or direct PGG variants; results are based on Dictator, Ultimatum, and Third-Party Punishment Games.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (offers, MAO).
- Games are one-shot and do not capture repeated interaction or dynamic effects.
- Cultural and societal context may limit generalizability to laboratory PGG settings.
- Punishment is measured as willingness to reject or pay to reduce another's payoff, not as explicit costly punishment in a PGG context.
