## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, contrary to standard game-theoretic equilibrium predictions, increasing penalties for law violations can induce temporary compliance (reduced violation rates) among drivers, even if the long-run equilibrium violation rate is unchanged. The system dynamics model shows that the path to equilibrium is slow and oscillatory, so transient effects of penalty increases are significant and may be policy-relevant. Automatic penalty management (adjusting penalties in response to observed violation rates) can further reduce the amplitude of behavioral fluctuations. Information delays between players can prolong or even prevent convergence to equilibrium, making penalty management more effective in stabilizing behavior. The findings are about behavioral compliance, not aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it models a two-player (police vs. driver) enforcement game with behavioral outcomes (violation and patrol rates) rather than group payoffs. However, it provides indirect evidence that punishment (penalty) increases can have significant transient effects on compliance, even if equilibrium analysis suggests no effect. For prediction tasks involving dynamic or transient effects of punishment, or where compliance rates are a proxy for efficiency, the findings suggest that punishment can be effective in the short run and that adaptive punishment management can stabilize behavior. The model is not a PGG or direct variant, so transfer to PGG efficiency predictions should be cautious and limited to qualitative insights about the dynamics of punishment and compliance.
