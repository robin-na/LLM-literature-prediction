Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the mechanisms of norm enforcement (restorative vs. punitive), the role of third parties, and the social processes of compensation and reintegration. It does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare in a formalized or quantitative sense, nor does it use a public goods game or direct analog.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study documents that in Enga customary courts, norm violations are addressed primarily through restorative justice: compensation to victims, reintegration of wrongdoers, and broad third-party participation in settlements. Punitive sanctions (punishment) are rare and not institutionally emphasized. Third parties contribute to compensation both during and outside court hearings, motivated by kinship, reputation, and community harmony. Repeated offenders and free riders receive less support over time. The system is designed to maintain cooperation and social cohesion, not to maximize punishment or deterrence. The paper argues that such restorative systems are more effective for sustaining cooperation in small-scale societies than third-party punishment, but does not provide quantitative efficiency or payoff data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides contextual and ethnographic evidence that, in small-scale societies like the Enga, restorative justice and third-party compensation are preferred over punitive sanctions for norm enforcement. While it does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or group payoff, it suggests that punishment is not the primary or most effective means of sustaining cooperation in such environments. For prediction tasks involving public goods games or efficiency outcomes, this paper is mainly informative for understanding the limits of punishment's effectiveness and the importance of alternative, restorative mechanisms. It does not provide direct evidence for how punishment affects efficiency in public goods games or similar experimental settings.

## Important Limitations
- The study is ethnographic and observational, not experimental or game-based.
- No quantitative efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported.
- No direct analog to public goods games; the context is real-world norm enforcement in a specific society.
- Punishment is rare and not systematically manipulated or compared to a no-punishment baseline.
- Findings may not generalize to larger-scale, anonymous, or less kin-based societies.
