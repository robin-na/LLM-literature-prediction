## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing heterogeneous social institutions (pool punishment, anti-social punishment, and social security/reward) in a spatial prisoner's dilemma framework can promote the evolution of pro-social groups and suppress second-order free-riding. The model shows that increasing the cost of institutional investment and the growth factor of the institutional pool generally promotes cooperation and higher average payoffs, but only up to an optimal point. The introduction of a non-wasteful model with an upper limit on individual punishment/reward further improves the efficiency of the system by reducing resource waste and allowing pro-social groups to survive under harsher social dilemmas. The network (spatial) structure enhances these effects compared to well-mixed populations. While the main outcomes are strategy proportions, the paper also analyzes average payoffs, showing that institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can increase group welfare compared to baseline scenarios without such institutions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the design of punishment and reward institutions—including their cost, effectiveness, and resource allocation rules—can significantly affect the efficiency (average payoff) of groups in social dilemma games. While the model is a spatial prisoner's dilemma rather than a standard public goods game, the mechanisms are closely related, especially for pool punishment and institutional reward. The findings suggest that enabling punishment (and reward) institutions, especially with non-wasteful resource allocation and optimal parameter settings, will generally increase group efficiency compared to control conditions without such institutions. The results also highlight the importance of network structure, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the presence of anti-social punishment as moderators. However, the lack of direct efficiency ratios and the use of a prisoner's dilemma rather than a PGG limit the direct quantitative transferability to PGG-like environments.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a spatial prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game, so direct quantitative transfer to PGG environments is limited.
- Outcomes are primarily reported as proportions of strategy types, not as explicit efficiency ratios relative to full cooperation.
- All results are from theoretical analysis and agent-based simulations; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- Parameter ranges and model structure may not map directly onto real-world or laboratory PGG designs.
- Anti-social punishment and reward mechanisms are modeled, but their real-world analogs may differ in important ways.
