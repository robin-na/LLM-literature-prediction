Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are expected payoffs for each role, and the stability of equilibria in terms of strategy proportions. The model is fully theoretical and simulation-based, with no empirical data.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a government-managed punishment and reward mechanism in a repeated N-player trust game can sustain trust and investment, leading to higher group payoffs. There is a critical threshold of punishment intensity: below it, untrustworthy behavior dominates and payoffs are low; above it, trust and investment are maintained, and payoffs increase. The model also shows that as punishment intensity increases further, the system can reach a stable coexistence of strategies with high trust and investment. The results are robust across different group sizes and parameter values, and are supported by both theoretical analysis and numerical simulations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated trust-game-like environments with group structure, introducing or increasing the intensity of punishment (and associated rewards for reporting) can substantially increase group efficiency/payoff, provided the punishment is above a critical threshold. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, so the qualitative prediction that punishment increases efficiency is likely to transfer to PGG-like settings, especially those with repeated interactions and third-party enforcement. The results are most informative for settings where punishment is implemented by an external authority and is contingent on reporting.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model explicitly varies the number of investors (N_I) and trustees (N_T), with group size N = N_I + N_T. Results are shown for N_I=10, N_T=5 and N_T=1, and the effect of punishment is robust across these values.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The game is repeated with expected number of rounds R=1/(1-omega), with omega=0.9 and R=10 in examples. The effect of punishment is analyzed in the repeated setting.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Investment is binary: investors choose to invest or not (conditional or unthinking). Trustees choose to be trustworthy or untrustworthy. No continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The parameter r (0<r<1) is the relative productivity of the prosocial strategy, analogous to MPCR. Payoff formulas depend on r, and results are shown for r=0.8.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a fine of n*delta per reported untrustworthy trustee, where delta is the punishment intensity. The cost to the punisher (government) is not explicitly modeled as a loss, but delta is the key parameter.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is directly parameterized by delta (fine per report). Increasing delta increases the impact of punishment and is the main moderator of efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Investors who successfully report untrustworthy trustees receive a reward of delta per report. Reward and punishment are implemented together.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward per successful report is delta. The cost to the government is not explicitly modeled as a loss, but delta is the key parameter.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is directly parameterized by delta (reward per report). Increasing delta increases the incentive for reporting and trust.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The expected number of rounds R is known and fixed in the model. The game is repeated with known parameters.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, the government announces the number of untrustworthy trustees, and investors can report. This is a form of summary information.

## Important Limitations
- The model is fully theoretical and simulation-based; there is no empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a repeated N-player trust game, not a standard public goods game, so results may not transfer perfectly to PGG settings.
- Punishment and reward are implemented by an external authority (government), not by peers, which may differ from many PGG experiments.
- The cost of punishment and reward to the government is not explicitly modeled as a loss, so net efficiency may be overestimated compared to real-world settings where enforcement is costly.
- The model assumes perfect reporting and enforcement; in practice, reporting may be noisy or costly.
