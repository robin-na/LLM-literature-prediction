Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical analysis of evolutionary dynamics in public goods games with optional participation and institutional incentives (punishment and reward). The main outcomes are average payoffs (group welfare) and the stability of cooperation, defection, and non-participation. Behavioral outcomes (strategy frequencies) are analyzed as part of the replicator dynamics, but the main focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with optional participation, institutional punishment (penalties) is highly effective at promoting full cooperation and maximizing group welfare, even when the same level of punishment would only produce bi-stability (coordination problem) under compulsory participation. Optional participation allows the population to escape from the inefficient defection equilibrium, making cooperation globally stable for a wide range of punishment strengths. In contrast, institutional rewards combined with optional participation do not robustly promote cooperation or welfare: they often lead to non-participation and only marginally improve group welfare over the baseline. The model provides explicit analytical thresholds for when each regime occurs, and shows that the minimal punishment needed to achieve full cooperation decreases as group size increases.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with optional participation and institutional punishment, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency (group welfare) compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, rounds, and incentive strengths, provided the punishment is above a threshold that decreases with group size. The model also predicts that institutional rewards are much less effective in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when optional participation and institutional punishment are present. However, the results are theoretical and assume infinite populations and well-mixed groups, so empirical generalizability may be limited.

## Important Limitations
- The results are entirely theoretical and based on replicator dynamics in infinite, well-mixed populations; empirical generalizability to finite, real-world groups is not tested.
- The model assumes institutional (centralized) incentives, not peer-to-peer punishment or reward.
- No stochasticity, learning, or bounded rationality is modeled; all dynamics are deterministic.
- No communication, reputation, or history effects are included.
- The model does not address the cost of establishing or maintaining the sanctioning institution beyond the entrance fee.
- No explicit modeling of finite rounds, so predictions may not directly transfer to finite repeated games.
