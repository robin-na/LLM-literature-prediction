# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Patience predicts cooperative synergy: The roles of ingroup bias and reciprocity
Authors: Espín, A. M., Correa, M., & Ruiz-Villaverde, A.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group cooperative synergy (relative group performance improvement)
- individual and group scores in problem-solving tasks
Non-payoff outcomes:
- social preferences (selfish, competitive/spiteful, efficiency, egalitarian choices)
- delay discounting (patience)
- perceived partner cooperativeness
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is 'group cooperative synergy', defined as the relative improvement in group performance over individual performance. Social preferences and patience are also measured, but the primary focus is on payoff-based group outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the mean patience of group members predicts higher group cooperative synergy in a problem-solving task with intergroup competition. Both short-term and long-term patience measures are positively associated with group efficiency. Patient individuals are more likely to make efficiency-maximizing and less likely to make competitive/spiteful choices in social preference tasks. The effect of patience on group synergy is not due to ingroup bias but is linked to reciprocal fairness. The design does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, but patient individuals are more likely to reward cooperative and punish uncooperative group members in allocation tasks.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that individual differences in patience (delay discounting) are a strong moderator of group efficiency in intergroup competition settings structurally similar to repeated social dilemmas. While not a standard PGG or punishment experiment, the findings suggest that group composition (patience) can be as important as institutional design for predicting efficiency. The absence of explicit punishment/reward means the results are most relevant for baseline or control conditions, or for understanding individual-level moderators. The study does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment, but it does show that patient individuals are more likely to engage in reciprocal (reward/punish) behaviors in allocation tasks, which may inform expectations about punishment effectiveness in similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups were composed of 3 students (with one group of 2 and one of 4 due to class size).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each condition (individual and group) involved a single problem set (one-shot per condition), not repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was not allowed in the individual condition, but allowed within groups in the group condition.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the structure: one individual and one group problem set, each with a fixed time limit.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the experiment, participants received feedback on scores of randomly selected students; not during the main task.

## Important Limitations
- The main group task is not a standard public goods game and does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms.
- Social preferences were measured using hypothetical (non-incentivized) allocations, which may be subject to social desirability bias.
- The group task involved only a single round (one-shot) per condition, limiting generalizability to repeated games.
- Possible confounding of cooperative behavior with coordination or communication skills.
- No control for individual intelligence or cognitive ability.
- Potential for reverse causality between group synergy and social preferences.
- Sample is limited to undergraduate business students in a classroom setting.
