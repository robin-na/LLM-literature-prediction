# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation in teams: The role of identity, punishment, and endowment distribution
Authors: Weng, Q., & Carlsson, F.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment points assigned
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the contribution rate (proportion of endowment contributed to the public good). Punishment behavior (punishment points assigned) is also analyzed. Group payoff, efficiency, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed, though contribution rate is closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that (1) strong identity (via human knot) increases contribution rates in heterogeneous teams without punishment, but not in homogeneous teams; (2) strong identity (via online chat) increases contribution rates in homogeneous teams; (3) punishment increases contribution rates when punishment is effective (1:3), even under strong identity, but not when punishment is less effective (1:2) and identity is strong; (4) strong identity does not further enhance contributions or punishment when punishment is available; (5) punishment of deviations from the team average does not depend on the target's endowment. The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (as measured by contribution rate) depends on the effectiveness of punishment and the strength of identity-building. When punishment is effective (punishment impact per unit cost is high), it increases contributions even under strong identity. When punishment is less effective and identity is strong, punishment does not increase contributions. However, the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, for similar public good games, enabling effective punishment is likely to increase efficiency (by increasing contributions), but the effect may be moderated by identity-building and punishment effectiveness. The absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All teams have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Online chat used as a strong identity-building activity in some treatments; not available during contribution or punishment decisions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0 ≤ c_i ≤ E_i.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.5 (each ECU contributed returns 0.5 ECU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is always 1 ECU per point; effectiveness (impact per point) is either 2 or 3 ECUs lost by target.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is either 1:3 (1 ECU cost, 3 ECU impact) or 1:2 (1 ECU cost, 2 ECU impact). Punishment increases contributions only when effectiveness is high (1:3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown others' contribution rates and team total contributions after each period.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players are identified by numbers that are randomly shuffled each period.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not group payoff or efficiency; efficiency is not directly reported or analyzed.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- Punishment is only studied as peer punishment with fixed cost and effectiveness; no central or institutional punishment.
- No reward mechanism is included, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- Identity-building is operationalized via specific activities (human knot, online chat), which may not generalize to all forms of team identity.
