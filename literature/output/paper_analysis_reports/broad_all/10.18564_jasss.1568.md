## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that co-operative punishment (where the cost of punishing free-riders is shared among group members) is much more effective at maintaining a high proportion of contributors in a simulated public goods game than either no punishment or altruistic punishment (where punishers pay the cost individually). The effectiveness of punishment depends on the ratio of cost to punished versus cost to punisher, and on the efficiency of detecting free-riders. Co-operative punishment allows pro-social behavior to invade and persist in populations of free-riders, while altruistic punishment does not. However, the primary outcome is the proportion of contributors, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the structure of punishment (co-operative vs. individual) is a critical moderator of cooperation in public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, the maintenance of high contribution rates under co-operative punishment suggests that efficiency would likely be higher in such settings, especially when the cost of punishment is shared and detection of free-riders is effective. For prediction tasks, the results imply that enabling co-operative punishment mechanisms (where costs are distributed) should be expected to yield higher efficiency than individual punishment, all else equal. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of quantitative predictions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 50 agents as default, with mention of similar results for 500 and 2,000 agents.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent has a lifetime of 10 seasons (rounds); simulations run for up to 200 or 1000 time steps.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The synergy parameter alpha is set to 1 in the main simulations, which is analogous to a marginal per-capita return of 1.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher (Y) and to punished (K) are varied; effectiveness of punishment depends on K/Y ratio.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (E) is varied; higher effectiveness leads to more contributors.

## Important Limitations
- The study is based on agent-based simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- The primary outcome is the proportion of contributors, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- Parameter values (e.g., punishment cost, effectiveness) are set by the model and may not map directly to real-world settings.
- No direct analysis of payoff-based efficiency outcomes, limiting quantitative prediction for efficiency.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback among agents.
