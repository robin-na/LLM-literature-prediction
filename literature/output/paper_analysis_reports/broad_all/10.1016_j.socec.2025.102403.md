The effect of sanctions on contributions is analyzed, but not the effect on group efficiency or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that cognitive manipulations (time pressure and motivated delay) do not affect the probability that groups vote to adopt sanctions in a public goods game. However, motivated delay reduces average contributions to the public good, and when sanctions are adopted, they are more effective at increasing contributions in the motivated delay condition. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the reported results. The findings suggest that while cognitive mode does not affect rule adoption, it can affect the behavioral response to sanctions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a linear PGG with endogenous voting on a fixed-cost, non-deterrent sanction, the adoption of sanctions is not affected by cognitive manipulations (time pressure or motivated delay). While the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly reported, the results suggest that sanctions can increase contributions, especially under motivated delay, but the cost of sanctions may offset these gains. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that the presence of a costly, group-level sanction can increase contributions, but efficiency effects depend on whether increased contributions are sufficient to cover sanction costs. The paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence on efficiency, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only contributions and voting behavior are analyzed.
- The sanction is a fixed, group-level rule rather than individual peer punishment, limiting generalizability to other punishment mechanisms.
- The cognitive manipulations (time pressure, motivated delay) are only applied at the voting stage, not during contribution decisions.
- Online subject pool may differ from laboratory or field settings.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so results are specific to punishment-only environments.
- No direct feedback or summary information about others' contributions is described, which may affect generalizability.
