# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: A comparison of punishment rules in repeated public good games - An experimental study
Authors: Decker, T., Stiehler, A., & Strobel, M.
Journal: Journal of Conflict Resolution
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group profit
- efficiency (group total profit after all sanction costs)
- individual profit
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment proposals
- punishment carried out
- justice (profit-contribution correlation, variance in profits/contributions)
- rule approval/bidding
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (group profit, efficiency) and behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, justice, rule approval). Efficiency is defined as group total profit after all sanction costs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that collective punishment rules generally induce more punishment activity and higher contributions than the individual rule. The most severe collective rule (maxi) achieves the highest contributions but not the highest efficiency, as the costs of punishment offset the gains from increased cooperation. Efficiency (group profit after punishment costs) does not differ significantly between rules, and the relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is reversed compared to the relationship with contributions. Emotional punishment, especially in the most severe rule, can damage efficiency. Justice (as measured by profit-contribution correlation and variance) improves with more severe collective rules. Participants are more likely to support collective rules if they expect higher profits and if the rule requires a higher degree of consent (e.g., unanimity).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public good game with punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not guarantee higher efficiency or group profit. The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the severity and structure of the punishment rule: more severe rules can increase cooperation but may reduce efficiency if punishment costs are high, especially due to emotional punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency with punishment is non-monotonic and depends on the balance between increased cooperation and punishment costs. Game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology, and the collective/individual nature of punishment are critical moderators. The paper's results caution against assuming that more severe or more available punishment will always improve efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per session, with different rules in different phases (1-10, 11-15, 16-20).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; all interaction was via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous from 0 to 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (pot multiplied by 1.6, split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit is 1:1 with fine imposed; in collective rules, cost is split among punishers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (1 ECU cost = 1 ECU fine); varies in who pays and how much is imposed depending on rule.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of periods in each phase; instructions given at start of each phase.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw others' contributions, punishments received, and costs incurred, but not individual punishment proposals.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were identified by ID numbers; IDs were constant within a phase but changed between phases.

## Important Limitations
- No control condition without punishment; cannot directly compare to a no-punishment baseline.
- Results may not generalize to other group sizes, MPCRs, or punishment cost structures.
- Emotional punishment may be context-dependent and could differ in other settings.
- Efficiency differences between rules were not statistically significant, possibly due to high variance and limited sample size.
- All participants were students, which may limit external validity.
