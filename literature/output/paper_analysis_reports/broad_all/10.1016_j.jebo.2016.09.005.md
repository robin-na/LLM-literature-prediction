# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Trust and in-group favoritism in a culture of crime
Authors: Meier, S., Pierce, L., Vaccaro, A., & La Cara, B.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust (amount sent in trust game)
- trustworthiness (amount returned in trust game)
- cooperation rate in prisoner's dilemma (with and without punishment)
- in-group favoritism (difference in behavior toward in-group vs. out-group)
- punishment behavior (amount and frequency of punishment assigned)
- dictator game giving
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust, trustworthiness, cooperation rate, in-group favoritism, and punishment behavior. No explicit analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. Payoff outcomes are not the focus; behavioral outcomes are.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Punishment mechanisms increase cooperation rates in one-shot prisoner's dilemma games in both high- and low-Mafia neighborhoods, but do not close the gap in trust or cooperation between the two. In high-Mafia schools, punishment intensifies in-group favoritism, leading to higher cooperation with in-group members but not with out-group members. In low-Mafia schools, punishment does not increase in-group favoritism and may even reduce it. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. The main outcomes are behavioral: trust, cooperation, and in-group bias.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that introducing punishment in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma increases cooperation rates, but the effect on efficiency is not reported. The design is adjacent to a public goods game (binary PD, not multi-person PGG), so results may partially transfer. The effect of punishment is context-dependent: in environments with strong in-group norms (high-Mafia), punishment increases in-group favoritism and does not resolve overall trust/cooperation deficits. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment may increase cooperation rates but not necessarily efficiency, especially in settings with strong parochial norms. The absence of efficiency or group payoff data limits direct applicability to efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Prisoner's dilemma is two-player; all games are two-person interactions (trust game, dictator game, PD).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot; each game is played once per participant, with different partners for each game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants before, during, or after the games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Prisoner's dilemma is all-or-nothing (binary choice: pass or keep endowment). Trust and dictator games allow continuous choices.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the prisoner's dilemma, the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 2 (each €1 passed is doubled for the recipient). In the trust game, the amount sent is tripled.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point in the punishment stage costs the punisher 1 and reduces the punished party's payoff by 3.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each unit spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told each game is one-shot and independent; the number of games and their order are known.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; only behavioral outcomes (trust, cooperation, in-group favoritism, punishment) are analyzed.
- The experimental design uses one-shot, two-player prisoner's dilemma games, not multi-person public goods games, so results are only adjacent to PGG settings.
- The sample is limited to high school students in three schools in two neighborhoods, which may limit generalizability.
- Causal inference is limited by possible unobserved differences between neighborhoods beyond Mafia involvement.
- No analysis of how punishment affects overall group welfare or efficiency; only cooperation rates and behavioral patterns are reported.
- Punishment is third-party and anonymous; not all features of real-world or standard PGG punishment are present.
