Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical and review article focusing on the psychological and cognitive constraints on the evolution and occurrence of cooperation and punishment in animals and humans. It does not report direct efficiency, payoff, or welfare outcomes, but discusses the conditions under which punishment and reciprocity can stabilize cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, although theoretical models (including those based on the Prisoner's Dilemma) predict that punishment can stabilize cooperation, actual evidence for punishment as a mechanism for sustaining cooperation in nonhuman animals is rare. Most observed 'punishment' is better characterized as harassment, which yields immediate rather than delayed benefits. The rarity of punishment and reciprocity in animals is attributed to cognitive constraints, particularly the need for individual recognition, low temporal discounting, and sufficient memory to track interactions. In contrast, humans possess cognitive adaptations that enable widespread use of punishment and reciprocity to sustain cooperation. The paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games or their variants.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and comparative context for why punishment may or may not be effective in sustaining cooperation, especially in nonhuman animals. It highlights the importance of cognitive prerequisites (recognition, memory, discounting) for punishment to function as a stabilizer of cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence or data that can be used to predict the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar experimental designs. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment may depend on whether the game environment allows for individual recognition, repeated interactions, and low discounting, but it does not quantify these effects or relate them to efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses the necessity of individual recognition for punishment to function as a stabilizer of cooperation, implying that identifying who punished is critical for punishment to have its intended effect.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report original experimental or quantitative data on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods or similar games.
- No direct analysis of game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, punishment cost) as manipulable variables.
- Payoff-related outcomes such as efficiency, welfare, or group earnings are not measured or reported.
- Most evidence is drawn from comparative animal behavior and cognitive psychology, not from controlled public goods game experiments.
