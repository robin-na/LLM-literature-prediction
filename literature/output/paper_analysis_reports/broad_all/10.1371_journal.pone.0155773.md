# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Power Asymmetries and Punishment in a Prisoner's Dilemma with Variable Cooperative Investment
Authors: Bone, J. E., Wallace, B., Bshary, R., & Raihani, N. J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- mean cooperation level
- propensity to punish
- likelihood of increasing cooperation after punishment
- likelihood of retaliation after punishment
- opt-out frequency
Outcome notes:
The study explicitly focuses on behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment, retaliation, opt-out) rather than payoffs or efficiency. Authors state that payoffs are determined by arbitrary game parameters and thus focus on behavioral responses to punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a two-player iterated prisoner's dilemma with variable cooperative investment and a punishment option, punishment did not increase the likelihood that targets would increase their cooperation, regardless of whether the punisher was strong or weak, or whether the game was symmetric or asymmetric. Both weak and strong players retaliated against punishment, and the threat of punishment from strong players may have led to higher baseline cooperation from weak players, but actual punishment had no effect on subsequent cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral responses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in two-player games with variable investment and a punishment option, enabling punishment does not increase cooperation or efficiency, even when punishment is more effective for some players (power asymmetry). However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The behavioral results suggest that, in similar two-player designs, enabling punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may provoke retaliation. The findings are most relevant for adjacent game designs (iterated PD with variable investment) and may be less transferable to standard public goods games or multiplayer settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) iterated prisoner's dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game lasted 50 rounds, but players were told the game would last between 20 and 100 rounds to avoid end effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (cooperation) was variable, not all-or-nothing; players chose a cooperation level between 0 and 5 seconds.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. For each second both players cooperated, both got one unit (MPCR = 1 for each player per unit contributed). The game is a prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game, but the marginal return is specified.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is always 1 unit per punishment, for both weak and strong players.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: weak players impose 1 unit fine per 1 unit cost (1:1), strong players impose 6 unit fine per 1 unit cost (1:6).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told the game would last between 20 and 100 rounds (actual: 50), so the exact number of rounds was not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw whether their partner chose a higher, lower, or equal cooperation level (but not the exact value), and both players' payoffs for the round. After punishment, players saw their own and their partner's choice and payoff from step 2, as well as cumulative payoffs for both players for that round and their own total payoff.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew whether their partner punished them; punishment was not anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all analyses focus on behavioral responses (cooperation, punishment, retaliation, opt-out).
- The game is a two-player iterated prisoner's dilemma with variable investment, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to multiplayer or standard PGG settings.
- Punishment was a fixed, binary action (punish or not), not a variable or escalating investment.
- The cost of punishment and retaliation may have been too low to deter defection or retaliation.
- Neutral language and abstract framing may have limited the salience of power asymmetries.
- Players could opt out of rounds, which may affect the interpretation of cooperation and punishment dynamics.
