Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2021

empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is efficiency gain, defined as group earnings relative to the Nash equilibrium and the efficient outcome. The paper also reports on contributions, monitoring, and punishment behavior, but these are secondary to the efficiency analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players (fixed composition) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) per session, pre-announced to participants.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; groups are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses any integer amount from 0 to 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 ECU and reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 ECU spent reduces target's payoff by 3 ECU.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total number of rounds (20) is pre-announced and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, players learn their stage two payoff, the number of punishment points received, and their final payoff. They also receive signals about others' contributions, with accuracy depending on monitoring.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players cannot identify who punished them. Player labels are randomly reassigned each period.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world public goods dilemmas.
- The control condition (no punishment) is not directly reported; all treatments include punishment with varying monitoring costs.
- The design does not include a pure punishment-enabled, perfect-information baseline; all information is imperfect unless monitoring is purchased.
- The findings may not generalize to larger groups, different MPCRs, or environments with communication or rewards.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is confounded with the cost and availability of monitoring; results may differ if information is free or always perfect.
