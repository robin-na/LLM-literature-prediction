# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: In-group defense, out-group aggression, and coordination failures in intergroup conflict
Authors: De Dreu, C. K. W., Gross, J., Méder, Z., Giffin, M., Prochazkova, E., Krikeb, J., & Columbus, S.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual wealth (final earnings)
- aggressor group success rate (proportion of episodes won)
- defender group survival rate
- wasted resources (investments not recovered)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contributions to group pool
- within-group variance (dispersion) in contributions
- number of noncontributors per group
- coordination (intraclass correlation of contributions)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group success rates (aggressor/defender), individual wealth, and behavioral measures of contribution and coordination. Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but group payoff and resource waste are analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Peer punishment increases contributions to out-group aggression but does not improve the aggressor group's success rate or overall group payoffs; it leads to more wasted resources and lower individual wealth. In contrast, sequential decision-making (a coordination mechanism) increases coordination within aggressor groups, nearly doubles their success rate, and reduces wealth loss for aggressors (but increases it for defenders). The results suggest that punishment can motivate higher contributions but does not solve the coordination problem or improve efficiency, while coordination mechanisms can enhance group success and payoffs in competitive intergroup settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in intergroup contest games with costly contributions and the possibility of peer punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not improve group efficiency or payoffs—indeed, it can reduce them due to the direct costs of punishment. Coordination mechanisms (like sequential decision-making) are more effective at improving group success and payoffs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment (with the cost/impact structure used here) should not be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, and may even decrease it. The effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the coordination context and the cost/impact structure of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consists of two groups of three players (six total).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of two blocks of five episodes (rounds) each: five baseline and five treatment (punishment or sequential decision-making).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; players were in separate cubicles and unaware of others' identities.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: players choose any amount from 0 to 20 Experimental Euros.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is effectively zero: all contributions are wasted unless the group wins, in which case the aggressor group can capture the defender's remaining resources. This is a contest game, not a standard PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 Experimental Euro and reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 Experimental Euros.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (impact:cost ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of episodes (rounds) in each block.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each episode, players are informed about their group's total contribution, the other group's total contribution, and the resulting earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Within-group punishment is anonymous: group member identifiers are reshuffled each round to decouple punishment from reputation and reciprocity.

## Important Limitations
- The game is an intergroup contest, not a standard public goods game; all contributions are wasted unless the group wins, so efficiency is not defined as in standard PGGs.
- Efficiency is not directly reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome; only group payoffs, resource waste, and success rates are analyzed.
- Punishment is only within-group and anonymous; no between-group punishment or reputation effects.
- The cost/impact structure of punishment is fixed (1:3), and results may not generalize to other punishment technologies.
- The laboratory setting uses small groups (3 per side) and a limited number of rounds, which may not generalize to larger or repeated real-world settings.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about the interaction of punishment and reward.
