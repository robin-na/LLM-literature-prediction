## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that cooperation in commons dilemmas is highly context-dependent and that institutional features such as communication, information about others' actions, and the ability to sanction (punish or reward) are critical for building trust and reciprocity, which in turn support higher cooperation. It reviews evidence that allowing punishment or graduated sanctions in lab and field settings increases cooperation and joint outcomes, but does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff. The chapter also notes that the effect of design dimensions (e.g., group size, repetition, communication, sanctioning) on cooperation is variable and context-specific.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that enabling punishment or sanctions in public goods or CPR-like games increases cooperation and can improve group outcomes, especially when combined with communication and information about others' actions. However, it does not provide direct, quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff effects, nor does it systematically analyze the effect of specific game design dimensions on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the general expectation that punishment mechanisms (especially graduated sanctions) are associated with higher cooperation and likely higher efficiency, but the evidence is indirect and not parameterized for specific game designs.
