# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputation Formation and the Evolution of Cooperation in Anonymous Online Markets
Authors: Diekmann, A., Jann, B., Przepiorka, W., & Wehrli, S.
Journal: American Sociological Review
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- probability of sale
- selling price
- total group welfare (implied by mutually beneficial trade)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- feedback frequency
- reciprocity in feedback
- altruism in feedback
- timing of feedback
- strategic feedback behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the probability of sale and the selling price, both of which are direct payoff-based outcomes for sellers. Feedback behavior is also analyzed, but as a mechanism for reputation formation and thus indirectly for cooperation and welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Using large-scale eBay auction data, the study shows that sellers with more positive feedback (reputation) are more likely to sell their items and receive higher prices, while negative feedback reduces both outcomes. The effect of negative feedback is stronger than that of positive feedback. Participation in the feedback system is high, and feedback behavior is consistent with strong reciprocity and altruism, which helps maintain the reputation system. The reputation system thus mitigates trust problems and supports cooperation in one-shot, anonymous exchanges, increasing overall market efficiency and welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that reputation-based feedback systems in anonymous, one-shot exchange environments (adjacent to PGGs) increase efficiency by incentivizing cooperative behavior. While not a direct PGG or punishment experiment, the findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of introducing or strengthening reputation/feedback mechanisms in similar environments: efficiency (measured as successful trade and higher prices) increases when reputation mechanisms are active and well-participated. The effect is stronger for negative feedback (punishment) than for positive feedback (reward). The study also highlights the importance of high participation in the feedback system, which is sustained by reciprocity and altruism. For prediction, the presence of a reputation/feedback mechanism (even if not formal monetary punishment) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a baseline with no such mechanism.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each auction is a dyadic (buyer-seller) interaction; group size is 2. The market as a whole is large and anonymous, but each transaction is between two parties.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Most interactions are one-shot (single round) exchanges; repeated interactions between the same buyer and seller are rare.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. eBay provides discussion forums and chat rooms, but these are not used for most transactions and are not central to the analysis.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The decision to cooperate (ship/pay) is all-or-nothing in each transaction; there is no partial contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (negative feedback) is costless in monetary terms but may have a time/effort cost and risk of retaliation. The effect of negative feedback is strong.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Negative feedback has a strong negative effect on future sales and prices, indicating high punishment effectiveness.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Positive feedback (reward) is available and has a positive effect, though less than negative feedback.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward (positive feedback) is costless in monetary terms but requires time/effort.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Positive feedback increases future sales and prices, but the effect is smaller than for negative feedback.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each auction is a one-shot transaction; the structure is clear to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reputation (feedback summary) is publicly visible to all market participants and is the main mechanism for incentivizing cooperation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Feedback is not anonymous; the identity of the rater is known, which enables reciprocity and retaliation.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and cannot fully rule out unobserved confounding, though fixed effects are used to mitigate this.
- The environment is not a public goods game but a large-scale, one-shot, dyadic market; findings are adjacent but not directly transferable to PGGs.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as feedback (reputation), not as direct monetary sanctions.
- The study does not experimentally manipulate the presence or absence of the reputation system.
- Repeated interactions are rare but not impossible; some strategic behavior may exist but is hard to identify.
- Product heterogeneity (especially in DVDs) may affect the generalizability of the results.
