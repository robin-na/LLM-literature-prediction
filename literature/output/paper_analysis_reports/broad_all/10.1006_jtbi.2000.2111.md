## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that strong reciprocity—cooperation combined with costly punishment of defectors—can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, even when repeated interaction is unlikely. Theoretical results show that the ability to inflict punishment at low cost (high punishment effectiveness) and the presence of a sufficient fraction of strong reciprocators can induce cooperation among self-interested agents, raising group fitness. The model predicts that punishment increases group efficiency, especially when the cost to punishers is low and the impact on defectors is high. Laboratory evidence is reviewed to support these claims, showing that allowing punishment in public goods experiments prevents the decay of cooperation and increases average contributions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially when punishment is effective (high impact per cost) and not too costly for punishers. The model identifies key moderators: group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the probability of future interaction. The reviewed experimental evidence (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000) shows that punishment prevents the decline of cooperation and can even increase it to near full cooperation, implying a positive effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment will have a more positive effect on efficiency when the punishment technology is strong and the cost is low, and that group size and the structure of repeated interaction also matter.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and does not report new experimental data; it relies on previously published experiments for empirical support.
- The model assumes fixed group size and random group formation, which may not capture all real-world group dynamics.
- The model focuses on evolutionary fitness and group survival, which may not map directly to monetary efficiency in laboratory public goods games.
- No direct analysis of the effect of communication, reward, or default contribution framing.
- The reviewed experiments are limited to specific parameterizations (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) and may not generalize to all public goods game designs.
