# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment
Authors: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per subject per round
- group total payoff
- social welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public account
- frequency of free-riding
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average payoff (efficiency) and average contribution. Payoff is reported as average points per subject per round, and is directly compared across treatments. Contribution is also analyzed, but efficiency/payoff is a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players, randomly matched each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication was allowed between subjects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous/integer from 0 to 20; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (each point contributed yields 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous and costless to players; the penalty is a deadweight loss deducted from the punished player's account, not paid by another player.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is determined by the probability and severity; all treatments have same expected penalty (10 points), but actual impact on efficiency varies by probability/severity structure.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed that the game has 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see the sum of group contributions, lowest group contribution, and their own payoffs each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous and automatic; no information about the identity of the punished player is given to others.

## Important Limitations
- Punishment is exogenous and automatic, not peer-administered; results may not generalize to endogenous punishment settings.
- All punishment treatments have the same expected penalty, so results do not address effects of varying expected punishment magnitude.
- The experiment uses random rematching each round, which may reduce reputation effects compared to fixed groups.
- The study is conducted with Chinese undergraduate students; external validity to other populations is uncertain.
- The number of rounds is limited (10), so long-term dynamics are not observed.
- No reward or positive sanction treatments are included, so results are specific to punishment-only environments.
