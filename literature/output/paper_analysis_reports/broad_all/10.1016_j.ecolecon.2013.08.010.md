# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Co-managing common-pool resources: Do formal rules have to be adapted to traditional ecological norms?
Authors: Vollan, B., Prediger, S., & Frölich, M.
Journal: Ecological Economics
Year: 2013

empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Non-payoff outcomes:
- rule compliance
- rule breaking frequency

- area choice
- sustainability norm
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings and the number of grazing areas in good condition, both of which are direct measures of efficiency/welfare. Rule compliance and behavioral outcomes are also analyzed, but the focus is on efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This field-lab experiment with communal farmers in southern Africa shows that the efficiency of formal, democratically chosen rules for managing a common-pool resource depends on their alignment with local ecological norms. The quota rule (limiting extraction) increases group earnings and resource availability in both high and low sustainability groups, but is unpopular. The rotation rule only increases efficiency in low sustainability groups (who previously failed to coordinate), but is ineffective or even counterproductive in high sustainability groups (who already tacitly coordinated for sustainability), due to high rates of rule breaking. The lottery rule is ineffective in both types of groups. Rule compliance is lower when rules conflict with local norms, and rule breaking is not mainly motivated by profit. Thus, the effect of rule-based interventions on efficiency is highly context-dependent.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of introducing a formal rule (akin to a punishment or restriction mechanism) on efficiency in a common-pool resource game, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect depends on the compatibility of the rule with pre-existing group norms. Rules that align with local sustainability norms (e.g., quotas in groups that value sustainability) can increase efficiency, while rules that conflict with these norms (e.g., rotation in high-norm groups) may reduce efficiency due to non-compliance. The presence of a sanctioning mechanism (imperfect monitoring and penalty) is not sufficient to ensure compliance or efficiency gains. Thus, the effect of punishment-like interventions on efficiency is moderated by group-level norms and prior behavior, not just by the formal game design. This paper is highly informative for settings where the main intervention is a formal rule or restriction, but less so for classic peer-punishment PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 5 players; fixed matching throughout the session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session has 20 rounds, divided into two stages of 10 rounds each (no-rule and rule stage).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication about the rules or the prior game was not allowed during the voting phase; no evidence of chat during play.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (grazing intensity) is discrete: 0, 1, or 2 units per round; not strictly all-or-nothing, but limited discrete choices.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff structure is nonlinear and depends on group intensity and resource state; not a classic linear MPCR, but the marginal return to restraint is positive for group welfare.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a penalty for rule violation: with probability 1/6, violators must return all tokens earned in that round (i.e., lose their round earnings). No cost to other players; not peer punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic (1/6 chance of detection per round); penalty is loss of all round earnings. No direct cost to the punisher (the experimenter/government).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 20 rounds, with a rule change after round 10.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocation of grazing rights and rule violations are publicly revealed; area conditions are known to all. No explicit mention of summary statistics, but resource state is common knowledge.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not implement classic peer punishment; sanctions are imposed by the experimenter/government, not by other players.
- The design is a framed field-lab experiment with a specific ecological context (grazing management in southern Africa), which may limit generalizability to standard PGGs.
- The main intervention is a formal rule with probabilistic monitoring and penalty, not a voluntary punishment institution.
- The effect of punishment is confounded with the type of rule (quota, rotation, lottery), and the study does not isolate the effect of punishment per se.
- Group-level norms and prior behavior are major moderators, which may not be observable in other settings.
- Sample size for some rules (e.g., quota) is small, limiting statistical power for those conditions.
