Theoretical models focus on norm compliance, cooperation, and the evolution of social norms, with some discussion of effort levels and morale as proxies for group performance. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus in public goods games.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper presents theoretical arguments and formal models showing that costly punishment (including decentralized, altruistic punishment) can sustain norm compliance and cooperation in social dilemmas similar to public goods games. It also models the interaction between punishment and reputation as mechanisms for norm enforcement. The models predict multiple possible equilibria (high or low compliance/cooperation), with path dependence based on initial conditions. While the models are closely related to public goods games, the main outcomes are norm compliance and cooperation rates, not efficiency or group payoff. The models do not provide direct evidence or predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in public-goods-like environments can increase norm compliance and cooperation, especially when reputation effects are weak. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper suggests that punishment is likely to increase cooperation and norm compliance, which may translate into higher efficiency, but the models do not quantify this effect. The paper is most useful for understanding the mechanisms and conditions under which punishment sustains cooperation, rather than for making direct efficiency predictions.

- mpcr: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Models use group production functions and shared output, but do not specify or vary MPCR as a parameter.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as costly to the punisher; higher punishment cost reduces the frequency of punishment. The effect of punishment on norm compliance is discussed, but not on efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled via the penalty C imposed on violators; higher C increases compliance. The effect is on norm compliance, not directly on efficiency.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some models assume agents know the fraction of norm followers (mu), but no explicit manipulation or analysis.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Outcomes are focused on norm compliance, cooperation, and punishment behavior, not on efficiency, group payoff, or surplus.
- Models are closely related to public goods games but are not exact instantiations; some models use binary compliance rather than continuous contribution.
- No quantitative predictions or effect sizes for efficiency or payoff outcomes with or without punishment.
- No analysis of how specific game design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR) moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
