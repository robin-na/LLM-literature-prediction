## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that asymmetric punishment, where only one group can punish another and only across network layers, can substantially increase the fraction of cooperators in spatial prisoner's dilemma games on interdependent networks. The effect is stronger when the punishment is more severe (higher fine per unit cost). When the temptation to defect is low, punishment is more effective than reward at promoting cooperation; when the temptation is high, reward becomes more effective. The main reported outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not group payoff or efficiency. The paper does not report efficiency or total group payoff, so its findings are most directly relevant to predicting cooperation rates rather than efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in agent-based models of spatial prisoner's dilemma games with asymmetric punishment, increasing punishment strength or effectiveness increases cooperation rates. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it is not directly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling or strengthening punishment will likely increase cooperation rates, which may be correlated with higher efficiency, but this is not directly tested. The paper also shows that the effect of punishment depends on the temptation to defect and the cost-to-fine ratio. For prediction tasks, this paper is most useful for understanding how punishment design (asymmetry, cost-to-fine ratio) affects cooperation rates, with only indirect implications for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each network is an L x L lattice (L=400 to 1000), so each layer has 160,000 to 1,000,000 players. The model is agent-based and simulates large populations.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 50,000 Monte Carlo steps, with outcomes measured at stationary state. Not a fixed number of rounds as in lab experiments, but a large number of time steps.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between two discrete strategies: cooperate or defect (binary choice).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is parameterized by α; cost-to-fine ratios of 1:1, 1:3, and 1:5 are tested. Higher cost-to-fine ratio (more effective punishment) increases cooperation rates.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the cost-to-fine ratio (1:1, 1:3, 1:5). Higher effectiveness (greater fine per unit cost) increases cooperation rates.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward is modeled in a comparison (from previous work and in Fig. 8). Reward can promote cooperation, especially when the dilemma is strong, but is less effective than punishment when the dilemma is weak.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a simulation of spatial prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- The main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not measured or reported.
- The model assumes very large populations and spatial structure, which may not match typical lab or field experiments.
- Punishment is asymmetric and only occurs across network layers, which is a specific and possibly uncommon institutional design.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented; all results are from agent-based simulations.
