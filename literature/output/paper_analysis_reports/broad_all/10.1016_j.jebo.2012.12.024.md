# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strategies of cooperation and punishment among students and clerical workers
Authors: Bigoni, M., Camera, G., & Casari, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- strategy adoption (e.g., always defect, grim trigger, tit-for-tat)
- frequency and type of personal punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates and strategy adoption. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff is discussed in theoretical framing but not as a primary empirical outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that allowing personal punishment increases cooperation rates in indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma games for both students and workers. Students show a larger increase in cooperation and are more likely to adopt conditional cooperation strategies (e.g., forgiving or unforgiving trigger strategies) when punishment is available, while workers shift more toward unconditional cooperation. Workers are less likely to use personal punishment than students. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing personal punishment increases cooperation rates in indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma games, with the effect being more pronounced among student subjects. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation rates and strategy adoption, not efficiency. The design is adjacent to public goods games (pairwise PDs in groups of four with random matching), so caution is needed in generalizing to standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players, randomly matched into pairs each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Indefinitely repeated game with continuation probability 0.95 per period; expected length ~20 rounds per cycle; 5 cycles per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage game payoffs: mutual cooperation = 25 each, mutual defection = 10 each, temptation = 30, sucker = 5. Not a standard PGG, but a pairwise PD.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punisher pays 5 points to reduce opponent's earnings by 10 points per punishment action.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment action costs 5 and reduces the target's payoff by 10 (punishment effectiveness = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is indefinitely repeated with random termination; players do not know the exact number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players only observe actions and outcomes in their own pair; no information about other group members' actions or payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always within a pair and both players know if they were punished by their current opponent; no information about punishment outside the pair.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated prisoner's dilemma with random matching, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; only cooperation rates and strategy adoption are analyzed.
- The subject pools (students and clerical workers) may not be representative of broader populations.
- The punishment mechanism is limited to personal punishment within pairs; no group-level or third-party punishment.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation effects.
