# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The age of consequences: Unraveling conflict's impact on social preferences, norm enforcement, and risk-taking
Authors: Coutts, A.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- punishment frequency
- reward frequency
- risk-taking (contribution in risky PGG)
- survey-based risk aversion
- survey-based trust
- survey-based perceived community cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contributions in public goods games (including baseline, punishment, reward, and risk variants), frequency of punishment and reward, and risk-taking. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. Some tables include earnings as a control variable, but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that exposure to conflict (via radio reception of hate messages during the Rwandan genocide) is associated with higher contributions in public goods games, but this effect is entirely driven by individuals who were children during the genocide. In games with punishment and reward options, conflict exposure increases the likelihood of punishing and rewarding others, but this effect is driven by those who were older during the genocide. Risk-taking, as measured by contributions in a risky public goods game and by survey, is higher for all age groups exposed to conflict. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. However, it does show that the presence of punishment increases contributions relative to baseline, and that conflict exposure increases the propensity to punish.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment and reward options affect contribution behavior in public goods games, and how these effects are moderated by past conflict exposure and age at exposure. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment increases contributions (relative to baseline), and that the propensity to punish is higher among those exposed to conflict as adults. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect evidence that punishment can increase cooperation, but does not quantify the effect on group payoff or efficiency. The detailed game design information and behavioral results are useful for understanding the context and potential moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game group consisted of 12 players, fixed across all games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of two rounds: a baseline round and a second round with either baseline, punishment, reward, or risk variant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players; decisions were made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous in increments of 100 RWF from 0 to 400 RWF.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR was 0.25 (group fund tripled and divided among 12 players). In the risk version, MPCR was either 1/12 or 5/12, with expected value 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost was 50 RWF to the punisher per penalty assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment reduced the punished player's earnings by 100 RWF (punishment impact per unit cost = 2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. A reward version was implemented in which players could reward others at a cost.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost was 50 RWF to the rewarder per reward assigned.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward increased the rewarded player's earnings by 100 RWF (reward impact per unit cost = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there would be two rounds, but were not told in advance which variant would be played in the second round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the punishment and reward versions, each player saw the contribution of their matched partner. In the baseline, individual contributions were not disclosed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward were assigned anonymously; players did not know who punished or rewarded them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as primary outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
- Behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment, reward) are reported, but not mapped to efficiency or welfare.
- The study is observational with respect to conflict exposure (using radio reception as an instrument), not a randomized experiment on punishment itself.
- Sample is from rural Rwanda nearly two decades after the genocide; generalizability to other contexts may be limited.
- Potential confounds from migration, post-genocide radio exposure, and direct effects of hate messaging are discussed but cannot be fully ruled out.
