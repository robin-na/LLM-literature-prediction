## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that egoistic punishment can evolve and promote high levels of cooperation in both peer and pool punishment modes, outperforming altruistic punishment in a wider range of parameter settings (especially at higher punishment costs and lower fines). The mechanism is robust to different strategy updating rules and population structures. However, the paper does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff; instead, it focuses on the prevalence of cooperation and the survival of different strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (egoistic vs. altruistic) and the parameterization of punishment cost and fine can substantially affect cooperation rates in public goods games. However, because the main outcomes are cooperation rates and not group efficiency or total payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The results suggest that egoistic punishment mechanisms may be more effective at sustaining cooperation under a broader set of conditions, which could indirectly imply higher efficiency, but this is not directly measured or reported.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for a large number of iterations (e.g., 10^6 for relaxation, 5000 for sampling), but the game is not a fixed finite number of rounds as in lab experiments.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor r (2.0, 3.5, 3.8, 4.0) is used; MPCR is r/5 for group size 5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is varied from 0 to 1; higher cost generally reduces cooperation, but egoistic punishment is more robust to high cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine beta, 0 < beta <= 1) is varied; higher fines can increase cooperation but effects are non-monotonic due to cyclic dominance.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main outcomes are cooperation rates and strategy frequencies, not direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The model assumes fixed group size and spatial structure, which may not generalize to all public goods game settings.
- No direct analysis of group payoff or efficiency is provided, so implications for efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included in the model.
