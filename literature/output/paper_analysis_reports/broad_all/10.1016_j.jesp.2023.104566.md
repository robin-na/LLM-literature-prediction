# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Conflict, cooperation, and institutional choice
Authors: Jin, S. X., Columbus, S., van Lange, P. A. M., & Balliet, D.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- beliefs about others' contributions
- personal norms of cooperation
- normative expectations
- institutional choice (voting for sanctioning institution)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates, beliefs, norms, and votes for sanctioning institutions. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus as primary outcomes. Some payoff information is provided in descriptive statistics (e.g., average bonus earned), but not as a main outcome or in relation to punishment effects.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two experiments, higher conflict of interests (lower MPCR) leads to lower cooperation, lower beliefs about others' cooperation, and lower normative expectations and personal norms of cooperation. However, the degree of conflict does not increase the likelihood that individuals or groups vote to establish a sanctioning institution (peer punishment, centralized punishment, or gossip/ostracism). Centralized punishment is more likely to be chosen than peer punishment or gossip/ostracism, but this is not moderated by conflict level. The main outcomes are behavioral (contributions, beliefs, norms, votes), not efficiency or group payoff. There is no direct evidence on how enabling punishment affects efficiency or group payoff relative to a no-punishment baseline.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how conflict of interests and institutional choice affect cooperation, beliefs, and norms in public goods games, but does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment. Thus, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. However, it does show that the mere availability of punishment institutions (when endogenously chosen) is not more likely under high conflict, and that cooperation declines more rapidly under high conflict. The lack of efficiency or payoff analysis means this paper should be used for context and mechanism, not for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in fixed groups of 4 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 5 rounds per block; Study 2: 10 rounds per block. Both baseline and institutional choice blocks.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No general chat or communication allowed. In the gossip/ostracism condition, participants can send notes about others, but this is a structured gossip mechanism, not free chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can contribute any amount from 0 to 10 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is manipulated: 0.75 (low conflict) vs 0.33 (high conflict).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: 1 MU cost to punisher per deduction point; deduction point reduces target's earnings by 3 MUs. Gossip/ostracism: 1 MU cost per exclusion point. Centralized punishment: cost is via contributions to a fund.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: 1 MU cost yields 3 MU deduction. Gossip/ostracism: 1 MU cost per exclusion point; most-excluded player is removed for one round. Centralized punishment: if fund threshold is met, low contributors are deducted 15 MUs.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is implemented in the experiment; only punishment and ostracism/gossip are available. Rewards are discussed in literature review.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds in advance (5 or 10, depending on study/block).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants receive information on each member's contribution and earnings in the PGG.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants only learn the total number of deduction points received, not who assigned them. In gossip/ostracism, gossip notes are anonymous and exclusion is anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus as a main outcome, so it cannot directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, beliefs, norms, institutional choice), not payoff-based.
- Punishment institutions are endogenously chosen by group vote, and are not always implemented, limiting the ability to compare efficiency with and without punishment.
- No reward mechanisms are implemented, so the study cannot inform about reward effects.
- The study is conducted in a decontextualized online lab setting, which may limit external validity.
- No communication or chat is allowed except for structured gossip in the ostracism condition.
