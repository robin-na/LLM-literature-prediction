# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: An Experimental Investigation of Electoral Delegation and the Provision of Public Goods
Authors: Hamman, J. R., Weber, R. A., & Woon, J.
Journal: American Journal of Political Science
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group-level contributions to the public good (as % of endowment)
- frequency of full contribution vectors (social optimum)
- individual and group payoffs (tokens earned)
- aggregate welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocator selection and reelection rates
- frequency of majority tyranny and stage game outcomes
- opt-in rates to delegation
- communication content (collectivist vs individualist speech)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group-level contributions (as a share of the endowment), which directly map to efficiency, and the frequency of achieving the social optimum. Individual and group payoffs are reported in tokens. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures such as voting, opt-in, and communication patterns.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Electoral delegation, where a group elects a member to allocate contributions in a linear public goods game, results in sustained high efficiency and group payoffs, with most groups achieving or nearly achieving the social optimum. In contrast, standard voluntary contribution games (with or without communication) see contributions and efficiency decline to near zero. Communication boosts efficiency, but only when paired with delegation does it sustain high contributions. When delegation is endogenous (players can opt in), high efficiency is only achieved if communication is allowed, as it facilitates coordination and trust. The main mechanism is the selection of prosocial allocators and the ability to sanction selfish ones. Majority tyranny (where a coalition exploits a minority) is observed but infrequent. The results show that democratic delegation can overcome the free-rider problem and lead to efficient public goods provision.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a low MPCR and large groups, introducing a democratic delegation mechanism (electoral selection of a central allocator) dramatically increases efficiency compared to standard voluntary contribution mechanisms. Communication further enhances this effect, especially when delegation is endogenous. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of electoral delegation (even without explicit punishment) can substitute for or outperform punishment in achieving high efficiency, especially when communication is allowed. The control efficiency (voluntary contribution) is very low in this design, but efficiency with delegation is near the social optimum. The results are most applicable to settings with large groups, low MPCR, and centralized decision-making via election.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 9 players were used in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Part 1: 5 periods; Part 2: at least 15 periods, then continuation with 0.75 probability per period (indefinite horizon).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Preplay electronic chat (up to 90 seconds) was allowed in some treatments. Communication increased efficiency, especially when combined with delegation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous from 0 to 100 tokens (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.15 (k=1.35, n=9).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told there would be at least 15 periods in Part 2, then continuation with 0.75 probability per period (indefinite horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects saw individual allocations by each group member, total public good contributions, and their own earnings.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or reward mechanisms are present, so results may not generalize to settings where explicit sanctions are possible.
- The delegation mechanism is not a punishment institution, but a form of centralized decision-making; effects may differ from standard punishment treatments.
- All groups are of size 9, so results may not generalize to smaller or much larger groups.
- MPCR is low (0.15), which may limit generalizability to games with higher returns to cooperation.
- Laboratory setting with student subjects may not capture all dynamics of real-world public goods provision.
- Endogenous delegation only achieves high efficiency when communication is allowed; without communication, efficiency remains low.
