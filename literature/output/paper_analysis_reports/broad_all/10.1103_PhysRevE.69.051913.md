## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that in a two-species repeated cooperation game with differential adaptation rates, the slower-adapting species (host) evolves to provide just enough cooperation to keep the faster-adapting species (symbiont) maximally cooperative. This 'miser-slave' dynamic leads to highly asymmetric payoffs: hosts receive nearly all the benefit, while symbionts receive very little, even though they are highly cooperative. The total group payoff (efficiency) is much less than the maximum possible (full mutual cooperation), because the host withholds cooperation. The model provides a theoretical explanation for observed asymmetries in natural symbioses, where hosts benefit much more than symbionts. The findings are robust across a range of cost-benefit ratios and are supported by both analytic and simulation results.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a theoretical mechanism showing that, in repeated cooperation games with contingent strategies and differential adaptation rates, the introduction of a contingent response (analogous to punishment or reward) by one party can lead to highly asymmetric efficiency outcomes. Specifically, if the 'punishing' party adapts more slowly, it can extract maximal cooperation from the other party while contributing minimally itself, resulting in low overall efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the structure of contingent responses and adaptation rates can strongly moderate the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms. However, the model is not a standard public goods game, and the 'punishment' is implemented as a contingent reward/withholding rather than explicit costly punishment. Thus, while the findings are informative about the potential for punishment/contingency to reduce efficiency via asymmetry, direct quantitative predictions for PGGs with explicit punishment should be made with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model involves two types: 100 hosts, each with 25 symbionts. Interactions are pairwise between a host and each of its symbionts, not a group public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 100 host generations, with 25 symbiont generations per host generation. Within each host, symbionts interact repeatedly with the host.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cooperation levels are continuous between 0 and 1 for both host and symbiont.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The benefit-to-cost ratio (b/c) is a key parameter; both cost and benefit are specified for each party.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Host can reward symbiont by increasing its own cooperation in response to symbiont cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to host per unit of cooperation is parameterized (c_h).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Benefit to symbiont per unit of host cooperation is parameterized (b_{h→s}).

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a two-species repeated dyadic cooperation game.
- Punishment is not explicit or costly; instead, the host's contingent cooperation functions as a form of selective reward/withholding.
- The model assumes hosts can respond to individual symbionts, which may not always be biologically realistic.
- Population sizes are fixed and do not fluctuate, which may affect generalizability.
- The inheritance regime is simplified (no vertical transmission of symbionts), which may understate the ease of evolving cooperation.
- Findings are theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.
