# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: An Experimental Test of the Deterrence Hypothesis
Authors: Schildberg-Hörisch, H., & Strassmair, C.
Journal: Journal of Law Economics & Organization
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount taken (by B from A)
- frequency of taking everything
- frequency of taking nothing
- adaptation of taking behavior across treatments
- hysteresis/crowding out of fairness
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount taken by B from A, interpreted as a measure of 'crime' or norm violation. No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported, as the game is not a public goods game and does not aggregate group surplus in the same way. The study focuses on behavioral responses to punishment incentives, not on efficiency or total welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that introducing small or intermediate punishment incentives can increase the amount stolen compared to no punishment, contrary to the deterrence hypothesis. Only very strong punishment incentives reduce stealing. The effect is driven by heterogeneity: 'selfish' subjects respond to incentives as predicted, but 'fair-minded' subjects increase stealing when incentives are introduced, possibly due to crowding out of fairness. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, as the design is not a public goods game but a dyadic stealing game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment on norm-violating behavior (stealing) is non-monotonic: small and intermediate punishments can increase violations, while only strong punishments deter. However, because the design is not a public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff, its direct value for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments is limited. The main lesson is that punishment can backfire under some conditions, especially when intrinsic motivation is present, but the mapping to efficiency outcomes in multi-player PGGs is indirect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by two players: A (passive) and B (active, can steal).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays two rounds (two treatments), but each round is a one-shot game with a new partner (perfect stranger design).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; decisions are made in anonymity.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The amount to steal is continuous: B can take any amount from 0 to 90 (A's endowment). Not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed fine (f) deducted from B if caught; cost is to the punished, not to a punisher (no peer punishment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic: with probability p, B is caught and pays fine f. The effectiveness is the expected value of p*f.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the structure: two rounds, each with a new partner, and which part will be paid is randomly selected.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or a close variant; it is a dyadic stealing game with no group surplus or efficiency measure.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual behavioral outcomes (amount stolen).
- Punishment is imposed by the experimenter, not by peers, so peer punishment dynamics are not tested.
- The mapping from these results to efficiency effects in multi-player public goods games is indirect and may not generalize.
- The study uses a student sample in a laboratory setting, which may limit external validity.
- No analysis of repeated interaction or long-term group dynamics.
