# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When Punishment Doesn't Pay: Cold Glow and Decisions to Punish
Authors: Ouss, A., & Peysakhovich, A.
Journal: Journal of Law & Economics
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate punishment cost (public pot depletion)
- individual and group earnings (reported in passing)
- comparison to Beckerian optimal deterrence (theoretical benchmark)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned (number of rounds or penalty units)
- frequency of taking (norm violation)
- maximum acceptable possible penalty (MAPP) for takers
- crowding out of punishment
- fairness judgments
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: punishment assigned, taking behavior, and willingness to punish. Payoff/efficiency is discussed in relation to theoretical benchmarks (Beckerian optimum), but not directly measured as group efficiency or total welfare in the main results. Some aggregate cost/welfare implications are discussed, but not as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across a series of lab experiments, the authors show that individual punishment decisions are driven by 'cold glow' (private satisfaction from punishing) rather than by optimizing social efficiency. When punishment costs are shared by the group, individuals assign higher punishments than when they pay the cost themselves, resulting in over-punishment and excessive social cost. When the probability of apprehension is low, punishers do not increase punishment to compensate, so deterrence is insufficient and social efficiency is not achieved. When multiple punishers can assign punishment, their decisions are not crowded out by others' punishments, leading to excessive total punishment. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, taking behavior), but the implications for efficiency are discussed in terms of deviation from optimal deterrence and social cost.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on institutional details: (1) If punishment costs are externalized (group pays), enabling punishment can reduce efficiency due to over-punishment and excessive social cost. (2) If the probability of apprehension is low, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency because punishers do not adjust their behavior, leading to under-deterrence. (3) When multiple punishers are present, lack of crowding out can lead to excessive total punishment and lower efficiency. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effect is moderated by cost structure, probability of apprehension, and the number of punishers. For prediction, the presence of group-funded punishment, low apprehension probability, or multiple punishers should be expected to reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Experiment 1: groups of 8-12; Experiment 2: groups of 3; Experiment 3: groups of 4. The number of punishers (one vs. multiple) is directly manipulated in Experiment 3, showing that multiple punishers can lead to excessive total punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 20 rounds; One-Round Take condition: 1 round; Experiment 2 and 3: one-shot games. The number of rounds is manipulated to test for deterrence and cold-glow motives.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (taking) is a binary choice: take or not take. Punishment is assigned in discrete units (rounds or penalty units).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but the social return to not taking is positive: taking benefits the taker by 2 or 20/30 points and harms another by 3 or 30/40 points, plus reduces the public pot. The marginal per-capita return is not explicitly calculated but can be inferred from the payoff structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is manipulated: in Private, punisher pays 2 MU per round; in Public, group pays 5 MU per round; in other experiments, assigner pays 1 point per 5 or 10 points of punishment. Lower private cost (or externalized cost) leads to higher punishment and lower efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is exclusion for rounds or deduction of points. The effectiveness per unit cost is not systematically varied, but the impact per unit is described (e.g., 1 round of exclusion, 10 points per penalty unit).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in advance (e.g., 20 rounds, 1 round, or one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players are informed of their own and others' actions in some respects (e.g., assigners and punished know the punishment assigned), but assigners do not know the identity of the punished. No evidence of full group summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: assigners do not know the identity of the punished, and punished do not know the identity of the assigner.

## Important Limitations
- The experiments are not standard public goods games but rather 'taking' games with punishment; thus, results are adjacent but not exact for PGG environments.
- Efficiency and payoff outcomes are discussed in relation to theoretical benchmarks (Beckerian optimum) rather than directly measured as group efficiency or total welfare.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, taking behavior), not direct measures of group payoff or efficiency.
- The lab setting may not capture all complexities of real-world punishment or cooperation.
- Sample sizes are moderate, and participants are mostly university students or online workers, which may limit generalizability.
