# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Accountability as a Warrant for Trust: An Experiment on Sanctions and Justifications in a Trust Game
Authors: Herne, K., Lappalainen, O., Setälä, M., & Ylisalo, J.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- sender earnings
- responder earnings
- net earnings after punishment
- gross earnings before punishment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount sent by senders
- amount returned by responders
- punishment points assigned
- content of justifications
- reciprocity in allocations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (payoff), as well as behavioral measures (contributions, returns, punishments, justifications). Efficiency is directly addressed via net earnings, especially in relation to punishment costs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment used a 2x2 design (punishment/no punishment x justification/no justification) in a three-player trust game (two senders, one responder, repeated stranger matching, 6 rounds). The opportunity for senders to punish responders (at a cost: 1 point spent reduces responder's payoff by 3) did not significantly increase the amounts sent or returned, nor did it improve group efficiency. In fact, net earnings for responders were lower in punishment treatments due to the cost of punishment, and group efficiency was not improved. Justification requirements (responders must explain their choices) increased contributions and returns, but this effect was not robust when controlling for the amount received. The combination of punishment and justification did not outperform justification alone in terms of efficiency. The main effect of punishment was to reduce responder payoffs via costly sanctions, not to increase group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a trust-game-like environment with costly punishment (punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment does not increase group efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of punishment. The design is adjacent to public goods games (not a standard PGG, but a trust game with two senders and one responder, continuous contributions, no communication, 6 rounds, known horizon, no rewards). The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be lower than or similar to the control (no punishment), unless the punishment is costless or much more effective. The paper also shows that justification requirements can increase contributions, but this does not translate into higher efficiency when punishment is present. Thus, for prediction, enabling costly punishment in similar trust or PGG-adjacent games is likely to reduce or not improve efficiency relative to control.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players (2 senders, 1 responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 6 rounds; number of rounds is known to participants.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed except for required justifications by responders in relevant treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-12 points), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point sent is tripled (multiplier = 3), but since there are two senders and one responder, the marginal per-capita return is not standard PGG. For each sender, 1 point sent yields 3 points to the responder, who may return any amount.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: 1 point spent by sender reduces responder's payoff by 3 points. This cost structure is central to the finding that punishment reduces efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point spent by sender reduces responder's payoff by 3 points (punishment multiplier = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No explicit reward mechanism; only punishment is available. However, responders can return points to senders, which is the core of the trust game.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed in advance that there are 6 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all group members are shown all decisions made in that round (amounts sent, returned, punishments assigned, and justifications if applicable).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishments are assigned individually and responders can see the amount punished by each sender, but identities remain anonymous (no personal identification).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a trust game with two senders and one responder, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is only from senders to responder, not peer-to-peer as in many PGGs.
- Sample size per treatment is moderate (54 subjects per cell), limiting statistical power.
- No communication or reputation effects beyond required justifications; groups are reshuffled each round.
- No explicit reward mechanism; only punishment is studied.
- Efficiency results are specific to the punishment cost/impact structure used (1:3).
- Findings may not generalize to settings with different punishment or reward technologies, or with more/less rounds.
