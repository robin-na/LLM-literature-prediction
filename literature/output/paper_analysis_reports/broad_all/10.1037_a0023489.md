# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reward, Punishment, and Cooperation: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Balliet, D., Mulder, L. B., & Van Lange, P. A. M.
Journal: Psychological Bulletin
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- contribution rate
Outcome notes:
The meta-analysis focuses on cooperation and contribution as the main dependent variables, measured as the degree of cooperation in social dilemmas. Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are discussed in the text but are not the primary outcomes in the meta-analytic effect sizes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The meta-analysis finds that both punishment and reward robustly increase cooperation in public goods and related social dilemma games. The effect of punishment is stronger when it is costly to the punisher and when participants interact repeatedly with the same group (partner matching). Decentralized punishment is more effective than centralized punishment, but this is largely explained by the correlation with costliness. Group size and cost-to-fine ratio do not significantly moderate the effect. While the main outcomes are cooperation rates, the paper discusses that punishment can reduce group efficiency in the short run (due to punishment costs) but may increase efficiency in the long run as cooperation stabilizes and punishment use declines. The effect of punishment varies by country, with some countries (e.g., Russia) showing no positive effect. The paper does not provide meta-analytic estimates of efficiency or group payoff, but references to primary studies suggest that efficiency effects are context-dependent and may be negative in the short term and positive in the long term.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods and similar social dilemma games, enabling punishment increases cooperation rates, especially when punishment is costly and the game is repeated with fixed partners. However, the paper does not provide direct meta-analytic estimates of efficiency or group payoff, which are the target outcomes for the prediction task. The discussion and references to primary studies suggest that efficiency may initially decrease when punishment is introduced (due to punishment costs), but can increase over time as cooperation rises and punishment use declines. For prediction, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be more positive in longer, repeated games with partner matching and costly punishment, but may be less positive or even negative in short or one-shot games, or when punishment is free or centralized. The lack of direct efficiency outcomes in the meta-analysis limits the precision of quantitative prediction for efficiency, but the qualitative patterns are informative for understanding moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Group size (player count) was coded and analyzed as a moderator, with a range from 2 to 16 (mode = 4), but was not found to significantly moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds (iterations) was coded and analyzed. Punishment is more effective in iterated games, especially with partner matching. The effect increases with more iterations, though the meta-regression is only marginally significant.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation when it is costly to the punisher. Costly punishment (vs. free) is a significant moderator.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Cost-to-fine ratio (punishment effectiveness per unit cost) was coded and analyzed, but was not found to significantly moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Reward is analyzed as a separate treatment in the meta-analysis, but not as a coexisting option in the same game as punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Costly rewards are more effective at increasing cooperation than free rewards.

## Important Limitations
- The meta-analysis focuses on cooperation and contribution rates, not directly on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Efficiency is discussed only in the narrative and not meta-analyzed.
- There are fewer studies on rewards than on punishment, reducing statistical power for moderator analyses involving rewards.
- Some studies were excluded due to missing data necessary for effect size calculation.
- Group size variation is limited to small groups (2-16, mode 4), so findings may not generalize to larger groups.
- Cultural variation is substantial; in some countries (e.g., Russia), punishment does not increase cooperation or efficiency.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and may be negative in the short term due to punishment costs.
- No direct analysis of game design features such as chat, all-or-nothing choices, default contribution framing, MPCR, or information feedback.
