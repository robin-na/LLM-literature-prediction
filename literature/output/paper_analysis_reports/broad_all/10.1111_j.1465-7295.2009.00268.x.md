## Source

Authors: Carpenter, J., & Seki, E.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2011

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for field outcomes is fishing productivity (kg per trip). In the experimental game, both group total contribution and individual earnings are reported. Non-payoff outcomes include behavioral measures from the experiment (contribution, disapproval, etc.) and survey-based social preference measures.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study uses a field experiment with fishermen and students, employing a repeated public goods game (VCM) with a costly, non-material 'social disapproval' mechanism (unhappy face) in the last five rounds. Pooling fishermen (who share catch and income) are more productive in real life, and this is linked to higher social preference measures from the experiment. The social disapproval mechanism increases contributions among poolers, and social preference measures (conditional cooperation, propensity to disapprove, response to disapproval) are significant predictors of both experimental contributions and real-world fishing productivity. The effect of social preferences on productivity is substantial, and the productivity advantage of pooling boats is largely explained by these preferences. The 'punishment' in the experiment is not material, so the findings are most directly about social (rather than monetary) sanctions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that social preference mechanisms, even when implemented as costly but non-material signals (social disapproval), can increase both experimental and real-world efficiency in public-goods-like environments. However, the 'punishment' here is not material: it is a costly signal with no direct monetary penalty. The findings suggest that enabling even non-material, costly social sanctions can increase efficiency, especially among groups with strong social ties or cooperative institutions. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social disapproval mechanism (even without material punishment) is likely to increase efficiency, particularly in groups with pre-existing cooperative norms. The results are most applicable to settings where punishment is non-material but salient, and less directly informative about material punishment effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experimental groups were always 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per group: 5 standard VCM, 5 with social disapproval.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0-10 coins per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.5 (each coin contributed is doubled and split among 4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to send a social disapproval signal is 10 yen (1 coin) per unhappy face.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a costly, non-material signal (unhappy face); no direct monetary penalty to recipient.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told there would be 10 rounds, but were not told about the rule change until after round 5.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the group total contribution and individual contributions in random order (anonymized).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (unhappy face) is anonymous; recipients see only the number of signals, not who sent them.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is non-material: it is a costly signal (unhappy face) with no direct monetary penalty, so results may not generalize to material punishment settings.
- Sample size is small (53 participants, with 27 fishermen split into two groups), limiting statistical power and generalizability.
- The field context (fishermen with strong pre-existing social ties and real-world team production) may not generalize to more anonymous or less cohesive groups.
- No direct comparison to a material punishment treatment; only social disapproval is tested.
- The experiment does not manipulate the presence/absence of punishment across groups; all groups experience both baseline and social disapproval rounds.
