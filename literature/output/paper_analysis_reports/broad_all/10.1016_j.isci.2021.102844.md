# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Combination of institutional incentives for cooperative governance of risky commons
Authors: Sun, W. W., Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., Szolnoki, A., & Vasconcelos, V. V.
Journal: Iscience
Year: 2021

## Study Type
theory; empirical subtype=N/A; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group achievement (fraction of groups that succeed in achieving collective targets)
- institution prevalence
Non-payoff outcomes:
- evolutionary dynamics of strategy frequencies (cooperators, defectors, executors)
- average fine on defectors
- average reward on cooperators and executors
Outcome notes:
The main outcome is 'average group achievement', defined as the fraction of groups that reach the collective target. This is a behavioral outcome related to cooperation, not a direct measure of efficiency or total group payoff. The paper does not report group efficiency as the ratio of actual to maximal possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in collective-risk social dilemma games, institutional pure reward strategies (where executors only reward cooperators) are more effective than pure punishment or hybrid strategies in promoting group achievement (i.e., the fraction of groups that reach the collective target). This result holds for both fixed and flexible incentive schemes, and is robust to changes in risk level, mutation rate, and enhancement factor. Local sanctioning schemes are more effective than global ones. The main outcome is group achievement, not efficiency or total group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency effects is not possible from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. It suggests that, in collective-risk social dilemmas, institutional punishment alone is less effective than reward or hybrid schemes in promoting group achievement (a proxy for cooperation). However, because the main outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, but rather the fraction of groups reaching a behavioral target, the findings should be used with caution for efficiency prediction. The game structure (collective-risk, institutional incentives, evolutionary dynamics) is close but not identical to standard PGGs. The results suggest that institutional punishment may not increase efficiency as much as reward, but do not provide quantitative efficiency estimates.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main model uses group size N=4 (see parameter values in figure captions), but population size Z=100. The group size is fixed in the main results.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost-to-benefit ratio c/b=0.1 is used (c=0.1, b=1), but the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is not explicitly named. The structure is similar to PGG with a threshold for group success.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost parameter is given as π_t=0.03 (cost to executor for punishing).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness parameter π_e=0.3 (impact per unit cost) is specified.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is a central focus; executors can reward cooperators.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is not given a separate symbol, but is part of the institutional incentive scheme; see Methods for details.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the institutional parameters; see Methods.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is group achievement (fraction of groups reaching the target), not efficiency or total group payoff.
- The model is theoretical and evolutionary, not empirical or experimental.
- The game is a collective-risk social dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or welfare outcomes.
- Institutional incentives are modeled; peer-to-peer incentives and other mechanisms are not analyzed.
- Structured populations and information/observation costs are not considered.
