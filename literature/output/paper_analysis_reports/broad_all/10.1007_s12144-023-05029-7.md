# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Altruistic punishment in intergroup context
Authors: Ünal-Kocaslan, O., & Akgün, S.
Journal: Current Psychology
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount spent on punishment (altruistic punishment)
- emotional responses (anger, guilt, feeling good)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount participants spend to punish norm violators in a third-party punishment game. No group or total payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. Emotional responses are also measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Participants are willing to engage in costly punishment of unfair resource allocations, supporting the concept of altruistic punishment. The amount spent on punishment is higher for unfair than fair allocations. Ingroup favoritism is observed in that participants punish more harshly when the victim is an ingroup member, but there is no effect of the perpetrator's group identity. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on punishment behavior and emotional responses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that costly punishment is robustly observed in third-party punishment games, especially when unfairness is directed at ingroup members. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it uses a third-party punishment game with a dictator-like allocation, not a standard PGG. The findings are most useful for understanding when and how punishment is deployed, not its effect on group efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always played with 3 players: distributor (A), recipient (B), and third-party punisher (C).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 20 rounds (16 unfair, 4 fair). Study 2: 8 rounds (4 unfair, 4 fair).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment choice is continuous: participants can choose any amount between 0 and 20 units/tickets to spend on punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 unit/ticket to reduce the perpetrator's payoff by 3 units (Study 1: 1 unit spent removes 3 from A; Study 2: 1 ticket spent removes 3 from A).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each unit spent by punisher reduces perpetrator's payoff by 3 units).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds in advance (Study 1: 20 rounds; Study 2: 8 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the allocation decision of player A and the group membership of A and B before making their punishment decision.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; no evidence that punished players can identify the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior and emotional responses are measured.
- The game is a third-party punishment game, not a standard public goods game, so findings may not generalize directly to PGG environments.
- Data were collected remotely due to the COVID-19 pandemic, so experimental control over participants' environments was limited.
- Punishment was the only behavioral option; participants could not reward or compensate, which may have influenced responses.
- The endowment used for punishment was relatively small and symbolic (money units or raffle tickets), which may limit external validity.
- Potential demand characteristics: participants may have behaved in line with perceived experimental expectations.
