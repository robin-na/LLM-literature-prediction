# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation in social dilemmas: Free riding may be thwarted by second-order reward rather than by punishment
Authors: Kiyonari, T., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and amount of punishment assigned
- frequency and amount of reward assigned
- contribution/cooperation rates
- evaluations of other players (trustworthiness, cooperativeness, generosity, likability, goodness, dependability)
Outcome notes:
The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. All main outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward given, cooperation, and social evaluations).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that, in one-shot public goods games, participants are much more likely to support cooperation through reward (both first- and second-order) than through punishment. First-order punishment of defectors occurs, but second-order punishment of nonpunishers is rare or absent, and punishers are not socially endorsed. In contrast, both first- and second-order rewards are common, and rewarders are socially endorsed. The presence of both punishment and reward options does not increase support for punishment. The paper does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, four-player public goods games with binary contribution and surprise sanctioning opportunities, punishment is not socially supported at the second order, and reward is more likely to be used and socially endorsed. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The behavioral results suggest that, in similar game designs, punishment may not be self-sustaining and may not improve efficiency unless supported by other mechanisms. The findings are most relevant for predicting the likelihood of punishment and reward behaviors, not their direct impact on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments used four-person groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were one-shot (single round) with subsequent surprise sanctioning stages.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was not permitted between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was binary: contribute full endowment or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each C$5 contribution was doubled and divided among 4 players: MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: for each $1 spent, $3 was deducted from the target.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: $1 spent removes $3 from target (3:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward was available in some conditions; in Study 3, both reward and punishment were available.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost: for each $1 spent, $3 was added to the target.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: $1 spent gives $3 to target (3:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was one-shot; sanctioning opportunities were a surprise but the number of rounds was clear.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown the contributions and sanctioning decisions of other players (by code name).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were identified by code names; sanctioning actions were attributed to specific code-named players.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main outcomes are behavioral.
- All games are one-shot (no repeated interaction), which may limit generalizability to repeated or real-world settings.
- The sample consists of undergraduate students in a laboratory setting, which may not generalize to other populations.
- Sanctioning opportunities were a surprise and not anticipated by participants, which may affect behavior compared to settings where punishment/reward is expected.
- The design uses preprogrammed computer players rather than real human group interaction, which may affect ecological validity.
- The effect of punishment on actual group efficiency or surplus is not measured or reported.
