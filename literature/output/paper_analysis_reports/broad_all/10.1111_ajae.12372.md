Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are theoretical steady states of cooperation (share of compliers) and resource stock, with some discussion of payoffs via the difference between social and private extraction. Efficiency is discussed in terms of achieving the socially optimal steady state, but explicit efficiency ratios or group payoffs are not the primary dependent variable. The empirical calibration is used for parameterization, not for direct outcome measurement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that social network structure (cohesiveness) and the severity of the resource dilemma jointly determine the effectiveness of informal (social pressure) enforcement in promoting cooperation. Network-oriented policies can reduce the minimum share of compliers needed for full cooperation, but only up to 26% in the calibrated case. Legal enforcement (fines/subsidies) can complement informal enforcement, but subsidizing the costs of social pressure may eliminate stable partial cooperation. The number of agents has only minor effects unless changed drastically. The model suggests that policy effectiveness depends on initial conditions (stock, share of compliers, network structure) and that mixed enforcement strategies should be applied cautiously, as they may interact in non-additive ways.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment (here, social pressure) in public-goods-like common resource games depend on network cohesiveness, the perceived severity of the dilemma, and the cost structure of punishment. The model suggests that punishment can increase the basin of attraction for cooperation and reduce the critical mass of compliers needed, but the effect is bounded. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency (move the system toward the socially optimal steady state), but the magnitude of the effect is limited by network structure and agents' perceptions. The findings are most relevant for settings with costly, decentralized punishment and binary contribution choices, and less so for standard lab PGGs with explicit monetary punishment. The results are qualitative and parameter-dependent, not direct empirical estimates.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The model allows for arbitrary (large) n, and sensitivity analysis varies the number of agents. The number of agents has only minor effects on equilibrium outcomes unless changed drastically.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is dynamic and continuous-time, with repeated binary choices until a steady state or resource depletion. There is no fixed number of discrete rounds as in standard PGGs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents make a binary choice each period: comply (socially optimal extraction) or defect (privately optimal extraction).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is implicit in the difference between social and private extraction payoffs. Sensitivity analysis varies the 'defector's extra benefit', which is analogous to MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The cost to compliers of exerting social pressure is a key parameter, varied in the model. High costs can stabilize partial cooperation; subsidizing costs can eliminate such equilibria.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The effectiveness of social pressure (maximum impact) is parameterized and varied. Higher maximum social pressure increases cooperation, but only up to a point.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Legal enforcement can include subsidies (rewards) for compliance, but the main focus is on fines and social pressure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Agents are assumed to have perfect information about their neighbors' actions (monitoring), which supports social pressure.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical, with numerical calibration but no direct experimental or field data on actual efficiency or group payoffs.
- The model is adjacent to, but not an exact public goods game: it is a common property resource extraction game with binary compliance choices and dynamic resource stock.
- Punishment is modeled as decentralized, costly social pressure, not as explicit monetary punishment as in standard PGG experiments.
- Efficiency is discussed in terms of steady-state outcomes and critical mass of compliers, not as explicit group payoff ratios.
- Findings are parameter-dependent and qualitative; quantitative predictions for other settings require careful mapping of model parameters.
- No direct analysis of communication, reward cost/effectiveness, or identification of punishers.
- Empirical calibration is based on a specific aquifer case and may not generalize to other resource or PGG contexts.
