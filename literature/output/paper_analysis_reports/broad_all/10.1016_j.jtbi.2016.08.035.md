The main dependent variable is the average payoff of different strategies (linkage vs non-linkage), and whether a strategy is evolutionarily stable (i.e., can resist invasion by others). The study uses mathematical analysis and agent-based simulations, not human experiments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that a linkage strategy—where defectors in a social dilemma are excluded from indirect reciprocity benefits—can be evolutionarily stable and maintain high group efficiency, but only when there are implementation errors in the social dilemma and no perception errors. In the absence of errors, linkage and non-linkage strategies are neutral, allowing non-linkage strategies to drift in and eventually enable defectors to invade, collapsing cooperation and efficiency. Perception errors (misjudging others' actions) undermine the stability and efficiency of linkage strategies, as they disrupt the ability to assortatively cooperate and exclude defectors. The results highlight that the effectiveness of exclusionary sanctions (linkage) for maintaining efficiency is highly sensitive to the type and prevalence of errors in the system.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-like exclusion mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that exclusionary sanctions (linkage) can increase efficiency, but only if players can reliably observe and agree on others' actions (i.e., low perception error) and if there is some noise in implementation (so that linkage strategies can distinguish themselves from non-linkage cooperators). If perception errors are common, the efficiency benefit of punishment/exclusion is undermined. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is implemented as exclusion from future benefits rather than direct costly punishment, and where the information environment is well-specified. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with explicit costly punishment, but the mechanisms and moderators (error types, information) are highly relevant for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations and models use group size n=100; analysis discusses effects in large groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 'unit' consists of a one-shot SD game and 20 rounds of indirect reciprocity; 100 units per simulation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution in the social dilemma is binary: cooperate or defect.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In SD, cooperation costs C=5 and creates benefit B=10 shared among n-1=99 others; so mpcr = 10/99 ≈ 0.101.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as exclusion from indirect reciprocity, not as direct costly punishment; effectiveness depends on information accuracy.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the structure: 1 SD game + 20 IR rounds per unit, 100 units per simulation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Behavioral histories in both stages are known to all group members (except when perception errors are modeled).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know who is excluded (i.e., who is not helped in IR) based on public reputational information.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not an empirical experiment with human subjects.
- Punishment is implemented as exclusion from indirect reciprocity, not as explicit costly punishment as in standard PGGs.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation choices.
- The effect of perception errors is modeled, but the realism of error rates and their mapping to real-world settings is uncertain.
- Findings may not generalize to environments with explicit costly punishment or different information structures.
- Only strategies based on the Standing strategy in indirect reciprocity are considered; other possible strategies may yield different results.
