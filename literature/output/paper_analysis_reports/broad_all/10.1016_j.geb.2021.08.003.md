## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares a standard two-player public goods game (PG) to three pledge-and-review (P&R) variants: fixed disagreement payoffs (F), heterogeneous/certainty (C), and uncertainty (U). In all P&R treatments, after pledges are made, both players must vote to accept the pledges for them to be implemented; otherwise, both receive a disagreement payoff. Efficiency (average payoffs as a fraction of the full cooperation benchmark) is significantly higher in all P&R treatments than in the control PG game. The efficiency gain is persistent in the fixed disagreement payoff treatment, with no decline over 20 rounds, while some decline is observed in the other P&R treatments. There is no significant difference in efficiency between the three P&R variants. The mechanism allows conditional cooperators to avoid the typical decline in cooperation seen in repeated public goods games. The P&R institution functions as a form of second-party punishment (via voting down low pledges), but with endogenous costs and without explicit costly punishment tokens.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that adding a pledge-and-review (P&R) mechanism—where players can veto low contributions after seeing pledges—substantially increases efficiency in a two-player, 20-round public goods game with continuous contributions and no communication. The effect is robust to variations in disagreement payoffs (fixed, heterogeneous, uncertain). For prediction, if the control (PG) efficiency is known, enabling a P&R-style review stage is likely to increase efficiency by about 10% of the full cooperation benchmark, with the effect persisting over repeated rounds (at least in the fixed disagreement payoff case). The P&R mechanism is not identical to standard costly punishment (no explicit punishment tokens), but functions similarly by allowing players to block low contributions at a cost. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, continuous-contribution games without chat, with full feedback, and a review/veto stage after pledges.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use two players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete but not all-or-nothing: players choose from {0,2,4,6,8,10,12}.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.75 (payoff function: U_i = 12 - x_i + 0.75*(x_i + x_j)).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects receive full feedback on their own and their partner's choices and payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the two-player setting, each player knows their partner's identity in the sense of knowing who voted, but no explicit mention of anonymity; likely partners are anonymous but actions are fully revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses only two-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- The punishment mechanism is not explicit; it is implemented via vetoing pledges rather than costly punishment tokens.
- No communication is allowed, so results may not generalize to settings with chat or negotiation.
- The contribution choice is discrete (increments of 2 from 0 to 12), not fully continuous.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may affect external validity.
- No explicit analysis of how results would change with different MPCR values or group sizes.
