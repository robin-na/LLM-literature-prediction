## Source
Title: Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions
Authors: Harrell, A.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings (welfare) after accounting for costs of punishment
- individual earnings
- public good production
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- cooperation rates
- punishment received
- punishment sent
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both contribution/cooperation rates and group/individual earnings (welfare), with explicit analysis of how punishment affects group earnings after accounting for the cost of punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 9 rounds in non-punishment phase, 9 rounds in punishment phase, then a one-shot round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: 0 to 20 MUs per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU contributed is doubled and distributed equally among 4 players. MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 MU to deduct 3 MUs from another participant.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 MU spent deducts 3 MUs from target.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment (deductions) was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told in advance how many rounds they would complete (unknown horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants could view their own and others' contributions and earnings, sorted by unique letter identifier.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the peer punishment condition, participants could see who punished whom. In the leader condition, the punisher was always the leader and was known.

## Important Limitations
- The maximum amount of punishment a participant could receive differed between conditions (10 MUs from the leader vs. up to 30 MUs from peers), which may affect the comparability of punishment impact and welfare destruction.
- The study used only four-person groups, so results may not generalize to larger groups.
- The experiment was conducted in a laboratory setting with anonymous interactions, which may not fully capture real-world dynamics.
- The persistence of leader effects was measured using a similar public good game (one-shot), so some of the effect may be due to behavioral inertia rather than true norm internalization.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so results are limited to punishment-based institutions.
