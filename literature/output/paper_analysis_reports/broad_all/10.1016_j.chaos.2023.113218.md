The paper does not report group payoff, total earnings, or efficiency as defined by the ratio of actual to maximal possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that indirect reciprocity mechanisms based on reputation can strongly promote fairness in the ultimatum game. Specifically, updating responders' reputation via image scoring leads to a very high level of fairness (over 95%), while updating proposers' reputation alone increases fairness above the baseline (no reputation) for many social norms, with a few norms achieving over 80% fairness. When both proposers' and responders' reputations are updated, most social norms yield even higher fairness than updating proposers' reputation alone, but never exceed the effect of updating responders' reputation alone. The results are based on evolutionary game-theoretic modeling and analysis of strategy dynamics, not on empirical or experimental data. The main dependent variable is the frequency of fair offers and rejections of unfair offers, not group payoff or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, adjacent evidence for the prediction task. While it does not study public goods games or efficiency/payoff outcomes directly, it analyzes how indirect reciprocity and reputation mechanisms affect fairness (a behavioral outcome) in the ultimatum game, which is a related but distinct environment. The findings suggest that mechanisms analogous to punishment (here, rejection of unfair offers, i.e., altruistic punishment) can strongly promote norm compliance and fairness, especially when reputation is at stake. However, because the outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game, the results should be used only as weak, indirect evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size is a parameter in the model (e.g., Z=50 in main results), but the main findings are not about varying player count.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes repeated interactions and evolutionary updating, but does not specify a fixed number of rounds per game; instead, it uses steady-state analysis.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) choices are all-or-nothing: proposer chooses between a fair split (equal) or an unfair split (minimal offer).
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Rejection of unfair offers is interpreted as altruistic punishment, which imposes a cost on both proposer and responder (both get nothing). The cost is not parameterized or varied.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection of unfair offers, which always results in zero payoff for both players. No variation in punishment effectiveness.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players (or the population) are informed of others' reputations, which are updated and shared according to the social norm. No mention of other summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The paper studies a simplified ultimatum game, not a public goods game or its direct variants.
- The main outcome is behavioral fairness (frequency of fair offers and rejections), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- All results are theoretical/model-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Punishment is implemented as rejection of unfair offers, which is not directly analogous to costly punishment in PGGs.
- No analysis of how findings would translate to efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games.
