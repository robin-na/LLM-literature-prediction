## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that responsible punishment and cooperation can co-evolve and become stable in populations when interactions are non-anonymous and reputation can be built. In anonymous settings, cooperation and responsible punishment do not emerge, and antisocial punishment can persist. The model provides a threshold for the amount of information (reputation) needed for responsible punishment to be adaptive. The results are robust to errors in reputation and extend to public goods games with more than two players, though larger group sizes raise the threshold for reputation needed. The paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, but the emergence of cooperation and responsible punishment implies higher group welfare than in the absence of these behaviors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency, but only when reputation or non-anonymity is present. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by whether players can observe each other's actions and reputations. In anonymous settings, punishment does not promote cooperation or efficiency. The model also suggests that larger group sizes make it harder for punishment to be effective. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or payoff outcomes, its value for direct quantitative prediction is limited; it is most useful for understanding the qualitative role of reputation, anonymity, and group size as moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Model is primarily pairwise (n=2), but results are claimed to extend to larger group sizes (public goods games). Larger group sizes increase the threshold for reputation needed for responsible punishment and cooperation to evolve, making punishment less effective in promoting cooperation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: donors either cooperate (give benefit b at cost c) or defect (give nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `contextual` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is gamma; varied in analysis. The threshold for reputation needed for responsible punishment depends on gamma.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `contextual` under this dimension. Punishment reduces the donor's payoff by beta per act; both gamma (cost) and beta (impact) are parameters. The threshold for reputation depends on these values.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The key manipulation is whether players can observe others' reputations (i.e., non-anonymity). When reputation is visible, responsible punishment and cooperation emerge.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Non-anonymity means that punishment acts are observable and attributed to individuals, enabling reputation effects.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not report explicit efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- The main model is pairwise; extension to public goods games is discussed but not analyzed in detail in the main text.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation and punishment choices.
- No explicit round structure or temporal dynamics as in repeated games; evolutionary time is used instead.
- No reward mechanism is included in the main analysis.
