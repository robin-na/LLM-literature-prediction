## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games is highly contingent on group type and information. Highly cooperative groups achieve near-optimal payoffs without punishment, and introducing punishment reduces efficiency due to its cost. Uncooperative groups only benefit from severe punishment if they are informed about their own composition and implement the institution early; otherwise, punishment is often misapplied or subverted, leading to low efficiency. Middle cooperators benefit from punishment only in the absence of information about group composition. Information about group cooperativeness allows groups to better match their institution choice to their needs, increasing overall efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment is not uniform and is moderated by group composition and information.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games is not a simple function of the game design dimensions alone, but is critically moderated by group composition (cooperativeness) and information about that composition. For prediction tasks, this means that the same punishment institution can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency depending on whether the group is composed of high, middle, or low cooperators, and whether they know this. In the absence of such information, punishment is often over- or under-used, reducing efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction from this paper are the presence and cost of punishment, the severity/technology of punishment, the availability of information about group composition, and the baseline cooperativeness of the group. Control efficiency (no punishment) is a strong predictor: if control efficiency is high, adding punishment reduces efficiency; if control efficiency is low, adding severe punishment with information can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of comprehension and early institution choice for realizing efficiency gains from punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 5 players in both sorting and institution formation games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Institution formation game consists of 6 phases of 4 rounds each (24 rounds total).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to endowment (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.3 (each token contributed increases group payoff by 0.3 per player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Institutional cost per player: 5 tokens for Mild, 20 tokens for Severe punishment per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Mild: 50 token penalty if <100 contributed; Severe: 90 token penalty if <100 contributed. Severe is more deterrent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of phases and rounds in advance (6 phases, 4 rounds each).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, individual contributions and payoffs of all members are displayed in random order (not linked to identities).

## Important Limitations
- External validity is limited: the experiment uses student samples and artificial group sorting, which may not generalize to real-world groups.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to depend on group composition and information, which are not always observable or manipulable in field settings.
- No informal or peer punishment is studied; only formal, automatic punishment institutions are considered.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are included, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The study does not vary player count, MPCR, or punishment cost/tech across a wide range; results are for a specific parameterization.
- Comprehension and learning effects are important moderators, but may differ in other populations or with different instructions.
