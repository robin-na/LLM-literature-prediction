## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that in optional public goods games, allowing all forms of punishment (including antisocial punishment) leads to the breakdown of the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group efficiency. Punishment does not increase average payoffs or cooperation rates; instead, it is used by all strategy types to protect against invasion, resulting in a 'tragedy of the commons' where costly punishment is widespread and destructive. Only when punishment is weak (low impact per cost) and the public good is highly efficient (high multiplier) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained. Theoretical results are supported by large-scale simulations and are robust to parameter variation. Preliminary behavioral experiments are consistent with the model's predictions, but the main results are theoretical.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the set of available punishment strategies. If only pro-social punishment (cooperators punishing defectors) is allowed, punishment can greatly increase efficiency. However, if antisocial punishment is possible, punishment does not improve efficiency and can even reduce it. The effect is robust across player count, group size, and a wide range of parameters. The efficiency impact of punishment is more positive when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is high and punishment is weak (low punishment impact per cost). For prediction, the presence of antisocial punishment options and the effectiveness of punishment are key moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Theoretical model uses group size n=5 and population size N=100 in main simulations; experiments use groups of 4. The main results are robust to group size, but the effect of punishment on efficiency is not strongly moderated by player count in the reported results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical model is based on repeated evolutionary rounds (Moran process, agent-based simulations). Experiments are one-shot games. The main results are about evolutionary steady-state, not finite repeated games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous in the main model (any amount up to endowment), but Experiment 2 uses discrete contribution levels (0, 10, 20, 30, 40). The main theoretical results are not about all-or-nothing contributions.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is r/n. Theoretical results show that higher r (and thus higher mpcr) increases cooperation and efficiency, and reduces antisocial punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit (gamma) is varied in simulations. The effect of punishment cost is discussed, but the main moderator is punishment effectiveness (beta).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology (beta: impact per unit cost) is a key moderator. High punishment effectiveness (large beta) leads to more antisocial punishment and lower efficiency. Only when punishment is weak (small beta) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Theoretical model is evolutionary and does not have a fixed number of rounds. Experiments are one-shot games; subjects know the game is one-shot.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Players must know others' actions to assign punishment, but the paper does not detail the exact summary information shown. The model assumes information transfer (e.g., gossip) so that loners can punish cooperators.

## Important Limitations
- Main results are theoretical and based on evolutionary models; only preliminary experimental evidence is provided.
- Experiments are one-shot games and may not capture long-term evolutionary dynamics.
- The model assumes perfect information about others' actions for punishment, which may not hold in real-world settings.
- No analysis of reward mechanisms; only punishment is considered.
- The effect of group size (player count) is not systematically varied in the main results.
- The model does not address communication or reputation effects.
