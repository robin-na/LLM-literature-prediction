# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Costs for switching partners reduce network dynamics but not cooperative behaviour
Authors: Bednarik, P., Fehl, K., & Semmann, D.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- link-breaking rate
- reciprocation of defection
- willingness to cooperate with new partners
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games and the frequency of breaking links (partner switching). No explicit group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported, though payoffs are described in the game structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that introducing costs for forming new links in dynamic networks reduces the rate at which participants break links (i.e., switch partners). However, cooperation rates in the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma remain high across all dynamic treatments, regardless of the cost level, and are significantly higher than in static networks where no partner switching is allowed. The ability to break links—even if rarely used—appears to be enough to maintain high cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of these dynamics on efficiency cannot be directly determined.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games with dynamic partner networks, the introduction of costs for switching partners reduces network dynamism but does not reduce cooperation rates. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment or partner-switching mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings suggest that the mere threat of partner switching can sustain cooperation, but the efficiency implications (e.g., total group payoff relative to maximum possible) remain unaddressed.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PD game lasted 30 rounds; participants were not told the duration.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was forbidden except via computers for game actions; no chat or free-form communication allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (ORANGE) or defect (BLUE) in each PD game.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not informed about the duration of the game (unknown endpoint).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown their own and their partners' decisions and payoffs after each round, but not outcomes of partners' interactions with others.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Pseudonyms were used for in-game identification; participants could see which partner broke a link.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, not public goods games; results may not fully generalize to PGG environments.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are present; partner switching is not framed as punishment.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported; only cooperation rates and link-breaking rates are analyzed.
- Partner assignment after link breaking is random; no active partner choice or reputation-based selection.
- Maximum number of partners is fixed at three, which may constrain network effects.
