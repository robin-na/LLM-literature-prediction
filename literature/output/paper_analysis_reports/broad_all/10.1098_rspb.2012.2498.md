## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that social exclusion is a more effective and robust mechanism than costly punishment for sustaining cooperation and high efficiency in public goods games. When excluders can effectively and cheaply exclude defectors, the group can reach and maintain the efficient outcome where all contribute and defectors are eliminated. Costly punishment, even when effective, is vulnerable to invasion by non-punishing cooperators (second-order freeriders), leading to a decline in punishers and eventual dominance by defectors, which collapses efficiency. The model demonstrates that social exclusion can solve both the emergence and maintenance problems of cooperation, provided the cost of exclusion is not too high. The results are robust to changes in group size and to stochastic effects. The paper also notes that if monitoring costs are high, or if exclusion is not self-serving, the advantage of social exclusion disappears.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling a punishment mechanism that takes the form of social exclusion (where defectors are excluded from benefit sharing and the cost of exclusion is not too high) will lead to a large increase in group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across group sizes and does not require repeated interaction or reputation. In contrast, enabling costly punishment (where punishers pay to reduce defectors' payoffs) is less likely to increase efficiency, especially in the long run, due to vulnerability to second-order freeriders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social exclusion mechanism with low cost and high effectiveness should be treated as a strong positive moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment. The specific model parameters (group size, multiplication factor, cost of exclusion/punishment, effectiveness) are all explicitly analyzed, allowing for quantitative predictions if mapped to empirical settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size n is a key parameter in the model; results are shown for n=5, and robustness is checked for n=4 and n=10. The main qualitative results hold across group sizes, but the threshold for exclusion cost becomes more restrictive as n increases.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is r/n, with r>1 and r<n. The model explicitly analyzes how the multiplication factor r and group size n affect payoffs and the threshold for punishment/exclusion effectiveness.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (gamma for costly punishment, bar-gamma for exclusion) is a key parameter. Lower cost increases the effectiveness and stability of punishment/exclusion. High cost undermines both mechanisms.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (beta for costly punishment, bar-beta for exclusion) is explicitly modeled. Higher effectiveness increases the ability of punishment/exclusion to sustain cooperation and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Only the self-serving aspect of social exclusion is modeled; exclusion that is costly and not self-serving (i.e., does not increase the excluder's payoff) is not analyzed.
- The model assumes perfect detection of defectors at no or little cost; if monitoring is costly, the advantage of exclusion disappears.
- The model does not include repeated games, reputation, or group selection, which may be important in real-world settings.
- The evolutionary model assumes infinite population and continuous time, which may not map directly to finite, discrete experimental settings.
