# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Information transparency and equilibrium selection in coordination games: an experimental study
Authors: Liu, J., & Riyanto, Y. E.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- beliefs about opponent's cooperation
- frequency of mutual cooperation (payoff-dominant equilibrium)
- frequency of mutual defection (risk-dominant equilibrium)
- frequency of disequilibrium outcomes
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rate, beliefs, and the distribution of equilibrium outcomes. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings as primary outcomes, though mutual cooperation is the payoff-dominant equilibrium.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Revealing information about the existence and structure of centralized punishment or reward mechanisms in a stag hunt game increases the rate of mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant equilibrium) compared to a control where only final payoffs are shown. The effect is stronger for punishment than for reward. The mechanism works both by increasing beliefs in others' cooperation and, for punishment, by directly increasing cooperative choices. In contrast, when the underlying game remains a prisoner's dilemma (i.e., the punishment/reward is too small to change the equilibrium), revealing the mechanism does not increase cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in mutual cooperation implies higher group welfare in the context of the stag hunt game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that, in two-player, repeated stag hunt games with centralized punishment or reward, making the institutional mechanism transparent increases cooperation rates, especially when punishment is highlighted. While efficiency is not directly measured, the increase in mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant outcome) suggests that efficiency would also increase. The results are most relevant for coordination games adjacent to public goods games, rather than standard PGGs. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment and making its mechanism salient is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such information, especially when the punishment is strong enough to change the equilibrium structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) coordination games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 25 real periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/choice is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized and exogenous; a S$2 penalty is imposed on unilateral defection in the punish_stag1 treatment. The cost is not paid by another player but is a reduction in the defector's payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is perfectly effective: a S$2 reduction in the defector's payoff per unilateral defection. No cost to the punisher (since it's centralized).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward exists in the reward_stag2 treatment: S$2 reward for mutual cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is centralized and exogenous; S$2 is added to each player's payoff for mutual cooperation. No cost to another player.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is perfectly effective: S$2 added per player for mutual cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know there are 25 rounds; this is stated in the procedures.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of their opponent's decision at the end of each period. No mention of additional summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a two-player stag hunt (coordination) game, not a public goods game or its direct variant, so results may not generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured or reported; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, beliefs, equilibrium selection) are analyzed.
- Punishment and reward are centralized and exogenous, not peer-administered, so findings may not transfer to decentralized or endogenous punishment settings.
- The effect of punishment/reward is confounded with information transparency; the study does not isolate the effect of enabling punishment/reward from the effect of making the mechanism salient.
- All-or-nothing (binary) choices may not capture dynamics present in continuous contribution games.
