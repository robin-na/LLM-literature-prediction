## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical analysis and simulations demonstrate that costly punishment enables the emergence and maintenance of cooperation in joint effort games, but only when participation is voluntary. In the voluntary regime, the stationary distribution is dominated by punishers, leading to higher group payoffs and efficiency. In the compulsory regime, defectors dominate and group efficiency collapses. This result is robust for both 'weak' and 'strong' altruism (i.e., whether or not contributors benefit from their own contributions). The findings imply that the combination of voluntary participation and punishment is critical for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency is highly contingent on whether participation in the public goods game is voluntary or compulsory. If participation is voluntary, introducing punishment leads to a regime dominated by punishers and high group efficiency. If participation is compulsory, punishment does not prevent the dominance of defectors and group efficiency remains low. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of voluntary participation (i.e., whether players can opt out) is a key moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment. The model also suggests that these results are robust to variations in group size, population size, and the strength of selection, as long as the basic structure (voluntary participation, costly punishment) is maintained.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Only a limited set of strategies are considered (non-participants, cooperators, defectors, punishers); more complex or conditional strategies are not analyzed.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not address spatial or network structure.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms beyond punishment.
- No analysis of reward mechanisms or positive incentives.
- The evolutionary dynamics do not map directly onto finite-round experimental games.
