The paper is a theoretical and review commentary, synthesizing behavioral, cognitive, and neuroscientific evidence about norm enforcement and punishment. It references empirical studies (including public goods games and ultimatum games) that report on cooperation and punishment, but does not itself report new data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. ## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that the ability to enforce social norms through punishment is central to the evolution and maintenance of large-scale human cooperation. It reviews evidence from behavioral economics (including public goods games and ultimatum games) showing that the possibility of punishment increases cooperation and norm compliance, and that individuals are willing to incur costs to punish norm violators. The commentary also synthesizes neuroscientific findings on the brain regions involved in norm enforcement, proposing a model in which domain-general cognitive processes (such as value learning, mentalizing, and response selection) are repurposed for social norm enforcement. The paper notes that both punishment and reward can promote cooperation, but does not provide new empirical data on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and mechanistic support for the idea that punishment increases cooperation and norm compliance in public-goods-game-like environments, referencing empirical studies that show this effect. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff outcomes with and without punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the general expectation that enabling punishment will increase cooperation, but does not allow for precise prediction of efficiency changes based on game design dimensions or control efficiency. Its value is primarily in contextualizing the importance of punishment and identifying relevant behavioral and neural mechanisms.
