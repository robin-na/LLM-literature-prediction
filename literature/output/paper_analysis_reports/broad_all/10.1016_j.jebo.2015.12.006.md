## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that peer punishment, when feasible and not prohibitively costly, enables groups to sustain full contribution to public goods and achieve high efficiency, even in large groups. The model provides conditions under which peer discipline is feasible and optimal, and shows that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the cost and effectiveness of the punishment and audit technology. The model also shows that, in the absence of peer punishment, only small groups can achieve efficient outcomes, while large groups fail to contribute. The results are robust to various extensions, including renegotiation, spillover costs, and generalized auditing procedures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling peer punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that punishment and audit costs are not too high. The model gives explicit formulas for group utility as a function of game parameters, including player count, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in larger groups and when the punishment technology is effective and not too costly. The model also highlights the importance of the cost structure of punishment and auditing, and the quality of monitoring signals, for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental validation.
- Assumes ex ante identical group members and a specific structure of sequential auditing.
- Does not model repeated public goods games (only one-shot contribution with repeated punishment phase).
- No communication or chat between players beyond the initial agreement phase.
- No reward mechanisms are considered; only punishment is modeled.
- Assumes perfect rationality and common knowledge of the mechanism.
- Extensions such as renegotiation, spillover costs, and generalized matching are discussed but not empirically tested.
