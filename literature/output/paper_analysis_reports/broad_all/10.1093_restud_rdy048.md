## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the ability to sustain efficient cooperation (trust/lending) in large societies with random matching and one-sided moral hazard depends critically on the information structure available to punishers (lenders). With perfect bounded memory, punishment unravels and efficiency collapses. With coarse information (binary flag for recent default), temporary exclusion can sustain high efficiency, with the exact level depending on the profitability of lending and the exclusion length. The model provides explicit formulas for borrower and lender payoffs under different regimes. When lending is very profitable, some inefficiency is unavoidable due to the need for randomization and endogenous adverse selection. The results generalize to other sequential-move games with one-sided moral hazard.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining efficiency depends on the information structure: coarse information (pooling recent defaulters) enables efficient punishment and high payoffs, while fine-grained information leads to unraveling and zero efficiency. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that punishment will only improve efficiency if the punishment mechanism is supported by an information structure that prevents cherry-picking of near-rehabilitated defectors. The model is not a PGG or direct PGG variant, but the logic applies to trust games and other one-sided moral hazard settings. The findings are most relevant for settings with random matching, temporary exclusion, and information constraints.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game or a direct PGG variant; it is a trust game with one-sided moral hazard and random matching.
- All results are theoretical; there is no empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes a continuum of agents and infinite horizon, which may not match finite, small-group lab experiments.
- Punishment is only via exclusion (not costly punishment or peer punishment as in PGGs).
- The results depend critically on the information structure; real-world settings may not match the modeled coarse/fine dichotomy.
- No analysis of communication, reward, or other institutional features common in PGG experiments.
