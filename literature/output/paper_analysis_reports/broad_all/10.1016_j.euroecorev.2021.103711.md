# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When a nudge is (not) enough: Experiments on social information and incentives
Authors: Chen, J., Fonseca, M. A., & Grimshaw, S. B.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (proportion investing in group account)
- punctuality (proportion arriving on time)
- beliefs about others' behavior
- attitudinal measures (norms, beliefs)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are binary behavioral outcomes: whether participants cooperated (invested) or defected (kept) in a large-N binary prisoners' dilemma, and whether participants were punctual in a field punctuality task. No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported. Some discussion of payoffs and externalities is present, but the main analysis is on behavioral outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In the large-N binary prisoners' dilemma, both financial incentives (reward or fine) and positive social information (majority invest) increase cooperation rates among unmotivated types. Negative social information (majority keep) reduces cooperation among motivated types. The combination of incentives and positive social information does not produce additional gains for unmotivated types, and may even crowd out intrinsic motivation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the behavioral results imply that punishment/reward mechanisms can increase cooperation rates, especially among those not intrinsically motivated to cooperate. The effect of social information is context-dependent: it is more effective when the externality of cooperation is salient and financially quantifiable.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about the effect of punishment/reward mechanisms and social information on cooperation rates in a large-N binary prisoners' dilemma with MPCR = 0.05. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the increase in cooperation rates from punishment/reward treatments among unmotivated types suggests that enabling punishment/reward would likely increase efficiency relative to control. The effect is stronger when the externality is salient. The design dimensions (large group, binary choice, no chat, no explicit payoff feedback beyond own outcome, known group size, no identification of punishers) are all specified. The absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative prediction, but the qualitative direction is clear: punishment/reward increases cooperation and likely efficiency, especially among defectors.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Lab experiment uses groups of at least 35 players (large-N prisoners' dilemma).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Two one-shot rounds (day 1 and day 2), with only day 2 used for treatment effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: invest full endowment or keep it.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.05 (5%), with group size at least 35.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (fine) is implemented as a 33% reduction in group account payoff for defectors; cost to punisher is not explicit, as punishment is imposed by the experimenter, not by peers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each fine reduces group account payoff by 33% for defectors; not peer-administered.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward treatment: cooperators receive a 33% bonus on group account payoff.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is implemented by the experimenter; cost to rewarder is not explicit (not peer-administered).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 33% bonus to group account payoff for cooperators.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the experiment is over two days (two rounds).

## Important Limitations
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported; all main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates).
- Punishment and reward are implemented by the experimenter, not by peers; thus, the cost structure and psychological effects may differ from peer-sanctioning public goods games.
- The game is a binary (all-or-nothing) prisoners' dilemma, not a standard linear public goods game with continuous contributions.
- Social information is provided as a nudge (majority invest/keep), not as real-time feedback on actual group behavior.
- No communication or chat is allowed; results may not generalize to settings with communication.
- The effect of punishment/reward on efficiency is inferred from cooperation rates, not directly measured.
