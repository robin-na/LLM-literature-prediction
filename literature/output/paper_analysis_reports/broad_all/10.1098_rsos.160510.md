# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Exploring the trade-off between quality and fairness in human partner choice
Authors: Raihani, N. J., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Royal Society Open Science
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- expected pay-offs to chooser from partner selection
- chooser willingness to sacrifice own payoff for fairness
Non-payoff outcomes:
- partner choice preference (rich vs poor, fair vs stingy)
- perceived fairness
- beliefs about others' choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the chooser's partner selection, but the analysis explicitly compares expected pay-offs (i.e., efficiency for the chooser) under different partner choice scenarios. The study also interprets willingness to forgo higher payoffs as a form of costly punishment or norm enforcement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
When given a choice between a rich-stingy and a poor-fair partner in a modified Dictator Game, choosers often select the fair partner, even at a cost to their own expected pay-off. This tendency is stronger when the wealth of partners is unstable. When both partners are equally fair or unfair, choosers prefer the richer partner, aligning with maximizing expected pay-off. The findings indicate that fairness is prioritized over ability (wealth) in partner choice, especially under uncertainty, and that individuals are willing to incur personal costs to avoid rewarding unfairness.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in environments where individuals can choose partners, people may sacrifice efficiency (personal or group payoff) to avoid unfair partners, especially when partner quality is unstable. While not a public goods game, the findings are relevant for predicting that the introduction of partner choice or similar sanctioning mechanisms may not always increase efficiency—if individuals use these mechanisms to punish unfairness even at a cost to themselves. The results suggest that the effect of punishment or exclusion on efficiency may be less positive or even negative when individuals are motivated by fairness rather than payoff maximization.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves two players (dictator and receiver), with a third-party chooser in the partner selection phase.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each dictator plays two rounds (games), and choosers select a partner for the second round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dictators choose between two discrete options: give 50% or 20% of endowment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the two-game structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choosers are shown the endowments and decisions of two dictators.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a modified Dictator Game, not a public goods game, so findings may not fully generalize to PGG environments.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanism; punishment is inferred from costly partner rejection.
- Partner choice is one-shot for each chooser, not repeated or dynamic.
- Payoff-based efficiency is measured at the individual chooser level, not at the group or system level.
- The experiment is conducted online with MTurk participants and small stakes, which may affect generalizability.
- No direct manipulation of punishment or sanctioning technology; only indirect evidence about costly punishment via partner choice.
