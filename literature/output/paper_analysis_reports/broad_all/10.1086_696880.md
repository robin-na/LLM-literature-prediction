## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that legal sanctions (punishment) can be more efficient than reputational sanctions (boycotts) in deterring undesirable behavior, even when both are costly. This is because legal sanctions have an inframarginal effect: increasing damages not only deters more lawsuits at the margin but also increases the penalty for existing lawsuits, improving deterrence at lower cost. Reputational sanctions lack this inframarginal effect, so increasing their severity is less efficient. The efficiency advantage of legal sanctions is greatest when courts are accurate and when litigation outcomes are public. When court error is high or when consumers have independent, reliable information about quality, reputational sanctions become more important. The model predicts that the optimal regime combines both types of sanctions, but leans toward legal sanctions unless litigation is highly error-prone or costly. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide experimental or empirical estimates, but it offers clear comparative statics and policy implications for the efficiency of punishment mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (legal sanctions) in repeated moral hazard environments can increase efficiency relative to relying solely on reputational sanctions, especially when punishment is not too costly or error-prone and when outcomes are public. The inframarginal effect of punishment means that its efficiency benefit can be greater than that of reputational sanctions, which only deter at the margin. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment should increase efficiency, particularly when the punishment mechanism is accurate and visible. However, if punishment is highly error-prone or costly, or if reputational information is highly reliable, the efficiency gain from punishment may be reduced or even reversed. The model is not a direct PGG or lab experiment, so quantitative predictions should be made with caution, but the qualitative comparative statics are clear and relevant.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model is not a direct public goods game or laboratory experiment, but rather a repeated moral hazard model with a firm and sequential consumers.
- The structure is not a fixed group game; instead, it is a firm facing a sequence of short-lived consumers, which may limit direct applicability to standard PGG settings.
- The model assumes rational, forward-looking agents and perfect commitment to strategies, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- Quantitative predictions for efficiency effects are not provided; only qualitative comparative statics are available.
