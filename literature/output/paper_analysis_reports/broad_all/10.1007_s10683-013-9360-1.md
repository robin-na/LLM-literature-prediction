# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: New insights into conditional cooperation and punishment from a strategy method experiment
Authors: Cheung, S. L.
Journal: Experimental Economics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings (used in model equations and as basis for utility, but not as main dependent variable)
- payoff differences (used in theoretical discussion and as basis for punishment/contribution decisions)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- conditional contribution behavior
- punishment assigned (conditional on others' contributions)
- classification of subjects (conditional cooperator, free-rider, other)
- willingness to punish
- beliefs about punishment expected
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: conditional contributions and punishment assigned in response to others' contributions. Payoff/efficiency is discussed in theory and as a background, but not as a main outcome variable. No direct analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings as a treatment effect.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that both conditional cooperation and punishment in a public goods game are sensitive to the full distribution of others' contributions, not just the average. When punishment is available, more subjects contribute and a substantial fraction are willing to punish, with punishment increasing as the target's contribution falls below the punisher's and as the third player's contribution rises. However, the study does not directly report or analyze group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings as a function of punishment availability. The main findings are about the behavioral patterns of contribution and punishment, and the heterogeneity of types (conditional cooperators, free-riders, others).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment affects contribution and punishment behavior in a one-shot, three-player public goods game with a strategy method. However, it does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does support the qualitative expectation that punishment increases contributions and that the effect depends on the distribution of others' contributions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is informative about mechanisms and behavioral responses, but not about the magnitude of efficiency changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played with 3 players per group in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays two one-shot games (one with, one without punishment), no repeated rounds within a game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: 0, 2, 4, or 6 points (not all-or-nothing, but not fully continuous).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 (a = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment costs the punisher 1 point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment reduces the target's earnings by 3 points (effectiveness e = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know each game is a one-shot; no repeated rounds within a game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are shown the (hypothetical) contributions of the other two players in each scenario during the strategy method elicitation.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution and punishment choices), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No direct analysis or reporting of group efficiency or total earnings as a function of punishment availability.
- The strategy method may elicit different levels of punishment than direct-response methods; this is acknowledged as a potential limitation.
- The simplified environment (3 players, 4 discrete contribution levels) may limit comparability to standard PGG designs with more players and continuous choices.
- No communication or feedback between rounds; only one-shot games are studied.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings do not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
