The main outcomes are the evolutionary dynamics of cooperation, defection, and institutional reward/punishment strategies. Payoff is used as a mechanism for strategy updating, but the paper does not directly report group efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff as a primary outcome. The focus is on the prevalence and stability of cooperation under different institutional incentive regimes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing institutional pure punishment or pure reward into a collective-risk social dilemma game can promote cooperation, but with important differences. Pure reward leads to stable coexistence of cooperators and defectors, with the domain of coexistence expanding as reward intensity increases. Pure punishment produces more complex dynamics, including monostable, bistable, tristable, and quadristable outcomes, and can more effectively suppress free-riding when punishment intensity is high or the implementation threshold is low. In finite populations, lower thresholds for implementing incentives (reward or punishment) promote cooperation, while higher thresholds favor defection. The cost and intensity of incentives modulate these effects. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff; instead, it focuses on the evolutionary stability and prevalence of cooperative strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of institutional punishment on cooperation in collective-risk public goods games, especially regarding how punishment intensity, cost, and implementation threshold affect the prevalence of cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on group efficiency or total payoff, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under certain punishment regimes) are likely to be associated with higher efficiency, assuming the mapping from cooperation to efficiency is known. The results are most relevant for settings with institutional (not peer) punishment, nonlinear threshold public goods, and no direct cost of cooperation for punishers/rewarders. The findings suggest that lower punishment thresholds and higher punishment intensity (relative to cost) are more likely to promote cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if cooperation is strongly linked to group payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental results.
- Outcomes focus on evolutionary stability and prevalence of cooperation, not direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The model uses a collective-risk threshold public goods game, which differs from standard linear PGGs.
- Punishment and reward are institutional (not peer-to-peer), and the cost structure assumes incentivizers do not pay the cost of cooperation.
- No consideration of communication, repeated discrete rounds, or information feedback as in many lab experiments.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with peer punishment, linear PGGs, or different cost structures.
