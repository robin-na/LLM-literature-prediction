## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (probability of cooperation strategy)
- evolutionary stability of cooperation vs. selfishness
- sensitivity of cooperation to parameter changes (spillover, reward, penalty, cost, risk)
Outcome notes:
The paper models the evolution of cooperation strategies in R&D networks using evolutionary game theory. The main outcomes are the stability and prevalence of cooperation vs. selfishness, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff functions are specified, but the main results are about strategy selection and stability, not aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the evolution of cooperation in R&D networks depends on the balance of knowledge spillover, government reward, government penalty (punishment), cooperation cost, and cooperation risk. Increasing government penalty (punishment) and reward, and decreasing cost and risk, promote the stability of cooperation. The model shows that under certain parameter regimes, cooperation becomes evolutionarily stable, while in others, selfishness dominates. The effect of punishment (penalty) is to accelerate and stabilize cooperation, but the paper does not quantify efficiency or group payoff. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and numerical simulation of the evolutionary game model.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, theory-based evidence that introducing or increasing punishment (government penalty) in a social dilemma-like environment can promote cooperation, especially when combined with rewards and low costs/risks. However, the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The results suggest that, in similar networked cooperation settings, punishment is likely to increase cooperation rates, which may translate to higher efficiency if payoffs are aligned with cooperation. The paper is most useful for understanding the qualitative direction of punishment effects and the importance of cost, risk, and reward as moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a two-player (dyadic) evolutionary game between two SDN enterprises.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is an infinite-horizon evolutionary game (replicator dynamics), not a finite-round repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between two discrete strategies: cooperation or selfishness (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a government-imposed penalty (D), not as a cost paid by a player. The cost is exogenous and not borne by other players.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a penalty D; increasing D increases the incentive to cooperate. The effectiveness is direct and exogenous.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Government reward (W) is present and varied; increasing W promotes cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is modeled as an exogenous government transfer; the cost to the rewarder (government) is not analyzed.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is direct: higher W increases cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- The setting is not a standard public goods game; it is a dyadic R&D cooperation model with custom payoffs.
- Outcomes are about cooperation rates and stability, not efficiency or group payoff.
- Punishment and reward are imposed by an external authority (government), not by peers.
- No analysis of marginal per-capita return or standard PGG parameters.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff.
