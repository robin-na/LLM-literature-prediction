The paper also analyzes the frequencies of strategies and the stability of equilibria, but these are used to interpret the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental validation.
- The model assumes a large, well-mixed population and continuous-time replicator dynamics, which may not capture all features of real-world or experimental settings.
- Punishment is implemented as a random institutional sanction with maximal effectiveness (payoff reduced to zero), which may not generalize to all punishment mechanisms.
- No explicit modeling of rounds, feedback, or learning; results may not directly translate to finite repeated games.
- No consideration of reward mechanisms or communication between players.
