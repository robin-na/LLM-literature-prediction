# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms
Authors: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G.
Journal: Brain Sciences
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot (contribution rate)
- neural activation patterns (fMRI)
- self-reported expectations about others' contributions
- risk aversion and social comparison scores
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot (i.e., contribution rate). No direct analysis or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a primary outcome. Payoff is only used to determine bonus payments, not as an analyzed outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per game (randomly assigned).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 75 trials (15 no incentive, 30 financial, 30 social); Study 2: 105 trials (21 per trial type).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions made anonymously in computer cubicles or MRI.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose any amount from 0 up to their endowment (in steps).
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. The starting point on the response bar is randomly chosen each trial; no fixed default contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Tokens contributed are multiplied by 1.6 and divided equally among 4 players; MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized and probabilistic; in financial punishment, tokens are subtracted from the participant if their contribution is below group average. The cost is not paid by another player but is a deduction from the participant's own payoff. The cost to the punisher (the institution) is not specified, but for the participant, the cost is the deduction.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: in financial punishment, tokens deducted from participant; in social punishment, loss of 'List Points' and negative ranking. The magnitude of punishment is variable (50-80% of income on selected trial).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both reward and punishment conditions are present, for both financial and social incentives.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is centralized; in financial reward, tokens are added to participant's payoff if above group average. The cost to the rewarder (the institution) is not specified, but for the participant, the reward is a bonus. In social reward, 'List Points' are awarded, which are costless to the institution.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: in financial reward, tokens added (50-80% of income on selected trial); in social reward, 'List Points' and positive ranking.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of trials is fixed and known to participants (75 in Study 1, 105 in Study 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. During the game, participants are not shown others' contributions or payoffs. At the end, in social incentive conditions, summary lists (Good/Bad Contributors) are shown to all participants.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are administered by a centralized authority (the experimenter), not by peers. In social conditions, participants' identities and performance are revealed to the group via lists, but the source of punishment/reward is always the institution.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not directly analyzed or reported.
- Punishment and reward are both probabilistic and centralized, not peer-administered; findings may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- The cost structure of punishment and reward to the institution is not fully specified.
- No direct analysis of how increased contributions translate to group efficiency or surplus.
- Participants never receive feedback during the game, only at the end, which may differ from standard repeated PGGs with immediate feedback.
- The social incentive relies on voluntary identity disclosure; participants who did not consent were excluded, which may bias the sample.
