# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: An evolutionary explanation for ineffective altruism
Authors: Burum, B., Nowak, M. A., & Hoffman, M.
Journal: Nature Human Behaviour
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount donated
- amount saved
- willingness to pay to prevent harm
- amount paid to reward donor
Non-payoff outcomes:
- empathy ratings
- character judgements
- rewarding behavior (as a proxy for social reward)
- sensitivity to efficacy/multiplier
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (donation, saving, rewarding, willingness to pay, empathy, character judgements). No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff is calculated in a public goods context. The closest outcomes are individual-level donation and reward decisions, and willingness to pay to prevent harm.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that people are generally insensitive to the efficacy (impact) of their charitable giving, in contrast to their sensitivity when making decisions for themselves or family. Social rewards (reputation, praise, monetary reward from third parties) are given for the act of giving, not for giving effectively or giving more. This insensitivity is not due to cognitive or emotional limitations, but rather to the evolutionary logic that social rewards are easier to coordinate around observable, well-defined actions (like giving) than around less observable or harder-to-define features (like efficacy or amount). The studies do not use public goods games or punishment, but the reward mechanism is conceptually adjacent to sanctioning. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it offers indirect evidence that social incentives (rewards) are most effective when they are based on observable, well-defined actions, and that people are not motivated by efficiency or impact when giving is socially rewarded. The findings suggest that, in environments where social rewards or punishments are based on easily observed behaviors (like contributing at all), these incentives may not improve efficiency if they do not target effective or high-impact contributions. The lack of sensitivity to efficacy in social rewards may limit the effectiveness of punishment or reward mechanisms in increasing group efficiency, unless the mechanisms are designed to be sensitive to impact. The paper is most relevant for understanding the limitations of social incentives in promoting efficient cooperation, rather than for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGGs with punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies are individual decision tasks or dyadic (donor and rewarder), not group public goods games. In reward studies, participants are paired with a donor (one-to-one).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies are one-shot (single decision per participant).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. In most studies, participants can choose any amount to donate, save, or reward (continuous choice), but in some reward studies, donors choose all or nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Donation multipliers (matching rates) are manipulated from 1x to 10x, but not in a group PGG context. In reward studies, every cent paid to reward is tripled (reward multiplier = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism is present in Studies 3-5: participants can pay to reward donors, and the reward is tripled.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1: participants pay 1 cent to give 3 cents to the donor (reward tech = 3).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward technology is 3:1 (every cent paid is tripled for the donor).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies are one-shot; participants know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the donor's decision (donate or not, amount, multiplier, etc.) in reward studies.

## Important Limitations
- No public goods game or group interaction; all studies are individual or dyadic.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only reward is studied.
- No efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes are reported; only individual-level donation, reward, and willingness to pay.
- All studies are one-shot; no repeated interaction or dynamic effects.
- Participants are from WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) societies, limiting generalizability.
- Findings may not transfer directly to public goods games or environments with punishment.
- Reward is not conditional on efficacy or amount, limiting the relevance for efficiency-focused interventions.
