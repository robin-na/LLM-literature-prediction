# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ire and punishment: Incidental anger and costly punishment in children, adolescents, and adults
Authors: Gummerum, M., López-Pérez, B., Van Dijk, E., & Van Dillen, L. F.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Child Psychology
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- second-party punishment (rejection rates in ultimatum game)
- third-party punishment rates (whether to punish)
- third-party punishment severity (amount spent to punish)
- self-reported emotions (anger, disgust, sadness, happiness)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral measures of costly punishment (rejection rates, punishment rates, punishment severity) in response to unfairness, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. No direct analysis of efficiency or group payoff is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that experimentally induced incidental anger increases costly punishment of unfair offers in the ultimatum game (second-party punishment) for children, adolescents, and adults. In third-party punishment games, incidental anger increases punishment severity for unfair offers in adults and adolescents, but not in children. The effect of anger is specific to unfair (unequal) offers and does not increase punishment of fair (equal) offers. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment rates and severity), not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the motivation for costly punishment in response to unfairness is influenced by emotional state and developmental stage, with self-relevance and norm appraisals playing key roles.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or related games. Its main contribution is to show that emotional state (incidental anger) can increase costly punishment of unfairness, especially when the violation is self-relevant (second-party) or, for adults, when it violates fairness norms (third-party). For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment rates and severity can be modulated by emotional and developmental factors, but it does not inform how these changes in punishment behavior translate into changes in efficiency or group payoff. The game designs used (ultimatum and third-party punishment games) are adjacent to, but not the same as, standard public goods games, and no efficiency or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: 2 players (proposer, responder); Third-party punishment game: 3 players (proposer, responder, punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant made 7 decisions (one per possible offer) using the strategy method; one decision was randomly selected for payoff.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: responder's choice is all-or-nothing (accept or reject each offer). Third-party punishment: punisher chooses whether to punish (binary) and how much to spend (continuous 0-5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party punishment: each point spent by punisher costs 1 point to punisher and reduces proposer's payoff by 2 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point spent by punisher reduces proposer's payoff by 2 points (2:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew they would make 7 decisions (one per possible offer) and that one would be randomly selected for payoff.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown the allocation decisions (offers) made by Person A, but not summary statistics about others' behavior.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All roles were anonymous; punished players could not identify who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The games used are ultimatum and third-party punishment games, not public goods games or direct variants.
- The effect of punishment on cooperation, efficiency, or group welfare is not assessed.
- Emotion induction method (autobiographical recall) may have demand characteristics; only self-reported emotions are used for manipulation check.
- Developmental and cultural generalizability may be limited by sample (UK children, adolescents, adults).
