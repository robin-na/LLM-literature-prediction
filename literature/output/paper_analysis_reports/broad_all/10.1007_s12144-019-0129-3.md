# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Neural correlates of proposers' fairness perception in punishment and non-punishment economic games
Authors: Chen, M. L., Zhu, X. L., Zhang, J., Ma, G. P., & Wu, Y. H.
Journal: Current Psychology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proposer offer choice (fair, relatively unfair, absolutely unfair)
- reaction time
- event-related potential (MFN amplitude)
- self-reported guilt
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral choices of proposers (offer type), neural responses (MFN), and self-reported guilt. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Proposers overwhelmingly choose absolutely unfair offers in the dictator game (non-punishment), but shift to more relatively unfair or fair offers in the ultimatum game (punishment). The presence of punishment leads to more fair behavior by proposers, consistent with the idea that punishment deters unfairness. Neural data (MFN amplitude) and self-reported guilt indicate that proposers experience cognitive conflict and guilt when making unfair offers, even when punishment is not possible. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment (ultimatum game) leads to more fair offers by proposers compared to the absence of punishment (dictator game). However, because the study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. It is informative for predicting behavioral changes (offer distributions) under punishment, but not for the downstream efficiency prediction task. The design is adjacent to public goods games, but not a direct variant.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played between two players: one proposer and one responder.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 240 one-shot trials (120 punishment, 120 non-punishment), with a new partner in each trial. Each trial is a one-shot game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is categorical: proposer chooses from three discrete options (absolutely unfair, relatively unfair, fair), not continuous.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is binary: responder can reject the offer, in which case both get nothing. This is the standard ultimatum game punishment mechanism.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials and that each is a one-shot game with a new partner.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot directly inform efficiency predictions.
- The experiment uses a two-player ultimatum/dictator game, not a public goods game or direct variant.
- Offer choices are restricted to three discrete options, not continuous allocation.
- All participants are university students, limiting generalizability.
- Social context is artificial: responders are not present, and there is no face-to-face interaction.
- No analysis of how behavioral changes translate into group-level efficiency or surplus.
