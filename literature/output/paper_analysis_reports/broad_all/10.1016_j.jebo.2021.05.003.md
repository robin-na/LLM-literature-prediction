# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Covenants before the swords: The limits to efficient cooperation in heterogeneous groups
Authors: Koch, C., Nikiforakis, N., & Noussair, C. N.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- average earnings
- efficiency (earnings as % of maximum possible)
- average contributions (as a proxy for efficiency, but earnings are primary)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- frequency and content of covenants
- punishment frequency and amount
- feuds (cycles of reciprocal punishment)
- adherence to covenants
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings (efficiency) and contributions. Punishment and covenant behavior are also analyzed, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab experiment with heterogeneous groups (different returns from the public good), the authors compare three treatments: (1) communication and punishment available together (ComEarly), (2) punishment first, then communication added later (ComLate), and (3) communication only (ComOnly). Efficiency, measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible, is highest when both communication and punishment are available from the start (ComEarly: 87% of maximum), lower with only one instrument (ComLate: 60-68%; ComOnly: 76% in part 1, 60% overall). However, even with both instruments, most groups do not achieve full efficiency, as covenants typically require compromise between efficiency and equality. Early communication is crucial: it enables groups to establish covenants and avoid costly punishment cycles (feuds), leading to higher efficiency. Late communication cannot fully overcome the negative effects of prior sanctioning. The study demonstrates that while punishment and communication together improve efficiency, concerns for equality limit the ability of heterogeneous groups to reach the social optimum.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous returns, enabling both communication and punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) compared to either instrument alone. However, the efficiency gain is limited by the tendency of groups to compromise between efficiency and equality, especially when group members have different returns. The timing of communication is a key moderator: early communication (before punishment) is much more effective at raising efficiency than late communication. For prediction, this suggests that the presence of both communication and punishment, especially with early communication, should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, but not full efficiency. Control efficiency (with only communication) is a strong predictor, but the incremental effect of punishment depends on whether communication is also present and when it occurs. The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players (2 high-return, 2 low-return) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per session (2 parts of 10 rounds each).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Verbal (chatbox) communication allowed in specified periods; presence and timing of chat is a key treatment variable.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0-20 ECU per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Heterogeneous: 0.6 for high-return, 0.3 for low-return players; n*mpcr > 1 for both types.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Flat fee of 1 ECU per period to activate punishment; each punishment point costs 1 ECU to the recipient, no marginal cost per point to punisher after fee.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces recipient's earnings by 1 ECU; after paying the fee, punisher can assign any number of points (up to recipient's earnings).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 10 rounds per part; at start of part 1, they know a second part will follow but not its content.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see all group members' contributions and provisional earnings; after each punishment stage, they see who punished whom and how much.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not anonymous: subjects see who punished whom and the number of points assigned.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only one form of heterogeneity (returns from the public good based on real-effort task); other forms of heterogeneity may yield different results.
- Perfect monitoring is assumed: all contributions and punishments are fully observable and costless to monitor, which may not hold in real-world settings.
- The lab environment may not capture all complexities of field environments, such as diversity in wealth, background, or external enforcement.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment and communication are considered.
- The study does not vary group size, MPCR, or punishment parameters beyond the chosen design, so generalizability to other parameterizations is limited.
