# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Just ask? Preference revelation and lying in a public goods experiment
Authors: Robbett, A.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group profits
- group efficiency (payoff relative to Nash and social optimum)
- individual payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rate
- truthful reporting rate
- punishment assigned
- perceptions of dishonesty
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group profits (efficiency) and cooperation (contribution relative to dominant strategy). Punishment behavior and message truthfulness are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares four conditions in a repeated public goods game with private, randomly assigned types: (1) baseline voluntary contribution (VCM), (2) Mechanism (report type to a mechanism that enforces the socially optimal contribution for the reported type), (3) Revelation (report type to group members, then contribute voluntarily), and (4) Punishment (as Revelation, but with a costly peer punishment stage). The main findings are: (a) Mechanism and Revelation both increase cooperation and group efficiency relative to VCM, but Mechanism's effect fades over time; (b) Punishment does not increase cooperation or efficiency compared to VCM, and group profits are actually lower in the Punishment condition; (c) Punishment is mainly used to sanction obviously dishonest reports, not under-contribution, and is too weak to incentivize higher contributions; (d) The presence of punishment undermines the positive effect of truthful reporting on cooperation seen in the Revelation condition.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with private, heterogeneous incentives and observable reports/contributions, adding a peer punishment mechanism (with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio) does not increase efficiency or group payoffs compared to a baseline without punishment. In fact, efficiency is lower with punishment, likely due to underutilization and mis-targeting of sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar environments (3 players, 10 rounds, no chat, private types, punishment available after observing reports/contributions, punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment is likely to have a neutral or negative effect on efficiency relative to control. The results also highlight that the effectiveness of punishment depends on the clarity of norms and the ability to identify free-riders; when these are ambiguous due to private information, punishment may backfire or be ineffective.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 3 players, fixed for all 10 rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No free-form chat or communication was allowed; only structured reporting of type (with limited message options) in some conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were discrete (0-4 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 10 per token to each of the other two group members (i.e., each token contributed increases others' payoffs by 10).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 point to the punisher to reduce the target's payoff by 3 points (1:3 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 points lost per 1 point spent; this is a standard, relatively strong punishment ratio in the literature.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game had 10 periods (finitely repeated).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, participants saw each group member's contribution (and, in message conditions, their reported type).

## Important Limitations
- The public goods game uses private, randomly assigned types with heterogeneous incentives, which may limit generalizability to standard homogeneous PGGs.
- The punishment mechanism is only tested in a setting with private information about types; results may differ in settings with common knowledge of incentives.
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (3 players) and a short, finitely repeated game (10 rounds), which may not capture dynamics in larger or indefinitely repeated groups.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is tested, so results do not speak to the effects of rewards.
- The punishment mechanism is only available after observing both reported type and contribution, and punishment is anonymous; other punishment designs may yield different results.
- The study is conducted with undergraduate students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
