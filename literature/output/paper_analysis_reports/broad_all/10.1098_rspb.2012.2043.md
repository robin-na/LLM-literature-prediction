# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Patient and impatient punishers of free-riders
Authors: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Herrmann, B., & Gamella, J. F.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- likelihood of punishing (binary: whether a participant punishes or not)
- intensity of punishment (amount spent on punishment, secondary)
- contribution to public good
- delay discounting (impatience/patience)
- expectations about punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the likelihood of punishing, analyzed as a function of the punisher's own contribution (relative to group) and their delay discounting (patience/impatience). The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of punishment on group payoff or efficiency. Payoff-related outcomes are not meaningfully analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment in a one-shot public goods game is implemented by two distinct types of individuals: patient cooperators (who punish free-riders, likely for moralistic/future-oriented reasons) and impatient free-riders (who punish other free-riders, likely for competitive/spiteful reasons). The likelihood of punishing is positively associated with cooperation among patient individuals and with free-riding among impatient individuals. The paper does not analyze or report the effect of punishment on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides no direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. It does, however, offer insight into the psychological heterogeneity of punishers, which may be relevant for understanding the mechanisms behind punishment behavior. For the downstream prediction task, this paper does not provide usable data on efficiency outcomes or how game design dimensions affect efficiency with or without punishment. Its main value is in highlighting that the motivations for punishment can differ (moralistic vs. competitive) depending on individual patience and contribution behavior, but this is not tied to efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 anonymous players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; the game is anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how much (0-10 euros) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (pot is doubled and split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 euro to reduce another's earnings by 3 euros (1:3 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 (1 euro spent reduces target's earnings by 3 euros).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, the results (contributions) are made public to all group members before the punishment stage.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, total earnings, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- The experiment is one-shot, so findings may not generalize to repeated or multi-round public goods games.
- The main dependent variable is punishment behavior (likelihood and intensity), not payoff-based outcomes.
- The field sample is from small, semi-rural populations in Spain, which may limit generalizability.
- No manipulation or variation of game design dimensions beyond the standard one-shot PGG with punishment.
