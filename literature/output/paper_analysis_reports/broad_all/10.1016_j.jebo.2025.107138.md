# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Collective sanction enforcement: New experimental evidence from two societies
Authors: Kamei, K., Sharma, S., & Walker, M. J.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group payoff
- payoff matrices after punishment
- normalized fear and greed values
- incentive structure (dominant strategy, Nash equilibrium, stag-hunt transformation)
- aggregate punishment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rate
- punishment frequency
- punishment strength
- higher-order punishment frequency and strength

- personal and descriptive norms
- moral beliefs
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables include both payoff-based outcomes (group payoff, incentive structure, normalized fear/greed) and behavioral outcomes (punishment, cooperation, beliefs). The paper explicitly analyzes how punishment changes the incentive structure and group payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that third-party punishment of defectors is common in both the UK and India, but is much stronger in the UK. In the UK, adding multiple third parties increases aggregate punishment, and enabling higher-order punishment (punishment of other third parties for failing to punish or for anti-social punishment) further increases aggregate punishment. This transforms the game from a prisoner's dilemma to a stag-hunt, increasing the potential for efficient cooperation. In India, punishment levels are lower, and neither group size nor higher-order punishment opportunities are sufficient to change the incentive structure; defection remains the dominant strategy. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus positive and substantial in the UK (especially with higher-order punishment), but weak in India. The results highlight the importance of cultural context and prevailing norms in determining the efficiency effects of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in social dilemma games depends on the number of punishers, the availability of higher-order punishment, and the cultural context. In environments similar to the UK, enabling higher-order punishment among multiple third parties can transform the incentive structure to support efficient cooperation, while in environments like India, the same institutional changes have little effect on efficiency due to weaker punishment norms. For prediction, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by group size, higher-order punishment mechanisms, and societal norms. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency; the presence and effectiveness of punishment institutions and cultural context must be considered.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Varied between 3 (2 PD players + 1 third party) and 5 (2 PD players + 3 third parties) across treatments. Increasing the number of third parties increased aggregate punishment and improved efficiency in the UK, but not in India.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; not repeated. The design isolates punishment preferences without repeated game effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players; anonymity maintained.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decision is binary: PD players choose to send 16 points or not (cooperate or defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PD player can send 16 points, which are tripled for the recipient. For each point sent, the recipient gets 3 points. With 2 players, MPCR = 1.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 point per punishment point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point assigned reduces the punished player's payoff by 3 points (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly one-shot; participants know there is only one round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the decisions relevant to their role (e.g., third parties see PD players' choices for punishment decisions), but anonymity is maintained. No explicit mention of summary statistics, but belief elicitation about others' behavior is included.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; punished players do not know the identity of the punisher. Third parties can observe the punishment actions of other third parties for higher-order punishment decisions, but identities are not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot design, so long-term or dynamic effects of punishment and higher-order enforcement are not measured.
- The study uses a prisoner's dilemma rather than a standard public goods game, so results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Cultural context (UK vs India) is a major moderator; results may not generalize to other societies.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation effects, which may be important in real-world settings.
- Efficiency gains are measured in the short run; potential long-term welfare improvements from stabilized cooperation are not captured.
