# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Impact of Elections on Cooperation: Evidence from a Lab-in-the-Field Experiment in Uganda
Authors: Grossman, G., & Baldassarri, D.
Journal: American Journal of Political Science
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- change in contribution after punishment
- frequency and targeting of punishment
- cooperation in natural setting (proportion of crop sold via group)
Outcome notes:
The main experimental outcome is contribution to the public good (i.e., how much of the endowment is contributed), not group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff as a dependent variable, though it discusses the structure of payoffs and the social optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of a centralized sanctioning authority (monitor) in a PGG increases contributions to the public good compared to a baseline with no punishment. When the monitor is elected rather than randomly assigned, contributions increase further, suggesting a legitimacy effect. Both types of monitors punish defectors at a personal cost, but the actual punishment strategies are similar between elected and random monitors. The study does not report group efficiency or total earnings, so the direct effect on efficiency is not measured. The findings are robust to controls for monitor characteristics and are supported by observational data linking legitimacy to cooperation in real-world farmer groups.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized punishment in a PGG increases contributions, and that the legitimacy of the punisher (via election) further increases cooperation. However, because the main outcome is contribution rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used as indirect evidence for predicting efficiency effects. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the experimental manipulation is clear, but the lack of direct efficiency or payoff reporting limits the precision of predictions for efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experimental group consisted of members from a farmer association, with 30 participants per group on average. The exact group size for each PGG session is not specified, but the design implies groups of 6 (since 6 members per PO, and 6 POs per association, but each variant is played separately).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Baseline: 6 rounds. Monitor treatments: 2 preliminary rounds (no punishment), then 4 rounds with punishment (total 6 rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed. Participants were not allowed to talk throughout the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how to split 10 MUs between private and public accounts (0-10).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU contributed to the public account is doubled and redistributed evenly among all group members. Exact MPCR depends on group size (not precisely specified, but likely 1/number of players * 2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Monitor spends 1 MU to reduce a subject's payoff by 3 MUs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 MU spent by monitor reduces target's payoff by 3 MUs).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds is known and fixed (6 rounds in all variants).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all contributions to the public account are displayed publicly (in USH), from lowest to highest, but not linked to player identity. Mean contribution and payoffs for each contribution level are also displayed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Monitors point to contribution levels to assign punishment, but do not identify individual players. Players know which contribution levels are punished, but not who is punished.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution to the public good, not group efficiency or total earnings. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable.
- Group size for each PGG session is not precisely specified, which may affect calculation of MPCR and efficiency.
- The study uses a centralized punishment regime (monitor), not peer punishment, so findings may not generalize to peer-sanctioning settings.
- The experiment is conducted with pre-existing groups (farmer associations), which may differ from anonymous or ad hoc lab groups.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- The effect of punishment on actual group welfare (net of punishment costs) is not measured.
