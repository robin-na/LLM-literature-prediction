Year: 2009

Outcome notes:
The primary outcomes are behavioral: rates of defection, boldness, vengefulness, and metavengefulness. Payoff is used as a mechanism for strategy replication, but group or individual efficiency, welfare, or total payoff are not directly reported as outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of a public-goods-like dilemma, the addition of metanorms (punishing those who do not punish defectors) is necessary for the emergence of a behavioral norm against defection. When only first-order punishment is available, defection dominates. When metanorms are present, defection rates drop and punishment rates rise, indicating norm emergence. Decoupling the strategies for punishing defectors and non-punishers does not prevent norm emergence and can reduce the overall level of punishment required. In group-structured games with unequal power, metanorms alone do not produce a norm against defection; only with decoupled sanctioning structures does the strong group enforce a norm against the weak group's defection, leading to group dominance rather than universal cooperation. The outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategy values (boldness, vengefulness), not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence of punishment and especially metanorms (second-order punishment) can dramatically reduce defection rates in public-goods-like environments, even when punishment is costly. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (defection, punishment) rather than efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that enabling punishment (especially with metanorms) is likely to increase efficiency in analogous empirical games, but the magnitude and exact efficiency outcomes are not reported. The paper also highlights that group structure and the sophistication of the punishment mechanism (e.g., decoupling punishment types) can moderate the effect, sometimes leading to group dominance rather than overall cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 20 agents for the basic game, 30 agents (20 strong, 10 weak) for group games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 100 or 1000 generations, with each agent facing 4 defection opportunities per round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (defection) is a binary choice: defect or not defect.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Defection yields +3 to defector, -1 to each other agent (negative externality). Not a standard MPCR, but a fixed impact per defection.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs -2 to punisher per act (both for norm and metanorm enforcement).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment inflicts -9 on the punished agent per act (both for norm and metanorm enforcement). In group games, strong group can inflict -9, weak group -3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations have a fixed, known number of generations (100 or 1000).

## Important Limitations
- Outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategy values (defection, punishment), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The model is a simulation, not an empirical or experimental study with human subjects.
- Payoff structure is fixed and not systematically varied; sensitivity to payoff parameters is discussed but not explored.
- No direct mapping to standard public goods game MPCR; the game is a close variant but not identical.
- No communication, reputation, or memory; agents are minimally rational and only imitate successful strategies.
- Group-structured results show that metanorms can lead to group dominance rather than universal cooperation.
