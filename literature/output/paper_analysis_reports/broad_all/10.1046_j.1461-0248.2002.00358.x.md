## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that in asymmetric interactions, the ability of the exploited party to terminate the interaction acts as a deterrent to exploitation, increasing the efficiency (joint payoff) of the system. As the exploiter's power to prolong the interaction increases, exploitation rises, the victim's payoff falls (eventually becoming negative), and the system shifts from mutualism to parasitism. The temptation to cheat (benefit/cost ratio) and the rate at which mutualistic benefits diminish also modulate these outcomes. The model is supported qualitatively by empirical data from cleaner-fish systems, where larger clients (with more to gain from cleaning) experience less exploitation and longer interactions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that mechanisms allowing the exploited party to control the duration of interaction (analogous to exit, partner choice, or punishment in PGG-like games) can increase efficiency by deterring exploitation. While not a PGG or direct punishment experiment, the model is relevant for predicting that increasing the ability of group members to sanction or exit (or otherwise control exploiters) will generally increase group efficiency, especially when the temptation to cheat is not overwhelming. The model also suggests that efficiency gains from punishment or partner control are greatest when the benefits of cooperation are high and the costs of cheating are high relative to the exploiter's gain. However, the model does not address repeated group interactions, explicit punishment costs, or the full range of PGG design dimensions, so its predictive value is indirect.

## Important Limitations
- The model is strictly dyadic and does not generalize to group interactions or standard public goods games.
- Punishment is not modeled as a costly, explicit action; partner control is via exit/termination, not direct sanctions.
- No repeated interactions or rounds; the model is one-shot.
- No explicit cost or technology parameters for punishment or reward as in PGG experiments.
- Empirical support is qualitative and based on observational data from a specific biological system.
- Model does not address communication, information, or identification mechanisms.
