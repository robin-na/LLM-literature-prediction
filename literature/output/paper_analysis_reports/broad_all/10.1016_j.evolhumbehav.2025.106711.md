# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: People decide whether to share social information-and whether to share it accurately-on the basis of self-interest
Authors: Gomes, C. M., Mulhinch, M. R., & McCullough, M. E.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- propensity to share reputational information (gossip)
- accuracy of shared information (honest vs dishonest gossip)
- self-serving vs non-self-serving dishonesty
- influence of own prior generosity/greed on gossip behavior
- effect of incentives on gossip and dishonesty
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: whether participants share information about others (gossip), whether that information is accurate or dishonest, and whether dishonesty is self-serving. No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported. Some analyses relate to the likelihood of being chosen as a partner for a subsequent economic game, but this is not a direct measure of group efficiency or total earnings.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that people are more likely to share reputational information (gossip) when they have behaved generously in a prior economic game, and less likely when they have behaved greedily. The tendency to share information honestly or dishonestly is strongly influenced by whether the participant stands to benefit from being chosen as a partner for a subsequent economic game. When lying is possible and being chosen is incentivized, greedy or indifferent participants are more likely to share dishonest, self-serving gossip. Generous participants are more likely to share honest information, especially when honesty is required and being chosen is incentivized. The results support reputation-based partner choice and social competition theories over indirect reciprocity theory. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. However, it offers informative context about the strategic use of social information (gossip) in economic games with partner choice and reputation. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of information-sharing mechanisms (and their honesty) depends on participants' incentives and prior behavior, which may be relevant for understanding the role of reputation and information in environments with punishment or partner choice. Since no efficiency or payoff outcomes are reported, this paper should not be used as a primary source for predicting efficiency effects of punishment, but may inform mechanistic expectations about when information-based interventions (including punishment or reputation) are likely to be effective or undermined by dishonesty.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main experimental groupings are tetrads (groups of 4), with participants believing they are in a 4-person group for the reputation-sharing and partner choice stages.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment consists of three stages: (1) reputation-building (single take dictator game), (2) reputation-sharing (single opportunity), (3) partner choice and simultaneous take dictator games. Each is a single-shot or one-off decision, not repeated rounds.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed about the structure and number of stages in advance; all decisions are one-shot within each stage.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants can see summary information about others' prior behavior (generous, indifferent, greedy) in the reputation-sharing stage, depending on what is shared.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not use a public goods game or direct punishment; it uses a take dictator game and focuses on gossip and reputation.
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (gossip, honesty, dishonesty, self-serving lies).
- All interactions are one-shot or single-stage; there is no repeated interaction or long-term group structure.
- The social context is artificial: all partners are anonymous, and the information-sharing mechanism is highly structured.
- The study uses deception (sham players, manipulated group composition), which may limit generalizability.
- Findings may not transfer directly to environments with explicit punishment or to standard public goods games.
