Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are theoretical predictions about the stability and efficiency (payoff) of cooperation under different social norms in two-player simultaneous-move social dilemmas (Snowdrift, Prisoner's Dilemma, Stag Hunt). The analysis is in terms of expected payoffs and evolutionary stability, not empirical measurement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in two-player simultaneous-move social dilemmas, the stability and efficiency of cooperation via indirect reciprocity depend on the structure of the social norm (reputation updating rule) and the underlying game. In Snowdrift and Prisoner's Dilemma, strong punishment of defectors (assigning bad reputations for defection against good players) is necessary to maintain high efficiency (i.e., stable cooperation with high group payoff). In Stag Hunt, such punishment is less critical, and norms that unfairly favor reciprocators (always assign good reputation to reciprocation) can enhance the robustness and efficiency of cooperation. The paper also shows that punishing unconditional cooperators (who help bad players) is important for maintaining efficient cooperation in all three games. The efficiency outcome (i.e., group payoff relative to full cooperation) is maximized under certain social norms, but the effect of punishment-like mechanisms is context-dependent.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (via reputation loss) on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends on the underlying game structure and the details of the punishment mechanism (social norm). In games with strong temptation to defect (SG, PD), enabling punishment (bad reputation for defectors) increases efficiency by stabilizing cooperation. In coordination games (SH), such punishment is less necessary, and norms that favor reciprocators can be more effective. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency should be moderated by the type of social dilemma and the specific implementation of the punishment/reputation mechanism. The results are most directly relevant to two-player games, not standard multi-player PGGs, but the logic may transfer to small-group settings with reputation-based punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All models are for two-player (dyadic) simultaneous-move games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as assignment of bad reputation, which reduces future payoffs by making others less likely to cooperate. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the social norm and game type.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Indirect reciprocity provides both incentives for reward (good reputation) and punishment (bad reputation), but explicit reward actions are not modeled.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is implemented as assignment of good reputation, which increases future payoffs by making others more likely to cooperate. The effectiveness depends on the social norm.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players have public information about all reputations; reputation is shared among all players.

## Important Limitations
- The model is limited to two-player (dyadic) simultaneous-move games, not multi-player public goods games.
- Punishment is implemented only as reputation loss, not as a costly or explicit action.
- No explicit cost for punishing or rewarding; only indirect effects via reputation.
- No communication, chat, or explicit signaling is modeled.
- The model assumes public sharing of reputation information; does not consider private or noisy reputation.
- No finite or known number of rounds; analysis is in the infinite/repeated limit.
- Findings may not generalize directly to standard multi-player PGGs or to empirical settings.
