## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper shows that in simulated public goods games, a reputation system based on punitive actions (punishment scoring) allows for the evolution and maintenance of high cooperation frequencies in large groups, even when errors in reputation assessment are present. In contrast, reputation based on generosity (contributions) or rewards is much less effective: generosity-based reputation rarely leads to cooperation in large groups, and reward-based systems are unstable due to invasion by non-rewarders. The model assumes binary contribution and punishment choices, with payoffs used for evolutionary selection, but does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff as outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment-based reputation mechanisms are more effective than generosity- or reward-based reputation for sustaining cooperation in public goods games, especially as group size increases and errors in reputation assessment become more likely. However, because the main outcome is cooperation frequency rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting the likely positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, especially in large groups and error-prone environments. The model assumes all-or-nothing contribution and punishment choices, no communication, and full observability of actions (except for errors in reputation memory). The results are most informative for predicting the effect of punishment in large, anonymous, repeated PGGs with reputation tracking, but do not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (n) is a key manipulated parameter; increasing group size decreases cooperation frequency, but punishment-based reputation sustains higher cooperation at larger n than other mechanisms.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each individual plays m interactions per generation (default m=5), but strategies cannot condition on round number, so no end-game effects. The number of rounds is fixed per generation.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; all interactions are anonymous except for reputation tracking.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment decisions are binary (contribute or not; punish or not).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/n, with r set as a function of n (e.g., r=0.9n or r=0.66n in figures), so the incentive to cooperate is held roughly constant as group size increases.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is alpha (e.g., alpha=0.1); cost to punished is beta (e.g., beta=0.3 or 0.5), with alpha < beta.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is beta/alpha; both parameters are varied in some simulations (see Figs. A8, A9).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. A reward stage is modeled as an alternative to punishment in some simulations; reward-based reputation is compared to punishment-based and generosity-based reputation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost to rewarder is alpha; benefit to rewarded is beta (same parameters as punishment, but applied to rewarding cooperators).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is beta/alpha; same as for punishment, but applied to rewarding.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of interactions per generation (m) is fixed and known; no end-game effects are possible.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each interaction is public; all players know everyone else's reputation (except for errors in memory).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know who punished in the previous round, as reputation is based on punitive actions and is public.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical/simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main outcome is cooperation frequency, not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contribution and punishment choices.
- No communication or partner choice is allowed; groups are formed randomly.
- Reputation is based only on the most recent action (memory-1), and only first-order information is used.
- The model does not address the effect of second-order reputation norms or more complex social information.
- Parameter values (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness, group size) may not map directly to real-world or experimental settings.
