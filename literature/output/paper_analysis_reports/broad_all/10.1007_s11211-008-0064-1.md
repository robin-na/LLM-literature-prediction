## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review highlights that experimental public good games and related economic games (including with punishment) show that people often contribute more than predicted by self-interest and sometimes punish non-cooperators at a cost to themselves, even when reciprocity and reputation are ruled out. This pattern is observed across diverse societies. However, the review argues that these behavioral outcomes do not necessarily indicate group selection or strong reciprocity as an evolved adaptation, and that the experimental settings may not reflect ancestral environments. The review does not report or analyze efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on the theoretical interpretation of observed contribution and punishment behaviors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games. It reviews behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) from experimental games, but does not report or synthesize efficiency, group payoff, or welfare data. The main value for prediction is contextual: it affirms that punishment is present in many public good game experiments and that it affects contribution behavior, but it does not quantify or analyze efficiency effects. The review also cautions that experimental game results may not generalize to real-world or evolutionary contexts.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review describes public good games as being played with small groups, but does not specify exact player counts for each experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review discusses both one-shot and repeated (iterated) games, noting that generosity is reduced in one-shot games.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The review notes that in public good games, contributions are multiplied by a factor greater than 1.0 but less than the number of group members, which is the standard MPCR structure.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher, but no specific cost structure is given.

## Important Limitations
- This is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- The review focuses on behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) and their theoretical interpretation, not on group payoff or efficiency.
- The review critiques the ecological validity of experimental economic games and questions the generalizability of their findings to real-world or evolutionary contexts.
- No quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or welfare effects of punishment are provided.
