# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation and authoritarian values: An experimental study in China
Authors: Vollan, B., Landmann, A., Zhou, Y. X., Hu, B. L., & Herrmann-Pillath, C.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- frequency of full contribution
- frequency of zero contribution
- voting behavior
- association of values (e.g., deference to authority) with contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the contribution to the public good (out of 10 tokens). The paper does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare directly. However, contribution rates are closely related to efficiency in this linear PGG, but the paper does not explicitly calculate or report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a one-shot, linear public goods game with a mild, non-deterrent punishment for under-contribution, exogenously imposed punishment rules (authoritarian/top-down) significantly increase average contributions compared to no law or endogenously chosen (democratic) punishment rules. This effect is strongest among participants (especially workers) with high deference to authority, and weaker or absent among those with low deference to authority (especially students). The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but since the game is linear and contributions are the main outcome, higher contributions imply higher efficiency. There is no evidence of a democracy premium in this Chinese sample; instead, authoritarian implementation is more effective at increasing cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear PGG with a mild, non-deterrent punishment, enabling punishment via exogenous (authoritarian) imposition increases average contributions (and thus efficiency) compared to no punishment. The effect is context-dependent: in this Chinese sample, exogenous punishment is more effective than democratic (endogenous) punishment, especially among those with high deference to authority. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on cultural context and the method of rule implementation (exogenous vs. endogenous). However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so predictions must be inferred from contribution rates.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 4 (workers) or 5 (students) independent one-shot rounds; each round is a one-shot game with new random matching.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 10 tokens (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 (each token contributed returns 0.5 to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is automatic: a 2-point deduction for not fully contributing; no explicit cost to a punisher (punishment is not peer-administered).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed 2-point deduction for under-contribution, regardless of amount; not deterrent (does not make defection unprofitable).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds in advance (4 or 5 rounds, each independent).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No information about other players' contributions or voting choices is revealed during the experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is automatic and anonymous; no identification of who is punished or who triggered punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare directly; all inferences about efficiency must be made from contribution rates.
- Punishment is not peer-administered but is an automatic sanction for under-contribution, which may differ from standard peer punishment PGGs.
- The punishment is non-deterrent (does not make defection unprofitable), so effects may differ from games with stronger punishment.
- The study is conducted in China with specific samples (students and workers), and findings may not generalize to other cultural contexts.
- The effect of punishment is shown to be strongly moderated by individual values (deference to authority), which may limit generalizability.
- No direct analysis of group-level efficiency or surplus; only individual contribution rates are analyzed.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward or combined reward-punishment systems.
