# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The development of cooperation and cooperative norm enforcement in children and adolescents: The role of in-group favoritism
Authors: Huang, Y. F., Zhang, Y. L., Hu, X. M., Yang, W. Q., & Mai, X. Q.
Journal: Acta Psychologica
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public pool
- cooperative expectations
- third-party punishment assigned (tokens spent to punish free-rider)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution (tokens invested in the public pool), cooperative expectations (beliefs about others' cooperation), and punishment behavior (tokens spent to punish free-riders as a third party). No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. While the structure of the game allows for calculation of efficiency, the paper does not report or analyze efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that children and adolescents show in-group favoritism in both cooperation (higher contributions to in-group members) and norm enforcement (more lenient punishment of in-group free-riders), but these biases decline with age. Eighth graders show less bias in both cooperation and punishment. The effect of group membership on cooperation is mediated by cooperative expectations in fourth and sixth graders, but not in eighth graders. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined. The punishment mechanism is present as a third-party punishment opportunity, but only one-shot decisions are made, and the main outcome is tokens spent to punish, not group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the behavioral effects of punishment opportunities in a one-shot 4-player PGG with children and adolescents, specifically regarding in-group/out-group bias in cooperation and punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are described and can be extracted, but the main outcomes are contribution and punishment behavior, not efficiency. The findings are most useful for understanding how group membership and age moderate cooperation and punishment behavior, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the presence of punishment allows for third-party costly punishment of free-riders, but the effect on group efficiency is not measured.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays two one-shot rounds in Experiment 1 (one with in-group, one with out-group). Experiment 2 is a single one-shot punishment decision per condition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; participants are instructed to complete tasks independently and not to communicate.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can contribute any integer between 0 and 40 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5: tokens in the public pool are doubled and split evenly among 4 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 5:1 (participant pays 1 token to deduct 5 tokens from the free-rider).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 5:1 (1 token spent removes 5 tokens from target).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is mentioned in theoretical framing but not implemented in the experimental design.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds (two rounds in Experiment 1, one per group condition; one decision per group condition in Experiment 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown example outcomes and must answer arithmetic questions to ensure understanding, but in the actual decision, they do not see others' real choices (absent partner design).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as outcomes; only contribution, expectations, and punishment behavior are analyzed.
- The use of imaginary (absent) partners means there is no real-time interaction or feedback, limiting ecological validity and the ability to observe dynamic effects such as reputation.
- The design is one-shot (or two-shot) per condition, so repeated game effects and learning are not captured.
- The sample is limited to children and adolescents in China, and cultural or developmental generalizability may be limited.
- No adult comparison group is included, so developmental trends into adulthood are not observed.
- Potential contextual influences (e.g., classroom setting, peer effects) are not fully controlled.
- No analysis of the relationship between cooperation and punishment at the individual level due to fixed order of experiments.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so findings are limited to punishment and cooperation.
