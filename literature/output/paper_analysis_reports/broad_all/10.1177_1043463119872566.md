The model is theoretical and focuses on equilibrium payoffs (efficiency, welfare) under different institutional and strategic conditions. Non-payoff outcomes like cooperation rates are discussed as equilibrium behaviors, not as empirical measurements.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when actors can invest in strategic tie formation (i.e., commit to repeated interaction at a cost), and when the continuation probability and payoff structure are favorable, they will rationally choose to form ties and cooperate, resulting in higher group efficiency (payoff) than in the absence of such ties, where defection prevails. The efficiency gain from tie formation is explicitly quantified as the difference in equilibrium payoffs between mutual cooperation and mutual defection, net of tie formation costs. The model also generalizes to other social dilemma games (e.g., Trust Game) and discusses the role of institutional design in facilitating or hindering efficient outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling mechanisms for repeated interaction and voluntary commitment (analogous to endogenous punishment or sanctioning institutions) can substantially increase efficiency in social dilemma environments, compared to control conditions without such mechanisms. While the model is not a direct PGG or experimental study, its formal results on equilibrium payoffs, tie formation, and cooperation are highly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in repeated, two-player social dilemmas with commitment options. The explicit mapping of efficiency gains to game parameters (payoff structure, continuation probability, cost of commitment) can inform predictions about the impact of punishment-like or commitment-enabling design features in related experimental games.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The main model is an indefinitely repeated game (infinite horizon with continuation probability w), which is essential for supporting cooperation and efficiency gains via tie formation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD and Trust Game are both binary (cooperate/defect or trust/abuse), i.e., all-or-nothing choices.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as the threat of future defection (trigger strategy) in repeated games; its effectiveness depends on the continuation probability and payoff structure.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players know the game is indefinitely repeated with a known continuation probability (w), i.e., the horizon is uncertain but the structure is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the tie formation scenario, players observe each other's past actions, enabling conditional strategies (punishment/reward). In the 'stranger' scenario, no such information is available.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the repeated tie scenario, players know who defected and can condition future play accordingly (i.e., punishment is targeted and identifiable).

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- It focuses on two-player (dyadic) games, with only brief discussion of n-player extensions.
- The model assumes strict rationality and complete information, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- Punishment and reward are endogenous via repeated interaction, not via explicit costly punishment/reward mechanisms as in many PGG experiments.
- The model does not address stochastic or noisy implementation of strategies, or bounded rationality.
