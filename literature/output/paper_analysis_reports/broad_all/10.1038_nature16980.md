## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocation to distant co-religionist (impartiality in coin allocation)
- favoritism towards self or local co-religionist
- association between beliefs in punitive/knowing gods and allocation behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the number of coins allocated to a distant co-religionist versus self or local co-religionist in a random allocation game. This is a behavioral measure of impartiality or norm compliance, not a direct measure of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. There is no explicit analysis of group payoff, efficiency, or surplus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that across eight diverse societies, individuals who believe their gods are more punitive and knowledgeable are more likely to allocate coins impartially to distant co-religionists, rather than favoring themselves or local co-religionists. This association is robust to controls for economic and demographic variables and is specific to moralistic gods (not local gods). The effect is interpreted as support for the hypothesis that belief in supernatural punishment can expand the radius of impartial prosociality. However, the experimental task is a random allocation game measuring norm compliance and impartiality, not a public goods game with group production or explicit efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that belief in supernatural punishment is associated with more impartial, less parochial allocation behavior in a norm compliance context. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment mechanisms on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar environments. The design lacks group production, peer punishment, or explicit efficiency measures. Thus, while the findings may inform hypotheses about the psychological mechanisms underlying cooperation and norm compliance, they do not directly support prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGG-like games with or without punishment. The evidence is best interpreted as informative about the potential for supernatural punishment beliefs to reduce parochialism and increase impartiality, not about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in economic games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays individually, allocating coins between two recipients (self vs distant co-religionist, or local vs distant co-religionist). There is no group interaction or multiplayer decision-making as in a standard PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant allocates 30 coins per game, playing two games (total 60 allocations). Each allocation is a single-shot decision; there are no repeated rounds with feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; all decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each allocation is binary: the coin goes to one cup or the other (all-or-nothing per coin).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know they will allocate 30 coins per game (two games, 60 allocations total).

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a public goods game and does not feature group production, peer punishment, or explicit efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- The main outcome is a behavioral measure of impartiality (allocation to distant co-religionists), not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- Punishment is not implemented as a game mechanism; only beliefs about supernatural punishment are measured.
- No direct evidence is provided about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in PGG-like environments.
- The findings are correlational with respect to beliefs and allocation behavior; causality cannot be firmly established.
