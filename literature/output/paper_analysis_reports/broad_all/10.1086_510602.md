## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that multilocus population genetics provides a general framework for modeling the evolution of social behaviors, including cooperation and punishment. In a simple two-locus model (one for cooperation, one for punishment), it finds that cooperation and punishment cannot invade unless cooperation is already favored by relatedness (i.e., punishment does not expand the parameter space for cooperation). Moreover, increased relatedness directly selects against punishment, as punishment is mutually costly. The only benefit to punishment comes from its association with cooperation, but this is insufficient to overcome the direct costs. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on evolutionary stability and gene frequency change.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its findings are relevant for understanding the evolutionary logic of cooperation and punishment, and suggest that costly punishment may not increase cooperation or group benefit except under restrictive conditions. However, because it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it should not be used as a primary source for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes in experimental public goods games. It may be useful for mechanistic or theoretical context, especially regarding the evolutionary logic of punishment and cooperation.

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Theoretical models consider pairs of individuals (2-player games) for cooperation and punishment; some models discuss groups of arbitrary size in principle.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Models are one-shot games per generation (single round per interaction), but evolutionary dynamics occur over many generations.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution choices are all-or-nothing (cooperate or defect; punish or forgive).

- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment cost to the punisher (a, e) is a model parameter; varied in analysis.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes as in public goods game experiments.
- Models are limited to simple two-player, one-shot games per generation; results may not generalize to larger groups or repeated interactions.
- Punishment is modeled as a genetic strategy, not as a behavioral response to observed defection in a repeated or multi-player context.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Findings may not directly translate to laboratory or field public goods games with human subjects.
