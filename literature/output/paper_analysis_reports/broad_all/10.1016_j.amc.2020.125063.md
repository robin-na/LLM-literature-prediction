# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Co-evolution of influence-based preferential selection and limited resource with multi-games on interdependent networks
Authors: Sun, C. B., & Luo, C.
Journal: Applied Mathematics and Computation
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency (pro-social and anti-social)
- punishment intensity
- villager satisfaction with leader
- leader attitudes and values
- leader demographics
Outcome notes:
The main behavioral outcome is leaders' willingness to engage in costly third-party punishment in a trust game. No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. Villager satisfaction is measured, but not as a direct payoff or efficiency metric.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that female water point leaders (WPLs) in rural Namibia are younger and better educated than male WPLs, but are often related to traditional elites. In a trust game with third-party punishment, female WPLs punish less frequently (not statistically significant) and significantly less intensively than male WPLs. However, these differences in punishment behavior do not translate into differences in villager satisfaction with leaders. The experiment does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence on gender differences in third-party punishment in a trust game, which is adjacent to public goods games. It shows that female leaders punish less, but this does not affect satisfaction. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The design and findings may be useful for understanding how leader characteristics affect norm enforcement, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The trust game involves three roles: Villager 1, Villager 2, and the leader (WPL) as third-party punisher. Each experimental group consists of these three participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The trust game is one-shot (single round) per pair, with leaders making conditional punishment decisions using the strategy method.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in the trust game are discrete: Villager 1 chooses 'right' or 'down'; Villager 2 chooses 'right' or 'down'.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For every $1 spent on punishment by the leader, the punished villager loses $3 (punishment cost to punisher = 1 per unit).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each $1 spent by the leader reduces the punished villager's earnings by $3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a one-shot (single round) experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Villagers are informed of the name of the leader (WPL) they are matched with, but decisions are made anonymously and payouts are structured to ensure anonymity.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game with third-party punishment, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior and villager satisfaction are measured.
- Sample size for leaders is small (32 WPLs), limiting statistical power for gender comparisons.
- Findings on punishment do not translate into efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, limiting direct relevance for efficiency prediction tasks.
- Cultural and institutional context may limit generalizability to other settings or standard laboratory PGGs.
