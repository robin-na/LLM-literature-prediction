# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Who Punishes? Personality Traits Predict Individual Variation in Punitive Sentiment
Authors: Roberts, S. C., Vakirtzis, A., Kristjánsdóttir, L., & Havlícek, J.
Journal: Evolutionary Psychology
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=other_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- anger towards transgressor
- desire to punish transgressor
- amount participant thinks transgressor should pay
- amount participant would personally pay to punish
- willingness to help group member
- sympathy for group member
- amount willing to give to help
Outcome notes:
All outcomes are self-reported responses to hypothetical scenarios, not actual payoffs or efficiency. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are measured or estimated.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that individual differences in willingness to punish a transgressor in a public-goods-like scenario are predicted by personality traits, especially lower Agreeableness. Other traits (Conscientiousness, Extraversion) have weaker or more limited associations. The study uses hypothetical scenarios where participants indicate their anger, desire to punish, and willingness to pay to punish a free-rider, but no actual economic game is played and no payoff or efficiency data are collected. The findings are relevant for understanding who is likely to engage in costly punishment, but do not inform the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or variants. It does not manipulate or measure any game design dimensions relevant to efficiency, nor does it report control or treatment efficiency. Its findings are about individual differences in punitive sentiment, not about the consequences of punishment for group outcomes. Therefore, it should not be used to predict efficiency effects of punishment based on game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Scenario specifies groups of 10 (participant plus 9 others).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Scenario is a one-shot interaction; participants are told the group is broken up after the scenario.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Scenario asks how much the participant would be willing to pay to punish, but does not specify the cost-to-impact ratio or actual mechanism.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Scenario is explicitly one-shot; participants know the group is broken up after the scenario.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Scenario describes that participants know the contributions of others (e.g., the transgressor contributed less).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses hypothetical scenarios, not real economic games with actual payoffs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are measured or reported.
- No manipulation or measurement of the effect of punishment on group outcomes.
- No formal public goods game structure (e.g., no explicit MPCR or multiplier).
- Personality measured with a brief (TIPI) scale with low internal consistency for some traits.
- Findings may not generalize to incentivized or real-world settings.
