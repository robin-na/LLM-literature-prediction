Behavioral outcomes (e.g., strategy adoption) are discussed as mechanisms, but the focus is on payoff-based efficiency and welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in the absence of strong governance mechanisms (punishment, compensation, or high ecological performance appraisal), all parties tend to adopt minimal effort strategies, resulting in low efficiency and poor environmental outcomes (prisoner's dilemma). Introducing punishment (penalties for non-compliance, ecological damage compensation), increasing the weight of ecological indicators in local government performance appraisal, and combining these tools can shift the system to a more efficient equilibrium, where local governments and enterprises fully implement and comply with environmental policies, even if central government supervision is weak. The effect of any single tool is limited, but their combination is necessary to achieve high efficiency in governance.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms (penalties, compensation for damage) and reward mechanisms (performance appraisal) are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from introducing punishment in public-goods-like governance settings. The results suggest that, in adjacent multi-agent settings, punishment alone may not be sufficient, but when combined with other governance tools, it can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence and magnitude of punishment, reward, and their combination should be considered as key moderators of treatment efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to weak incentives.

- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward exists in the form of performance appraisal for LGs (promotion incentives) and transfer payments (S, γS).
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost of rewards is modeled as transfer payments (S) from CG to LGs and from LGs to SEs (γS).

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirically validated with real-world data.
- Parameter values are chosen based on economic assumptions and prior literature, not direct measurement.
- The model does not include the public as a stakeholder, which may be important in real-world governance.
- The evolutionary game is adjacent to, but not an exact variant of, the standard public goods game; the structure is more complex and multi-level.
- No direct experimental or field data on actual efficiency changes from punishment interventions.
