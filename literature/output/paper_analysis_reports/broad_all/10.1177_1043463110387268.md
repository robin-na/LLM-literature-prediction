## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The key finding is that enabling agents to build management institutions in a CPR environment leads to much higher efficiency and group payoffs compared to scenarios where agents act only on individual beliefs. In the base (no-institution) model, payoffs are 12–35% of the optimum; with institution building, payoffs rise to 72–91% of the optimum. The effect is robust to changes in cost, resource abundance, and agent tolerance. The models suggest that the feedback loop between beliefs and institutions is critical: institutions prevent the selection of purely self-interested, over-exploiting agents, allowing cooperation and sustainable resource use to persist. The results are consistent with empirical findings from CPR experiments and field studies, though the models do not include explicit punishment or sanctioning.

## Prediction Guidance
For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that the introduction of effective, collectively agreed-upon management institutions in CPR/public-goods-like environments can transform group efficiency from low (tragedy of the commons) to high (near-optimal). While the models do not include explicit punishment, the institution acts as a binding rule, functionally similar to enforced cooperation. The results suggest that, in designs where institution-building is possible and effective, one should predict a large positive jump in efficiency relative to the control. However, the absence of explicit punishment or sanctioning means the findings are most directly relevant to institution-building or rule-setting treatments, not to punishment per se.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agent-based model uses n=100 agents; analytical model discusses n as a parameter. No direct analysis of player count effect on efficiency, but model structure allows for variation.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 2,000 periods (each of 10 rounds); equilibrium and long-run outcomes are reported.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Simulation length is fixed and known to the modeler; not relevant for agent knowledge.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled; institutions function as binding rules but do not include monitoring or penalties for noncompliance.
- Agents always comply with institutional rules; no cheating or rule-breaking is allowed, which limits realism for some empirical settings.
- No communication or social influence among agents is modeled; belief updates are individual and based on payoffs.
- The model is theoretical/simulation-based and not empirically calibrated to real-world data.
- Findings are most directly relevant to institution-building or rule-setting interventions, not to punishment treatments per se.
