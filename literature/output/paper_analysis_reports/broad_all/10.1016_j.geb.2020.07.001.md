# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Corrupt police
Authors: Abbink, K., Ryvkin, D., & Serra, D.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- citizen average payoff
- officer average payoff
- combined payoffs (group welfare)
- payoff ratio (citizen-to-officer)
- inequality
Non-payoff outcomes:
- crime rate (law-breaking rate)
- frequency of fines
- frequency of bribes
- bribe size
- targeting of bribes
- conditional cooperation
- behavioral persistence
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are both payoff-based (average payoffs, welfare, inequality) and behavioral (crime rate, bribe frequency, etc.). Efficiency is directly addressed via group payoffs and their relation to full cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three main conditions: no police, honest police (can only fine), and corrupt police (can fine or demand bribes). With no police, crime rates are very high and citizen payoffs are low. Honest police reduce crime and increase citizen payoffs. Corrupt police also reduce crime compared to no police, but citizen payoffs are much lower than with honest police due to high bribe payments to officers, resulting in strong inequality. Bribes are used as a substitute for fines, and are often targeted at law-breakers, but imperfectly so—many law-abiding citizens are also extorted. When the maximum bribe is capped at the fine level, crime rates rise to near the no-police level, showing that the deterrent effect of bribes depends on their size. Reward mechanisms for officers based on low bribery rates (but not low crime rates) moderately reduce both bribery and crime, and increase citizen payoffs, but do not eliminate the efficiency loss from corruption.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that enabling punishment (via police, even if corrupt) increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment baselines, but the efficiency gain is much smaller when punishment is corrupt and redistributive (bribes) rather than legal (fines). The efficiency effect of punishment depends on both the targeting and the cost/impact of punishment: when bribes are large and targeted at law-breakers, crime is deterred, but citizen payoffs are much lower due to wealth transfer to officers. If bribes are capped at the fine level, the deterrent effect is lost. Reward mechanisms for officers based on low bribery rates can partially mitigate the efficiency loss from corruption. For prediction, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the structure (honest vs. corrupt, bribe size, targeting) and the presence of anti-corruption incentives are critical moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each society consists of 9 citizens and 3 police officers; citizens interact in groups of 3 with 1 officer per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for 15 rounds in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (law-breaking) is a binary choice: break the law or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each law-breaker gains 20 ECU, each of the other two in the group loses 20 ECU. This is a negative externality game, not a standard PGG, but the marginal per-capita return (for the group) is -20/3 per law-breaker.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For fines: cost to punisher (officer) is zero; for bribes: cost to punisher is negative (officer gains the bribe).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Fines: 27 ECU (net loss to citizen is 24 after redistribution); bribes: up to 50 ECU (full loss to citizen, full gain to officer). Bribes are more severe than fines, and their deterrent effect depends on size.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In some treatments, officers receive small rewards based on low crime or low bribery rates.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is 6 ECU per citizen not breaking the law (or not paying a bribe), up to 54 ECU per round per officer. Cost is external (from experimenter/NGO), not from other players.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is effective only in the 'low bribery' condition, moderately reducing both bribery and crime.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 15 rounds, and one is randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Citizens see the law-breaking choices of the other two citizens in their group and the officer's action toward themselves, but not about other groups or other officer actions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Officers and citizens are anonymous; no identifiers are shown. Citizens do not know which officer punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a negative externality/prisoner's dilemma variant, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to standard PGGs.
- Punishment is centralized (by officers), not peer-to-peer; peer punishment effects are not tested.
- Bribes are redistributive, not costly to the punisher, so the efficiency effects of punishment differ from standard costly punishment in PGGs.
- Lab setting with student subjects may limit external validity to real-world police/citizen interactions.
- Reward mechanisms are small relative to possible bribe income; effects may differ with larger or endogenous rewards.
- Imperfect targeting of punishment (bribes) means some law-abiding citizens are punished, which may not match all real-world settings.
