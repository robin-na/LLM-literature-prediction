# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Third-party punishers do not compete to be chosen as partners in an experimental game
Authors: Batistoni, T., Barclay, P., & Raihani, N. J.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount invested in punishment/helping (costly signal)
- amount entrusted in Trust Game (by bystander)
- percentage of endowment returned in Trust Game (trustworthiness)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of being chosen as a partner
- preference for helpers vs punishers
- investment in punishment/helping as a function of observability and partner choice
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: investment in punishment/helping, partner choice, and trust/trustworthiness in a Trust Game. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff in a public goods context. The closest payoff-related outcomes are individual-level trust and trustworthiness in a dyadic Trust Game, and the cost paid for punishment/helping.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that third-party punishment and helping are both used as signals of cooperative intent, but helping is a stronger and less ambiguous signal than punishment. Observability increases investments in both, but the possibility of partner choice does not further escalate investments. Higher investments in punishment or helping increase the likelihood of being chosen as a partner and being trusted, but this effect is much stronger for helping. Investments in both are positively associated with actual trustworthiness in a Trust Game. The study does not address group efficiency or total payoff in a public goods game, but provides evidence on the reputational and signaling functions of punishment and helping.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent to PGGs, using a Dictator Game with third-party punishment/helping and a subsequent Trust Game. The main outcomes are individual-level signaling, partner choice, and trust/trustworthiness, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment is a weaker and more ambiguous signal of cooperative intent than helping, and that reputational incentives may increase costly punishment/helping, but do not escalate under partner choice. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in PGGs, this paper provides only indirect, contextual evidence about the social and reputational motivations for punishment, not about its effect on group payoff or efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main third-party punishment/helping stage involves a Dictator, a third-party, and a passive receiver (3 per group), with a bystander added in the Trust Game (dyadic). The signaling/partner choice stage involves pairs of third-parties and a bystander.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes a single decision per stage; the design is one-shot (not repeated rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (punishment/helping) is continuous: third-parties can invest any amount from $0.00 to $0.45 in $0.01 increments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:1; each $0.01 spent by the third-party reduces the dictator's payoff by $0.01.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (fee-to-fine ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. There is a helping (reward) condition, between-subjects; third-parties can help the receiver instead of punishing the dictator.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (help) cost is 1:1; each $0.01 spent by the third-party increases the receiver's payoff by $0.01.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (fee-to-help ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure and that the game is one-shot; no uncertainty about number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In some conditions, bystanders are informed of third-party investments (knowledge conditions); in others, they are not.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Bystanders are informed of the specific investment of each third-party in the knowledge and partner choice conditions, allowing identification for partner selection.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game; it uses a Dictator Game with third-party punishment/helping and a subsequent Trust Game, so findings may not generalize to PGG efficiency.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported; all outcomes are individual-level or dyadic.
- The study is conducted online with a predominantly Western sample, limiting generalizability.
- Comprehension rates were low, and some analyses are sensitive to inclusion/exclusion of participants who failed comprehension checks.
- The design is one-shot, not repeated, so dynamic effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency are not addressed.
- Partner choice did not yield direct, guaranteed material benefits, which may have limited competitive signaling effects.
- Analyses were not pre-registered, and some analytical decisions were made post hoc.
