## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, cooperation typically declines over time due to negative affect and conditional cooperation. However, when a punishment option is introduced, cooperation rates remain high, as punishment deters defection. The motivation to punish is linked to affective responses, such as anger at defectors and satisfaction from punishing. The paper notes that punishment can sometimes be misapplied (e.g., punishing cooperators), which can undermine cooperation. The findings are based on a synthesis of prior experimental studies, not new empirical data, and focus on behavioral and affective outcomes rather than direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong qualitative support for the idea that introducing punishment in public goods games can sustain high cooperation rates and prevent the typical decline seen in repeated games without punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report new experimental results. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is likely to increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency, provided punishment is targeted at defectors. The review also cautions that punishment can be counterproductive if misapplied. The paper is most useful for understanding the mechanisms and moderators of punishment effects, but less so for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment reduces the punished player's earnings by more than the cost to the punisher (e.g., 1 cost, 3 deduction).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects are typically informed about the number of rounds (e.g., 10 or 15), and sometimes surprised with a restart.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In punishment games, players see the contributions of others before assigning punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or quantitative efficiency outcomes.
- Outcomes discussed are primarily behavioral (contribution, cooperation, punishment) and affective, not direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Findings are based on synthesis of prior studies, which may have varying designs and parameters.
- The review highlights moderators and mechanisms but does not provide direct quantitative guidance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment.
- Some claims about affect and motivation are based on self-report or neural correlates, which may not directly translate to economic outcomes.
