The main outcomes are the expected total cost of interference (investment), the stationary frequency of cooperation, and the expected number of interventions. These are payoff-related in the sense of resource expenditure and group-level cooperation, but not group payoff or efficiency in the public goods sense. No direct analysis of group payoff, surplus, or efficiency as defined by total group earnings relative to a cooperative optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This work provides analytical and numerical results showing that an external decision-maker can promote high levels of cooperation in a population of self-interested agents by using a cost-efficient, state-dependent rewarding scheme. Rather than always rewarding cooperators, it is more cost-effective to reward only when the number of cooperators is below a threshold. The optimal threshold depends on the per-individual reward cost, the intensity of selection, and the desired cooperation level. For a wide range of parameters, this targeted approach achieves the same or higher cooperation at a lower total cost than always-on institutional incentives. The model does not directly analyze group payoff or efficiency in the public goods sense, but the results are closely related to maximizing cooperation frequency while minimizing resource expenditure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is relevant for predicting the effect of reward-based interventions (not punishment) on cooperation and cost-efficiency in Prisoner's Dilemma-like settings. It suggests that targeted, conditional incentives can be more cost-effective than unconditional ones, especially under weak selection. However, the model is not a public goods game and does not analyze punishment, so direct quantitative predictions for PGG efficiency with punishment are not possible. The findings support the idea that the design of incentive schemes (e.g., when and whom to reward) strongly moderates the cost-effectiveness of cooperation promotion, and that always-on incentives may be suboptimal. For downstream prediction, the results are most informative for settings with external, state-dependent rewards, and less so for punishment or standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N is a key parameter in the model (e.g., N=100 in figures). The model is for finite, well-mixed populations.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between two discrete strategies: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The core intervention is an external reward (investment) given to cooperators, either always or conditionally.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Per-individual reward cost (theta) is a key parameter; higher cost can achieve higher cooperation but increases total expenditure.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is modeled as the increase in payoff per unit investment; higher effectiveness (or higher theta) increases cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is based on the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game or its direct variants.
- Only external reward/investment (not punishment or sanctions) is modeled; no analysis of punishment effects.
- Outcomes focus on cooperation frequency and cost of intervention, not group payoff or efficiency as defined by total group earnings relative to a cooperative optimum.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are theoretical and simulation-based.
- The model assumes a well-mixed, finite population and does not address structured populations or multi-player games (though these are mentioned as future work).
- The model does not address the source of the incentive budget or the second-order free rider problem.
