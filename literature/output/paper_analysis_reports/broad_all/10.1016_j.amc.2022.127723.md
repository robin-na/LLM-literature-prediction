## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prosocial punishment expenditure
- antisocial punishment expenditure
- contribution amount
- proportion of individual types (Collaboration Enthusiast, Fence Sitter, Rational Bystander)
- peer punishment preference
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are behavioral: expenditures on prosocial and antisocial punishment, contribution levels, and the distribution of behavioral types. The model does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes, though payoff is used in the learning rule and as a basis for strategy updating.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that the effectiveness and efficiency of central power (e.g., the Rule of Law) determine the prevalence of antisocial punishment in a population, while prosocial punishment remains stable. Antisocial punishment is highest at intermediate levels of central power and suppressed when central power is either very strong or very weak. The population can be decomposed into three basic types (Collaboration Enthusiast, Fence Sitter, Rational Bystander), and the central power shifts the proportions of these types, which in turn determines the observed punishment and contribution behaviors. The model is validated at the 'phenomenal level' by comparison to cross-regional experimental data, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for understanding how institutional context (central power) shapes the behavioral landscape of punishment and cooperation in public goods games. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions and control efficiency. It is most useful for predicting how changes in central authority or social norms might shift the prevalence of antisocial punishment and, indirectly, contribution rates. For direct efficiency prediction, empirical or simulation studies that report group payoff or efficiency are needed.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups in the model are of size n=4; population size and number of groups are varied in robustness checks, but the main results use n=4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Construction stage uses 300 rounds (also checked with 500 and 1000); expression stage uses 10 rounds.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is mu/n = 1.6/4 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays e_ij; punished loses 3*e_ij. e_ij is randomly assigned from 1 to 10.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed at 3 units lost per 1 unit spent.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Central power redistributes penalties as rewards to high contributors in the construction stage.

## Important Limitations
- The model does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes; main outcomes are behavioral (punishment expenditures, contribution rates, type proportions).
- Findings are based on a theoretical and simulation model, not direct empirical or experimental measurement of efficiency.
- The model assumes fixed punishment effectiveness and cost structure (3:1 ratio), which may not generalize to all PGG settings.
- No direct manipulation or analysis of communication, framing, or information dimensions.
- Central power is modeled as an exogenous parameter; real-world institutions may be more complex.
