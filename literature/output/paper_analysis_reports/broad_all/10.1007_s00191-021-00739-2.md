## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that cooperation in groups and societies is fragile and subject to collapse due to endogenous factors such as group growth, increasing complexity, cognitive limits, and the ceremonialization of institutions. Collapse is often sudden and asymmetric with respect to emergence, meaning that the conditions under which cooperation breaks down are not simply the reverse of those under which it emerges. Theoretical mechanisms include the role of group size (with a cognitive upper limit for informal cooperation), the importance of network structure (with clustered and heterogeneous networks supporting more robust cooperation), and the increasing costs of monitoring and punishment as cooperation declines. The paper also notes that high punishment cultures may be associated with lower cooperation and socio-economic thriving. However, all findings are theoretical or based on secondary synthesis; no new empirical or experimental data are presented.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for how group size, network structure, and the costs of monitoring and punishment may affect the emergence and collapse of cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that as groups grow or as network structures become less favorable, cooperation may collapse suddenly and may not be easily restored. The paper also highlights that high punishment costs and cultures may undermine cooperation. However, because the paper is theoretical and does not report empirical data on efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in specific public goods game designs. Its main value is in highlighting potential moderators and mechanisms that could inform the interpretation of empirical results from other studies.
