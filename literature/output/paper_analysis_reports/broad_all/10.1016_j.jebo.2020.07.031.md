## Source
Title: Norm compliance, enforcement, and the survival of redistributive institutions
Authors: Gürdal, M. Y., Torul, O., & Vostroknutov, A.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior

- post-tax income
- difference between pre-tax and post-tax income
- income Gini coefficient
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution ratio (contribution/income)

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the contribution ratio (behavioral). Some payoff-related outcomes (post-tax income, Gini, difference between pre- and post-tax income) are reported, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed. Fairness perception is also a key outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that in the absence of enforcement, contributions to redistribution decay rapidly due to free riding, even among individuals with a high propensity to follow rules. Exclusion (random checks with exclusion for non-compliance) slows the decay but does not prevent it. Only full enforcement (computer-enforced contributions) sustains high contributions and reduces post-tax income inequality. Rule-following individuals initially try to follow the rules but eventually reduce contributions in response to free riding. Rule-followers prefer institutions with strong rules when enforcement is present, but switch to unregulated institutions when enforcement is absent and free riding is prevalent. The paper reports post-tax income and Gini coefficients, but does not directly analyze efficiency as group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum. The findings suggest that some form of enforcement is necessary to sustain redistribution and prevent the collapse of cooperative institutions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of enforcement (a form of exogenous punishment) on group outcomes in public-goods-like environments. While it does not implement a classic costly punishment mechanism, it shows that enforcement (computer-enforced contributions) is necessary to sustain high contributions and reduce inequality; exclusion as a sanction slows but does not prevent decay. In the absence of enforcement, contributions and redistribution collapse. The results suggest that, in similar designs, enabling effective enforcement or punishment is likely to increase group welfare and reduce inequality, but the paper does not report efficiency in the sense of group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark. The findings are most informative for settings where enforcement is exogenous and not costly to the enforcer. The evidence is less directly applicable to classic peer punishment with explicit costs. Control efficiency (without enforcement) is low due to free riding; treatment efficiency (with enforcement) is likely much higher, but the exact ratio is not reported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sessions had 16-19 subjects; groupings within institutions are endogenous and variable, as subjects choose institutions each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous between 0 and full income, except in Enforcement treatment where minimums are enforced by institution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each institution's tax pool is divided equally among its members; the marginal per-capita return depends on group size and is not fixed, but is always less than 1 (since the contributed amount is split among all in the institution).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Enforcement is either perfect (computer-enforced, no free riding), exclusion (random checks with exclusion for non-compliance), or absent. Higher 'punishment tech' (enforcement) sustains contributions and reduces inequality.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see their own and others' average initial income, average contribution, and average final income in their institution.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not implement classic peer punishment with explicit cost to the punisher; enforcement is exogenous (computer-enforced) or via exclusion.
- Efficiency, defined as group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark, is not directly reported or analyzed.
- Payoff-based outcomes are reported (post-tax income, Gini), but the main dependent variable is contribution ratio (behavioral).
- Institution group sizes are endogenous and variable, as subjects choose institutions each round.
- The exclusion treatment uses random checks and exclusion, which is not directly comparable to standard punishment mechanisms.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with costly peer punishment or different group structures.
