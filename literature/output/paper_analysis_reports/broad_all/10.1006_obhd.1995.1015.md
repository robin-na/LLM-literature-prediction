# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: FRAMING IN RESOURCE DILEMMAS - LOSS AVERSION AND THE MODERATING EFFECTS OF SANCTIONS
Authors: MCCUSKER, C., & CARNEVALE, P. J.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 1995

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation (amount contributed to public fund or not taken from public fund)
- contributions to sanctioning system
- frequency of receiving reward or penalty
- exploitation of cooperative others
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is cooperation (contribution or non-extraction), not group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. No explicit analysis of efficiency, welfare, or group payoff is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both penalty and reward sanction systems increase cooperation in resource dilemmas, with reward sanctions producing the highest cooperation. The difference in cooperation between 'give' and 'take' frames (i.e., public goods vs. commons) disappears when sanctions are present. The effect of sanctions is robust across two experiments, and the framing of the sanction (give vs. take) affects contributions to the sanction fund but not cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the direct effect of sanctions on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that endogenous, group-funded reward and penalty sanctions increase cooperation in public-goods-like resource dilemmas, with reward sanctions being more effective. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation rates, not payoffs. The design details (e.g., player count, rounds, sanctioning mechanism) are informative for mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency data limits direct applicability to the efficiency prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject was told they were in a group of 8 (1 subject + 7 simulated others).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 12 rounds (trials) per experiment, though subjects were told there would be 10-20.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions were made via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/take decisions were continuous from 0 to 25 tickets per round.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Public fund doubled and divided equally among all group members at the end; exact MPCR per round not specified, but structure is equivalent to a standard PGG with end-of-game payout.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects could contribute 0-25 tickets per round to the penalty fund from their personal fund; cost per unit is 1 ticket lost per 1 ticket added to the fund.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Penalty fund total is subtracted from the least cooperative individual's personal fund; 1:1 cost-to-impact ratio.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward sanction condition present; most cooperative individual receives the total reward fund.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects could contribute 0-25 tickets per round to the reward fund from their personal fund; cost per unit is 1 ticket lost per 1 ticket added to the fund.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward fund total is added to the most cooperative individual's personal fund; 1:1 cost-to-impact ratio.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told there would be 10-20 rounds, but there were always 12; the exact number was not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects received feedback on their own and others' (aggregate) contributions/takings, public fund size, and personal fund sizes after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only cooperation and sanction contributions are measured.
- All other group members were simulated by computer, not real human participants.
- The sanctioning system is endogenous (group-funded), which may differ from externally imposed punishment/reward systems.
- The number of rounds was not known to participants in advance, which may affect endgame behavior.
- The study uses a lottery ticket paradigm, which may not map directly to monetary payoffs or real-world public goods.
- No analysis of the cost-effectiveness or net welfare impact of sanctions is provided.
