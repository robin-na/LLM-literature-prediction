# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Effect of Leader Reward and Punishment Behaviors on Subordinates' Budget Reports
Authors: Chen, M. L.
Journal: Engineering Economist
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of slack retained (PSR)
- budgetary slack
- honesty in budget reporting
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the proportion of slack retained (PSR), which measures the extent to which subordinates overstate costs in their budget reports. This is a behavioral measure of dishonesty/slack creation, not a direct measure of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. No group payoff or efficiency outcome is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that hands-off (no reward or punishment) is least effective in reducing budgetary slack. Punishment alone reduces slack more than reward alone, but the combination of reward and punishment (carrot-stick) is most effective. The effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is large relative to the benefit of slack creation. Rewards alone, regardless of size, have little effect. Requiring a factual assertion (honesty) also reduces slack. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (slack/honesty).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment, especially when combined with reward, reduces dishonest behavior (budgetary slack) in a principal-agent reporting game. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it is a one-superior/one-subordinate budget reporting game, not a multi-player public goods game. The findings are most useful for understanding the behavioral impact of punishment and reward on honesty/slack, but less so for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session is a one-superior/one-subordinate game (effectively 2 players, but only the subordinate is a human participant; the superior is a computer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 20 rounds (10 rounds with one incentive level, 10 rounds with another).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; all interaction is via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (cost report) is continuous in $5 increments between actual cost and $900.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to the superior is $1 per unit, reducing subordinate's earnings by $5 (high) or $2 (low). Both high and low punishment cost/effectiveness are manipulated.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is $5 or $2 per $1 cost to the punisher. High punishment is more effective at reducing slack than low punishment.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward is available in carrot and carrot-stick treatments. Reward alone has little effect on slack reduction.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward cost to the superior is $1 per unit, increasing subordinate's earnings by $5 (high) or $2 (low).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is $5 or $2 per $1 cost to the rewarder. Reward alone is not effective at reducing slack.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed of the number of rounds and the structure of the experiment.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-superior/one-subordinate (principal-agent) design, not a multi-player public goods game.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (slack/honesty) are measured.
- All participants are students, not real managers.
- The setting is abstract and excludes real-world influences such as reputation, monitoring, and uncertainty.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as explicit monetary incentives, which may not capture all real-world leader behaviors.
