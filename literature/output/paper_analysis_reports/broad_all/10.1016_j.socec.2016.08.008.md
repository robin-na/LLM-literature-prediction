# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The advantage of hierarchy: Inducing responsibility and selecting ability?
Authors: Otto, P. E., & Bolle, F.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group production (total public good generated)
- average incomes of all workers
- average group payoff
- principal's payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- allocator's own contribution
- satisfaction ratings
- fairness ratings
- anger ratings
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group production (total public good generated) and average incomes, which are direct measures of efficiency. Non-payoff outcomes such as contribution rates and subjective ratings are also reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares three regimes: standard PGG (voluntary contributions), AG (randomly assigned allocator determines all contributions), and PAG (principal selects allocator each round). Both AG and PAG significantly increase group production and average incomes relative to PGG. PAG (principal selection) yields the highest efficiency, with further increases in satisfaction and fairness ratings. The effect is not due to learning or order effects. The mechanism works by centralizing responsibility and leveraging selection, not by explicit punishment. In treatments with heterogeneous productivity, the most productive worker is more likely to be appointed allocator, but does not exploit the position to reduce own effort. The findings show that hierarchy can substitute for punishment in sustaining high efficiency in public goods provision.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing hierarchical control (an allocator or principal-allocator structure) in a public goods game can substantially increase efficiency, even in the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a central authority who can determine or influence contributions should be expected to yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those typically attributed to punishment. The results are robust across group size (n=3), number of rounds (10 per regime), and both homogeneous and heterogeneous productivity. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is not available but hierarchy is, efficiency can be high. For prediction, if a game design includes a mechanism where one player can set others' contributions (with or without selection by a principal), expect efficiency to be much higher than in standard PGG, even if punishment is absent.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consist of 3 workers plus 1 principal (in PAG), but the core public goods game is played by 3 workers.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each regime (PGG, AG, PAGci, PAGici) is played for 10 rounds; total of 40 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is mentioned; all decisions are made via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: workers can contribute any amount from 0 to 20 eurocents in 1 cent increments.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Treatment 1, MPCR is 0.5 (each cent contributed yields 0.5 cent to each of 3 players); in Treatment 2, MPCR varies by worker productivity (0.4, 0.5, 0.6).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed before each stage about the number of rounds (10 per regime).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are shown a summary table with all available information about previous rounds. In AG and PAGci, contributions of allocators are revealed with their worker number.

## Important Limitations
- The hierarchical mechanisms studied (allocator/principal) are not punishment or sanctioning mechanisms per se, but rather centralization of contribution decisions; thus, direct transferability to punishment-enabled PGGs may be limited.
- Group size is fixed at 3 workers (plus principal in some regimes), so results may not generalize to larger groups.
- The experiment uses a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- No explicit punishment or reward options are present, so the findings are about hierarchy as a substitute for, not a complement to, punishment.
- The design does not include communication or chat between players, which may affect generalizability to settings with communication.
