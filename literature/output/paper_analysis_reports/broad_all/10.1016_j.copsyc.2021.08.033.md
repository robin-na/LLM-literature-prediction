The review discusses both payoff-based outcomes (individual and relative payoffs, sometimes group payoff) and behavioral outcomes (cooperation, strategy use, punishment via non-cooperation). Efficiency per se is not the main focus, but payoff and surplus distribution are central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that extortionate strategies in iterated Prisoner's Dilemma games can enforce high cooperation from coplayers, but at the cost of an unequal payoff distribution favoring the extortioner. When coplayers attempt to punish extortioners by withholding cooperation, both parties' payoffs decrease, demonstrating a form of costly punishment. In symmetric settings, this can discipline extortioners, but in asymmetric settings (e.g., with replacement power or extra incentives), extortioners can persist and maintain higher payoffs. The review highlights that the efficiency of the group (total payoff) may not increase with extortion, as the gains are captured by the extortioner and punishment reduces total surplus. The findings are based on a synthesis of theory and several experimental studies, but the review itself does not report new experimental data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It suggests that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, costly punishment via withholding cooperation) on efficiency depends on the symmetry of the game and the power structure. In symmetric games, punishment can reduce the extortioner's advantage but at a cost to both players, lowering efficiency. In asymmetric games, extortioners can avoid being disciplined and extract higher payoffs, further reducing the coplayer's share. The review does not directly address public goods games or classic punishment institutions, but the mechanisms discussed (costly punishment, power asymmetry, incentive structures) are relevant for predicting when punishment will improve or reduce efficiency in related environments. The evidence is more about distributional outcomes and the limits of punishment than about average efficiency increases from punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Most experiments discussed are 2-player (Prisoner's Dilemma), but one experiment involves 3 players (employer and two employees). The effect of player count is not directly analyzed, but the review notes that power asymmetry (e.g., one vs. two) changes outcomes.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Iterated games with 60 rounds or blocks of 10 rounds are discussed. Longer games allow for strategy evolution and punishment attempts. The number of rounds is not systematically varied.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Prisoner's Dilemma is a binary (all-or-nothing) choice: cooperate or defect. The review does not discuss continuous contribution games.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as costly withholding of cooperation (not direct punishment). The cost is the loss of own payoff. The review notes that this reduces both players' payoffs.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is indirect (withholding cooperation), and its effectiveness is limited, especially in asymmetric games. In symmetric games, it can discipline extortioners but at a cost.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Number of rounds is specified in experiments (e.g., 60 rounds), but it is not clear if players know the total number in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In the employer-employee experiment, all subjects are informed about the decisions of all group members after each round. This may affect strategy and punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The review focuses on Prisoner's Dilemma games, not public goods games; findings may not fully generalize to PGG environments.
- Punishment is implemented as costly withholding of cooperation, not as explicit punishment institutions; this limits direct applicability to PGG punishment treatments.
- Efficiency is not directly measured; most outcomes are individual or relative payoffs, not group efficiency ratios.
- The review synthesizes prior experiments and theory but does not report new experimental data.
- Power asymmetry and incentive structures are discussed, but not systematically manipulated across all dimensions relevant to PGGs.
