# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Structure of Human Prosociality: Differentiating Altruistically Motivated, Norm Motivated, Strategically Motivated, and Self-Reported Prosocial Behavior
Authors: Böckler, A., Tusche, A., & Singer, T.
Journal: Social Psychological and Personality Science
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount given in dictator game
- amount given in trust game
- amount invested in punishment (second and third person punishment games)
- helping in Zurich Prosocial Game
- donations in donation task
- prosocial choices in social value orientation
- social discounting
- self-reported prosocialness
- Machiavellianism
- empathy (Interpersonal Reactivity Index)
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral and self-report measures of prosociality, including punishment behavior, giving, helping, and strategic/norm-based actions. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported. All outcomes are at the individual level and concern choices or propensities, not group-level payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that prosocial behavior is multidimensional, with four robust factors: altruistically motivated, norm motivated (including punishment and reciprocity), strategically motivated, and self-reported prosociality. Punishment behavior (in second and third person punishment games) loads on the 'norm motivated' factor, indicating its role in norm enforcement. The study replicates standard findings in economic games (e.g., people punish unfairness, give more in dictator than in punishment games, help more when reciprocated), but does not analyze efficiency or group payoff. The factor structure is stable across two large samples. Altruistic prosociality is associated with lower negative affect and higher cognitive skills. Gender, age, and income show differential associations with the factors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that punishment behavior is a distinct, norm-motivated component of prosociality, separable from altruistic and strategic motivations. However, it does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The design details of the punishment games (one-shot, anonymous, fixed punishment effectiveness) are informative for mapping to game dimensions, but the lack of payoff or efficiency outcomes limits its direct predictive value for the downstream task. The study is most useful for understanding the motivational structure underlying punishment and cooperation, not for quantifying efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All economic games are two- or three-player (e.g., dictator game: 2 players; second person punishment game: 2 players; third person punishment game: 3 players).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) or have a small fixed number of rounds (e.g., 2 rounds for dictator game, 2 or 3 rounds for punishment games). No repeated or iterated games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all games are anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment choices are continuous (participants choose how many MUs to give or punish, in increments of 1 MU). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a public goods game, but in the trust game, transferred amount is tripled before being assigned to the other player (implies a multiplier of 3 for the trust game). In punishment games, for every MU spent on punishment, 3 MUs are subtracted from the target.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In punishment games, Player B (or C) can invest MUs to punish Player A; for every MU invested, 3 MUs are subtracted from Player A. The cost to the punisher is 1 MU per unit of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (for every 1 MU spent, 3 MUs are deducted from the punished player).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed about the number of rounds (all games are one-shot or have a fixed, small number of rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the endowments and choices of other players as required for the game (e.g., in punishment games, Player B sees Player A's offer; in ZPG, participants see the other player's actions).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are anonymous; players do not know the identity of others. Punishment is not attributed to a specific individual beyond the game role.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus; all outcomes are individual-level behavioral or self-report measures.
- No public goods game or direct PGG variant is included; the closest are dictator, trust, and punishment games.
- Punishment is studied only in one-shot, anonymous, small-group games, not in repeated or group public goods contexts.
- No analysis of how punishment affects efficiency or group payoff; cannot inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- No manipulation of punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness) beyond the fixed 3:1 ratio.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are present.
