Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average group contributions (interpreted as efficiency), group earnings, and Gini coefficients. Behavioral outcomes such as contribution ratios and allocation rules are also analyzed, but the primary focus is on efficiency and inequality.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 rounds; GM is introduced in round 6.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication is allowed; all interactions are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: any integer between 0 and endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (beta) is varied across treatments (1.2, 1.6, 1.8, 2.2); higher beta leads to higher efficiency under GM.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed about the number of rounds in each phase; instructions for rounds 6-15 are given after round 5.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see summary of all players' endowments, contributions, allocations, and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocations are made anonymously, but group members are identified as player 1, 2, or 3; identities are not persistent across rounds.

## Important Limitations
- The mechanism tested (GM) is not a standard punishment or reward mechanism; it is a two-stage allocation based on observed contributions, so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs may be limited.
- All experiments are with 3 players and Chinese university students; generalizability to larger groups or other populations is untested.
- No chat or communication is allowed; real-world teams may have richer communication.
- Endowment heterogeneity is randomized each round, which may differ from real-world settings where roles are stable.
- The GM is tested only in a lab setting with full anonymity and random rematching; effects may differ in repeated or fixed groups.
- No explicit test of costly punishment or reward; only endogenous allocation is studied.
