# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Voting, punishment, and public goods
Authors: Kroll, S., Cherry, T. L., & Shogren, J. F.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (percentage of potential payoff realized by the group)
- average group payoff
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- cheating frequency
- punishment frequency
- number of punishers
- punishment cost
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum) and contribution levels. Punishment and cheating rates are also reported, but efficiency is explicitly analyzed and compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a linear public goods game with five players and repeated rounds, the introduction of a nonbinding voting mechanism alone does not significantly increase efficiency or sustain higher contributions compared to the standard individual contribution baseline. However, when a costly peer punishment mechanism is added—allowing group members to punish those who deviate from the majority-voted contribution plan—both contributions and efficiency increase substantially and persistently. The efficiency in the nonbinding-vote-with-punishment treatment is significantly higher than in the nonbinding-vote treatment and approaches the efficiency observed in the binding-vote (third-party enforced) treatment. The cost of punishment is more than offset by the gains in group payoff, so overall efficiency rises with punishment enabled.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with five players, 10 rounds per stage, and a marginal per-capita return of 0.4, enabling costly peer punishment in a nonbinding voting environment leads to a large and persistent increase in efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust and the efficiency gain outweighs the cost of punishment. Thus, when predicting the efficiency of a public goods game with punishment enabled, this paper supports a substantial positive adjustment relative to the control (no-punishment) efficiency, provided the punishment mechanism is similar (costly, peer-administered, and targeted at non-cooperators). The findings are directly relevant for games with similar design parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment had two stages of 10 rounds each (20 rounds total).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous from 0 to 10 tokens (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (a) was 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost was nonlinear: e.g., 1 punishment point cost 1 token, 5 points cost 9 tokens, 10 points cost 30 tokens. Each punishment point reduced the target's earnings by 10%.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point cost the punisher 1-30 tokens (nonlinear) and reduced the punished's earnings by 10% per point, up to 100%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were not told in advance that there would be a second stage of 10 rounds; after period 10, they learned there would be 10 more rounds. Within each stage, the number of rounds was known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the proposals, votes, and (in punishment treatments) who was punished and by how much. Contribution summaries were available.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was not anonymous; group members could see who punished whom and by how much.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment used homogeneous groups of university students, which may limit generalizability to more diverse or real-world populations.
- The game was a linear public goods game with fixed group size (n=5) and specific parameters (mpcr=0.4); results may differ with other group sizes, payoff structures, or marginal returns.
- Punishment was only allowed against those who deviated from the majority-voted proposal, not for any other reason.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The experiment did not test heterogeneous endowments or preferences, which may affect the impact of punishment.
- The number of rounds was limited (20 total), and long-term or repeated play effects are not explored.
- Subjects were not informed in advance about the second stage, which may affect behavior at the stage transition.
