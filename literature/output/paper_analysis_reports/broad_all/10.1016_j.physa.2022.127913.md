## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the introduction of prosocial punishment in public goods games, especially when implemented on small-world networks, can substantially increase group efficiency (average payoff) by suppressing defectors and supporting the dominance of cooperative strategies. The effect is strongest when the network structure balances local clustering (which supports network reciprocity) and shortcuts (which allow punishers to reach defectors). Both the strength of punishment (fine) and the sensitivity of punishment implementation are important moderators. Regular lattices support cooperation but mainly favor cooperator-driven punishers, while random networks suppress punishment and cooperation. Small-world networks enable both high cooperation and high efficiency, with an optimal range of network randomness maximizing the effect. The model provides direct predictions for how efficiency changes as a function of punishment parameters and network structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when the game is played on small-world networks with appropriate parameter settings (moderate randomness, sufficient punishment fine, and intermediate feedback sensitivity). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing punishment will lead to a substantial increase in efficiency if the network structure allows for both local clustering and shortcuts. The results also indicate that the effect of punishment is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the network topology. The paper's detailed mapping of efficiency and strategy frequencies across parameter spaces can be used to inform quantitative predictions of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use large networks (N=40,000 for RRNs, N=200x200=40,000 for RLs and small-world), with each player participating in overlapping groups of size G=k+1 (k=6). The effect of absolute player count is not directly analyzed, but group size is fixed.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run until steady state (strategy frequencies stabilize), not a fixed number of rounds. The model is evolutionary and does not use a fixed round structure as in human experiments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each cooperator or punisher contributes 1, defectors contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=3.5, 4.0, 4.5) with group size G=7 (k=6+1). Higher r (thus higher mpcr) increases the region where cooperation and efficiency are sustained.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is modeled such that the total fine imposed on defectors is covered by the cost paid by punishers. The punishment fine (alpha) is varied, and higher alpha increases the effectiveness of punishment and efficiency up to a saturation point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is directly manipulated via the fine parameter alpha. Higher alpha increases the impact of punishment per unit cost, up to a point.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players have local information about their group composition (needed for feedback-driven punishment), but there is no explicit summary information as in human experiments.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no human or field data are used.
- The model assumes infinite or very large populations and evolutionary updating, which may not capture all dynamics of finite repeated games.
- Punishment is probabilistic and feedback-driven, which differs from standard fixed-probability or deterministic punishment in many lab experiments.
- No reward mechanism is considered, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- Network structure is fixed and does not evolve; real-world social networks may be more dynamic.
- The model does not include communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms beyond network structure and punishment.
