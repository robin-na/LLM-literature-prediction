# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Information-sensitive Leviathans
Authors: Nicklisch, A., Grechenig, K., & Thöni, C.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average profit
- group profit
- individual profit
- welfare
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution
- institution choice
- punishment assigned
- migration between institutions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average profit (efficiency), contributions, and institutional choice. Punishment behavior and migration between institutions are also analyzed. Efficiency is operationalized as average profit relative to the maximum possible.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend strongly on the information environment and the type of punishment institution. Under perfect information, decentralized punishment leads to the highest contributions and profits (efficiency), outperforming centralized punishment and no punishment. As information becomes less accurate (signals about others' contributions are noisy), both contributions and profits decline in all institutions, but especially in DecPun. Under moderate noise, CenPun can attract more citizens and sometimes matches or slightly exceeds DecPun in efficiency, provided the authority avoids punishing cooperators. Under high noise, neither punishment institution yields substantial efficiency gains over no punishment. The key moderator is the extent to which punishment is misapplied to cooperators, which is more likely in DecPun under noise. The paper provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes with punishment enabled versus disabled, across different institutional and informational settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the information environment and the punishment institution's design. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially decentralized) can substantially increase efficiency under perfect information. However, as information becomes noisy, the efficiency gain from punishment is reduced, and the difference between decentralized and centralized punishment narrows. The presence of a central authority who avoids punishing cooperators can maintain or improve efficiency under moderate noise, but not under high noise. For prediction, the paper suggests that the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost/tech, and especially the information structure (accuracy of contribution signals) are critical moderators. The results also imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on these dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each matching group consists of 10 citizens plus 1 authority. Group size within each institution varies endogenously as citizens migrate, but the total player count is fixed at 10 (excluding the authority).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is played for 32 periods, grouped into 8 phases of 4 periods each. Participants know the total number of periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete but not all-or-nothing: citizens choose any even number from 0 to 20 ECU (i.e., 0, 2, 4, ..., 20).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each ECU contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided equally among citizens in the institution. MPCR = 1.6 / group size (varies with institution size).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 ECU and deducts 3 ECU from the punished. In CenPun, the cost is shared among all other citizens; in DecPun, the punisher pays directly.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (3 ECU deducted per 1 ECU spent). The technology is identical in DecPun and CenPun.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of periods (32) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the start of each phase, citizens are shown the number of citizens, average contribution, and average profit in all three institutions for all previous periods. After each period, citizens see their own payoff and total punishment received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: citizens do not know who punished them, only the total amount of punishment received. Identification numbers are randomly reassigned each period.

## Important Limitations
- The authority in CenPun is randomly assigned and not democratically selected or competitively appointed, which may limit generalizability to real-world centralized institutions.
- The subject pool is from a society with low levels of antisocial punishment, which may underestimate the attractiveness of centralized punishment in other contexts.
- The experiment does not include reward mechanisms, so results may not generalize to environments with both punishment and reward.
- Group size within institutions is endogenous and can vary, which may confound some effects.
- The information structure is manipulated only via signal accuracy; other forms of uncertainty or information asymmetry are not explored.
- Punishment is always anonymous; the effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
