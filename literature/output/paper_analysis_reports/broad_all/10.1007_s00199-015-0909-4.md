## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with endogenous partner choice and no information flow, the most efficient Nash equilibrium is not the symmetric trust-building equilibrium (where all players initially defect to build trust), but rather a bimorphic equilibrium where some players always cooperate and others always defect and leave. This equilibrium is locally stable and, under a 'small stake' condition (when the temptation to defect is not too large), is more efficient than any equilibrium with only trust-building strategies. The efficiency gain comes from allowing a higher share of cooperators, even though some are exploited by defectors. The paper provides explicit conditions for when this efficiency dominance holds and characterizes the parameter regions where the presence of defectors improves efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with endogenous partner choice and no information flow, the introduction of unconditional defectors can increase equilibrium efficiency compared to trust-building (punishment-based) equilibria, especially when the temptation to defect is not too large (small stake condition) and the effective discount factor (survival rate) is high. The efficiency of the equilibrium is explicitly characterized as a function of the payoff parameters and the share of cooperators/defectors. While the model is not a standard public goods game and does not feature explicit costly punishment, it is highly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated games with endogenous partner selection and limited punishment options. The results suggest that, under certain conditions, the presence of defectors can be efficiency-improving, and that efficiency does not always monotonically increase with the strength or prevalence of punishment strategies.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes a continuum of players (measure 1), i.e., a very large population. All results are derived in this large-population limit.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is played over an infinite time horizon with a per-period survival probability (discount factor).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The stage game is a standard two-action Prisoner's Dilemma: each player chooses C or D (all-or-nothing).
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is implemented by ending the partnership (voluntary separation). Its effectiveness is limited by the lack of information flow to new partners; no personalized or costly punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinite horizon with a known per-period survival probability (discount factor); players know the structure.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (ending the partnership) is always directly observed by the partner, but there is no information flow to new partners.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with endogenous partner choice, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- There is no explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism; punishment is implemented only via voluntary separation (ending partnerships).
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are theoretical.
- The model assumes a continuum of players (infinite population), which may not capture small-group dynamics.
- No communication, reputation, or information flow to new partners is allowed; this limits the applicability to environments with some information transmission.
- Efficiency results depend on the 'small stake' condition (temptation to defect not too large) and high survival rates (high discount factor).
