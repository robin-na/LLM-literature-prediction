# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment under threat: The role of personality in costly punishment
Authors: Volk, S., Nguyen, H., & Thöni, C.
Journal: Journal of Research in Personality
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- assigned punishment (deduction points assigned to others)
- expected punishment (deduction points expected from others)
- PGG contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are assigned punishment and expected punishment, both measured as deduction points. PGG contribution is also measured, but the focus is on punishment behavior and its relationship to personality and threat. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that individuals high in Honesty-Humility engage in costly punishment for prosocial reasons, independent of threat, while those high in Emotionality engage in costly punishment in response to perceived punishment threat. Agreeableness does not predict punishment behavior. The experiment uses a two-person PGG with a punishment stage where each participant can punish a member of another team and can be punished by a different member of a third team, ruling out direct reciprocity. The main outcomes are assigned and expected punishment, both measured as deduction points. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, but discusses that punishment under threat is likely inefficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and personality drivers of costly punishment in a PGG-like environment with punishment threat, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. The design is a two-person PGG with continuous contributions, no communication, and a punishment stage where punishment is costly (1 token cost to punisher, 3 token loss to punished). The findings suggest that punishment can be driven by threat rather than prosocial motives, and the authors argue that such punishment is likely inefficient, but this is not empirically demonstrated. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that punishment under threat may not improve efficiency and may even reduce it, especially when driven by non-prosocial motives, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG group consists of 2 players. Punishment is assigned across teams, but the core PGG is two-person.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a one-shot (single round) PGG with a punishment stage.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was strictly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: any integer portion of 20 tokens can be contributed.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.75 (each contributed token yields 0.75 to each of 2 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the punished subject's income by 3 tokens.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a single round (one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No information about the other person's decisions was provided during the experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; team members cannot identify each other, and the punisher and punished are from different teams.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment and expectation behavior are measured.
- The experiment is one-shot, so dynamic effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency cannot be assessed.
- The study is focused on personality and psychological mechanisms, not on the aggregate effects of punishment on group outcomes.
- The sample is limited to Indonesian undergraduates, which may limit generalizability.
- Motives for punishment are inferred from personality and mediation analysis, not directly measured.
- The punishment design is nonstandard: punishment is assigned to unrelated third parties, not to one's own group member.
