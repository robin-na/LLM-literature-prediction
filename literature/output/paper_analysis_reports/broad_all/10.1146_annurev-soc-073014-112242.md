## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews sociological mechanisms that promote cooperation in social dilemmas, including public goods games. It finds that both formal (centralized) and informal (peer) punishment can increase cooperation, but the welfare effects are ambiguous due to the costs of sanctioning and potential for over- or under-punishment. Communication and endogenous rule formation improve outcomes. Centralized punishment (e.g., by elected leaders) is more effective and less prone to retaliation than peer punishment. However, the presence of sanctions and extrinsic incentives can undermine intrinsic motivation and trust, leading to lower cooperation when such mechanisms are removed. Reputation and status also promote cooperation, but can introduce ambiguity about motivations. Network structure (e.g., density, centrality, dynamic ties) shapes cooperation, with denser and more dynamic networks generally supporting higher cooperation, but also potentially undermining generalized trust.

## Prediction Guidance
This review provides high-level theoretical and empirical synthesis relevant to predicting the effects of punishment in public goods games and similar environments. It supports the general finding that punishment increases cooperation, but cautions that the net effect on efficiency or group welfare depends on the costs and structure of punishment, the presence of communication, and whether enforcement is centralized or peer-based. The review suggests that communication and endogenous institution formation are important moderators, and that centralized punishment is more effective than decentralized peer punishment. However, it also warns that punishment can undermine intrinsic motivation and trust, potentially reducing efficiency in the long run. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and moderated by game design features such as communication, institution formation, and enforcement structure, but does not provide quantitative estimates or direct efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- This is a theoretical review and does not provide new empirical data or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- The review synthesizes findings from a wide range of studies, but does not systematically report or meta-analyze efficiency or group welfare outcomes.
- Most evidence is indirect, focusing on cooperation, contribution, and norm enforcement rather than direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
- Game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and contribution granularity are not systematically addressed.
- The review highlights context-dependence and potential negative side effects of punishment (e.g., undermining intrinsic motivation), but does not specify conditions under which punishment increases or decreases efficiency.
