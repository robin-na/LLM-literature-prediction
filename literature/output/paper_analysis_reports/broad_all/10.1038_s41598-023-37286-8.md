# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strengths of social ties modulate brain computations for third-party punishment
Authors: Tang, Z. X., Qu, C., Hu, Y., Benistant, J., Moisan, F., Derrington, E., & Dreher, J. C.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment amount assigned by third-party
- reaction time
- fMRI neural activation patterns
- computational model utility (subjective value of punishment options)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of punishment assigned by the third-party observer. No group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes are reported. The computational model includes a utility function, but this is not equivalent to group efficiency or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that third-party punishment increases as the social distance between the punisher and the norm violator increases. The costliness of punishment reduces the amount of punishment assigned, but the effect of social distance remains robust. Computational modeling shows that participants integrate social distance, inequity aversion, and cost to punish when making punishment decisions. fMRI results identify brain regions involved in these computations. However, the study does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the willingness to punish norm violators depends on the social distance between the punisher and the violator, and that costliness of punishment reduces punishment levels. However, because the experiment is a third-party punishment variant of the Dictator Game (not a public goods game), and does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, especially the role of social distance and cost, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three roles: dictator (A), recipient (S), and third-party observer (participant). The participant always acts as the third-party; the dictator and recipient are simulated or hypothetical.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant completed 6 sessions of 54 trials each (324 trials total), with each trial being a one-shot decision. There is no repeated interaction with the same counterpart.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment choices are discrete: participants choose from 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, or 30 CNY to spend on punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the 'costly' condition, each unit of punishment costs the third-party 1 CNY; in the 'control' (free) condition, punishment is costless. Costly punishment reduces the amount of punishment assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment reduces the dictator's payoff by 3 CNY per 1 CNY spent by the third-party (punishment multiplier = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure of the experiment and that each trial is a one-shot decision; the number of trials is fixed and known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the dictator's allocation to the recipient and the available punishment options for each trial.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a third-party punishment Dictator Game, not a public goods game or a repeated social dilemma.
- No group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior and neural activation are analyzed.
- The study does not examine the effect of punishment on cooperation rates or group-level outcomes.
- All roles except the third-party are simulated or hypothetical; there is no real interaction between multiple human players.
- The findings may not generalize to public-goods-game-like environments or to settings where efficiency is the primary outcome.
