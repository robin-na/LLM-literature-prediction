# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: HIGHER PUNISHMENT, LESS CONTROL? EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE ON THE INSPECTION GAME
Authors: Rauhut, H.
Journal: Rationality and Society
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- inspectee earnings (losses over time)
- inspector earnings (roughly stable over time)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- theft rate (crime rate)
- inspection rate (control rate)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are theft (crime) and inspection (control) rates. Group or system efficiency, welfare, or total surplus are not directly reported, but the design is constructed so that theft is inefficient (gamma < 1), and the paper discusses welfare loss in theory. Inspectee and inspector earnings are reported descriptively, but not as a main outcome or in a way that allows direct calculation of efficiency ratios.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that increasing the strength of punishment reduces both the rate of inspection (control) and the rate of theft (crime), contrary to the strict game-theoretic prediction that only inspection should decrease. The effect on inspection is strong and robust, while the effect on theft is also significant but not as strong as predicted by standard theory. The behavioral adaptation to changes in punishment is slower than predicted by rational learning models. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates; efficiency or group welfare is not directly measured, but the design ensures that theft is inefficient (gamma = 0.5). Inspectees lose money over time, while inspectors' earnings are roughly stable. The findings suggest that punishment has mixed effects on strategic behavior in inspection settings, and that human adaptation is slower and less complete than predicted by theory.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about the behavioral effects of punishment in an inspection game with inefficient theft, which is structurally adjacent to public goods games. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. However, since theft is inefficient (gamma < 1), reductions in theft due to higher punishment would increase group efficiency, while reductions in inspection (which is costly) could also increase efficiency if inspection is not needed. The paper shows that higher punishment reduces both theft and inspection, suggesting that the net effect on efficiency would depend on the relative magnitudes of these changes and the cost structure. The design and findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is formal, inspection is costly, and theft is inefficient. The lack of direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions, but the qualitative direction of effects (higher punishment reduces both crime and control) is informative for adjacent settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session has 20 subjects, divided into inspectees and inspectors. The core game is played in groups of 4 (2 inspectees, 2 inspectors), but matching is random each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods per session, with 15 periods in each punishment condition (low/high).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interaction is anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (theft) is all-or-nothing: each inspectee can choose to steal a fixed amount or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The efficiency factor for theft is gamma = 0.5, meaning theft is inefficient (the thief gains 5, the victim loses 10). This is analogous to an MPCR of 0.5 for the 'public bad' of theft.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is imposed automatically if theft is detected; the cost to the punisher (inspector) is the inspection cost (k = 5 tokens), not the punishment itself. The punishment cost to the thief is p = 6 (low) or 25 (high).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the size of p (6 or 25 tokens) per successful inspection. The cost to the inspector is fixed (k = 5), and the reward for successful inspection is r = 10.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Inspectors receive a reward (r = 10 tokens) for successful inspection (catching a thief).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the inspector for attempting inspection is k = 5 tokens, regardless of outcome.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is r = 10 tokens per successful inspection, for a cost of k = 5 tokens per attempt.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (30) and the structure of the experiment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, inspectees learn the decisions of their matched inspectee and inspector, and whether they have been punished. Inspectors learn the decision of their matched inspectee. Both learn their current income.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are behavioral (theft and inspection rates), not direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The game is structurally adjacent to public goods games but is not a standard PGG; results may not transfer directly.
- No direct calculation or reporting of efficiency ratios or total surplus; only descriptive earnings for each role.
- The experiment uses a fixed, all-or-nothing theft decision, not a continuous contribution or public good.
- The punishment mechanism is formal and tied to inspection, not peer punishment as in many PGG experiments.
- The sample is university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
- Adaptation to punishment changes is slower and less complete than predicted by rational learning models, which may affect generalizability.
