## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a public goods game with 4-person groups and 10 rounds, adding a costly punishment stage (1 token cost, 3 token impact) significantly increases average contributions compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, average group earnings are lower in the punishment condition (19.4 tokens) than in the no-punishment condition (23.6 tokens), indicating that the efficiency cost of punishment outweighs the gains from increased cooperation. The study also finds that initial group contribution heterogeneity strongly predicts long-run outcomes, and that the between-subjects design (no prior exposure to the no-punishment game) yields higher contributions than the within-subjects design used in previous studies. The findings generalize to other cities with similar socio-economic profiles.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 4 players, 10 rounds, and a punishment mechanism (cost 1, impact 3, anonymous, no chat, no rewards), enabling punishment increases average contributions but reduces group efficiency/payoff compared to the no-punishment baseline. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is higher than the treatment (punishment) efficiency, due to the costliness of sanctions. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no rewards, anonymous punishment) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for similar settings. The results suggest that, unless punishment is very efficient or antisocial punishment is rare, introducing costly punishment may not improve group efficiency even if it raises cooperation.

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see all group members' contributions and payoffs, but not identities.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly reported; only average group earnings and contributions are provided, requiring inference for efficiency.
- The study is conducted in a single city (Istanbul) and may not generalize to all cultural contexts, though some cross-city comparisons are made.
- Antisocial punishment is present and may limit the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.
- The experiment uses a between-subjects design, which may not be directly comparable to within-subjects studies.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
