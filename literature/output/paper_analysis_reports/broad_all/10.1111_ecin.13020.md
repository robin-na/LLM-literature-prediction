# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social punishment for breaching restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Takahashi, R., & Tanaka, K.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- costly punishment behavior (measured as reduction in donation to norm-breakers at personal cost)
- donation amount to stores (proxy for punishment)
- probability of full-reduction and no-reduction actions
- effects of information provision on punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the reduction in donation to a store (at personal cost) as a proxy for costly punishment. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The experiment is a 'joy-of-destruction' minigame, not a public goods game or direct group payoff context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that individuals are willing to engage in costly punishment against stores that breach social norms (COVID-19 restrictions), as evidenced by a significant reduction in donations to such stores at personal cost. The average reduction for norm-breakers was about 35 yen (11% of the average donation). However, when participants were told that norm-breaking stores adhered to safety guidelines or faced bankruptcy risk, the level of costly punishment was reduced to be statistically indistinguishable from the control (complier) group. The results suggest that punishment for norm-breaking is sensitive to the presence of competing or contrasting norms, but the outcome measured is not group efficiency or total payoff, but rather the willingness to punish at a cost.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and behavioral drivers of costly punishment in a norm-violation context, but does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as in public goods games. The findings are relevant for understanding when and why individuals engage in costly punishment, and how this can be moderated by information about other norms. However, because the experiment is not a public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff, it is only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments. The main value is in showing that punishment can be reduced by introducing competing normative considerations.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes decisions individually; no group interaction. 1600 individuals participated, but the game is not multiplayer.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 5 one-shot minigames (one per industry), with one randomly selected for payment. No repeated interaction with the same target.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; decisions are made individually and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (reduction) choices are discrete in increments of 20 yen, from 0 to 100 yen reduction; not strictly all-or-nothing, but not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 20 yen per 100 yen reduction in donation (i.e., 0.2 cost-to-impact ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 20 yen spent by participant reduces donation by 100 yen (5x impact per unit cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know they play 5 minigames, one per industry, and that one will be randomly selected for payment.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or direct group interaction; it is a one-shot, individual decision to reduce a donation to an anonymous store.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual costly punishment behavior is measured.
- The mechanism by which information about competing norms reduces punishment is not directly identified.
- The study cannot disentangle all possible motivations for donation reduction (e.g., inequity aversion vs. punishment motivation), though robustness checks are performed.
- Findings may not generalize to multiplayer, repeated, or strategic environments typical of public goods games.
