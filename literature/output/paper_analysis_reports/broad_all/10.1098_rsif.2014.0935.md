## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. This policy outperforms pure reward or pure punishment in both effectiveness (range of conditions for full cooperation) and efficiency (cumulative cost to reach/restore cooperation). The findings hold for both well-mixed and spatial populations, and are robust to changes in group size, incentive leverage, spatial structure, and other model details. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.

## Important Limitations
- The results are entirely theoretical and based on replicator dynamics and spatial evolutionary models, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with perfect implementation and rational agents.
- No discrete rounds or time horizon; results may not map directly to finite repeated games.
- No communication, reputation, or social learning mechanisms are included.
- No analysis of stochasticity, implementation errors, or bounded rationality in the main text (though some robustness checks are in the supplement).
- No explicit analysis of the effect of information feedback or summary statistics shown to players.
