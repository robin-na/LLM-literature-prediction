# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups
Authors: Benard, S., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Social Science Quarterly
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group efficiency (total group earnings divided by maximum possible group earnings)
- total group earnings
- frequency of group failure
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate (percentage of endowment contributed)
- punishment behavior (amount spent on punishment by high-ranking member)
- manipulation of apparent threat (amount spent to increase/decrease announced threat)
- likelihood of being elected to high rank
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable for the main prediction is group efficiency (payoff-based). Contribution rate and punishment behavior are also analyzed, but efficiency is the main welfare outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 21 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or face-to-face interaction; all interaction via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can contribute any amount in whole dollar increments up to their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4; each L$1 contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: L$1 to reduce another's earnings by L$3 (punishment tech = 3). Only the high-ranking member can punish.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: L$1 spent reduces target's earnings by L$3.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds (21) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw the contributions of all group members each round; high-ranking member saw all contributions before punishing; all saw summary info for elections.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was assigned by the high-ranking member; it is implied that the identity of the punisher (the high-ranking member) is known, as only they can punish. No mention of anonymity.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only small groups (4 players), so results may not generalize to larger or more complex groups.
- All participants were undergraduates at a single university, limiting external validity.
- The only available punishment is from the high-ranking member; no peer punishment or reward mechanisms are present.
- The method of attaining high rank (democratic vs. resource-based) is the only manipulated variable; other institutional features are held constant.
- Manipulation of apparent threats is undetectable to others, which may limit the generalizability of findings about deception.
- Exploratory findings about punishment effectiveness and response to punishment are sensitive to influential cases and should be interpreted with caution.
