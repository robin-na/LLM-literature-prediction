# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperative behavior cascades in human social networks
Authors: Fowler, J. H., & Christakis, N. A.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution to public good
- spread of contribution behavior (cooperation cascades)
- punishment assigned and received
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good by each subject in each round, and how this is influenced by the contributions and punishment behaviors of others in previous rounds and at various degrees of separation. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of punishment on group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in both standard and punishment-enabled public goods games, individuals' contributions are causally influenced by the contributions of others in their group, and this influence spreads to others in subsequent rounds and groups, up to three degrees of separation. The presence of punishment increases contributions (as established in the original Fehr & Gachter 2002 study), but in this analysis, punishment does not fundamentally change the pattern or extent of behavioral cascades. Punishment can trigger cooperative cascades, but punishment behavior itself does not spread. The main outcome is the spread and persistence of cooperative behavior, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the spread of cooperative behavior in public goods games with and without punishment, but it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. However, it does confirm that punishment increases contributions (as in the original Fehr & Gachter 2002), and that the presence of punishment does not fundamentally alter the network dynamics of cooperation spread. For efficiency prediction, this paper is only indirectly informative, as it does not quantify payoff or efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject participated in 6 rounds (periods) of the public goods game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: subjects could contribute any amount between 0 and 20 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each MU contributed yields 0.4 MUs to each of 4 group members).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the punishment condition, each MU spent on punishment reduces the target's income by 3 MUs (punisher pays 1 MU per point assigned).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each MU spent reduces target's payoff by 3 MUs).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism does not exist; only punishment is available.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, all contributions by group members were revealed to each participant, along with payoff outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; subjects did not know who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main results are about contribution behavior and its spread.
- The study uses a specific laboratory design with random group assignment and anonymity, which may limit generalizability to real-world or repeated interaction settings.
- The analysis is based on previously published data (Fehr & Gachter 2002), and does not introduce new experimental manipulations of efficiency or payoff structure.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff is not addressed, so the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment.
