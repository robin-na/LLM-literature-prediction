# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: What makes a sanction stick? The effects of financial and social sanctions on norm compliance
Authors: Nelissen, R. M. A., & Mulder, L. B.
Journal: Social Influence
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution to group fund
- frequency of punishment assigned
- perceived potential for punishment/disapproval
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average contribution per round. No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The link to efficiency is indirect, as higher contributions in a standard PGG with MPCR < 1 imply higher group payoffs, but this is not explicitly calculated or reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both financial and social sanctions increase cooperation (measured as average contribution) in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-sanction control. Financial sanctions produce higher contributions than social sanctions while the sanctioning opportunity is present. However, after the removal of sanctions, contributions drop much more in the financial sanction condition than in the social sanction or control conditions. Social sanctions thus create more persistent cooperation norms. The study does not report group payoff or efficiency, but the contribution results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency effects in similar game designs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that both financial and social punishment mechanisms increase cooperation in a standard PGG, and that the effect is larger for financial punishment while the mechanism is active. However, the persistence of cooperation after punishment is removed is greater for social sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment will be positive for both financial and social punishment, but the durability of the effect may depend on the type of punishment. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution rates, but the mapping is straightforward in standard PGGs. The paper's design and results are highly informative for settings with 4 players, continuous contributions, no communication, and known MPCR.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds total: 6 with sanctions (Phase 1), 3 without sanctions (Phase 2), plus a practice round. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed. Players were seated in individual cubicles and remained anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: each player could invest any amount (0-4 Euro) in the group fund each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.5/4 = 0.375. The group fund was multiplied by 1.5 and distributed evenly among 4 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the financial sanction condition, assigning a 1 Euro fine to another player cost the punisher nothing (punishment was costless to assigner).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment assigned in the financial sanction condition reduced the target's payoff by 1 Euro (max 3 per round). In the social sanction condition, punishment was the expression of disapproval (no monetary effect).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment (financial or social) was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, individual contributions were displayed, but the order was randomized so that contributions could not be consistently assigned to specific players.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were informed of the number of punishments/disapprovals they received, but not the identity of the punisher(s).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes directly; all results are based on contribution rates.
- Punishment in the financial sanction condition was costless to the punisher, which is less common than costly punishment in many PGG studies.
- The sample size is moderate (N=84, 3 conditions), and all participants were undergraduate students, which may limit generalizability.
- The experiment used a deception (fake program crash) to remove sanctioning opportunities, which may affect external validity.
- The mapping from contribution to efficiency is indirect and assumes standard PGG payoff structure.
