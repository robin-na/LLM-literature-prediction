# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Intermediation Reduces Punishment (and Reward)
Authors: Coffman, L. C.
Journal: American Economic Journal-microeconomics
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- receiver's average payoff
- group earnings / total surplus
- average punishment (as a deduction from first mover's earnings)
- frequency of zero earnings for receiver
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned (level and frequency)
- use of intermediary
- beliefs about others' behavior
- reflection/awareness of motives
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment assigned (behavioral) and receiver/group payoffs (payoff-related). Efficiency and welfare are directly analyzed via group earnings and receiver's payoff. The study also reports on beliefs and reasoning, but these are secondary.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that when a first mover can choose to act selfishly either directly or via an intermediary, third-party punishment for selfishness is significantly lower when the action is taken through the intermediary. This is true even when the intermediary is passive and cannot be blamed. As a result, the poorest player (receiver) earns much less on average (from $4.50 to $1.63), and group earnings fall (from $19.55 to $17.24) when intermediation is available. The effect is robust to various controls and alternative explanations (e.g., diffusion of responsibility, confusion, lack of reasoning, or mere presence of a third party). The reduction in punishment is not explained by standard models of fairness or responsibility diffusion. The paper also finds that even when subjects are aware that intermediation is used to avoid punishment and that it harms the poorest player, they still punish less. A follow-up study shows a similar effect for rewards: indirect charitable giving is rewarded less than direct giving.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of an intermediary (a form of indirectness) in a punishment-enabled environment can dramatically reduce the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining pro-social outcomes, leading to lower efficiency and welfare. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs allowing for indirect actions or intermediation—even with punishment enabled—will likely see much lower efficiency than comparable direct-action games. The control (no intermediary) game with punishment achieves high receiver payoffs and group efficiency, but adding the intermediation option sharply reduces both, even though punishment is still available and costless. Thus, the presence of intermediation is a strong negative moderator of punishment's positive effect on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main game has 4 players: first mover, intermediary, receiver, and punisher. Control (no intermediary) has 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 4 rounds, with each subject playing each role once. No feedback until all rounds are completed.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices (how much to keep/send) are discrete but not all-or-nothing; first mover chooses from $5 to $10 in $1 increments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher (can reduce first mover's payout to any nonnegative amount at no cost).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher can reduce first mover's payout to any nonnegative amount (full effectiveness, costless).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told there are 4 rounds and that they will play each role once; paid for one randomly chosen round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are shown the amount sent/kept, whether the DG was sold, and for how much. No feedback about others' behavior until all rounds are completed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; no evidence that punished players know the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The main experiment is not a public goods game or a direct PGG variant; it is a dictator game with third-party punishment, so some findings may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Punishment is costless and fully effective, which is not typical of most PGG or field environments.
- The presence of an intermediary is a specific form of indirectness; other forms of indirectness or diffusion of responsibility may operate differently.
- The sample is limited to young adults (under 30) in a lab setting, which may limit generalizability.
- The study does not report direct measures of group efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but does report group earnings and receiver payoffs.
- No communication or repeated interaction; all decisions are one-shot within each role.
