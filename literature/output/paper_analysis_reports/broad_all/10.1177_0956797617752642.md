# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Why Hate the Good Guy? Antisocial Punishment of High Cooperators Is Greater When People Compete To Be Chosen
Authors: Pleasant, A., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Psychological Science
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- antisocial punishment (punishment of high cooperators)
- moralistic punishment (punishment of low cooperators)
- contributions to the public good
- feelings of competition
- correlations with personality measures
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variables are antisocial punishment, moralistic punishment, and contribution levels. Group or individual payoffs, efficiency, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed as outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when participants in a public goods game with punishment are competing to be chosen for a subsequent trust game, antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is much higher than in a standard PGG with punishment. This effect is present from the first round and persists across rounds. In the control condition, contributions increase over rounds (as is typical when punishment is available), but in the markets condition, contributions do not increase, suggesting that antisocial punishment suppresses the escalation of cooperation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral pattern implies that the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment may be undermined or reversed in contexts with high antisocial punishment driven by partner competition.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the context of partner competition (biological markets) can substantially increase antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, which in turn suppresses the usual increase in cooperation seen with punishment. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings imply that in environments where partner choice or competitive altruism is salient, enabling punishment may not improve and may even harm group efficiency, as antisocial punishment undermines cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of partner competition or similar social comparison incentives is a critical moderator: in such settings, the efficiency benefit of punishment is likely reduced or reversed, even if control efficiency is known. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in the PGG; in the markets condition, a 5th participant acts as observer but does not play the PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Five rounds of the PGG with punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could contribute any amount from L$0 to L$10 each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each L$1 contributed is doubled and split among 4 players, so MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each L$1 spent on punishment reduces the target's earnings by L$3.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (L$3 reduction per L$1 spent).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told the number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw what everyone contributed that round. In the markets condition, the observer saw each player's average contribution after the PGG.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: participants were not told who punished them, only the total amount punished.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes directly; only behavioral outcomes (punishment and contributions) are analyzed.
- Assignment to condition was not random but based on session attendance, though the authors argue this did not bias results.
- The laboratory setting may not fully capture real-world dynamics of antisocial punishment and partner competition.
- Punishers' identities were anonymous; in real-world settings, visibility of punishment may alter behavior.
- The sample is limited to university students, which may affect generalizability.
