## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use 30 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how much (0-20 ECU) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher is 1 ECU per punishment point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's earnings by 3 ECU.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated 30 times.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of all group members' contributions and, in punishment treatments, of punishment assignments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In RP, fixed IDs and full information allow players to identify who punished whom, enabling retaliation. In SP, IDs are randomized each round, preventing identification across periods.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is limited to small groups (4 players) and a specific MPCR (0.4); results may not generalize to larger groups or different payoff structures.
- No communication or chat is allowed; the effect of punishment in environments with communication is not tested.
- The study does not disentangle the effects of rich punishment strategies from the effects of rich information (identifiability of punishers).
- The time horizon is 30 rounds; while this is long, the possibility that even longer horizons could yield different results is not fully ruled out.
- Non-material utility from punishing or being punished is not measured; only material payoffs are considered for efficiency.
