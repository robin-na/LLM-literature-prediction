# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The role of institutional incentives and the exemplar in promoting cooperation
Authors: Wu, J. J., Li, C., Zhang, B. Y., Cressman, R., & Tao, Y.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average group contribution
- proportion of conforming, cooperating, and defecting individuals
- individual reactions to reward and punishment (change in contribution)
- transition rates between behavioral types
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average group contribution per round. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings, but discusses expected payoffs in the methods and theoretical analysis. The experimental results focus on contribution levels, not direct payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Institutional punishment increases average group contributions above control and reward-only treatments, and the combination of reward and punishment (IRP) is most effective at promoting cooperation. The effect is robust across three incentive schemes (fixed, increasing, decreasing). The study finds that individuals react more strongly to punishment than to reward, especially among high contributors. The presence of 'cooperating-individuals' (exemplars) in a group is associated with higher average contributions, as conformists tend to follow their lead. The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff, but the design and behavioral findings are highly relevant for understanding how institutional punishment affects cooperation in repeated PGGs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that institutional punishment (and especially combined reward and punishment) increases average group contributions in repeated PGGs with four players and 50 rounds. While efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the contribution results suggest that punishment is likely to improve efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The experimental design and manipulation of incentive schemes are informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar lab PGG settings, but direct quantitative prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone due to the lack of payoff/efficiency reporting.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 50 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects interacted anonymously via computer screens; no mention of chat or communication allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject decides how much (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total contributions multiplied by 1.6 and split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is institutional, not peer: the cost is not paid by another player but is implemented by the institution. The amount of punishment is fixed or varies by scheme (16-25.6 units), but the cost to the punisher is not directly relevant as there is no peer punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per event is 16-25.6 units, depending on scheme. Probability of being punished depends on contribution relative to group average.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is present in IR and IRP treatments.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is institutional, not peer: the cost is not paid by another player but is implemented by the institution. The amount of reward is 16-25.6 units, depending on scheme.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact per event is 16-25.6 units, depending on scheme. Probability of being rewarded depends on contribution relative to group average.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The number of rounds (50) is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Not explicitly described, but standard PGG protocol and analysis of group average contributions suggest participants see at least group-level outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are institutional and anonymous; participants know if they were punished or rewarded, but not by whom (since it is not peer-administered).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is average group contribution, not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or welfare; only theoretical expected payoffs are discussed.
- Institutional punishment/reward is not peer-administered, so findings may not generalize to peer-punishment settings.
- All groups are of fixed size (4 players) and fixed number of rounds (50), limiting generalizability to other group sizes or time horizons.
- No communication or chat between players; results may differ if communication is allowed.
