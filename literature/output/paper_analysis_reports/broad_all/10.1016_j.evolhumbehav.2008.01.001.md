# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The price you pay: cost-dependent reputation effects of altruistic punishment
Authors: Nelissen, R. M. A.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount transferred to partner in trust game
Non-payoff outcomes:
- partner preference
- perceptions of fairness
- perceptions of friendliness
- perceptions of generosity
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount transferred to a partner in a trust game, which is interpreted as a measure of trust or preference, not group efficiency or total payoff. No public goods game or group efficiency is measured; the design is a trust game with information about prior punishment behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that individuals who invest more in costly third-party punishment are perceived as more fair and trustworthy, and are subsequently entrusted with more money in a trust game. This effect is contingent on the cost being a reliable signal (i.e., known relative to endowment). The effect is mediated by perceptions of fairness, not by general likability or generosity. The study does not measure group efficiency or public goods provision, but rather the reputational and trust consequences for punishers.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public goods games. However, it does provide indirect evidence that costly punishment can enhance the punisher's reputation and increase trust from others, but only when the cost is interpretable as a reliable signal. For prediction tasks focused on group efficiency in PGGs, this paper is only adjacent: it suggests that punishment may have reputational benefits for the punisher, which could, in theory, affect cooperation and efficiency in repeated or reputation-sensitive environments, but it does not test this directly. The design and outcomes are not directly transferable to PGG efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The trust game is played between two players (participant and fictional partner), but the punishment scenario involves three fictional individuals. No group interaction as in a PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Single-shot trust game; no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between participants; all decisions are made privately.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution (transfer) in the trust game is continuous (any amount from 0 to 4 euros). Punishment amount is also continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is manipulated: each euro spent reduces the allocator's endowment by 2 euros. The amount spent (absolute and relative to endowment) is the key independent variable.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed: 1 euro spent reduces the target's payoff by 2 euros.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Single-round game; participants know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown a table with the amount each fictional player spent on punishment and their total endowment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told which player spent which amount on punishment; punishment is fully identified.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or measure group efficiency; it uses a trust game with information about prior punishment behavior.
- Outcomes are individual-level (trust, reputation, partner preference), not group-level efficiency or welfare.
- The punishment scenario is hypothetical for the partner; only the trust game involves real stakes for participants.
- Sample sizes are modest, and some effects (e.g., difference between weak punishers and nonpunishers) may be underpowered.
- The findings may not generalize to real group interactions or to settings where punishment affects group efficiency.
