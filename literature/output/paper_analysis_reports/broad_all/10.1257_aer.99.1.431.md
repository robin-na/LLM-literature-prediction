Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are expected/actual earnings and the proportion of SWM decisions (efficiency proxies). Behavioral outcomes such as charity, envy, and reciprocity are also analyzed, but efficiency and earnings are central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment systematically measures the effects of induced group identity on social preferences and economic outcomes in a set of 24 two-person sequential allocation games (including dictator and response games with punishment/reward options). The key findings are: (1) participants are more likely to make social-welfare-maximizing (SWM) choices and earn higher payoffs when matched with ingroup members, and less likely to do so with outgroup members, compared to a control with no group identity; (2) participants are more likely to reward ingroup members for good behavior and less likely to punish them for misbehavior, while the opposite holds for outgroup members; (3) the gap in efficiency/payoff between ingroup and outgroup matches is driven more by losses in outgroup matches than gains in ingroup matches. The study also finds that the presence of group identity (even when randomly assigned) is sufficient to generate these effects, and that design features like chat can increase group attachment but have limited behavioral impact.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent: punishment is less likely to be used against ingroup members (reducing efficiency losses from antisocial punishment), but more likely against outgroup members (increasing efficiency losses). The presence of group identity can thus moderate the efficiency impact of punishment, with ingroup contexts supporting higher efficiency and outgroup contexts supporting lower efficiency, relative to a no-identity baseline. The findings suggest that group composition and identity salience are important moderators for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment mechanisms, even in games that are not classic PGGs but share key features (sequential allocation, punishment/reward options, payoff externalities).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) sequential allocation games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session includes multiple one-shot games (24 sequential games per session), not repeated rounds of the same game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Some treatments include a group chat stage to enhance group identity; chat increases group attachment but has little effect on efficiency or punishment behavior.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are sequential allocation games with discrete choices (e.g., enter/stay out, left/right), not continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is varied across games; logit regressions show that higher punishment cost reduces likelihood of punishment, and this effect is moderated by group identity.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (damage to target per unit cost) is varied across games; higher damage increases likelihood of punishment, with group-contingent effects.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some games allow for reward (positive reciprocity) as well as punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is varied across games; higher cost reduces likelihood to reward.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness (benefit to target per unit cost) is varied across games.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of games in the session; no feedback until the end.

## Important Limitations
- The games are two-person sequential allocation games, not classic public goods games; results may not fully generalize to multi-person PGGs.
- Group identity is induced in the lab and may not capture the strength or complexity of real-world group identities.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and may differ in larger groups or with repeated interactions.
- The study does not analyze classic PGG efficiency or MPCR directly; findings are most applicable to dyadic allocation games with punishment/reward options.
- Behavioral effects of chat and other group-enhancing mechanisms are limited in most games, suggesting that group attachment does not always translate to efficiency gains.
