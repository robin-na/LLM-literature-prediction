Outcome notes:
The paper reviews and synthesizes experimental evidence on cooperation, punishment, and norm enforcement in public goods and Prisoner's Dilemma games, but does not directly report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. The main dependent variables discussed are behavioral: contributions, punishment actions, and norm compliance.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the opportunity to punish norm violators in public goods games leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation rates, often approaching full cooperation. Punishment is frequently administered by group members or even third parties, and is motivated by non-selfish concerns for norm enforcement rather than self-interest. The costliness of punishment reduces its frequency and strength, but punishment remains prevalent even when it is costly. The presence of punishment opportunities transforms the dynamics of cooperation, preventing the typical decline in contributions over time seen in standard public goods games without punishment. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative analysis of efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong qualitative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games leads to much higher and more stable cooperation rates, and that the effect is robust across various experimental designs. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that introducing punishment will increase efficiency, provided that efficiency is closely linked to contribution rates. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes directly, its value is primarily in establishing the behavioral mechanism and the robustness of the punishment effect on cooperation. It also highlights that the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as group size and information structure, are important moderators, but does not provide quantitative estimates for these effects on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The paper discusses public goods games with n >= 2, and provides examples with n=2 (Prisoner's Dilemma) and n=4 (public goods). Group size is not systematically varied or analyzed for its effect on punishment or efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The paper discusses both one-shot and finitely repeated games (e.g., 10 or 20 periods), but does not systematically analyze the effect of number of rounds on outcomes.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both all-or-nothing (Prisoner's Dilemma) and continuous (public goods) contribution games are discussed. The main public goods experiments allow continuous contributions.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. MPCR is described as b/n, with b > 1 and b < n. Example values are given (e.g., E=20, b=2, n=4), but not systematically varied.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly for the punisher. The paper notes that as the cost increases, punishment becomes less frequent and weaker, but punishment remains prevalent even when costly.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is described (e.g., 1 MU spent reduces target's payoff by 2-4 MUs, or by 3 MUs in third-party punishment). Not systematically varied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Subjects are informed about the number of periods in repeated games (e.g., 10 or 20 periods).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players are informed about others' contributions after each round, but identities are not revealed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is administered anonymously; personal identities are not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new experimental data or direct quantitative analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- All outcome evidence is based on behavioral measures (contribution, punishment) rather than direct payoff or efficiency metrics.
- The paper synthesizes results from multiple studies, so specific game parameters and their effects are not systematically analyzed.
- No analysis of reward mechanisms or communication effects.
- No quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
