## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds a significant negative correlation between basal cortisol and costly punishment: individuals with lower basal cortisol are more likely to punish uncooperative group members in a public goods game. The game used is a standard linear PGG with four players, six rounds, and a punishment stage where punishment is costly to the punisher and three times as costly to the punished. The study does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or the effect of punishment on group welfare. The focus is on individual differences in punishment behavior, not on the aggregate effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the behavioral drivers of costly punishment in a standard PGG with punishment, but does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. The game design is standard and matches the canonical PGG with punishment, so the behavioral findings are relevant for understanding who punishes and why. However, for the downstream prediction task—predicting efficiency with punishment enabled—this paper does not provide direct outcome data or comparative analysis. It may be useful for understanding heterogeneity in punishment behavior, but not for predicting efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Six periods (rounds) of the public goods game were played.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions were computer-mediated and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous from 0 to 20 MUs per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU contributed was multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing another player cost 1 MU per unit, up to 10 MUs per target per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 1 MU spent on punishment reduced the target's payoff by 3 MUs.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were six periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were given accurate information on the contributions of the other three players.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The study is correlational and does not provide causal evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
- No direct analysis or reporting of efficiency, group welfare, or total earnings as a function of punishment.
- Sample is limited to healthy young male university students; results may not generalize to women or other populations.
- Cultural generalizability is unclear, as the study was conducted in a single country and context.
- The focus is on individual differences in punishment behavior, not on the aggregate or group-level effects of punishment.
