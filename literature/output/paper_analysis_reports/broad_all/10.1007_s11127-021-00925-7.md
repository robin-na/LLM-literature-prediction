# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Understanding cross-cultural differences in peer reporting practices: evidence from tax evasion games in Moldova and France
Authors: Romaniuc, R., Dubois, D., Dimant, E., Lupusor, A., & Prohnitchi, V.
Journal: Public Choice
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- percentage of income declared (tax compliance rate)
- frequency of audits
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of peer reporting (whistleblowing)
- normative views on social acceptability of reporting
- beliefs about social acceptability
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of income declared (tax compliance), which is a behavioral measure of compliance rather than direct group payoff or efficiency. There is no explicit calculation of group efficiency or total welfare, but higher compliance implies higher group tax revenue, which is related to efficiency in a public goods context. The study also reports on the frequency of peer reporting and social norms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that enabling costly, anonymous peer reporting (whistleblowing) in a repeated tax evasion game leads to significantly higher tax compliance (i.e., a higher percentage of income declared) compared to a control condition where only social observation is possible. This effect is observed in both Moldova and France, even though Moldovan participants view whistleblowing as less socially acceptable and engage in it less frequently than French participants. The increase in compliance is attributed to the deterrent effect of the possibility of being reported, not just the actual frequency of reporting. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff directly, but the increase in compliance is a strong proxy for improved group outcomes in a public-goods-like context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly, anonymous peer reporting mechanism in a repeated tax evasion game (a close variant of a public goods game) increases compliance rates, which is closely related to group efficiency. The effect is robust to large cross-cultural differences in the acceptability and frequency of reporting. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment (here, in the form of peer reporting to a central authority) will increase efficiency or group payoff relative to a control with no such mechanism, even if actual punishment is rare. The effect is not strongly moderated by cultural differences in the willingness to punish, at least in the settings studied. However, the outcome measured is tax compliance, not direct group payoff or efficiency, so the mapping is indirect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 participants in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds in all treatments.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between participants; design implies no chat allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (income declaration) is continuous: participants choose what percentage of their income to declare.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost to report (punish) is 2 ECU per report in the whistleblowing treatment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each report triggers an audit of the target; if evasion is detected, the penalty is 1.5 times the tax owed on undeclared income. The punishment is thus an increased audit probability and a fixed penalty.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only costly punishment (reporting).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Number of rounds (15) is fixed and likely known to participants, but not explicitly stated if announced.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the whistleblowing and social observation treatments, participants are shown the percentage of income declared by each of the other group members after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reporting (punishment) is anonymous; the punished (audited) individual does not know who reported them.

## Important Limitations
- The outcome measured is tax compliance (percentage of income declared), not direct group efficiency or total payoff.
- The game is a tax evasion game, not a standard public goods game; mapping to MPCR and efficiency is indirect.
- No explicit group payoff or efficiency calculations are reported.
- Cultural context is limited to Moldova and France; generalizability to other settings is uncertain.
- Punishment is implemented as costly, anonymous peer reporting to a central authority, not direct peer-to-peer punishment.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
