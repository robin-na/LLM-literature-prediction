## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that evolutionary psychology provides a useful framework for understanding cooperation, punishment, and norm enforcement in organizational and business ethics contexts. It reviews evidence from public goods games and related experiments (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002) showing that the availability of punishment increases cooperation rates and reduces free-riding. The review also discusses the concept of strong reciprocity, where individuals are willing to punish norm violators at a cost to themselves, and suggests that such mechanisms are critical for sustaining cooperation in groups. However, the paper does not present new empirical data or directly analyze efficiency or group payoff; instead, it synthesizes existing theoretical and empirical work to propose directions for future research.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and contextual support for the idea that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation in public goods games and similar environments, referencing well-known experimental findings. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff effects, nor does it systematically analyze how game design dimensions moderate these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the general expectation that enabling punishment will increase cooperation and likely improve group outcomes, but it does not provide the detailed, dimension-specific evidence needed for precise prediction of efficiency changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The paper describes public goods games with typical group sizes (e.g., four participants) but does not analyze the effect of player count on outcomes.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Sequential rounds are described as standard in public goods games, and the paper notes that cooperation tends to deteriorate over rounds without punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution choices in public goods games are described as continuous (proportions of endowment), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes example with 40% return from the common account, but does not analyze variation in MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher; the presence of costly punishment increases cooperation, but the effect of varying the cost is not analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as a fee paid to reduce another's payoff, but the effectiveness per unit cost is not analyzed.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Altruistic rewarding is discussed as part of strong reciprocity, but not in the context of public goods games with explicit reward options.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes that after each round, subjects are told how many points were contributed to the common account.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not present new empirical data or direct analyses of efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Findings are based on summaries of other studies, and the paper does not systematically analyze how game design dimensions moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Payoff-related outcomes such as efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed.
- Dimension-level effects (e.g., varying punishment cost, player count) are not empirically tested or quantified.
