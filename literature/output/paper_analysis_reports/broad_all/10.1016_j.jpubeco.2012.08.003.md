# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Gun for hire: Delegated enforcement and peer punishment in public goods provision
Authors: Andreoni, J., & Gee, L. K.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- net earnings (group and individual)
- welfare (earnings minus punishment costs)
- total costs of punishment
- public good provision (as it relates to group earnings)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- frequency of hiring delegated punishment
- frequency and cost of peer-to-peer punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is net earnings (welfare), defined as earnings after costs of punishment. Contribution rates and punishment behavior are also reported, but the focus is on efficiency/welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated linear public goods games with four players, the introduction of a delegated punishment mechanism (either imposed or endogenously hired) leads to a substantial and immediate increase in both contributions and net earnings (welfare), compared to both the no-punishment baseline and the peer-to-peer punishment regime. When both delegated and peer-to-peer punishment are available, subjects overwhelmingly choose to implement the delegated mechanism, which then crowds out peer punishment and further increases efficiency. The delegated mechanism is low-cost, targets only the largest free rider, and is effective at deterring free riding. The efficiency gains are robust across different baseline conditions (with or without peer punishment as the initial regime).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and repeated rounds, enabling a delegated punishment mechanism (with a low per-person cost and a punishment that targets only the largest free rider) increases group efficiency (net earnings) substantially compared to both no-punishment and peer-to-peer punishment regimes. The efficiency gains are primarily due to a reduction in costly peer-to-peer punishment, not just increased contributions. The findings are highly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the punishment is centralized, low-cost, and targets the worst free rider. The results are less directly informative for other forms of punishment (e.g., peer-to-peer only, or high-cost/low-impact punishment), but the contrast with peer-to-peer punishment is explicitly tested and shows delegated punishment is superior for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session has 20 periods: 10 baseline, 10 treatment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates 0–5 tokens between public and private good.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token in the public good returns $2 to each of 4 players (total $8), so MPCR = 2/3 = 0.666...
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Peer-to-peer: $1 cost to punisher per $3 deduction to target. Delegated: group pays $2 to hire, then lowest contributor is punished by a deduction (size varies, see mechanism). Delegated mechanism is low cost relative to peer-to-peer.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Peer-to-peer: $1 cost yields $3 deduction. Delegated: group cost is $2, but punishment is targeted and sized to deter the largest deviation. Delegated mechanism is more efficient in deterring free riding.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 10 periods per game (20 total).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see anonymous information about each group member's contributions, private good allocation, and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishments are anonymous; players do not know who punished whom in peer-to-peer punishment. Delegated punishment is implemented by a computer administrator.

## Important Limitations
- The delegated punishment mechanism is implemented by a computer, not a human authority; real-world delegation may involve additional complexities (e.g., abuse of power, enforcement errors).
- The cost and structure of the delegated mechanism are fixed and low; results may differ with higher costs or less targeted punishment.
- All groups are of size 4; results may not generalize to larger or smaller groups.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or negotiation are not tested.
- The experiment uses repeated random rematching, which may limit reputation effects compared to fixed groups.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are tested, so results are specific to punishment-based enforcement.
