# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Costly punishment does not always increase cooperation
Authors: Wu, J. J., Zhang, B. Y., Zhou, Z. X., He, Q. Q., Zheng, X. D., Cressman, R., & Tao, Y.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per round
- group payoff
- payoff per round for mutual cooperation (ALLC)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- frequency and timing of punishment use
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (cooperation, defection, punishment) and payoff-based outcomes (average payoff per round, group payoff).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that, in a repeated two-player Prisoner's Dilemma with Chinese participants, the introduction of costly punishment either had no significant effect or reduced the frequency of cooperation, and led to a significant decrease in average group payoff (efficiency) in one treatment. In the other treatment, the decrease in payoff was not statistically significant. The results are contrasted with previous US-based studies, where costly punishment increased cooperation but did not improve group payoff. The authors attribute the difference to cultural factors, particularly attitudes toward reputation and authority. The study provides direct evidence that costly punishment does not always increase cooperation or efficiency, and may even reduce them depending on context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in repeated two-player Prisoner's Dilemma games (an adjacent but not exact variant of the public goods game), enabling costly punishment can reduce group efficiency (average payoff), especially when cooperation does not increase. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and may depend on cultural context and game structure. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency, and in some settings (e.g., repeated dyadic games, certain cultures), it may even lower it. The paper also highlights that increased cooperation does not always translate to higher efficiency if punishment costs offset cooperative gains.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) repeated Prisoner's Dilemma.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds per interaction is stochastic: after each round, there is a 0.75 probability of another round. Average of 22 interactions per subject, average of 79 total rounds per subject.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; interactions are anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: cooperate, defect, or punish (in treatments). No continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In C1/T1, cooperation means paying 1 for the other to get 2 (mpcr=2); in C2/T2, paying 1 for the other to get 3 (mpcr=3).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment means paying 1 unit for the other to lose 4 units.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4 units lost per 1 unit spent.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds; after each round, there is a 0.75 probability of continuation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are shown the other person's choice and both payoff scores.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In each dyad, players know who punished whom, as all choices are revealed after each round. However, all interactions are anonymous between different dyads.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated two-player Prisoner's Dilemma, which is adjacent but not identical to standard public goods games.
- Findings may not generalize to multiplayer or one-shot public goods games.
- Cultural context (Chinese university students) may limit generalizability to other populations.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment is available.
- The stochastic number of rounds may affect strategies differently than a fixed or known number of rounds.
