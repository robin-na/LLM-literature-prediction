# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Transcranial direct current stimulation of the rLPFC shifts normative judgments in voluntary cooperation
Authors: Liu, X. L., Li, J. B., Wang, G. R., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., & Fu, X. G.
Journal: Neuroscience Letters
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- normative judgment (what is the 'right' contribution)
- actual contribution in one-off public goods game
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are normative judgments about appropriate contributions and actual contributions in a one-shot public goods game. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The study does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; it manipulates brain stimulation (tDCS) to the rLPFC and measures effects on judgment and contribution.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects both participants' normative judgments about appropriate contributions and their actual contributions in a one-shot, linear, asymmetric public goods game. Anodal stimulation (increasing rLPFC excitability) leads to higher normative judgments and higher contributions, while cathodal stimulation (decreasing rLPFC excitability) leads to lower judgments and contributions, relative to sham. The effect is robust across both 'rich' and 'poor' player types. No punishment, sanction, or reward mechanisms are present in the game, and no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Its findings are relevant to understanding the neural basis of normative judgment and voluntary cooperation, but not to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions reported (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds) may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this study should not be used as direct evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players (2 rich, 2 poor).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants played a one-off (single-shot) public goods game, but each player answered questions for 16 periods with 4 different identities. The actual contribution decision appears to be one-shot per identity.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; interactions were anonymous via computer terminals.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous, not all-or-nothing; players could choose how much to contribute from their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.6 (each unit contributed returns 0.6 to each player, as per the payoff function).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not informed about the number of periods they would play.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design, so the study does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff.
- No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported; only normative judgments and individual contributions are measured.
- The main manipulation is neurobiological (tDCS of rLPFC), not a game design feature relevant to the prediction task.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with punishment, sanctions, or repeated interactions.
