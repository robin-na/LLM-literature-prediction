# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How, when, and why recipients and observers reward good deeds and punish bad deeds
Authors: Whitson, J. A., Wang, C. S., See, Y. H. M., Baker, W. E., & Murnighan, J. K.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and intensity of punishment assigned
- frequency and intensity of reward assigned
- felt obligation to punish or reward
- felt emotions (anger, happiness, etc.)
- sense of power
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and amount of punishment or reward assigned by participants in response to observed or experienced good or bad deeds. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. All outcomes are at the individual level and focus on behavioral responses, not group-level payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that third-party observers are more likely to punish bad deeds (e.g., deception) than direct recipients, and that direct recipients are more likely to reward good deeds (e.g., honesty) than observers. These effects are mediated by feelings of obligation: observers feel a stronger obligation to punish, while recipients feel a stronger obligation to reward. The studies use one-shot, controlled scenarios with real monetary stakes, but do not involve repeated interaction, group provision, or public goods. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward assigned), not group efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the psychological and behavioral drivers of punishment and reward in one-shot, dyadic or triadic settings, but does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or welfare in public goods games or similar repeated social dilemmas. The findings suggest that the presence of observers may increase the likelihood and intensity of punishment for norm violations, but do not inform how this affects group payoffs or efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public goods games, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly about who is likely to punish and under what circumstances, not about the payoff consequences of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments use dyadic (2 players) or triadic (3 players: A, B, C) setups. Experiment 2 and 3 explicitly use 3-player scenarios (recipient, observer, actor).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments are one-shot (single round) interactions. Participants are told there will be no further interaction.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed. All interactions are via controlled instructions and choices.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward choices are continuous (participants choose an amount on a scale, not all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1/10 the impact (e.g., pay $1 to reduce other by $10 in Experiment 2; pay $0.10 to reduce by $1 in Experiment 3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed at 10x (each $1 spent reduces target by $10 in Experiment 2, $0.10 for $1 in Experiment 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism is available in all experiments; participants can choose to reward good deeds.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1/10 the impact (same as punishment).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is fixed at 10x (each $1 spent increases target by $10 in Experiment 2, $0.10 for $1 in Experiment 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are explicitly told the interaction is one-shot and there will be no further rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Observers are shown the actions and outcomes of other players (e.g., Person A's honesty/deception and Person B's payoff). Recipients know their own outcome.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward are direct and attributed; the punished/rewarded party knows who assigned it (recipient or observer).

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a public goods game or repeated social dilemma; it uses one-shot, dyadic or triadic interactions focused on honesty/deception.
- No group-level efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported; all outcomes are individual-level behavioral responses.
- The findings do not address the effect of punishment on group cooperation, efficiency, or surplus.
- The scenarios are stylized and may not generalize to more complex, repeated, or group-based environments.
- The paper does not report how punishment or reward affects future behavior or group outcomes.
