# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment sustains large-scale cooperation in prestate warfare
Authors: Mathew, S., & Boyd, R.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- per capita gain in livestock from raids
- risk of death per raid
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of free riding (desertion, cowardice, loot appropriation)
- frequency and type of punishment (verbal, corporal, fines)
- scale of cooperation (number of warriors, social units involved)
- norms and attitudes toward sanctioning
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are behavioral: participation, free riding, and punishment. Some data on per capita livestock gain and risk of death are reported, but there is no explicit calculation of group efficiency or total welfare relative to a cooperative optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The Turkana sustain costly, large-scale cooperation in warfare through informal punishment of free riders. Raiding parties are large and composed of unrelated individuals from multiple social units. Free riding (desertion, cowardice, unfair loot appropriation) is common, but is often sanctioned through verbal reprimands, corporal punishment, and fines. The system relies on third-party consensus and community enforcement, not formal institutions. Norms governing warfare are group-beneficial at the ethnolinguistic scale, not just for smaller social units. While the paper provides rich evidence on the mechanisms and scale of cooperation and punishment, it does not provide direct measures of efficiency or group payoff as defined in public goods games, nor does it experimentally manipulate punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative about the real-world operation of punishment and its role in sustaining cooperation at large scales, especially in the absence of formal institutions. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, nor does it use a controlled experimental design. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, the paper offers strong qualitative support for the idea that punishment can sustain cooperation and reduce free riding, even in large, anonymous groups. The evidence is most relevant for understanding the mechanisms and social context of punishment, rather than for parameterizing efficiency outcomes in laboratory PGGs. The findings suggest that punishment can be effective at large group sizes and in the absence of repeated interaction or kinship, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency changes due to punishment in a controlled game setting.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Raiding parties average 315 men (median 248), drawn from multiple settlements, age groups, and territorial sections.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is costly to the punisher in terms of time, effort, and possibly social risk, but not quantified in units. Fines involve the violator sacrificing an animal.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is effective in deterring free riding, but the impact per unit cost is not quantified.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Positive incentives (rewards for bravery) are mentioned as important but are diffuse and not directly measured.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants are aware of others' behavior (desertion, cowardice, loot appropriation) through discussion and observation, but not via formal summaries.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is public, administered by age-mates and community members; violators and punishers are known.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and ethnographic, not experimental; there is no control group or manipulation of punishment.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not measured in a formal or quantitative way as in public goods games.
- Outcomes are primarily behavioral (participation, free riding, punishment) rather than payoff-based.
- The context is warfare, which may differ in important ways from standard public goods games.
- No direct estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or total group welfare.
