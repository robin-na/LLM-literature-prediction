Outcome notes:
The main outcome is the frequency of cooperation (f_c), i.e., the proportion of cooperators in the population. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Payoff is used as an input to agent learning and update rules, but not as a reported group-level outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a punishment mechanism, where cooperators can choose to become punishers at a cost, increases the frequency of cooperation in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game. The effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low and the update period for strategy revision is short. The Q-learning-based periodic update rule (switching between Fermi and aspiration-driven rules) further enhances cooperation. However, the main reported outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. The model is not a public goods game but a spatial pairwise prisoner's dilemma with punishment and learning dynamics.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation rates in spatial social dilemmas, especially when punishment is not too costly and agents update strategies frequently. However, because the model is not a public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff, it should be used only as weak, adjacent evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. The findings suggest that low punishment cost and frequent update opportunities are likely to make punishment more effective at promoting cooperation, but the translation to efficiency is uncertain.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model uses a square lattice of size L x L, with L typically 100 or 200 (i.e., 10,000 or 40,000 players), and also explores other network types with N=2000. The player count is fixed for each simulation.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for T=100,000 time steps, with outcomes averaged over the last 5,000 steps. The number of rounds is fixed and known.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each round.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (rho) is a key parameter, varied in simulations (e.g., 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7). Lower punishment cost increases cooperation; high cost reduces or eliminates the effect.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (beta) is set (e.g., beta=0.8), but not systematically varied in the main results. The model specifies the impact of punishment on defectors.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of time steps (rounds) is fixed and known in the simulation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game but a spatial pairwise prisoner's dilemma; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- The main outcome is the frequency of cooperation, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- No direct analysis of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff is provided.
- The model is entirely simulation-based and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is modeled as a binary action available only to cooperators, which may differ from real-world or experimental PGG designs.
