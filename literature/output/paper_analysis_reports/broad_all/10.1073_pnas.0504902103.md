# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Stabilizing the Earth's climate is not a losing game: Supporting evidence from public goods experiments
Authors: Milinski, M., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Marotzke, J.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- cooperation rate
- frequency of high contributions
- punishment via indirect reciprocity (social reward/withholding support)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: how much subjects contribute to the climate pool (in €), the frequency of cooperation (contributing €1 or €2), and the effect of reputation and information on these behaviors. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. The closest payoff-related outcome is the amount invested in the climate pool, but this is not redistributed to players and does not constitute group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that cooperation in a climate public goods game can be substantially increased by making contributions public (enabling reputation effects) and by providing expert information about climate change. Social rewards (support in the indirect reciprocity game) reinforce cooperative behavior, and non-cooperators are socially 'punished' by being less likely to receive support. However, the game design does not include direct monetary punishment, and the outcome measured is not group efficiency or total earnings but rather the behavioral willingness to contribute to a public good with real-world impact. The findings suggest that reputation and information can be powerful motivators for cooperation in public goods dilemmas, even when the benefits are not directly redistributed to the contributors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that making contributions public (enabling reputation/reward) and providing expert information can increase cooperation rates in a public goods context, even when direct monetary punishment is absent and the public good is not redistributed. However, because the outcome is not group efficiency or total earnings, and because the 'punishment' is social (withholding support in a separate game) rather than direct, the findings are only adjacent to the core prediction task. The results suggest that enabling reputation or reward mechanisms may have a positive effect on cooperation, but the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes in standard PGGs with or without punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session, alternating between public goods and indirect reciprocity games. Players did not know the total number of rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; players were separated by partitions and made decisions silently.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were discrete: €0, €1, or €2 (not all-or-nothing, but not continuous either).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each €1 contributed is doubled (multiplier = 2), but the pool is not redistributed to players; instead, it is used for a public good (press advertisement). Thus, the standard MPCR concept does not apply directly.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward exists in the form of social support in the indirect reciprocity game, where donors can give €1.50 (doubled to €3 for the receiver) based on observed cooperation history.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding costs the donor €1.50 and gives the receiver €3 in the indirect reciprocity game.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 2:1 (receiver gets €3 per €1.50 cost to donor) in the indirect reciprocity game.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In nonanonymous rounds, each player's decision and contribution were displayed below their pseudonym for 20 seconds. In the indirect reciprocity game, histories of giving were displayed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In nonanonymous rounds, contributions were linked to pseudonyms, enabling identification for social reward/punishment in the indirect reciprocity game.

## Important Limitations
- No direct monetary punishment mechanism; only social reward/punishment via indirect reciprocity.
- No analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare; outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates).
- The public good is not redistributed among players, so standard efficiency concepts do not apply.
- Players did not know the total number of rounds, which may affect cooperation dynamics.
- Subjects were university students in Germany; external validity to other populations is untested.
- Contributions were from an experimental endowment, not from participants' own earned money.
