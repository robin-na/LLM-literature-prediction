# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Team incentives and leadership
Authors: Drouvelis, M., Nosenzo, D., & Sefton, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (group earnings as % of max possible)
- combined earnings
- individual earnings (leader, worker, high/low productivity)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/effort levels
- delegation of leader power (gamma)
- reward allocation patterns
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (as defined by group earnings relative to maximum possible), combined and individual earnings, and effort/contribution rates. Delegation of leader power is also measured in the endogenous treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Introducing a leader with discretionary reward power in a repeated team production game (a close variant of the public goods game) increases group efficiency from about 30-33% to 72-76% of the maximum possible. Leaders use their power to reward high contributors and withhold rewards from low contributors, effectively incentivizing effort. This effect holds for both homogeneous and heterogeneous teams. However, the distribution of gains is highly unequal, with leaders capturing most of the surplus. When workers can choose how much power to delegate to the leader, they typically restrict it to less than half the team output, and the potential efficiency gains are not fully realized. The main reason is that workers do not benefit much from leadership, as leaders only compensate them for effort costs and keep most of the residual output.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in VCM-like team production games, introducing a central authority with discretionary reward power (a form of positive sanctioning) can dramatically increase group efficiency, even when the leader is self-interested. The effect is robust to team heterogeneity. However, the efficiency gains are distributed unequally, and when delegation is endogenous, reluctance to empower the leader limits the realized gains. For prediction tasks, the presence of a leader with reward power (as opposed to equal sharing) should be expected to increase efficiency substantially, but the effect may be moderated if the leader's power is limited or if workers can restrict delegation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where the 'punishment' dimension is implemented as positive reward power rather than costly punishment, but the mechanism (centralized sanctioning) is closely related.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in teams of 5 (4 workers + 1 leader).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consists of 10 rounds (Study 1) or 3 blocks of 10 rounds (Study 2).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/effort is continuous: each worker chooses 0-10 tokens to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In homogeneous treatments, each contributed token yields 60 points of team output; with 4 workers, MPCR = 60/30 = 2 (but payoff structure is not standard PGG, as output is redistributed). In heterogeneous, two workers have 80, two have 40.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Leader can allocate positive rewards (share of team output) to workers; this is the main treatment variable.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is not costly in the sense of reducing the leader's own endowment, but comes from the team output the leader controls; total rewards cannot exceed leader's share.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is allocated from the leader's share of team output; leader can assign any non-negative amount up to the total share.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the number of rounds (10 rounds per block, 3 blocks in Study 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, all team members are informed about all decisions and earnings for all team members for the round, as well as accumulated point earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward allocations are made by a single, identified leader; all team members know who the leader is and see the allocation.

## Important Limitations
- The 'punishment' or sanctioning mechanism is implemented as positive reward power (discretionary allocation of output), not as costly punishment; results may not generalize to negative sanctioning.
- Efficiency gains from leadership accrue mainly to the leader, with workers' earnings not significantly improved; this may affect willingness to delegate power in real-world settings.
- The game is a close variant of the public goods game (VCM), but not a standard PGG: output is redistributed by a leader or shared equally, not simply multiplied and distributed.
- All experiments are conducted in the lab with student subjects; external validity to field or organizational settings may be limited.
- No direct manipulation of punishment cost or technology; only reward power is studied.
