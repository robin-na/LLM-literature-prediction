## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, the choice of updating rule (BP, DP, LD) can dramatically change the long-run frequencies of defectors, cooperators, and punishers. For the same parameter values, the birth-death process leads to a population dominated by defectors, while the death-birth process results in a majority of cooperative strategies (cooperators and punishers). Link dynamics yields intermediate results. These differences arise even in well-mixed populations and are especially pronounced when mutation rates are not negligible and when more than two strategies interact. The paper does not report group efficiency or payoff, but the shift from defector to cooperator/punisher dominance implies a likely increase in group welfare under some updating rules.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the updating rule (a feature of evolutionary dynamics, not typically manipulated in lab experiments) can be a major moderator of the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, on efficiency. In public goods games with punishment, the same game design (including punishment parameters) can yield very different long-run outcomes depending on the updating rule: punishment may be ineffective (defector-dominated) under one rule and highly effective (cooperator/punisher-dominated) under another. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as indicating that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the evolutionary dynamics assumed, especially in theoretical or agent-based models. For empirical prediction, this suggests caution in generalizing across studies with different evolutionary assumptions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model 3 (public goods game with punishment) uses N=40 in the main stationary distribution analysis (Figure 6). Theoretical results are for arbitrary N, but N=40 is used in the main example.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical evolutionary models are run until stationarity; the Markov chain is analyzed in the long run. There is no fixed number of rounds, but the analysis is of the stationary distribution.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a discrete strategy choice: cooperate (contribute fixed c), defect (contribute nothing), punish (contribute and punish), or opt out (loner).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is r/N, with r=2.8 and N=40 in the main example, so mpcr=0.07.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers pay cost gamma per defector; gamma=0.6 in the main example.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punisher inflicts fine beta=1.5 per defector; punishment effectiveness is thus beta/gamma=2.5.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards are mentioned as possible extensions but not included in the analyzed model.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report direct efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all results are in terms of strategy frequencies and equilibrium structure.
- The evolutionary updating rule (BP, DP, LD) is not a manipulable dimension in most empirical or experimental studies, limiting direct applicability to lab or field data.
- Findings are for well-mixed populations; structured populations may yield different results.
- Mutation rates are non-negligible in the main analyses, which may not correspond to typical experimental settings.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented; all results are from mathematical analysis and simulation.
