## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a two-stage game-theoretic model in which players can cooperate or not, and, if there are non-cooperators, cooperators can choose to fight (coerce) or not. The key result is that the cost structure of coercion (punishment) is decisive: if coercion is cheap and effective (as in synchronous/remote attack, e.g., human projectile weapons), then robust equilibria with high cooperation and high efficiency are possible. If coercion is costly and ineffective (as in asynchronous attack, e.g., most non-human animals), only non-cooperation is robust, and group efficiency is low. The model thus predicts that enabling effective, low-cost punishment can transform a low-efficiency public goods game into a high-efficiency one, provided certain thresholds are met.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling effective, low-cost punishment (i.e., high punishment effectiveness per unit cost) in a public-goods-like environment can shift the equilibrium from low to high efficiency, especially as group size increases. The model is directly informative for the effect of punishment cost and punishment technology (effectiveness), and indirectly for player count and the structure of the contribution decision. It suggests that, all else equal, lowering punishment cost or increasing punishment effectiveness should make punishment more likely to increase efficiency. The findings are most relevant for one-shot or short games with simultaneous moves and no communication, and may be less directly applicable to repeated or highly structured lab experiments with additional features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Player count n is a parameter in the model; results are derived for arbitrary n, with some thresholds depending on n. Larger n increases the threshold for robust cooperation under synchronous attack.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a two-stage, one-shot game (not repeated rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (C) or not cooperate (NC).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The per capita benefit of cooperation (b) and cost (c) are explicit parameters; the structure is equivalent to a public goods game with mpcr = b/n.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is parameterized (r); lower cost (r) enables robust cooperation and high efficiency; high cost prevents it.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled via synchronous (remote) vs asynchronous (proximal) attack; high effectiveness (synchronous) enables cooperation and efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game structure is common knowledge; all players know the structure and number of stages.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model is a one-shot, two-stage game, not a repeated or multi-round public goods game.
- Punishment is modeled as preemptive or compensated coercion, not as post-facto punishment as in most lab experiments.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms beyond direct coercion.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are modeled.
- The model assumes perfect knowledge of payoffs and structure by all players.
- Applicability to real-world or experimental settings may be limited by the abstraction of synchronous vs asynchronous attack.
