## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that coupling real-time environmental feedback (temperature) to payoffs in a threshold public goods game can promote and stabilize cooperation. When the public good threshold is not met, temperature rises, which reduces all players' payoffs (environmental punishment). This feedback leads to cyclical dynamics where cooperation rebounds as payoffs fall, stabilizing temperature near a reference level. The model outperforms versions without feedback in both temperature control and cooperation rates. However, the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome, and the 'punishment' is environmental rather than peer-based.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is only indirectly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The 'punishment' mechanism is environmental (temperature rise reducing payoffs for all), not a peer or institutional punishment option. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but shows that environmental feedback can stabilize cooperation and temperature. The findings suggest that automatic, group-level sanctions (environmental feedback) can promote cooperation, but the results may not transfer directly to settings with explicit punishment options. Use this paper for contextual or mechanistic insight, not for direct quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model uses a finite, well-mixed population; main results use N=100.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model is iterated over many time steps (e.g., 10^6 steps), but not in discrete rounds as in typical lab PGGs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N, with r=2 and N=100 in main results.

## Important Limitations
- The model does not include peer or institutional punishment; only environmental feedback is present.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported as an outcome; temperature is used as a proxy for welfare.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment options or to laboratory PGGs with human subjects.
- No empirical or experimental data; results are from theoretical modeling and simulation.
- No direct analysis of how design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR) moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
