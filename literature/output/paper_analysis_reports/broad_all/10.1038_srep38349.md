# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Solving the second-order free rider problem in a public goods game: An experiment using a leader support system
Authors: Ozono, H., Jin, N., Watabe, M., & Shimizu, K.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average profit of leaders
- average profit of followers
- group profit
- frequency of high cooperation groups (as defined by contribution thresholds)
- total PGG contribution (as a proxy for group welfare)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency
- punishment type (linkage, group-focused, self-focused)
- support for leader
- contribution rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables include group and individual profits, as well as group contribution rates. The paper explicitly analyzes average profit for both leaders and followers, and the frequency of groups achieving high cooperation (defined by contribution thresholds). Non-payoff outcomes such as punishment frequency and support for the leader are also reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when leaders are given the ability to punish followers using resources provided by follower support (support-present condition), both the frequency and effectiveness of punishment increase compared to a no-support condition. This leads to a higher probability of groups achieving high cooperation (as measured by contribution rates) and higher average profits for both leaders and followers, especially under 'linkage' punishment leaders who punish both non-cooperators and non-supporters. The no-support condition, where leaders have externally provided punishment resources, results in weaker punishment and lower group cooperation. The findings suggest that the structure of punishment funding and the type of leader punishment strategy are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling leaders to punish using resources provided by group members (rather than external resources) increases the efficiency of punishment in public goods games, as measured by group and individual profits and the frequency of high-cooperation groups. The effect is especially pronounced when leaders punish both first- and second-order free riders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a leader support system, the type of punishment strategy, and the source of punishment capital are key moderators. The control (no-support) condition provides a baseline for efficiency, and the support-present condition demonstrates a substantial positive shift in efficiency when punishment is enabled and properly incentivized.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 6 players: 1 leader and 5 followers.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for 15 rounds (periods).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; experiment is described as anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: followers decide whether to contribute all 100 tokens or none.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contribution is doubled and distributed equally among 5 followers. For each 100 tokens contributed, 200 tokens are distributed, so each follower receives 40 tokens per contribution. MPCR = 200/5/100 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: leader spends 20 tokens to reduce a follower's tokens by 40 (punishment rate is double).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 token spent by leader reduces 2 tokens from follower (punishment rate = 2).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is implemented; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told in advance that the game will be repeated 15 times.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, all members are informed about who contributed, who supported, and who was punished, as well as the amounts.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All members are informed who has been punished and by how much after the leader's decision.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis of leader punishment type (linkage, group-focused, self-focused) is post-hoc; leader type is not experimentally manipulated.
- The experiment uses a fixed punishment rate and capital structure; results may differ with other parameterizations (e.g., higher punishment power).
- The leader support system is exogenously imposed; the emergence of such systems is not studied.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with university students, which may limit generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is included, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
