Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the equilibrium states of the population (which strategy dominates) as determined by payoff-based replicator dynamics and simulations. These are interpreted as efficiency/welfare outcomes, since domination by prosocial punishers or cooperators corresponds to high group welfare, while domination by defectors or antisocial punishers corresponds to low group welfare. The paper does not report empirical data but uses theoretical and simulation-based analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that prosocial punishment can evolve and promote cooperation (and thus high efficiency) in public goods games, even without auxiliary mechanisms like network structure or voluntary participation. The efficiency of the group (measured by which strategy dominates and thus the average group payoff) is increased when prosocial punishment dominates. The evolution of prosocial punishment (and thus efficiency) is facilitated by higher returns to the public good (mpcr), higher punishment effectiveness, and optimal punishment cost. Allowing unused punishment resources to be redistributed (non-wasteful punishment) further facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency. Antisocial punishment can dominate under low punishment cost or low public good return, reducing efficiency. Network structure can both hinder and facilitate the evolution of punishment, but under favorable conditions, it helps prosocial punishment dominate. The model provides phase diagrams mapping out these effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment can increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective, the cost is optimal (not too low or high), and the returns to the public good are high. The model also shows that if punishment is too cheap, antisocial punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency, while if it is too costly, punishment does not evolve. The presence of a reward mechanism (redistribution of unused punishment resources) further increases the likelihood of high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions such as mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward. The results are theoretical and should be interpreted as providing mechanistic and qualitative guidance rather than empirical effect sizes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size in the PGG is set to g=9 in most simulations; population sizes (N) are also specified (e.g., N=10000, N=40000, N=90000). The group size is a key parameter in the model.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is evolutionary and proceeds over many generations; in simulations, equilibrium is reached after many time steps. There is no fixed number of rounds as in a finite repeated game, but the evolutionary process is iterated until equilibrium.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: individuals can either cooperate (contribute c) or defect (contribute nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is r/g, where r is the enhancement factor and g is group size. r is varied in the phase diagrams and is a key moderator: higher r facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Cost to the punisher per unit of punishment is c' (set to 1 in most cases, but varied in Fig 2). Too low a cost favors antisocial punishment (lower efficiency), too high a cost prevents punishment from evolving (lower efficiency), and an optimal intermediate cost favors prosocial punishment (higher efficiency).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the punishment enhancement factor (rho), which multiplies contributions to the punishment pool. Higher rho (more effective punishment) facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the non-wasteful punishment model, unused punishment resources are redistributed as rewards to contributors, which facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are reported.
- Outcomes are based on evolutionary equilibrium and phase diagrams, not on direct measurement of efficiency in finite repeated games.
- The model assumes binary contribution choices and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- Punishment is modeled as institutional (pool) punishment, not peer punishment; results may not generalize to all punishment mechanisms.
- The model does not include communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms that may affect real-world outcomes.
- Parameter ranges and dynamics are specific to the model; real-world applicability may be limited.
