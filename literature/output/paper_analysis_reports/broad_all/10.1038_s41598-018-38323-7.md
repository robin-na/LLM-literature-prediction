# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Low-cost, high-impact altruistic punishment promotes cooperation cascades in human social networks
Authors: Bond, R. M.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution amount
- spread of cooperative behavior (contribution) through network
- punishment behavior (giving/receiving punishment)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is individual contribution in a public goods game, and the spread of contribution behavior through the network. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus is reported. Some discussion of previous work on payoff exists, but this paper focuses on behavioral outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in public goods games, the presence and structure of punishment (especially low-cost, high-impact punishment) increases individual contributions and the persistence and spread of cooperative behavior through the network. The effect of one player's contribution on another's is stronger and endures longer in the low-cost, high-impact punishment condition. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral measures (contribution and its network effects).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that low-cost, high-impact punishment increases individual contributions and the spread of cooperation in public goods games, but does not provide direct evidence about efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the results suggest that punishment (especially when low-cost and high-impact) is likely to increase efficiency if higher contributions translate to higher group payoffs, but this is an inference rather than a direct finding. The paper is most useful for understanding how punishment design affects contribution behavior and its propagation, rather than direct efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 participants per round in all versions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 6 rounds, each with a new group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; interactions were anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 MU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (total group contribution multiplied by 1.5 and split evenly among 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost varied: 1 MU (low-cost) or 3 MU (high-cost) per punishment event.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact varied: 1 MU (low-impact) or 3 MU (high-impact) per punishment event. Low-cost, high-impact punishment led to the greatest increase in cooperation spread.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was present; only punishment was available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds (6) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observed the contributions of their group members in each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants did not know who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus; all main outcomes are behavioral (contribution and its spread).
- Findings about efficiency or payoff must be inferred from contribution behavior, not directly measured.
- The study uses secondary data from previously published experiments; some design details are referenced from another paper.
- Network effects are measured in a specific, artificial network structure (randomly assigned, anonymous groups, no repeated partners), which may limit generalizability to real-world networks.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not inform about the interaction of punishment and reward.
