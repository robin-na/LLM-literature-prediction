## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment in public goods games is best explained by social reciprocity: individuals are motivated to punish norm violators (free riders) even when doing so is costly and does not improve their own material payoff, and even when the violator is outside their immediate group. Experimental and survey evidence shows that punishment is not solely driven by the desire to reduce payoff differentials, and that people continue to punish even when the cost of punishment exceeds the harm inflicted. The demand for punishment is relatively inelastic to price, and motivations are primarily normative. While the models present payoff calculations and show that punishment can stabilize cooperation under certain initial conditions, the empirical focus is on behavioral motivations and patterns rather than direct measures of group efficiency or total earnings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment mechanisms in public goods games can sustain higher levels of cooperation, but it does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff outcomes. The theoretical models show that, under certain conditions, populations with punishers can achieve higher average payoffs than those dominated by free riders, but these are model-based results rather than empirical efficiency measurements. The experimental and survey data are primarily about who punishes, why, and under what conditions, rather than about the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that punishment can support cooperation even when it is costly, and that motivations are primarily normative, but it does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, ability to punish outgroup) are discussed in the context of behavioral patterns, not efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical models use 2 and 4 players; experiments use groups of 4 and 8 (two groups of 4).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments typically last 10-20 rounds (mutual monitoring), 15 rounds (punishment price experiment), and 15 periods (social reciprocity experiment).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical models use binary (all-or-nothing) contribution and punishment decisions; experiments allow continuous contributions.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Theoretical model: 2 players, contribution multiplied by 1.5 and shared. Experiments: 4 players, each contribution returns 0.5 per group member (MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is varied: in models, 1 EMU to reduce 2 EMU; in experiments, 1 EMU to reduce 2 EMU, and in price elasticity experiment, cost per unit varies from 0.25 to 4.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness per unit cost is manipulated: 1:2 (cost:impact) in most experiments, varied in price elasticity experiment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Experiments specify a fixed number of rounds/periods, but it is not explicitly stated whether participants are told the total number in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the contributions of other group members and, in some experiments, the contributions of all participants (including outgroup).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the social reciprocity experiment, players do not know whether punishment came from ingroup or outgroup, to prevent retaliation.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes for the experimental treatments; focus is on behavioral motivations and patterns.
- Payoff-based outcomes are primarily discussed in theoretical models, not as empirical results.
- Experiments are conducted with undergraduate students in laboratory settings, which may limit generalizability.
- Survey vignettes are hypothetical and may not reflect actual behavior.
- The main empirical results are about punishment behavior and motivation, not about the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency.
