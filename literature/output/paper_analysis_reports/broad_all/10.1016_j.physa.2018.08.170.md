Authors: Podobnik, B., Gabor, A. M., & Kirbis, I. S.
Journal: Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications
Year: 2019

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- system growth rate (alpha)
- average agent reward
- total system size
- distribution of agent sizes (wealth, etc.)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- level of cooperation (probability p)
- corruption index (CPI) as proxy for cooperation
- surveyed perceived necessary fine to induce compliance
Outcome notes:
Theoretical model focuses on system growth and average reward as functions of cooperation and punishment. Empirical components are limited to cross-country correlation between fines and corruption, and a survey on perceived fine levels, not direct payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that the system's growth rate (interpreted as efficiency or collective payoff) increases monotonically with the level of cooperation, which itself increases with the level of punishment. There is a threshold: if punishment is too high, the system can collapse (negative growth), but within reasonable bounds, higher punishment leads to more cooperation and higher efficiency. Empirical data suggest that less corrupt (more cooperative) countries tend to have higher fines, supporting the model's prediction that higher punishment is associated with higher cooperation. Survey data indicate the perceived fine needed to induce compliance, but do not directly measure payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public-goods-like environments, enabling or increasing punishment increases efficiency (system growth rate), up to a point. The model is not a direct PGG but is closely related, and the main mechanism—punishment increasing cooperation, which increases collective payoff—transfers to PGG settings. The paper does not provide empirical estimates of effect size, but the qualitative direction is clear: punishment increases efficiency unless set excessively high. The model also highlights that the effect of punishment depends on the baseline level of cooperation and the distribution of punishment sensitivity in the population.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a cost (f) imposed on defectors; the model assumes altruistic punishment (no material gain for punisher). The effect of increasing punishment is analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is captured by the parameter f; higher f increases cooperation and system growth up to a threshold.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not a direct public goods game; it uses the Hawk-Dove game and population growth as proxies for efficiency.
- No experimental or field data on actual payoff or efficiency effects of punishment; empirical components are limited to cross-country fines and a survey on perceived fines.
- No direct manipulation or measurement of most game design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, communication).
- The model assumes altruistic punishment with no material gain for the punisher, which may differ from some PGG implementations.
- Empirical findings (e.g., fines vs. corruption) are correlational and not causal.
