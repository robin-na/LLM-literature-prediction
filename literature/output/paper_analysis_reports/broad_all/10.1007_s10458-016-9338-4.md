## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a commitment mechanism—where players can propose binding agreements before the PGG, with compensation for defection—substantially increases both cooperation and average payoffs, provided the cost of arranging commitment is not too high. The effect is robust across group sizes and compensation levels, as long as compensation is above a threshold. The optimal participation threshold (number of committers required) depends on the severity of the dilemma and the cost of commitment. The model shows that commitment mechanisms can transform the efficiency of the PGG, making group payoffs much closer to the fully cooperative optimum.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling credible commitment mechanisms in PGG-like environments can substantially increase efficiency, especially when the cost of arranging commitment is low relative to the benefit of cooperation. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a commitment mechanism (even if not a classic punishment) should be expected to increase efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the cost of commitment, the mpcr, and the participation threshold. The results are robust across group sizes and compensation levels (above a threshold). However, the model does not directly analyze classic punishment (costly peer punishment), so direct transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be done with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size N is a key parameter in the model; results are shown for N=5 and generalized for varying N. The effect of commitment on efficiency is robust across group sizes.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Both one-shot and repeated (multi-round) PGGs are analyzed. The model is extended to R>1 rounds, showing that longer commitments require stricter enforcement but still improve efficiency.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The multiplication factor r (mpcr = r/N) is a key parameter; higher r (less harsh dilemma) increases the effect of commitment on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model analyzes commitment mechanisms, not classic costly peer punishment; results may not transfer directly to punishment-enabled PGGs.
- All results are theoretical and simulation-based; no new empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The commitment mechanism assumes enforceable compensation for defection, which may not be realistic in all settings.
- No communication, reputation, or network structure is modeled.
- The model does not address the effect of information about others' actions or identities.
