# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Individual differences in behavioural inhibition explain free riding in public good games when punishment is expected but not implemented
Authors: Skatova, A., & Ferguson, E.
Journal: Behavioral and Brain Functions
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- presence/absence of zero contributions (free riding)
- correlations with BIS-anxiety and FFFS
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are individual contribution levels and whether participants made zero contributions (free riding). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both the implementation and the expectation of punishment (even when not actually implemented) increase individual contributions in a one-shot PGG compared to standard PGGs without punishment. Implemented punishment leads to higher contributions than non-implemented punishment. Individual differences in BIS-anxiety (a measure of behavioral inhibition) predict higher contributions and less free riding when punishment is expected but not implemented. FFFS (flight, fight, freeze system) does not predict contributions. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution levels, free riding), not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that both implemented and expected (but not implemented) centralized punishment increase contributions in a one-shot PGG, with implemented punishment having a stronger effect. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in contribution rates under different punishment regimes and for understanding the role of individual differences in behavioral inhibition. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling punishment (even if not always implemented) increases cooperation, but the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred from the reported outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG group consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each block consisted of multiple one-shot games (trials); the number of trials per block varied (e.g., 13 in punishment blocks).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; decisions were made anonymously in individual booths.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could divide their 20 MUs between private and public accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (the sum of all group contributions is multiplied by 0.5 and distributed equally).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was centralized and automatic; the cost to the punished was a fine, but there is no mention of a cost to a punisher (since it is not peer punishment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was a fixed fine for contributing less than the group average; the exact magnitude of the fine is not specified.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not explicitly informed how many blocks or trials they would play.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each trial, participants received feedback about the average group contribution and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was centralized and automatic; no identification of a punisher was possible or relevant.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only individual contribution and free riding behavior are analyzed.
- The punishment mechanism is centralized and automatic, not peer-based, which may limit generalizability to other PGG punishment designs.
- The number of real payoff-relevant trials is limited (only one per block), with the rest being hypothetical, which may affect ecological validity.
- The magnitude of the punishment fine is not specified, limiting the ability to compare effect sizes to other studies.
- The study focuses on individual differences in behavioral inhibition, which may not generalize to broader population-level effects.
