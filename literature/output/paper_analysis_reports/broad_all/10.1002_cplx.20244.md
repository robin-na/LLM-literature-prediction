## Source
Title: Evolution of Shame as an Adaptation to Social Punishment and its Contribution to Social Cohesiveness
Authors: Jaffe, K.
Journal: Complexity
Year: 2008

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of generosity alleles (pro-social behavior)
- frequency of punishment alleles
- frequency of shame alleles
- population-level dynamics of cooperation and selfishness
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the frequencies of behavioral alleles (generosity, punishment, shame) and the resulting population-level patterns of cooperation and selfishness. There is no direct reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare. The model does include resource accumulation and survival, but these are not analyzed as aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that shame can evolve as an adaptation to social punishment, increasing pro-social behavior (generosity) after punishment. When punishment is present (especially if costless to the punisher) or when shame is present, populations maintain higher levels of generosity and a balance between punishers and non-punishers. However, the model does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff, focusing instead on the evolutionary dynamics of behavioral traits. The results suggest that shame and punishment can stabilize cooperation for long periods, but with fluctuations between cooperative and selfish phases.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that punishment (and shame) can increase pro-social behavior in resource-sharing environments, which may translate to higher efficiency in public goods games. However, because the model is not a direct PGG and does not report efficiency or group payoff, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The main relevance is in showing that punishment and shame can stabilize cooperation, especially when punishment is not costly to the punisher. The findings are more about evolutionary stability and trait frequencies than about efficiency per se.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Population size is fixed at 1000 agents, but the model is not a round-based game with a fixed group size as in PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations run for 500 time steps (rounds), but these are evolutionary time steps, not repeated PGG rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Generosity (contribution) is continuous, in deciles of food wealth (0-10, i.e., 0-100%). Not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is a key parameter (1-5 units of food), paid by punisher and/or punished depending on simulation. Costless punishment (to punisher) is also modeled.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment impact is equal to cost (food units lost by punished and punisher). No explicit manipulation of effectiveness per unit cost.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. There is a form of 'egoistic reciprocity' where donations increase the donor's mineral wealth, but no explicit reward mechanism as in PGG reward treatments.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a direct public goods game; it is an agent-based simulation of resource sharing and punishment.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare; outcomes are frequencies of behavioral traits.
- Findings are about evolutionary stability and trait dynamics, not about payoff-based outcomes.
- The model assumes a large, fixed population and does not simulate small group repeated games.
- No explicit analysis of how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size) affect efficiency or payoff.
