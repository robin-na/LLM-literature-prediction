The main dependent variable is population mean payoff (explicitly calculated and compared across conditions), which is a direct measure of efficiency/welfare. The paper also reports on cooperation rates and punishment types, but these are secondary to the payoff analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a hierarchical public goods game, the presence of corrupt punishment can maintain civilian cooperation but undermines altruistic punishment and reduces population mean payoff. Introducing corruption control, where altruistic punishers can also punish or exclude corrupt punishers, increases both cooperation and efficiency. Asymmetrical punishment (social exclusion of corrupt punishers) is more effective than symmetrical punishment (equal fines for corrupt punishers and defectors) in promoting both cooperation and population mean payoff. The model provides explicit formulas and phase diagrams showing how efficiency depends on group size, punishment cost, fine, bribe, cop proportion, and the probability of spiteful corruption. The results are robust across a range of parameter values.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, quantitative theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with hierarchical roles and corruption. It shows that the introduction of punishment (especially with corruption control) increases efficiency, and that the effectiveness depends on the design dimensions such as group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the structure of punishment (symmetrical vs. asymmetrical). The explicit formulas and phase diagrams can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game parameters. The results are most applicable to settings with hierarchical roles and the possibility of corrupt punishment, but the general findings about the positive effect of well-designed punishment on efficiency are likely to transfer to standard PGGs with punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- It assumes infinite, well-mixed populations and evolutionary dynamics, which may not capture all features of finite, real-world groups.
- No communication, reputation, or network structure is included.
- No reward mechanism is modeled; only punishment and corruption control are considered.
- The model assumes fixed roles (cops vs. civilians) and does not allow for role switching.
- The forms of corruption (spiteful vs. bribery) are modeled probabilistically, which may not capture all real-world complexities.
