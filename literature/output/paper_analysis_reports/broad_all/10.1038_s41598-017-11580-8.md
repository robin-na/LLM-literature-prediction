# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Caring Cooperators and Powerful Punishers: Differential Effects of Induced Care and Power Motivation on Different Types of Economic Decision Making
Authors: Chierchia, G., Lesemann, F. H. P., Snower, D., Vogel, M., & Singer, T.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation factor score (latent variable from contributions in public goods, trust, dictator, etc.)
- punishment factor score (latent variable from punishment in 2nd/3rd party punishment, ultimatum, impunity games)
- individual game behaviors: contributions, punishment assigned, rejections, etc.
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are latent factor scores for cooperation and punishment, derived from multiple economic games. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. Outcomes are behavioral, not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Inducing a Care motive increases a latent cooperation factor (which includes public goods contributions), while Power induction increases a punishment factor (punishment in 2nd/3rd party punishment games, ultimatum, impunity). The two factors are largely orthogonal. Care increases cooperation relative to control, but does not increase punishment. Power increases punishment relative to Care, but not relative to control. No direct efficiency or payoff outcomes are reported, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that punishment and cooperation are distinct behavioral tendencies, and that motivational context (Care vs. Power) can differentially affect them. However, it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It does suggest that punishment behavior can be increased by Power motives, but this is not linked to group payoff or efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly by clarifying that punishment and cooperation are separable and can be independently manipulated.

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Player count varies by game; public goods game and other games are included, but exact player count for each is not specified in the main text. Standard public goods games typically use 4 or more players.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Games are described as one-shot or single-decision; repeated rounds are not mentioned for public goods or punishment games.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; standard lab protocols suggest no chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Contribution choices in public goods and other games are described as continuous (e.g., 'how much money to contribute'), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Public goods game is included, but MPCR value is not specified in the main text.
- punishment cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment games involve spending money to reduce others' payoffs, but the cost per unit is not specified in the main text.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is not specified in the main text.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Games are one-shot; participants know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. In punishment games, participants observe others' actions (e.g., how much another player transferred), but summary information in public goods games is not detailed.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation and punishment factor scores) are reported.
- Game parameters (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost, group size) are not specified in the main text, limiting the ability to map findings to specific game designs.
- Findings are based on latent factor analysis across multiple games, not on public goods games with and without punishment per se.
- No direct manipulation of punishment availability in public goods games; punishment is measured as a behavioral tendency across several games.
- Cannot determine the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games from this paper.
