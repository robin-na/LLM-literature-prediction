# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Caste and Punishment: the Legacy of Caste Culture in Norm Enforcement
Authors: Hoff, K., Kshetramade, M., & Fehr, E.
Journal: Economic Journal
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- third party punishment assigned (absolute and relative)
- probability of punishing norm violation
- cooperation rate by player B
- beliefs about expected punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of costly punishment assigned by a third party to a norm violator. The paper also reports on the probability of punishing, and on cooperation rates, but does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. Payoff-based outcomes are not analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that high caste individuals are much more willing to engage in costly third-party punishment of norm violations (defection in a sequential exchange game) than low caste individuals, when the victim is a member of their own specific caste. This difference is not explained by wealth, education, or political experience. The effect disappears when the victim is not a member of the punisher's own caste, indicating that in-group favouritism among high castes drives the result. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, but discusses the implications for collective action and norm enforcement capacity.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to punish norm violations is moderated by social identity (caste status and in-group affiliation), but does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that punishment may be more effective at sustaining cooperation in groups with strong in-group identity and high willingness to punish, but does not quantify efficiency outcomes. The game design is a close variant of a public goods or trust game with third-party punishment, but efficiency is not measured. Use this paper to inform moderators of punishment behavior, not direct efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three players: A (first mover), B (second mover/norm violator), and C (third party punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is played as a one-shot (single round) interaction.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; all decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: A can send all or nothing; B can return half or keep all.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If A sends 50 rupees, it is tripled to 150 (total 200 for B), so the marginal per-capita return for A is 0 if B keeps all, or 2 if B returns 100. The game is a sequential trust game, not a standard linear PGG, but the multiplier is 3x for the sent amount.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For each 2 rupees spent by C, B loses 10 rupees. Punishment is costly to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 5:1 (2 rupees spent by C reduces B's payoff by 10 rupees).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is explicitly one-shot; players know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the actions of others relevant to their own decisions (e.g., C learns A and B's actions after making conditional choices). No summary statistics are shown, but relevant information is provided for payoff calculation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the caste and (via last name) the group identity of others, but not their personal identity or village. Anonymity is maintained, but group identity is salient.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus; all main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, probability of punishing, cooperation rate).
- The game is a sequential trust game with third-party punishment, not a standard linear public goods game, though it is a close variant.
- Findings are about willingness to punish, not about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
- All subjects are adult males from rural Uttar Pradesh, India; generalizability to other populations or contexts is limited.
- The study focuses on the effect of social identity (caste) and in-group affiliation, not on game design parameters such as punishment cost or MPCR.
