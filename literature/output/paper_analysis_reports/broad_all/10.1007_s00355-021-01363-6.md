# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence
Authors: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S.
Journal: Social Choice and Welfare
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average individual payoff
- group average payoff
- total group earnings
- contribution to public good
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned/received
- voting for institution
- migration/moving between groups
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average individual and group payoffs (efficiency), as well as contributions, punishment, and institutional choice. Payoff outcomes are explicitly reported and analyzed (see Table 10, Fig. 8, and text in Section 3.2 and Appendix C).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group has 5 players; two groups per session. Group size is fixed at 5 in NMT, can vary in MT due to migration but starts at 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose any integer between 0 and 50.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 1.6/n, where n is group size. For n=5, MPCR=0.32.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS: 1 token cost to punisher per 3 tokens deducted from target (NMT: 1-to-2, MT: 1-to-3). In FS: fixed fee of 5 tokens per period plus 0.8 fine per token kept.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS: 1 token spent reduces target by 2 (NMT) or 3 (MT) tokens. In FS: 0.8 fine per token kept, plus 5 token fixed fee.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 30 periods in total.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see: (i) average final earnings of their group, (ii) average final earnings of the other group, (iii) rule set of their group, (iv) rule set of the other group, after each period.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants know how much punishment they received, but not who punished them or how much others received.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is limited to groups of 5 players and 30 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- The main efficiency comparison is between formal and informal sanctions, not between punishment and no-punishment baseline.
- Migration (open society) is implemented as costless, which may not reflect real-world frictions.
- The punishment technology differs slightly between treatments (IS: 1-to-2 in NMT, 1-to-3 in MT), which could affect comparability.
- All sessions are conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
