# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ambiguity on audits and cooperation in a public goods game
Authors: Dai, Z. X., Hogarth, R. M., & Villeval, M. C.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean net payoffs per period
- group earnings
- efficiency (as group payoff relative to full cooperation)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- beliefs about audit probability
- punishment received
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group payoffs (efficiency), individual contributions, and beliefs about audits. Efficiency is directly measured as mean net payoffs per period and compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with centralized sanctions triggered by audits, the study manipulates the frequency and regularity of audits (continuous vs. intermittent) and the severity of sanctions. Under ambiguity (unknown audit probability), intermittent audits with high fines (but lower frequency) produce similar or higher contributions and significantly higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audits with low fines. After audits are withdrawn, groups exposed to intermittent audits maintain higher cooperation and efficiency for longer, as their beliefs about audit probability decay more slowly. High frequency-high severity (continuous audits with high fines) also sustains cooperation and efficiency, but at higher cost. Reducing audit frequency or sanction severity too much undermines these effects. The results suggest that, for a given expected cost of sanction, intermittent audits with high severity are more efficient than continuous audits with low severity.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 3-player, 50-round linear PGG with centralized punishment, intermittent (irregular) audit schemes with high severity (punishment effectiveness) and sufficient frequency lead to higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audit schemes with lower severity, especially after audits are withdrawn. The effect is robust as long as audit frequency and severity are not too low. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency and other game dimensions constant, enabling punishment via intermittent, ambiguous audits with high severity will increase efficiency more than continuous, predictable audits with low severity. The paper also shows that both audit frequency and punishment effectiveness (severity) are key moderators: too few audits or too low severity reduces the efficiency benefit. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with centralized punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in fixed groups of 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions last 50 periods (rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose any amount from 0 to 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is centralized: the cost is a fine imposed on the punished, not paid by another player. The cost to the punisher (the authority) is not specified, but the fine to the punished is 1.25 or 3.75 times the deviation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the 'sanction coefficient' (1.25 or 3.75 times the deviation). Higher punishment effectiveness (severity) increases cooperation and efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is implemented; only punishment for negative deviations. Reward mechanisms are discussed as possible extensions.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 50 periods, but are not told when audits will stop.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, participants receive feedback on total group contribution, audit realization, and their own net payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only 3-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- Punishment is centralized and exogenous; results may not generalize to decentralized or peer punishment settings.
- No reward mechanism is implemented; only punishment for negative deviations is studied.
- Audit costs are not internalized in the efficiency calculation; real-world audit costs may affect optimal policy.
- Ambiguity is implemented as unknown audit probability, but real-world ambiguity may differ in form.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
