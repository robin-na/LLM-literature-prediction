# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The personal norm of reciprocity
Authors: Perugini, M., Gallucci, M., Presaghi, F., & Ercolani, A. P.
Journal: European Journal of Personality
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- allocation of monetary payoffs in hypothetical and incentivized games
- amount of money spent to punish or reward in allocation tasks
- Ultimatum Game acceptance/rejection and offers
Non-payoff outcomes:
- self-reported reciprocity (positive, negative, beliefs)
- correlations with personality traits
- scenario-based allocation choices reflecting reward/punishment tendencies
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are scenario-based or incentivized allocation choices reflecting willingness to reward or punish, not group efficiency or total group payoff. Some tasks involve monetary allocations, but the focus is on individual differences in reciprocity, not on group-level efficiency or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that individual differences in positive and negative reciprocity (as measured by the PNR questionnaire) predict how people allocate money to others in response to fair or unfair prior actions, both in hypothetical and incentivized settings. Positive reciprocity predicts greater rewarding of positive behavior, while negative reciprocity predicts greater punishment of negative behavior. These effects are robust across cultures (Italy and UK) and are distinct from general prosocial orientation or Big Five personality traits. However, the studies do not directly examine group efficiency, total welfare, or the effect of punishment on group-level outcomes in repeated public goods or social dilemma games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that individual differences in the propensity to punish or reward (reciprocity norms) influence how people respond to others' actions in allocation games, including costly punishment and reward. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or welfare in public goods games or similar repeated social dilemmas. The findings are most useful for understanding heterogeneity in punishment/reward behavior, not for predicting efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions. The allocation tasks are adjacent to PGGs but lack repeated group interaction, explicit MPCR, or group efficiency measures.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Most tasks are two-player (dictator, ultimatum, allocation games), not group settings. No group-level public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. All tasks are one-shot or scenario-based; no repeated rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Allocation choices are continuous (participants choose how much to allocate or punish/reward), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly to the punisher in allocation tasks (participants spend own money to reduce other's payoff).
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (each unit spent reduces other's payoff by one unit).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants can reward others by allocating more money to them.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is costly (giving more to the other means less for self).
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (each unit given to other is a unit not kept by self).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. All tasks are one-shot; participants know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants are informed of the other player's prior allocation or behavior before making their own decision.

## Important Limitations
- The studies do not use repeated public goods games or measure group efficiency/welfare.
- All tasks are one-shot or scenario-based, not repeated interactions.
- No direct measurement of the effect of punishment on group-level efficiency or total payoff.
- Findings are about individual differences in reciprocity, not about game design moderators of punishment effects.
- No marginal per-capita return or group structure; tasks are dyadic.
- No direct manipulation or analysis of most game design dimensions relevant to public goods games.
