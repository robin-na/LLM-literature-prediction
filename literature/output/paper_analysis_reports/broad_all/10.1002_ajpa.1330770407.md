Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- group productivity
- population-wide average fitness
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy stability (evolutionary/collective stability of TFT/NTFT)
- frequency of cooperation/defection
- scope of spite
Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and analyzes evolutionary stability in terms of absolute and relative fitness, which are directly tied to group and individual payoffs. It does not report empirical data but provides analytic conditions for when cooperation (with punishment) is stable and outperforms defection in terms of group productivity and average fitness.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment-based strategies (TFT/NTFT) can maintain high group efficiency and suppress defection when the population is structured into multiple groups or dyads, allowing intergroup competition. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation and high payoffs is reduced as the scope of spite (the number of groups a defector can affect) increases or as population structure becomes less partitioned. In the limit of a single group (no structure), punishment cannot prevent defection and efficiency collapses. The analysis generalizes to n-person games, showing that the same logic applies: partitioned populations with group-level competition allow punishment to sustain cooperation and high efficiency, while unstructured populations do not.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments will increase efficiency (group payoff) relative to control, but only when the game design includes sufficient population structure (multiple groups, limited overlap in group membership, or mechanisms for intergroup competition). If the game is a single group or highly overlapping, punishment will not improve efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the marginal per-capita return, group size, and population structure can be specified, and they highlight the importance of these dimensions as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Player count (n) is a key parameter in the n-person PD analysis. Larger group size (with sufficient group partitioning) allows for more robust maintenance of cooperation and efficiency via punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The analysis assumes an iterated game with repeated rounds (probability w of continuation). Higher w (more rounds) increases the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The game is modeled as a binary choice (cooperate or defect) in each round, which is equivalent to all-or-nothing contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The payoff structure (T, R, P, S) determines the marginal per-capita return. The analysis assumes standard PD inequalities (T>R>P>S>0), and the group benefit of cooperation is central to the efficiency results.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as immediate retaliation (TFT/NTFT), which imposes a cost on both the punisher and the punished. The cost is not parameterized separately but is implicit in the payoff structure.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the ability of TFT/NTFT to reduce the payoff of defectors. The analysis shows that this is sufficient to suppress defection under the right conditions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the two-person game, punishment is always targeted at the partner (identifiable). In the n-person generalization, the paper discusses both cases: with and without unique identification of defectors. Unique identification (as in TFT) increases the effectiveness of punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The analysis assumes idealized population structures (mutually exclusive groups/dyads or specified overlap) that may not map directly onto real-world or experimental PGG designs.
- Punishment is modeled as automatic retaliation (TFT/NTFT), not as a separate, costly, voluntary action as in many PGG experiments.
- No explicit parameterization of punishment cost or effectiveness beyond the structure of TFT/NTFT strategies.
- No consideration of communication, reward, or other mechanisms that may interact with punishment in real PGGs.
- The n-person generalization assumes either perfect or no identification of defectors, which may not capture the full range of possible information structures in PGGs.
