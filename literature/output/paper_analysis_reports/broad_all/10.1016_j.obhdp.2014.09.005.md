# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish non-cooperation: The moderating role of type of social dilemma
Authors: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- willingness to punish
- willingness to reward
- frequency of punishment
- frequency of reward
- size of punishment assigned
- size of reward assigned
- choice between reward and punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: whether and how much participants choose to punish or reward, and which type of sanction they select. No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The paper discusses efficiency and welfare in the introduction and discussion, but does not empirically analyze these outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that participants are more likely to reward cooperative behavior than to punish non-cooperative behavior, and that this tendency is stronger in public good dilemmas (where cooperation involves giving up private property) than in common resource dilemmas (where cooperation involves refraining from taking collective property). When given a choice, most participants prefer to reward rather than punish. The size of rewards is also larger than the size of punishments, especially in public good dilemmas. The study does not directly measure efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that the preference for reward over punishment may have implications for collective welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good and common resource games. It shows that, when given a choice, people are more willing to reward than to punish, and that punishment is used less frequently and less intensely, especially in public good dilemmas. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, it does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that the mere availability of punishment may not lead to high usage, especially if reward is also available, and that the type of social dilemma (public good vs. common resource) moderates willingness to punish. For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment may be less effective at increasing efficiency in public good dilemmas if participants are reluctant to use it, particularly when reward is also an option.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each social dilemma involved two players (A and B) whose behavior was observed by a third-party (C) who could sanction. The main focus is on the observer's willingness to sanction, not on group interaction among all three.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The social dilemma task was one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/harvest choices were continuous (0-100 coins), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.75 (contributions/remaining coins are multiplied by 1.5 and divided equally between two players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 coin to reduce target's payoff by 3 coins (cost: 1 per unit assigned).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment coin assigned reduces the target's payoff by 3 coins (effectiveness: 3 per unit cost).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward was available in some conditions (Experiment 1: between-subjects; Experiment 2: choice).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder pays 1 coin to increase target's payoff by 3 coins (cost: 1 per unit assigned).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward coin assigned increases the target's payoff by 3 coins (effectiveness: 3 per unit cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the task was a one-shot (single round) game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Observers (sanctioners) were shown the individual decisions (contributions/harvests) of the two players.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a third-party sanctioning paradigm, so the sanctioner is not personally affected by the social dilemma outcome.
- No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.
- The experiments are one-shot (single round), not repeated games.
- The findings may not generalize to situations where participants are directly involved in the social dilemma or where repeated interactions occur.
- The study does not examine the actual effect of punishment or reward on subsequent cooperation or group outcomes.
