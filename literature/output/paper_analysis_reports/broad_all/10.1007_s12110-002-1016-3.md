# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Altruistic cooperation during foraging by the ache, and the evolved human predisposition to cooperate
Authors: Hill, K.
Journal: Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective
Year: 2002

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- time spent in cooperative foraging activities
- frequency and types of cooperative acts
- distribution of cooperation by sex and kinship
- resource types associated with cooperation
Outcome notes:
The paper quantifies time spent in cooperative foraging and describes the types and distribution of cooperative acts, but does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or equivalent payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Ache men and women spend about 10% of their foraging time in cooperative acts that primarily benefit others, with some days exceeding 50%. Most cooperation is directed toward helping others acquire game, and the acts are often short-term altruistic. Cooperation is not limited to kin and is linked to widespread food sharing. The paper argues that such cooperation and sharing are part of an evolved human predisposition, and draws parallels to findings from experimental economics (including public goods games with punishment), but does not itself test or manipulate punishment, nor does it measure efficiency or group payoff in a controlled game-theoretic sense.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It is an observational ethnographic study of real-world foraging cooperation, not an experimental manipulation of game design dimensions. While it discusses the importance of cooperation, sharing, and the possible role of punishment in maintaining social norms, it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or the effects of punishment interventions. Its main value is in providing context for the prevalence and forms of cooperation in small-scale societies, and in supporting the plausibility of mechanisms (such as contingent sharing and informal sanctions) that are modeled in public goods game experiments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Foraging bands typically consisted of 15-60 individuals, with focal foraging groups in the sample averaging 6.3 adult women and 9.3 adult men. Not a controlled variable.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Communication is frequent and integral to cooperation (e.g., calling for help, providing information), but not manipulated as a variable.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Food sharing is described as a reward for cooperative foraging, but not as a formal or manipulated mechanism.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Foragers are aware of others' contributions and sharing; information is public in the social context.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and ethnographic, not experimental; it does not manipulate or control game design dimensions.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or equivalent outcomes.
- Punishment and sanctions are discussed only in reference to other literature and as possible mechanisms, not as observed or measured variables.
- No public goods game or formal game-theoretic structure is implemented; findings are not directly transferable to laboratory PGG settings.
- Sample sizes for focal follows are modest (50 man-days, 20 woman-days), and data may underestimate short cooperative acts.
