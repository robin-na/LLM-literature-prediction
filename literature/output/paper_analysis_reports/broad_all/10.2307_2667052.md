# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sanctioning systems, decision frames, and cooperation
Authors: Tenbrunsel, A. E., & Messick, D. M.
Journal: Administrative Science Quarterly
Year: 1999

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- expectations of others' cooperation
- decision frame (business vs ethical)
- trait ratings (honesty, trustworthiness, etc.)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rate (i.e., proportion of participants choosing the cooperative action), expectations of others' cooperation, and the perceived decision frame. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff tables are provided, but outcomes are not aggregated or analyzed as efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three studies, the paper finds that introducing a weak sanctioning system (low probability of detection, small fines) in social dilemma settings reduces cooperation rates compared to no sanctions. Strong sanctioning systems (high probability, large fines) increase cooperation rates above both weak-sanction and no-sanction conditions. The effect is mediated by the decision frame: sanctions make participants more likely to view the situation as a business decision, leading to cost-benefit reasoning. When the frame is ethical, cooperation is high regardless of sanctions. The studies use two-person and multi-person social dilemma scenarios with payoff tables, but do not report group efficiency or total payoff as outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by implication, efficiency) depends critically on the strength of the punishment system. Weak punishment can reduce cooperation below baseline, while strong punishment can increase it. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the psychological framing induced by punishment are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported, but the results imply that adding weak punishment may decrease efficiency, while strong punishment may increase it, conditional on the induced decision frame.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1 uses 2 players (dyadic prisoner's dilemma); Studies 2 and 3 use a multiparty scenario (number of players not precisely specified, but described as 'moderate-sized manufacturing plant' and 'industry').
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1 uses 4 repeated rounds; Studies 2 and 3 are one-shot scenarios.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: binary choice (request inspection or not); Studies 2 and 3: continuous choice (percentage of time to run scrubbers), but analyzed as dichotomous (cooperate = 80% or more, defect = less than 80%).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Payoff tables are provided for each scenario, allowing calculation of marginal returns, but MPCR is not explicitly stated. The structure is similar to a social dilemma with individual and collective payoffs.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is manipulated: Study 1 (fine = $30, low probability); Study 2 (fine = $50,000, <5% chance); Study 3 (weak: $50,000, <5%; strong: $2,000,000, ≥50%). The effect of punishment cost is central to the findings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via probability and magnitude of fine. Weak punishment (low probability, small fine) reduces cooperation; strong punishment (high probability, large fine) increases cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: participants are told there are 4 production runs. Studies 2 and 3: one-shot scenario, so the structure is clear.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: participants receive feedback about the other player's decision after round 2. Studies 2 and 3: participants estimate others' behavior but do not receive feedback.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only cooperation rates and psychological variables are analyzed.
- The experimental scenarios are not standard public goods games but are close variants (dyadic and multiparty social dilemmas with similar incentive structures).
- Punishment is always imposed by an external authority, not by peers.
- Reward mechanisms are not studied.
- The number of players in the multiparty scenarios is not precisely specified.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with peer punishment, communication, or different group sizes.
- The psychological framing mechanism may interact with other unmeasured cues in real-world or more complex laboratory settings.
