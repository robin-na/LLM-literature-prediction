## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper provides a formal model showing that in public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment technology increases both contributions and group welfare (efficiency), as long as the punishment is sufficiently effective and the share of compliers is not too low. The model gives explicit formulas for the increase in contributions and welfare as a function of the marginal per capita return, punishment cost, and the share of compliers. It also shows that the effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is less costly, the marginal per capita return is higher, and the share of compliers is higher. The model's predictions are consistent with experimental findings that punishment increases efficiency in public goods games under many conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games and close variants. It provides explicit theoretical predictions for how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, as a function of game design parameters (player count, marginal per capita return, punishment cost/tech, etc.) and the baseline (control) efficiency. The model can be used to generate quantitative or qualitative predictions for the downstream task, especially in settings where the control game is a standard public goods game and the treatment adds a punishment option. The paper also clarifies the conditions under which punishment will or will not increase efficiency, depending on the share of compliers and the effectiveness/cost of punishment.

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (phi) is explicitly modeled; lower punishment cost increases the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide new empirical data.
- The model assumes common knowledge of parameters and rationality, which may not hold in real experiments.
- The model is static (single-shot) and does not address repeated or dynamic public goods games.
- No explicit modeling of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback.
- The effect of punishment is contingent on the share of compliers and may not generalize to populations with very low compliance.
