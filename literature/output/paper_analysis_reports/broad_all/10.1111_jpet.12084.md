The paper is a theoretical model analyzing equilibrium levels of public good provision and enforcement fund contributions, with explicit focus on efficiency, welfare, and group payoff. No empirical or experimental data are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that voluntary contributions to a nongovernmental enforcement fund can sustain norm enforcement and increase public good provision (efficiency) in large societies, even when individuals are self-interested and there is no government. The positive effect of punishment/reward-based enforcement on efficiency is robust to increases in population size, provided the enforcement technology is nonrival. With rival enforcement, the positive effect diminishes as the population grows, but still remains above the no-enforcement baseline. The model also shows that the equilibrium level of public good provision is always below the first-best (Pareto-optimal) level, but is strictly higher than in the absence of enforcement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment/reward mechanisms (via a funded enforcement agency) increases efficiency (public good provision) relative to the baseline voluntary contribution game without enforcement, especially when enforcement is nonrival. The model offers explicit comparative statics for how efficiency gains from punishment depend on player count, enforcement technology, and other design dimensions. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that the presence and effectiveness of punishment/reward mechanisms are key moderators of efficiency, and that the positive effect of punishment is robust to increases in group size if enforcement is nonrival, but attenuates with rival enforcement. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with norm enforcement.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Player count (n) is a key parameter in the model. The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to be robust to increases in n when enforcement is nonrival, but diminishes with rival enforcement.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (not all-or-nothing); agents choose how much to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is embedded in the public good production function and is affected by the enforcement mechanism (via the effective price of the public good). Higher enforcement increases the effective return to contributing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/reward strength depends on resources contributed to the enforcement fund; higher cost of enforcement (rivalry) reduces the positive effect on efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/reward effectiveness (parameter beta, or enforcement technology) is a key moderator; higher effectiveness increases efficiency gains from enforcement.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The enforcement mechanism includes both public approval (reward) and disapproval (punishment) for deviations from the norm.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward and punishment are both funded from the enforcement fund; higher cost of enforcement reduces the net positive effect.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of reward (and punishment) is determined by the enforcement technology; higher effectiveness increases efficiency.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The enforcement mechanism is based on public approval/disapproval of deviations from the average contribution, implying that information about others' contributions is available or summarized.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental validation.
- The model assumes identical agents (income, preferences) in most analyses, limiting applicability to heterogeneous populations.
- The enforcement mechanism is abstracted as a function of resources contributed, and real-world complexities of monitoring, targeting, and implementation are not modeled.
- The model is static (one-shot) and does not address dynamic or repeated interactions.
- Distributional equity and fairness issues are acknowledged but not analyzed in detail.
