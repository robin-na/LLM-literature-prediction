## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a formal model in which agents internalize social norms (specifically, an 'E-norm' combining efficiency and equality) and experience emotional costs (shame, anger) when violating or observing violations of these norms. The model predicts that people will cooperate and punish norm violators even at a cost to themselves, and that punishment is targeted at those responsible for norm violations. The model is shown to qualitatively account for a range of experimental findings in games such as dictator, ultimatum, trust, centipede, Cournot, and public goods games. In the context of public goods games, the model predicts that the introduction of punishment opportunities increases cooperation by deterring selfish behavior, as selfish agents comply to avoid being punished by principled agents. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions or empirical estimates of efficiency or group payoff with and without punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a theoretical rationale for why punishment increases cooperation and norm compliance in public goods and related games, suggesting that the presence of punishment opportunities should increase efficiency relative to control conditions without punishment, especially when a sufficient proportion of players are 'principled' and willing to punish norm violations. However, the model is qualitative and does not specify the magnitude of efficiency gains or how they depend on specific game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR). The model does not provide direct evidence for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game parameters, but supports the general expectation that punishment opportunities will have a more positive effect on efficiency in environments where norm internalization and willingness to punish are present.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not present new empirical data or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff.
- The model is qualitative and does not specify how the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, or MPCR.
- The model is applied to a wide range of games, but public goods games are discussed only briefly and not analyzed in detail.
- No direct predictions or data are provided for the efficiency of control versus punishment-enabled conditions in public goods games.
- The model assumes a specific form of norm internalization and emotional response, which may not capture all relevant heterogeneity in real populations.
