# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: What do the followers want? The core functions of leadership
Authors: Ahmad, M. G., & Loch, C.
Journal: Leadership Quarterly
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group benefit (average value achieved by a pair per round, normalized to [0,1])
- total payoff (sum of medal values, mapped to monetary equivalents in some conditions)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- frequency of governance structure choices (individual, punishment, recommending leadership, enforcing leadership)
- retaliation cycles
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is group benefit (efficiency), measured as the average value achieved by a pair per round, normalized to the maximum possible (1.0). Non-payoff outcomes such as cooperation rate and governance structure choice are also reported, but efficiency is the primary focus for the relevant analyses.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in repeated two-player strategic games, allowing players to choose a punishment option (where one can assign a low-value payoff to the other after decisions are made) does not increase group efficiency. Instead, punishment is associated with lower group payoffs due to retaliation cycles and reduced cooperation. In all games, the average group benefit under punishment is lower than under individual decision-making or leadership. Leadership (especially recommending leadership) increases efficiency, while punishment governance leads to the lowest efficiency. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across games with and without monetary incentives.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player repeated games with public-goods-like social dilemmas, enabling punishment as a governance option (without central leadership) reduces group efficiency compared to control conditions without punishment. The effect is robust across different game structures and incentive types. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment option (with moderate cost and impact, as in this design) should be expected to decrease efficiency relative to control. The findings are directly relevant for settings where punishment is decentralized and not embedded in a leadership structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair played 10 repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/decision choices are discrete (e.g., A1/A2, B1/B2), not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as the punisher assigning a low-value payoff (e.g., no medal) to the other player; the cost to the punisher is not explicitly monetary but is opportunity cost (cannot achieve higher group value). In monetary-incentive games, the cost is the loss of potential group payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective in reducing the other player's payoff to the lowest possible (no medal), but this triggers retaliation and reduces group efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told in advance that there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, both players see the choices, governance structures, and outcomes for both themselves and the other player.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know which governance structure (including punishment) was chosen by each player in each round.

## Important Limitations
- All games are two-player (dyadic) rather than multi-player public goods games, which may limit generalizability to larger group settings.
- Punishment is implemented as a direct assignment of a low-value payoff, not as a deduction from the other player's endowment, and the cost to the punisher is not always explicit.
- No communication is allowed between players, which may affect the dynamics of cooperation and punishment compared to settings with chat or discussion.
- The leader is simulated by a computer and always acts in the group's interest, which may not reflect real-world leadership with self-interested leaders.
- The study uses both non-monetary (medal) and monetary incentives, but the stakes are relatively low, which may affect engagement and external validity.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the findings do not address the effects of positive incentives.
