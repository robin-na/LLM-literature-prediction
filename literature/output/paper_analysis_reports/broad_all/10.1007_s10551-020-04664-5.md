# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Consequentialist Motives for Punishment Signal Trustworthiness
Authors: Dhaliwal, N. A., Skarlicki, D. P., Hoegg, J., & Daniels, M. A.
Journal: Journal of Business Ethics
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- perceived trustworthiness of punisher
- amount of money entrusted to punisher in Trust Game
- partner choice in Trust Game
- perceived psychopathy of punisher
- desire to have punisher as a teammate
- perceived benevolence, integrity, competence
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are perceptions of trustworthiness, psychopathy, and related character traits, as well as behavioral proxies for trust (e.g., money entrusted in a Trust Game, partner choice). No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. The Trust Game is used as a measure of trust, not as a public goods game or to measure group efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that individuals who express or are perceived to have consequentialist motives for punishing norm violators are rated as more trustworthy, are entrusted with more money in Trust Game scenarios, and are chosen more often as cooperation partners compared to those with deontological motives. This effect is mediated by lower perceived psychopathy. The findings are robust across different phrasings of motives, experimental vignettes, and real-world recall. However, the studies do not examine actual group cooperation rates, efficiency, or payoff-based outcomes in public goods or similar games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff in public goods games or similar environments. Its findings are relevant for understanding how the rationale for punishment affects the punisher's reputation and trustworthiness, which may indirectly influence cooperation, but it does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Therefore, it should not be used as primary evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions or control efficiency. It may be useful for understanding psychological mechanisms or for modeling how punishment motives affect trust and partner selection.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Trust Game scenarios involve two players (Proposer and Decider). No public goods game or multi-player group interaction is used.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Trust Game is a one-shot scenario; no repeated rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The identity and motive of the punisher are always known to the observer/participant, as the studies focus on perceptions of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The studies do not use public goods games or measure group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes.
- All outcomes are based on perceptions (trustworthiness, psychopathy) or behavioral proxies for trust (money entrusted, partner choice), not actual group cooperation or efficiency.
- Punishment is not operationalized with explicit cost or impact parameters as in economic games; it is described in vignettes or as managerial acts.
- The findings are based on online samples (MTurk, Prolific) and may not generalize to real-world organizational or group settings.
- The results are limited to the reputational effects of punishment motives and do not address whether punishment actually increases cooperation or efficiency.
