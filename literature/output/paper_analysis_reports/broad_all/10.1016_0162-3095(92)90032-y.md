## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in n-person public goods games, the introduction of targeted punishment (retribution) allows for the stable evolution of cooperation in larger groups than is possible with reciprocity alone. When the benefits to a punisher from induced cooperation exceed the costs of punishing, cooperation and punishment can coexist at equilibrium, leading to higher average payoffs (i.e., greater efficiency) than in the absence of punishment. The model also shows that 'moralistic' strategies—where individuals punish both noncooperators and those who fail to punish—can stabilize cooperation (and any other costly behavior) even when the group benefit is not sufficient to compensate individual punishers. Increasing group size makes cooperation harder to sustain, but the negative effect is less severe with punishment than with reciprocity. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide empirical data, but it directly models payoff flows and evolutionary stability.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially in larger groups and with repeated interactions. The model specifies how the costs and effectiveness of punishment, group size, and the expected number of rounds interact to determine whether cooperation (and thus higher efficiency) can be sustained. It also warns that if punishment is too costly or group size is too large, cooperation may not evolve. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the structure of repeated interactions. However, the results are theoretical and assume evolutionary dynamics rather than short-run behavioral responses.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency is not reported as a normalized ratio but as average payoff/fitness; mapping to experimental efficiency may require interpretation.
- The model assumes evolutionary dynamics and infinite populations, which may not capture short-run or finite-group behavior.
- Punishment is assumed to be perfectly targeted and always effective; real-world limitations on monitoring, implementation, or errors in punishment are not modeled in detail.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or more complex social structures beyond group assortment.
- The model does not address the dynamics of moralistic strategies in full detail, especially their ability to invade when rare.
