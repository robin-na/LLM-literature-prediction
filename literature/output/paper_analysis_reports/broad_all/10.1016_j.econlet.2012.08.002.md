# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Peer punishment with third-party approval in a social dilemma game
Authors: Tan, F. F., & Xiao, E. T.
Journal: Economics Letters
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- punishment frequency and amount (on cooperators and defectors)
- frequency of antisocial punishment
- conditional cooperation after punishment
Outcome notes:
The main payoff-related outcome is average earnings per person. The primary behavioral outcome is cooperation rate. Punishment behavior is analyzed in detail. Efficiency per se is not directly reported, but average earnings are compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares two treatments in a repeated prisoner's dilemma: (1) baseline, where players can directly punish each other, and (2) TPA (third-party approval), where a third party must approve any punishment before it is implemented. The TPA treatment results in significantly lower cooperation rates (0.47 vs. 0.70) and marginally significantly lower average earnings (23.52 vs. 25.30 tokens per period) compared to the baseline. Third-party approval reduces antisocial punishment but also reduces punishment of defectors, which appears to undermine the positive effect of punishment on cooperation. The study suggests that, in settings where antisocial punishment is rare, requiring third-party approval for punishment can reduce both cooperation and group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with peer punishment, introducing a third-party approval step for punishment reduces both cooperation and average earnings compared to direct peer punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that adding procedural hurdles or external approval to punishment mechanisms may decrease the efficiency gains typically associated with peer punishment, especially when antisocial punishment is infrequent. The findings are most directly relevant to adjacent settings (prisoner's dilemma rather than public goods), but the mechanism—peer punishment with and without third-party approval—maps closely to PGG-like environments. The control (baseline) efficiency is higher than in the treatment with third-party approval, indicating a less positive effect of punishment on efficiency when third-party approval is required.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: A, B (active players), and C (third party/observer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for 20 periods (rounds).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (Option I) or defect (Option II).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 token to deduct 3 tokens from the other player (cost:impact ratio 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 tokens deducted per 1 token spent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is finitely repeated for 20 periods; this is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, all decisions are revealed to each player, including punishment proposals and outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. A and B can see each other's punishment proposals, even if not approved by C.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a prisoner's dilemma rather than a standard public goods game, so findings may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- The third party's role is limited to approving or vetoing punishment proposals and does not set punishment levels or have a stake in the outcome.
- Antisocial punishment was rare in this experiment, so the results may differ in environments where antisocial punishment is more common.
- Efficiency is measured via average earnings, not as a normalized efficiency ratio relative to full cooperation.
- The third party's earnings are independent and not revealed to the main players until the end, which may differ from real-world institutional settings.
