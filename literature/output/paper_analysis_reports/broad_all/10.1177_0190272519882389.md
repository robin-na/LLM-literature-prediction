# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Aggression, Conflict, and the Formation of Intimidating Group Reputation
Authors: Szekely, A., Andrighetto, G., Payette, N., & Tummolini, L.
Journal: Social Psychology Quarterly
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings of A and B
- group earnings
- efficiency (as group welfare/total earnings)
- conflict (costs inflicted)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned
- aggression (amount spent on punishment)
- frequency of peer punishment
- sending/keeping decision by A
- beliefs about punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are earnings (payoff), punishment assigned (aggression), and conflict (costs inflicted). The paper explicitly analyzes group and individual earnings as a measure of efficiency/welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when group members can use peer punishment to enforce an intimidating group reputation, they increase their punishment of outsiders (A), but this leads to higher conflict and lower earnings for both group members (B) and outsiders (A) in the short run. Without peer punishment, group reputation is exploited and aggression is low, with higher earnings. Individual reputation (with individual history) leads to more aggression than group reputation without peer punishment, but still results in higher earnings than the group reputation with peer punishment condition. Agent-based simulations suggest that in the long run, peer punishment can eventually increase group earnings as reputation deters conflict, but this is not realized in the 11-round experiment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling peer punishment to enforce group reputation in a conflict/aggression game increases punishment and conflict, but reduces efficiency and group earnings in the short run. For prediction tasks, this suggests that adding a peer punishment mechanism (with moderate cost and effectiveness) to a group setting where reputation is at stake will likely decrease efficiency compared to a control without punishment, at least over a moderate number of rounds. The effect may reverse in much longer games, but this is only supported by simulation, not experimental data. The design is not a standard public goods game, but is a social dilemma with conflict and punishment, so results are most transferable to adjacent settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 20 (or 18) subjects, but the game is played in dyads (A and B roles). The group reputation and peer punishment mechanisms operate at the level of all Bs in a session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment lasted 11 periods/rounds. Simulations ran for 100 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. A's contribution decision is binary: send €8 or keep €0.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:4 ratio: for each €1 spent, the target loses €4 (both for B punishing A and for peer punishment among Bs).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:4 (impact per unit cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the session lasts for 11 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Bs observe the punishment strategies of other Bs in all treatments. As observe either individual or group punishment history depending on treatment.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game; it is a conflict/aggression game with punishment, so results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- The experiment only runs for 11 rounds, so long-term effects of punishment on efficiency are not observed directly; long-run efficiency improvements are only supported by simulation.
- Peer punishment is only allowed in one treatment and is restricted to punishing those who punish less than the punisher, which may not generalize to all peer punishment settings.
- No communication or reward mechanisms are present, so findings may not apply to games with these features.
- The group size for peer punishment is relatively large (all Bs in a session), which may affect dynamics compared to smaller groups.
