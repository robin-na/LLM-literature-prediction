## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In dynamic, anonymous P2P environments modeled as public goods games, traditional punishment- and reward-based service differentiation mechanisms fail to sustain cooperation or high efficiency due to the presence of 'cooperator' mutants who contribute but do not punish or reward. Introducing a small entry fee and allowing voluntary participation enables the punishment-based scheme to achieve high average payoff and system efficiency, with punishers dominating the population most of the time. Reward-based schemes, even with entry fees, are less effective and more unstable, with defectors occupying the system more frequently. The effect is robust to group size, total population, and punishment cost. The average payoff in the punishment-based scheme approaches the ideal (but unfeasible) case of a reward-based scheme where rewarders can selectively reward only other rewarders and incur zero cost, but such selective rewarding is not feasible in anonymous, dynamic settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods game environments with anonymous, dynamic, and voluntary participation, punishment-based mechanisms with a small entry fee are much more effective at sustaining high efficiency (average payoff) than reward-based mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the control (no-punishment) condition is dominated by defectors. The results suggest that, if the game allows for voluntary entry and a small entry fee, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of group size, population size, or moderate changes in punishment cost. However, if there is no entry fee, neither punishment nor reward mechanisms are effective. The paper's results are most applicable to settings with anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, and may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total number of peers (M) is a parameter in the model and simulations (e.g., M=500). Results are shown to be robust to changes in player count.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly modeled as repeated over many rounds (evolutionary rounds), with group reshuffling each round. Simulations run for up to 10^5 rounds.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by the benefit parameter (alpha_1 or alpha_2) divided by group size (N). Explicitly modeled in payoff equations.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (c_u) is a key parameter; results show that even with high punishment cost, punishment-based schemes sustain high efficiency if entry fee is present.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost (c_p) is a parameter; varied in simulations. The ratio of penalty to cost is discussed.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward-based mechanisms are explicitly modeled and compared to punishment-based mechanisms.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost (c_r) is a parameter; simulations include both positive and zero reward cost cases.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact per unit cost (b_r) is a parameter; varied in simulations.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players can observe the behaviors of others in their group in each round (to determine who to punish/reward), but there is no long-term memory or summary.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data from human subjects.
- The model assumes anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, which may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.
- All costs and benefits are parameterized and may not reflect real-world values; practical implementation details are abstracted.
- The effectiveness of punishment-based schemes depends on the presence of a small entry fee and voluntary participation; without these, cooperation collapses.
- Reward-based schemes are only effective in the idealized case where rewarders can selectively reward other rewarders and incur zero cost, which is not feasible in anonymous, dynamic settings.
