Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variable is social efficiency (group payoff), defined as the total group payoff under Nash equilibrium for a given network structure. The model also analyzes the conditions under which universal contribution (full cooperation) or universal free-riding is the Nash equilibrium, and how network structure (hierarchical vs. distributed) emerges as a function of monitoring and punishment costs. Non-payoff outcomes include measures of network hierarchy and efficiency, but these are used to interpret the structure of cooperation, not as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the efficiency of punishment and monitoring technologies fundamentally shapes the optimal structure of cooperation in public goods games. When punishment is efficient (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the impact on free-riders), hierarchical (tree-like) organizations are optimal and can support universal cooperation (full efficiency) even in large groups. When punishment is inefficient (costly for punishers), distributed (collective) monitoring and punishment are optimal, but these structures are limited in scale by monitoring costs. The model provides explicit analytic conditions for when universal contribution (full cooperation) is a Nash equilibrium and socially efficient, and when universal free-riding is the only equilibrium. The model also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment networks (hierarchical vs. distributed) is an endogenous outcome of the costs and effectiveness of these institutions. Thus, the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency depends critically on the cost-effectiveness of punishment and monitoring, as well as group size.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that enabling punishment can increase group efficiency (by supporting universal cooperation) if punishment is sufficiently efficient (low cost, high impact), and if monitoring is not prohibitively costly. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds at which universal cooperation is efficient and stable, depending on group size, public goods multiplier, monitoring efficiency, and punishment efficiency. It also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment (hierarchical vs. distributed) is determined by these parameters, which in turn affects the scalability of cooperation. For prediction, the model implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is cheap and effective, monitoring is efficient, and group size is not too large for the given monitoring technology. If punishment is costly or monitoring is inefficient, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency, especially in larger groups.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The model is static and does not consider repeated interactions, learning, or dynamic adaptation.
- Only punishment (not reward) is modeled as an enforcement mechanism.
- The model assumes perfect implementation of monitoring and punishment as assigned by the network structure.
- The model does not consider heterogeneity in individual preferences, abilities, or endowments.
- The model does not address the robustness of efficient networks to shocks or second-order free-riding beyond basic discussion.
- No explicit modeling of information flows, communication, or identification of punishers beyond network assignment.
