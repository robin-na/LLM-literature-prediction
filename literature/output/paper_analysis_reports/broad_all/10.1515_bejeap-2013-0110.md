# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sticks and Carrots in Procurement: An Experimental Exploration
Authors: Bigoni, M., Spagnolo, G., & Valbonesi, P.
Journal: B E Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- surplus (sum of buyer and seller profits)
- buyer profit
- seller profit
- maximum profit offered to sellers
Non-payoff outcomes:
- effort in contractible (verifiable) and non-contractible (non-verifiable) tasks
- acceptance rates of offers
- frequency and duration of repeated relationships
- voluntary effort (beyond contract requirements)
- use of private vs public offers
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is surplus (group payoff), with detailed analysis of buyer and seller profits. Behavioral outcomes (effort, voluntary effort, offer acceptance) are also reported, but efficiency/surplus is the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that explicit incentives—whether framed as penalties (punishment) or bonuses (reward)—strongly increase total surplus (efficiency) in a repeated buyer-seller game with multitasking and endogenous matching. In the absence of explicit incentives, surplus and effort are low. When explicit incentives are available and used, effort in the contractible dimension rises sharply, while effort in the non-contractible dimension falls only slightly, resulting in a net efficiency gain. There is no significant difference in surplus between bonus and penalty framing, though buyers prefer penalties because they can extract a larger share of the surplus, sometimes through exploitative contracts. The effect of punishment (penalties) on efficiency is robust and positive.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (penalty contracts) in a repeated, competitive, multitask environment with endogenous matching and incomplete contracts leads to a substantial increase in efficiency (surplus) compared to a control without explicit incentives. The effect is similar for both punishment and reward, but buyers prefer punishment due to distributional advantages. The design is not a standard PGG but is adjacent: it is a repeated, group-based, real-effort game with endogenous matching and both contractible and non-contractible tasks. The findings support the prediction that, in similar environments, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control. The effect is robust to the presence of repeated interaction, endogenous matching, and multitasking.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 7-9 sellers and 5-7 buyers (always 2 more sellers than buyers). The game is played in groups, but not a fixed group as in standard PGG; matching is endogenous and competitive.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game lasts at least 15 rounds, then continues with 67% probability each round up to a maximum of 30 rounds. Average session length is 18 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort choices (both contractible and non-contractible) are continuous from 0 to 4, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return for effort is determined by the value function: v(e) = 8 + 16*(e1 + e2). Each unit of effort increases value by 16 points, but cost is quadratic: c(e1 + e2)^2.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Penalty (punishment) is 20 points; cost to buyer is paid up front as part of the contract, but the penalty is returned if the seller fails to meet the contractible target. The cost structure is not a classic PGG punishment, but is functionally equivalent in this context.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Penalty is 20 points per contract; if the seller fails to meet the contractible target, 20 points are deducted from the seller's payoff and returned to the buyer. The impact per unit cost is 1:1 for the 20-point penalty.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Bonus (reward) contracts are available in some treatments; buyers can choose to offer a 20-point bonus for meeting the contractible target.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Bonus is 20 points; cost to buyer is paid only if the seller meets the contractible target. The cost per unit reward is 1:1 for the 20-point bonus.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact per unit cost is 1:1 for the 20-point bonus.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts at least 15 rounds, then continues with 67% probability each round up to a maximum of 30 rounds. The end is uncertain after round 15.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants see their own and their counterpart's profit, contract terms, and actual effort. A history table is available with all previous period data.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Buyers and sellers are identified by persistent IDs; when a contract is signed, both parties know each other's ID. When a penalty or bonus is applied, the identity of the counterpart is known.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated buyer-seller procurement game with endogenous matching and multitasking, so results may not fully generalize to classic PGG settings.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as contract terms (penalties/bonuses) rather than as peer-to-peer post-contribution punishment as in PGGs.
- The subject pool consists of economics and business students, which may limit generalizability to broader populations.
- The cost and value functions are specific (quadratic cost, linear value), which may affect the magnitude of efficiency gains.
- Exploitative contracts are possible in the penalty condition, which may not have a direct analog in standard PGGs.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may limit relational or social effects.
