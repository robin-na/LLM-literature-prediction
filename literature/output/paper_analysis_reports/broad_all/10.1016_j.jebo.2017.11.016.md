# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Finding cooperators: Sorting through repeated interaction
Authors: Bernard, M., Fanning, J., & Yuksel, S.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total surplus (3*r per round)
- proposer and responder payoffs
- share of surplus
Non-payoff outcomes:
- return (cooperation) rate
- requested return
- transfer size
- relationship survival
- replacement frequency
- promotion (increase in transfer/requested return)
- starting small behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the responder's return (cooperation), which is used as a proxy for efficiency, but the paper also reports surplus and surplus sharing. The focus is on cooperation and sorting, not directly on group efficiency as in standard PGGs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that in the Baseline (replacement allowed), cooperation (measured by responder return) increases over time, surpassing the Fixed Partner treatment (no replacement) in later rounds, despite similar initial levels. The Contract Restrictions treatment (no replacement, no punishment) starts with lower cooperation but converges to the Fixed Partner level in later rounds. The ability to punish by reducing transfers does not significantly increase cooperation compared to the inability to punish. Instead, the ability to sort (replace uncooperative partners) is the key driver of higher long-run cooperation. Surplus sharing becomes more equal in long-term relationships, but overall efficiency is not maximized in any treatment. The findings suggest that sorting, not punishment, is the main mechanism for sustaining cooperation in this environment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in repeated gift-exchange games (adjacent to PGGs), the ability to punish (reduce transfers) does not significantly increase efficiency or cooperation compared to when punishment is not possible. Instead, the ability to sort (replace partners) is more important for sustaining cooperation and improving surplus. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment alone (without sorting/replacement) may not increase efficiency, and that the structure of partner matching is a critical moderator. The findings are most relevant for environments where partner selection or replacement is possible, and less so for standard PGGs with fixed groups and explicit costly punishment. The paper's results should be used to caution against overestimating the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games without sorting opportunities.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 20 subjects (7 proposers, 13 responders); matching structure is not a standard fixed group as in PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Indefinitely repeated game with continuation probability 0.8 (expected 5 rounds per supergame); number of supergames per session varied (min 7, max 11).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (return) is continuous: responders choose return from 0 to 10; proposers choose transfer from 0 to 40.
- default contribution framing: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Default decisions applied if no decision was taken, but not a substantive default framing for contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Return gifts are quadrupled (multiplier 4); responder's return r is multiplied by 4 for proposer. Not a standard PGG, but analogous to MPCR=4/number of players (here, dyadic).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as the ability to reduce transfers (to zero) or terminate relationships; in Contract Restrictions, punishment is disabled (cannot reduce transfer). The paper finds that enabling this punishment has little effect on long-run cooperation/efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is indefinitely repeated with random termination; players do not know the exact number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe their own and their partner's payoffs if matched; no information about other pairs' outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposer and responder IDs are visible within a supergame; history box details previous offers and outcomes with IDs.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a gift-exchange game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism; punishment is via transfer reduction or relationship termination.
- Partner matching is not fixed group as in PGGs; sorting/replacement is a key mechanism, which is not present in standard PGGs.
- Efficiency is proxied by cooperation (return) and surplus, but group efficiency as in PGGs is not directly measured.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, sorting) rather than direct group payoff or efficiency.
- Findings may not apply to one-shot or finitely repeated games, or to environments without sorting opportunities.
