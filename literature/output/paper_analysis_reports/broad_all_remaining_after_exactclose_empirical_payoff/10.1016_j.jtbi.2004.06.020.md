## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that a simple contingent movement rule ('Walk Away') enables cooperation to evolve and persist in spatial prisoner's dilemma environments, outperforming more cognitively complex strategies like TFT and PAVLOV under many conditions. Walk Away is robust to invasion, can invade populations of defectors at lower initial frequencies than other strategies, and is most successful when movement costs, error rates, and agent density are low. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and outcomes are measured by the evolutionary success of strategies (frequency in the population), not by direct group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that contingent movement (exit) can substitute for more complex memory-based or punishment-based strategies in promoting cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It does not study public goods games or explicit punishment, but shows that contingent movement (exit after defection) can promote cooperation and evolutionary success in spatial prisoner's dilemma environments. The findings suggest that mechanisms allowing agents to avoid defectors (analogous to exit or partner choice) can be highly effective, even without explicit punishment or memory. However, because the model does not report group efficiency or total payoff, and does not manipulate punishment cost or technology, its direct applicability to predicting efficiency in public goods games with punishment is limited. The results are most relevant for understanding the role of partner choice, mobility, and spatial structure as alternatives or complements to punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a spatial evolutionary prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled; contingent movement (exit) is not equivalent to costly punishment.
- Outcomes are reported as evolutionary success (frequency of strategies), not as group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- No direct manipulation or analysis of punishment cost, punishment technology, or reward mechanisms.
- Findings are based on agent-based simulations with specific parameterizations; real-world generalizability is uncertain.
- No direct measurement of group-level efficiency or surplus; only indirect proxies via strategy success.
