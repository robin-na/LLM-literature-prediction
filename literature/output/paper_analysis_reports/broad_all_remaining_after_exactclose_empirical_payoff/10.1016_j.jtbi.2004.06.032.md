Efficiency per se is not directly calculated, but group-level cooperation and average payoffs are closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that indirect reciprocity models with assessment rules that distinguish justified from unjustified defections (STANDING) are more effective at sustaining cooperation and suppressing defectors than simple scoring rules, especially in the presence of errors. However, even simple scoring can support cooperation if the benefit-to-cost ratio is high and players have enough interactions. The model demonstrates that the structure of the population, the number of observed interactions, and the error rates in action and perception all influence the stability of cooperation. Analytical results show that, under certain assumptions about the distribution of the number of rounds, stable mixtures of discriminating and non-discriminating altruists can exist, leading to high average payoffs. The findings suggest that costly punishment (refusing to help) can sustain cooperation, but is less robust than non-costly forms (STANDING).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation (and thus efficiency) depends on whether punishment is costly, the benefit-to-cost ratio, error rates, and the number of interactions. While the model is not a standard PGG, the parallels are strong: refusing to help is analogous to punishing defectors, and the group payoff is analogous to efficiency. The results imply that costly punishment can increase efficiency, but is less robust than non-costly punishment, and that the details of the assessment and action rules, as well as the game structure (e.g., number of rounds, observation probability), are important moderators. For prediction, the paper supports the view that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, especially when the benefit-to-cost ratio is high and errors are not too frequent, but the effect is sensitive to the costliness and implementation of punishment.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Simulations use 100 tribes of 100 players each (N=100, M=100). The effect of player count per se is not systematically analyzed, but the population structure is described in detail.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds per player is a key parameter (n/N, e.g., n=1000, N=100, so 10 rounds per player per generation). Analytical results show that higher average number of rounds increases the stability of cooperation and average payoff.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/helping is binary: donor either helps (G) or does not help (N).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the SCORING module, refusing to help (punishing) is costly because it reduces the punisher's own score, making future help less likely. STANDING removes this cost for justified defections. Costly punishment is less robust than non-costly punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is implicit in the model: refusing to help reduces the recipient's reputation and future help. The impact per unit cost is not directly parameterized.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The number of rounds per player is fixed in the main simulations (binomial/Poisson distribution), and this is shown to affect the stability of cooperation. Players know the structure of the game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players observe some interactions between others and update their private images. The probability of observing interactions (p_o) is varied, affecting cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a model of indirect reciprocity with binary helping decisions.
- Punishment is modeled as refusing to help, not as explicit costly punishment as in PGG experiments.
- Efficiency is not directly calculated; group-level cooperation and average payoffs are used as proxies.
- The model uses only two reputation states (good/bad), which may limit generalizability.
- No explicit communication, reward, or identification of punishers is modeled.
- Findings are based on simulations and analytical models, not empirical experiments.
