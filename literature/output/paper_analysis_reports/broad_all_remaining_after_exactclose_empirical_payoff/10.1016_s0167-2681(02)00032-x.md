## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment and reciprocity can support efficient outcomes in both repeated and sporadic interactions, but only under certain conditions. In repeated games, strategies that support cooperation (and thus efficiency) can be stable if complexity costs or implementation errors are considered, but many inefficient strategies can also be stable. In one-shot or sporadic interactions, punishment can induce cooperation and improve efficiency, but this stability is fragile: populations of punishers can be invaded by non-punishing cooperators or defectors, and the cost of punishment can erode its effectiveness. In public goods and common pool resource games, the presence of reciprocators willing to punish can lead to higher group payoffs, but only if monitoring and punishment costs are not too high and if group composition allows reciprocators to coordinate. The evolution of mixed populations (reciprocators and defectors) is common, and the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the game structure and population dynamics.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games can increase efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines, but only under specific conditions: punishment must be sufficiently effective (high impact per cost), not too costly, and the population must contain enough reciprocators or the ability to assort. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive; in some models, punishment does not prevent the evolution of inefficient outcomes, and the stability of efficient cooperation is sensitive to the cost and observability of punishment, as well as to the possibility of second-order free-riding. For prediction, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the ability to observe or assort by type. Control efficiency (without punishment) is often low, but the introduction of punishment can raise efficiency if these moderating conditions are met.

Models include both two-player (prisoner's dilemma) and multi-player (public goods, common pool resource) games. Both infinitely repeated and one-shot (single round) games are analyzed. Repetition is a key moderator: repeated games allow for more stable cooperation via reciprocity and punishment.

Most models use binary choices (cooperate/defect), i.e., all-or-nothing contribution. Some public goods models may allow for more general actions, but the main focus is on binary action spaces.

The cost-benefit ratio (alpha/beta) is a key parameter; higher benefit-to-cost ratios make cooperation and efficiency more likely. Equivalent to higher MPCR in public goods games.

Lower punishment cost makes punishment more effective at supporting cooperation and efficiency; high cost undermines stability of punishment strategies.

Both infinite and finite horizon games are discussed. Infinite repetition supports more stable cooperation; known finite horizon undermines cooperation.

Some models assume that players observe the full history of play or group composition, which enables conditioning on others' behavior and supports cooperation.

Observability of punishment or commitment to punish is discussed as a key moderator: when punishment is observable, it is more effective at supporting cooperation and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical survey and does not present new empirical or experimental data.
- Many models assume binary (all-or-nothing) choices and do not address continuous contribution settings.
- The stability of punishment-supported cooperation is often fragile and depends on strong assumptions (e.g., observability, low punishment cost, absence of second-order free-riding).
- Reward mechanisms are not analyzed, so the findings do not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The models often assume perfect rationality or extreme bounded rationality, with limited attention to intermediate cognitive constraints.
- Population structure is typically random matching; networked or clustered interactions are only briefly discussed.
- Theoretical results may not fully capture the complexity of real-world or experimental public goods games.
