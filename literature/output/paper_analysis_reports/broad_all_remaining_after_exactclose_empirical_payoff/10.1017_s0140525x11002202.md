## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that human cooperation and punishment behaviors in economic games, including public goods games, are guided by evolved fairness norms shaped by partner choice. It reviews evidence that people punish both low and high contributors in PGGs, often to restore fairness rather than to maximize group efficiency. The mutualistic approach predicts that individuals seek proportionality between contributions and rewards, and that punishment is used to rebalance unfair distributions. The paper notes that in real-world small-scale societies, exclusion and partner switching are more common than costly punishment. It also highlights that punishment in PGGs is sometimes antisocial (targeting high contributors) and is strongly shaped by local fairness norms. However, the paper does not report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff outcomes as a function of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and interpretive context for understanding why and how punishment may affect cooperation and fairness in PGG-like environments, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment. It suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on whether punishment is perceived as fair and proportional, and that exclusion or partner choice may be more effective than costly punishment in sustaining cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper is useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators (e.g., fairness norms, entitlement, partner choice), but not for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions.
