# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Trust and reciprocity in incentive contracting
Authors: Rigdon, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (percentage of potential gains from exchange extracted by P1 and P2)
- average group earnings
- average transfers and returns
- actual and desired payback (share of gains returned)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contract violation rate
- demand for reward/punishment
- gender differences in investment and return behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (as a percentage of potential gains from exchange), average transfers and returns, and the share of gains returned. Non-payoff outcomes include contract violation rates and behavioral demand for reward/punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that adding ex post punishment or reward options to a principal-agent game does not reduce efficiency or cooperation. Low-level incentives (1:1 cost-impact) have no significant effect compared to the control (implicit contract). High-level incentives (1:3 cost-impact), especially punishment, significantly increase both investment and returns, leading to higher efficiency (89.5% vs. 80% in control). High reward increases returns but not investment. Principals are more likely to use rewards than punishments, but the threat of high punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency. There is no evidence for 'motivation crowding out' or the 'Paradox of Organizational Trust' in this setting.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a bilateral trust/investment game with gains from exchange, enabling high-impact punishment (where the punishment impact is much greater than the cost to the punisher) increases efficiency relative to a control without punishment. Low-impact punishment or reward does not improve efficiency. The results suggest that, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar games, the effectiveness of punishment (punishment_tech) is crucial: only high punishment_tech leads to efficiency gains. The control efficiency (80%) is increased to 89.5% with high punishment. The findings are most directly relevant to principal-agent or trust games, but the mechanisms are adjacent to public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (principal and agent).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played the game once (one-shot game).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Principals can send a cheap-talk message (proposed division) to agents, but there is no free-form chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) and return decisions are continuous within bounds, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 3 (amount sent is tripled for the agent).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is either 1 (low) or 1 (high), but the impact varies (see punishment_tech).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is either 1 (low) or 3 (high). High punishment_tech (1:3) increases efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some treatments allow reward, some do not (between-subjects design).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost to rewarder is either 1 (low) or 1 (high), but the impact varies (see reward_tech).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward impact per unit cost is either 1 (low) or 3 (high). High reward_tech increases returns but not investment or efficiency as much as high punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot; this is common information.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is based on a principal-agent (investment) game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- All games are one-shot; repeated interaction effects are not studied.
- Only two players per group; group dynamics in larger settings are not addressed.
- No free-form communication; only structured cheap-talk messages are allowed.
- The lab setting may not capture all relevant real-world complexities.
