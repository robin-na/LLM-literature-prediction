The review synthesizes behavioral and neuroimaging evidence on cooperation, punishment, and their neural correlates, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes. It discusses how extrinsic incentives and punishment affect cooperation, but outcome measures are primarily behavioral (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment rates, neural activation) rather than payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proposes a dual-process neural framework for prosocial decision-making in social dilemmas, including public goods games and related paradigms. It reviews evidence that extrinsic incentives (such as punishment) and trust signals independently increase cooperation. Neuroimaging studies show that punishment threats activate cognitive control regions and increase cooperative behavior, particularly among self-regarding or Machiavellian individuals. The review also highlights that individual differences (e.g., social value orientation, Machiavellianism) moderate the impact of punishment and incentives on cooperation. However, the paper does not directly report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral and neural responses.

## Prediction Guidance
This review provides strong theoretical and mechanistic support for the idea that punishment increases cooperation in public-goods-game-like environments, especially for self-regarding individuals. It suggests that the effect of punishment is likely to be more pronounced when extrinsic incentives are salient and among participants with self-regarding preferences. However, because the review does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its value for prediction is primarily in identifying moderators (e.g., individual differences, presence of incentives, trust signals) and in supporting the general mechanism by which punishment increases cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used to inform the likely direction and moderators of punishment effects, but not to calibrate the magnitude of efficiency changes.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new experimental or quantitative data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Outcomes discussed are primarily behavioral (cooperation, punishment, trust) and neural (brain activation), not payoff-based.
- The review synthesizes across a range of social dilemma games (prisoner's dilemma, public goods, trust, dictator, ultimatum), so findings may not transfer exactly to standard public goods games.
- No systematic analysis of how specific game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, player count) quantitatively moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Does not provide direct evidence for the magnitude or direction of punishment effects on efficiency or group payoff.
