## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that status hierarchies can endogenously coordinate punishment in public goods games, leading to more optimal (i.e., less costly and more effective) use of punishment. High status actors are more likely to be expected to punish when punishment is seen as group-oriented, and their punishment is more likely to promote subsequent cooperation and reduce the need for further punishment. The author suggests that these dynamics can help groups realize public goods more efficiently, but this is a theoretical claim rather than an empirically demonstrated effect. The paper also discusses why typical experimental designs may fail to observe these effects, due to their suppression of status emergence and communication.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical arguments and literature synthesis suggesting that the presence of status hierarchies and the assignment of punishment responsibility to high status actors could improve the efficiency of punishment in public goods games by reducing unnecessary or counterproductive punishment and fostering cooperation. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game designs allowing status differentiation, communication, or leadership roles may see more efficient punishment use, but this is not quantified. The paper is less useful for direct prediction of efficiency from game design dimensions in the absence of empirical data.

## Important Limitations
