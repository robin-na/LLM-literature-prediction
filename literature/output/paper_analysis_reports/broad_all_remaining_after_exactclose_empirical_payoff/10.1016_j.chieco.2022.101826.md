# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Does parental migration impede the development of the cooperative preferences in their left-behind children? Evidence from a large-scale field experiment in China
Authors: Zhou, Y. X., Chen, S. W., Chen, Y. F., & Vollane, B.
Journal: China Economic Review
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level in public goods game
- cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the number of tokens contributed to the public good (contribution level), which is a behavioral measure of cooperation, not a direct measure of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that (1) children's cooperation (measured by contribution in a public goods game) increases with age; (2) paternal migration significantly reduces children's cooperation, while maternal or both-parent migration does not; (3) both exogenous and endogenous punishment mechanisms significantly increase cooperation, with exogenous punishment being more effective; (4) exogenous punishment can offset the negative effect of paternal migration on cooperation. The main outcome is contribution level, not efficiency or group payoff, so the findings are most directly about behavioral cooperation rather than payoff-based efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing punishment mechanisms (especially exogenous punishment) increases cooperation (contributions) in one-shot public goods games with children, and can offset negative effects of paternal absence. However, because the outcome is contribution level rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be mapped to efficiency outcomes with caution. The game design dimensions are well specified (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), and the effect of punishment is robustly positive on contributions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect but informative evidence that punishment is likely to increase efficiency in similar settings, especially when baseline cooperation is low due to social factors.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game group consisted of 3 players (children).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All public goods games were one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between players was not allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were discrete: children could contribute 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 tokens (even numbers up to 10). Not strictly all-or-nothing, but not fully continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (each token contributed is multiplied by 1.5 and split among 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous: if a player contributes less than 10, 2 tokens are deducted from their payoff. No cost to other players; punishment is imposed by rule, not by player action.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction of 2 tokens for under-contribution; effectiveness is high as it is automatic and certain.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Children knew each game was a one-shot (single round) game.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution level (cooperation), not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. Efficiency effects must be inferred indirectly.
- The public goods games are one-shot, not repeated, so dynamic effects of punishment are not studied.
- Punishment is exogenous (rule-based) or endogenous (by group vote), not peer-to-peer; this may limit generalizability to other punishment mechanisms.
- The sample is rural Chinese children; results may not generalize to adults or other cultural contexts.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so no evidence on interaction between punishment and reward.
