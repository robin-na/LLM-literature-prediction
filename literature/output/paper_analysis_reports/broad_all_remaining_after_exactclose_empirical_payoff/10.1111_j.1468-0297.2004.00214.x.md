# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Promises, threats and fairness
Authors: Ellingsen, T., & Johannesson, M.
Journal: Economic Journal
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean profit of investor
- mean offer
- proportion of investors
- acceptance/rejection rates
- group surplus (implied by investment and division)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and credibility of promises and threats
- message content (promise/threat)
- rejection of offers
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are investment rates, mean profit of the investor (seller), and the division of surplus. The study also analyzes the credibility and effect of promises and threats, but these are not the primary outcome. The efficiency-related outcome is the mean profit of the investor, which is directly reported for each treatment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a one-shot investment/bargaining game, allowing pre-play communication (either a promise from the buyer or a threat from the seller) increases the rate of investment and the mean profit of the investor (seller) compared to no communication. Buyer promises are always kept and lead to the highest investor profits (mean profit +8.75 SEK), while seller threats are less credible and less effective (mean profit -6.43 SEK). Without communication, mean profit is lowest (-12.86 SEK). The study provides direct evidence that communication, especially credible promises, can mitigate efficiency losses in social dilemmas similar to public goods or hold-up problems, even in the absence of formal punishment mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling credible communication (especially promises) between parties in a social dilemma increases efficiency and group payoff, even in a one-shot setting. While the game is not a standard public goods game, it is closely related in structure (investment creates surplus, division is bargained). The findings suggest that, in similar environments, introducing a channel for credible promises (but not necessarily punishment) can substantially improve efficiency. The results are most relevant for predicting the effect of communication or commitment devices, rather than punishment per se, but the analysis of threats (costly punishment) is informative: threats are less credible and less effective than promises. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the view that communication/commitment channels can substitute for formal punishment in improving efficiency, but that the effect depends on the credibility and nature of the commitment (promise vs. threat).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played between two players: one seller (investor) and one buyer (bargainer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays only a single round (one-shot game).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication is manipulated: in some treatments, one party can send a written message (promise or threat) before the investment decision. Communication increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The investment decision is all-or-nothing: the seller chooses whether to invest a fixed amount or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 100/60 = 1.67 for the group, but the division is determined by bargaining. The investment of 60 SEK creates 100 SEK surplus.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot (single round).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a one-shot hold-up/investment-bargaining game, not a repeated or standard public goods game.
- No formal punishment or reward mechanism is present; only endogenous threats (rejection) and promises (offers) are possible.
- The sample consists of undergraduate business/economics students from one institution, limiting generalizability.
- The effect of communication is tested only via written, one-way messages, not face-to-face or interactive chat.
- The study does not manipulate or measure punishment cost or technology in the PGG sense.
- The findings may not generalize to multi-player or repeated settings.
