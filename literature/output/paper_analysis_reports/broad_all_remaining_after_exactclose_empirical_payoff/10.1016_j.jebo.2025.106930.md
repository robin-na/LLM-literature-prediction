# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Norm enforcement on minorities: Evidence from traffic violations
Authors: Deng, X. Y., Wang, T., Xue, L., & Yang, S.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total group payoff
- individual payoff
- frequency of high-norm (fully compliant) outcome
- frequency of low-norm (fully noncompliant) outcome
- inequality between group members
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rule violation rate
- proportion of norm violators/followers
- punishment frequency and severity
- monitoring/observation frequency
- beliefs about others' compliance
Outcome notes:
The main experimental outcome is group and individual payoff, with explicit analysis of efficiency (payoff relative to the social optimum). Behavioral outcomes (violation rate, punishment, monitoring) are also reported and analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The laboratory experiment shows that the availability of peer punishment (especially when participants can choose whom to observe and punish) increases rule compliance, particularly among minority (outgroup) members who are more likely to be monitored. However, the introduction of punishment reduces total group payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline, due to the direct costs of punishing and being punished. The highest compliance (lowest violation rate) occurs when both observability and punishment are present, but this comes with increased inequality: minorities earn significantly less than majorities or locals. The frequency of fully compliant (high-norm) groups is higher with punishment, but overall efficiency (average payoff relative to the social optimum) is lower in punishment treatments than in control. Punishment is applied non-discriminatorily, but minorities are more likely to be observed and thus punished, leading to a 'compliance minority effect' and a 'local premium' in payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a public-goods-like environment with negative externalities and peer punishment, enabling punishment increases compliance but reduces efficiency (total group payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The reduction in efficiency is due to the costs of punishment and increased inequality, especially for minorities who are more likely to be monitored and punished. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in similar designs will likely decrease average efficiency, even if compliance increases. The effect is strongest when observability is endogenous (players choose whom to monitor) and when group composition allows for minority/majority distinctions. The findings are most directly relevant to settings with peer punishment, endogenous monitoring, and negative externalities from non-cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experimental groups consisted of 4 players in the street-crossing task (each round: 1 focal participant + 3 others).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 5 rounds of the street-crossing task, with different group compositions in each round.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Chat (text messaging) was allowed only in Stage 1 (group enhancement task) to build group identity, not during the street-crossing (punishment) task.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (rule-following) is binary: either wait for green light (comply) or cross early (violate).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each early crossing imposes a 4 yuan cost on each of the 3 other group members (total negative externality 12 yuan per violation). The marginal per-capita return for compliance is not directly stated but can be inferred from the payoff structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers can spend 1 yuan to reduce 3 yuan from the punished, or 2 yuan to reduce 6 yuan. Cost/impact is fixed and known.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (impact:cost) or 6:2 (impact:cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 5 rounds; one is randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see the behavior and payoffs of the observed player; summary information is provided as part of the feedback.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game but a rule-following task with negative externalities; results may not generalize to classic PGGs with positive externalities.
- Efficiency is reduced by punishment due to direct costs, but the setting is binary (all-or-nothing compliance) and may not capture continuous contribution dynamics.
- The group size is fixed at 4; effects may differ in larger groups.
- Minority/majority group composition is a central feature; results may not generalize to settings without salient group identity or minority status.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment is considered.
- The experiment uses a student sample in a laboratory setting, which may limit external validity.
- The negative externality structure (cost imposed on others) differs from standard PGGs where contributions create positive externalities.
