Journal: Theory and Decision

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is efficiency, measured as the sum of total payoffs. The paper also reports behavioral outcomes such as investment, return, and punishment rates.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares coordinated and uncoordinated punishment in a team investment game with two investors and one allocator. Coordinated punishment (where both investors must punish to inflict a penalty) increases joint investment and allocator return relative to uncoordinated punishment. However, this does not translate into higher efficiency, as the increased frequency of joint punishment in the coordinated condition destroys the potential efficiency gains. The highest efficiency (sum of total payoffs) is observed in the no-punishment condition. There are no significant efficiency differences between coordinated and uncoordinated punishment treatments after accounting for punishment costs. Thus, punishment—regardless of its form—does not improve efficiency in this setting.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a team investment game with binary investment and an allocator, enabling punishment (either coordinated or uncoordinated) reduces efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are: binary (all-or-nothing) investment, two investors and one allocator (asymmetric roles), fixed punishment cost, and varying punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, even if punishment increases cooperation or returns, the cost and frequency of punishment can offset these gains, leading to lower or unchanged efficiency. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, and the specific form of punishment (coordinated vs uncoordinated) does not significantly alter this outcome in this design. The results are most directly applicable to asymmetric, binary-contribution, repeated games with fixed matching and no communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 2 investors and 1 allocator (3 players per group, asymmetric roles).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 periods (rounds) per session, with fixed matching.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Investment decision is binary (invest or not).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If both invest, each pays 5 ECUs, joint surplus is 30 ECUs, split by allocator. Marginal per-capita return is not directly stated but can be calculated: for each investor, 5 ECUs invested can yield up to 15 ECUs if allocator returns all (30/2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 5 ECUs per investor, regardless of whether punishment is effective.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness varies by treatment: in UP_{30,60}, 1 punisher reduces allocator's payoff by 30%, 2 punishers by 60%; in CP_{0,60} and CP_{0,80}, only joint punishment is effective (60% or 80% reduction).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is played for 15 periods (finitely repeated, fixed matching).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects are informed about the decisions of each investor, the amount returned by the allocator, and the punishment decisions when feasible.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game but a team investment game with asymmetric roles (2 investors, 1 allocator), so findings may not generalize to symmetric PGGs.
- Investment is binary (all-or-nothing), not continuous.
- No communication or chat is allowed; results may differ if coordination is facilitated.
- Punishment is only directed at the allocator, not at peers.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- Fixed matching and repeated play may affect behavior compared to one-shot or random matching designs.
- The sample consists of undergraduate students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
- The study does not explore larger group sizes or more complex network structures.
