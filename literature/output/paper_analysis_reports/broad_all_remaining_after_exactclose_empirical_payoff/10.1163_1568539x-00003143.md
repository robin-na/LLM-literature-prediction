Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical and comparative analysis of the evolutionary role of group punishment and social selection in human societies, especially hunter-gatherers. It does not report quantitative payoff or efficiency outcomes, but discusses the role of punishment in promoting cooperation and curbing antisocial behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in mobile hunter-gatherer societies, group punishment—ranging from shaming and ostracism to capital punishment—has been a central mechanism for enforcing cooperation and curbing antisocial behaviors such as bullying, theft, and cheating. These sanctions are argued to have shaped the evolution of human conscience and moral norms, favoring individuals who are generous, honest, and humble. The author suggests that such punitive social selection has been critical for the emergence of egalitarian, cooperative societies, and for the evolutionary success of altruistic traits. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative analysis of efficiency or group payoffs in public goods game-like settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and ethnographic context for the role of punishment in promoting cooperation and suppressing free-riding in small-scale societies. It supports the idea that punishment can be a powerful tool for maintaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper offers indirect support for the positive role of punishment in sustaining cooperation, especially in small, face-to-face groups, but does not allow for parameter-specific predictions based on game design dimensions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not present experimental or quantitative data on efficiency, payoffs, or public goods game outcomes.
- Findings are based on ethnographic synthesis and evolutionary argument, not on controlled manipulation of game design dimensions.
