Outcome notes:
The model tracks behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, error types, classification thresholds) in an evolutionary agent-based simulation of the Prisoner's Dilemma with institutional punishment. No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported, though fitness is used for evolutionary selection.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that evolutionary pressures can lead to agents developing a cognitive bias that causes them to overestimate the probability of being observed and punished for norm violations. As a result, agents often cooperate (obey norms) even when they are not actually being observed, especially in environments with high uncertainty and/or strong punishment. The rate of cooperation increases with the magnitude of punishment, the probability of being observed, and the uncertainty in classifying situations as observed or not. However, much of this cooperation is 'irrational' in the sense that it is not based on accurate assessment of risk, but on evolved bias. The model does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that institutional punishment and cognitive bias can sustain high levels of cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that increasing punishment magnitude and the probability of observation can increase cooperation rates in social dilemma settings, even when actual monitoring is imperfect. However, because the model is based on the Prisoner's Dilemma (not a public goods game) and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, its direct applicability to predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments is limited. The findings suggest that punishment can be effective in promoting cooperation, especially under uncertainty, but do not quantify the impact on group efficiency or surplus. The model also highlights the importance of cognitive biases in sustaining cooperation, which may be relevant for interpreting experimental results in PGGs with punishment.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each generation consists of 100 agents, paired for PD games (i.e., 2-player games per interaction). The group size for each game is always 2.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent participates in one PD game per generation; the model is run for 5000 generations, but each interaction is one-shot per pair per generation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD game is binary: agents choose to cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing choice).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment magnitude (cost imposed on defectors) is varied: 10, 20, or 50 points. Higher punishment increases cooperation rates.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a direct deduction from defectors' fitness. Effectiveness is manipulated via magnitude (10, 20, 50). Higher effectiveness leads to more cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model uses the Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game, so findings may not transfer directly to PGG-like environments.
- No direct efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only cooperation rates and classification errors are analyzed.
- The model assumes fixed cognitive thresholds within an agent's lifetime, whereas real humans may adapt their beliefs over time.
- No communication, reputation, or learning mechanisms are included.
- Punishment is institutional and automatic, not peer-administered or voluntary.
- No reward or positive incentive mechanisms are modeled.
