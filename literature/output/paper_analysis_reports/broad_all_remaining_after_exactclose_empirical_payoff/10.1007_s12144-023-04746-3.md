# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Preemptive strikes or not: the role of emotion on intergroup defensive aggression
Authors: Song, X. Y., Lin, L. A., Wang, Z. C., Li, Y., & Liu, L.
Journal: Current Psychology
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- preemptive behavior (pressing red button)
- emotional responses (fear, hope)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency of preemptive attack (pressing the red button) in a group preemptive strike game. Emotional responses (fear, hope) are also measured. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported; only individual and group losses from attacks are described in the game rules.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a group preemptive strike game, introducing punishment for non-contributors or assigning leadership increases the likelihood that group members will engage in preemptive attack (press the red button). This effect is mediated by increased fear (which increases attack) and hope (which decreases attack). However, the main outcome is behavioral (attack frequency), not efficiency or group payoff. The game rules indicate that preemptive attacks are costly for both groups, suggesting that increased attack rates likely reduce group efficiency, but this is not directly measured or reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It shows that introducing punishment increases costly preemptive attack behavior in a group conflict game, which by the game rules reduces group welfare. However, the study does not report efficiency or total payoff, so the effect on efficiency must be inferred from the game structure. The findings suggest that in environments where punishment increases costly defensive aggression, efficiency may decrease. The game is not a standard public goods game, but the mechanism (punishment increasing costly group-protective action) is adjacent. Use this paper as indirect evidence that punishment can increase costly, inefficient group-protective behaviors in some intergroup settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group has 3 members; games are played between two groups (total 6 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played only once (one-shot); no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; participants separated by barriers.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/attack choice is all-or-nothing: press red button (attack) or not.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a penalty for non-contributors in the winning group: non-contributors lose 20 coins, contributors lose 10 coins. This increases attack rates, which by game rules increases group losses.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective: non-contributors are penalized more than contributors, which increases attack rates and likely reduces group efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is played only once (one-shot).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is preemptive attack frequency, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a group preemptive strike game with different strategic structure.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured or reported; effects on efficiency must be inferred from game rules.
- The study is conducted in a one-shot setting, not repeated rounds.
- No analysis of how punishment effects might differ with different punishment costs or effectiveness beyond the specific implementation used.
