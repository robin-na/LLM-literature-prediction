# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Electrophysiological Markers of Fairness and Selfishness Revealed by a Combination of Dictator and Ultimatum Games
Authors: Miraghaie, A. M., Pouretemad, H., Villa, A. E. P., Mazaheri, M. A., Khosrowabadi, R., & Lintas, A.
Journal: Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- behavioral indices of selfishness/fairness and conceitedness/altruism
- accept/reject rates for allocations/offers
- reaction times
- event-related potential (ERP) components (N1, P2, MFN, LPC) as neural markers
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral and neural correlates of fairness, selfishness, and punishment-related decisions in Dictator and Ultimatum Games. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Outcomes are primarily behavioral (accept/reject, indices) and neural (ERP components).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that distinct neural markers (N1, P2, MFN, LPC) are associated with behavioral profiles of fairness and selfishness in economic games. Selfish participants show neural signatures consistent with spiteful punishment (e.g., rejecting low offers in UG, resulting in zero payoffs for both), while fair participants show markers consistent with costly punishment (e.g., rejecting selfish allocations in DG). However, the paper does not report or analyze group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and does not manipulate or compare the presence/absence of punishment as a treatment. The findings are relevant for understanding the neural basis of punishment and fairness, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The games studied (DG and UG) are adjacent to PGGs but lack the group structure, repeated interaction, and explicit public goods provision. Punishment in UG is endogenous (rejection of unfair offers) and always results in zero payoff for both, but the study does not compare efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report group-level payoff outcomes. The main value for prediction is in the mechanistic and neural understanding of individual differences in punishment behavior, not in quantitative efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (allocator/recipient in DG, proposer/responder in UG).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 240 trials per game (DG and UG), divided into 5 blocks of 48 trials.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. In each trial, the participant can only accept or reject a randomly presented allocation (DG) or offer (UG); no continuous contribution choice.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In UG, rejection of an offer is a form of costly punishment: both players receive zero payoff. The cost is the loss of the responder's own share.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is binary and absolute: rejection of an offer in UG results in zero payoff for both. No gradation or variable impact per unit cost.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of blocks and trials (5 blocks of 48 trials per game).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus; all outcomes are behavioral or neural.
- The games are two-player (DG and UG), not group-based public goods games; results may not generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- Punishment is endogenous (rejection in UG) and always results in zero payoff for both; there is no treatment comparison with and without punishment.
- No manipulation of game design dimensions such as MPCR, group size, or reward mechanisms.
- Sample is limited to young, male, Iranian university students; results may not generalize to other populations.
- No direct measurement of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group welfare.
