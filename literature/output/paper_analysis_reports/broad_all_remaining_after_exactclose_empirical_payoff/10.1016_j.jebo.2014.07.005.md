# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strategic complexity and cooperation: An experimental study
Authors: Jones, M. T.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- first stage cooperation
- strategy prevalence (e.g., Grim Trigger, Tit-for-Tat, Always Defect)
- relationship between cognitive ability (ACT/SAT) and cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates and inferred strategy use. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff tables are described, but outcomes are not aggregated into efficiency or group payoff measures.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Increasing the complexity of strategies required to sustain cooperation in an indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma reduces the frequency of cooperation and increases the prevalence of simple selfish strategies. This effect is observed both when the cooperate action and when the defect action are made state-dependent, increasing the information processing required for cooperation or punishment, respectively. The study also finds that higher cognitive ability is associated with greater cooperation in the baseline, but this advantage is reduced when complexity is increased. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral measures such as cooperation rates and strategy inference.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that increasing the cognitive or strategic complexity of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas reduces cooperation rates, and by implication, would likely reduce efficiency or group payoff if measured. However, the study is not a public goods game and does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; instead, it relies on endogenous punishment via repeated game strategies (e.g., Grim Trigger). The findings are most relevant for predicting how complexity or cognitive burden might moderate the effect of punishment or cooperation-supporting mechanisms in repeated games, but do not provide direct evidence for the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games. Use this paper as informative indirect evidence about the role of complexity and cognitive ability in sustaining cooperation, but not for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGGs with or without punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are played in pairs (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each repeated game is played for an indefinite number of rounds with an 80% continuation probability; the actual number of rounds per game is randomly determined.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Action space is discrete with three choices (cooperate, defect, dominated action); not continuous or all-or-nothing in the PGG sense, but all actions are discrete.
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. A default choice is automatically entered if time expires: defect in the first stage, previous action in subsequent stages.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are indefinitely repeated with 80% continuation probability; the number of rounds is not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, players are shown the opponent's action and their own payoff for that stage.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is a repeated prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game or a direct variant; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; punishment is endogenous via repeated game strategies.
- No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; only cooperation rates and strategy prevalence are analyzed.
- The study focuses on the effect of strategic complexity and cognitive ability, not on institutional or design features typical of PGGs with punishment.
- Sample is limited to undergraduate students at a single university, which may limit generalizability.
