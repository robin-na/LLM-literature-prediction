## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the ability of groups to enforce cooperation through peer punishment depends critically on the structure of their internal communication networks. Specifically, the 'reach' or 'integration' of the network—how widely and quickly information about defections can spread—determines how easily cooperation can be sustained. Denser networks are not necessarily better; networks with high integration (no highly peripheral or bottleneck nodes) are optimal for supporting cooperation. The model also shows that there is an upper bound on the proportion of interethnic (cross-group) interactions that can be kept cooperative, determined by the network's reach. Improvements in communication speed, longer punishment phases, and more integrated networks all make cooperation easier to sustain. The paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, but rather the theoretical conditions for sustaining cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical guidance on how network structure moderates the effectiveness of peer punishment in sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For prediction tasks, it suggests that the effectiveness of punishment (and thus the likely efficiency gain from enabling punishment) depends not on network density but on integration/reach. However, the model is not a public goods game per se, and does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency. It is most useful for qualitative reasoning about the importance of network structure (especially communication reach) as a moderator of punishment's effect on cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model allows for arbitrary group size n; results are stated for general n, but some examples use n=7 or n=9.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Infinitely repeated game; results are for the infinite horizon case.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Action set is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each round.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a phase where the punisher receives a higher payoff (alpha) and the punished receives a lower payoff (-beta); costs and benefits are parameterized by alpha > 1 and beta > 0.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is determined by the number of punishers (network reach) and the length of the punishment phase (T); the more people who know, the more effective the punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is infinitely repeated; players know the structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players learn about others' actions only via network-based gossip; the model specifies who learns what and when.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is administered by coethnics who learn of a defection; the identity of the punisher is known within the network.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a formal theory, not an empirical or experimental study; no quantitative efficiency or payoff outcomes are reported.
- The game modeled is a repeated prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results are adjacent but not directly transferable to PGG settings.
- Outcomes are about the feasibility of sustaining cooperation, not about realized group efficiency or payoffs.
- Network structure is exogenous and fixed; the model does not address how networks form or change endogenously.
- No empirical validation or parameterization of the model is provided.
