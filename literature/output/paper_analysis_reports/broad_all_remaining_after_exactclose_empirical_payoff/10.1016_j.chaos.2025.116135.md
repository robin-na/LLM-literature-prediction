Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the proportions of different strategies (cooperators, punishers, defectors, bribers, bribe-recipients) and the resulting phase transitions. The paper frequently refers to 'contribution level' as a proxy for cooperation, but does not directly report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Payoff calculations are described in the model, but aggregate payoff or efficiency is not a primary reported outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in spatial public goods games with peer punishment, the introduction of bribery (defectors bribing punishers to avoid sanctions, and punishers accepting bribes) leads to complex phase transitions among strategies. When punishment intensity is low, bribery can increase the fraction of contributors (interpreted as higher cooperation), as bribe-recipients and bribers form alliances that resist defectors. As the bribe amount increases, the system can enter cyclic coexistence states involving bribers, bribe-recipients, and defectors. When punishment intensity is high, bribery undermines the maintenance of punishment and can reduce the contribution level. The effect of bribery is highly sensitive to the synergy coefficient (analogous to MPCR), punishment fine, and bribe amount. However, the paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff, focusing instead on the prevalence of strategies and contribution rates.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how punishment and bribery mechanisms interact to affect cooperation in spatial PGGs, but does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by proxy, efficiency) is moderated by the possibility of bribery and the parameters of the game (synergy coefficient, punishment fine, bribe amount). In environments where punishment is weak, bribery can increase cooperation; where punishment is strong, bribery can undermine it. However, since efficiency is not directly measured, these results should be used cautiously for predicting efficiency outcomes. The model is also spatial and evolutionary, which may differ from typical lab PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent is located on a square lattice (side length 200), interacting with 4 neighbors; each PGG group has 5 players (focal + 4 neighbors).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to hundreds of millions of time steps until a stable configuration is reached; not a fixed number of rounds as in lab PGGs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: agents either contribute cost c or nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy coefficient r is varied (e.g., r=3.5, r=4) with group size 5, so MPCR = r/5 (e.g., 0.7 or 0.8).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays cost alpha per punishment (e.g., alpha=0.1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is beta (fine imposed per punishment), varied in the model (e.g., beta=0.05, 0.1, 0.15).

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and agent-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are reported as strategy frequencies and contribution levels, not as group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The spatial, evolutionary setting may not generalize to well-mixed or laboratory PGGs.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes; contribution level is used as a proxy.
- No human subjects or real-world data; all results are from simulations.
- No communication, reward, or identification mechanisms are modeled.
