# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Equilibrium cooperation in two-stage games: Experimental evidence
Authors: Davis, D. D., & Holt, C. A.
Journal: International Journal of Game Theory
Year: 1999

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- first-stage cooperation rate
- second-stage punishment/reward propensities
- contingent strategy play
- session-level cooperation transitions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: rates of cooperation in the first stage, and the use of punishment/reward strategies in the second stage. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff values are described for equilibrium paths, but session-level or treatment-level efficiency is not calculated or compared.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the use of contingent punishment/reward strategies in the second stage of a two-stage game is strongly associated with higher rates of cooperation in the first stage. This effect is robust across different treatments, including those where the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium or is Pareto dominated. The structure of the second-stage equilibria does not reliably predict cooperation rates. Instead, initial cohort propensities and first-stage payoff structure are more important. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the availability and use of punishment/reward strategies can increase cooperation rates in two-stage games, even when the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The findings suggest that behavioral responses to punishment opportunities are important, but the translation to efficiency outcomes is not addressed. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can support cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played between two players (dyads).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 20 two-stage games; each game has two stages (not repeated with the same partner).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; decisions are made anonymously via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary (e.g., R1 or R2, C1 or C2); not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via second-stage choices that reduce the other player's payoff; the cost to the punisher is determined by the payoff matrix (e.g., R4 choice reduces C's payoff). The cost is not always explicit as a deduction, but is embedded in the payoff structure.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is determined by the payoff matrix; different treatments vary whether the punishment is a Nash equilibrium, Pareto dominated, or non-Nash.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward strategies are possible in the second stage (e.g., R3/C4 as a reward for cooperation).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is embedded in the payoff structure; not always explicit, but determined by the matrix (e.g., R3/C4 as a reward).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the payoff matrix; different equilibria correspond to different reward impacts.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of games and the structure in advance; each session consists of 20 games.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each game, participants see their own previous decisions, earnings, and the decisions of their partners in the current sequence. They do not see other players' histories or previous sequences.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, punishment/reward propensities) are analyzed.
- The game is a two-stage dyadic game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player or continuous-contribution settings.
- Punishment and reward are implemented via second-stage strategic choices, not via explicit costly punishment mechanisms as in many PGG experiments.
- Strong cohort effects and session-level heterogeneity limit the generalizability of findings.
- No communication or chat is allowed; results may differ in environments with communication.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency or total group payoff cannot be determined from the reported data.
