## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, evolutionary dynamics lead to two regimes: (1) a defection regime with low cooperation and low group payoff when punishment is weak or absent, and (2) a coordination regime with higher cooperation and higher group payoff when punishment is sufficiently strong and driven by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model predicts the empirically observed propensity to punish in lab experiments. The presence of punishment transforms the social dilemma into a coordination problem, enabling higher efficiency. Disadvantageous inequity aversion is shown to be evolutionarily dominant, and populations with this trait invade and outcompete purely self-regarding populations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency (group payoff) when punishment is sufficiently strong and is motivated by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model gives explicit formulas for the threshold propensity to punish and shows that, above this threshold, populations coordinate on higher contributions and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar design parameters (e.g., n=4, r=3, MPCR=0.4), enabling punishment will shift the group from low to high efficiency, provided the punishment regime is effective. The paper also predicts how efficiency and punishment propensity depend on group size and punishment effectiveness, which can inform predictions for other parameterizations. However, the paper does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency change, so predictions should be interpreted as strong theoretical expectations rather than empirical effect sizes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model and empirical validation focus on n=4; model also analyzes effect of varying n on punishment propensity and regime threshold.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Empirical experiments use 6 or 10 rounds; model is one-shot or repeated stranger matching, but evolutionary model is not round-based.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose any amount from their endowment to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = g/n = 1.6/4 = 0.4 in empirical validation; model allows for general g and n.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 MU per unit of punishment; punished loses r=3 MUs per unit.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness r=3 (punished loses 3 MUs per 1 MU spent by punisher); model analyzes effect of varying r.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects know the number of rounds in the empirical experiments (6 or 10), but this is not a focus of the model.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are informed about the contributions of their group mates.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. In at least one experiment, the timing of information about received punishment is varied (immediate vs. delayed), but it is not clear if the identity of the punisher is revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical; empirical validation is limited to matching predicted punishment propensities, not direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff.
- No direct empirical comparison of efficiency or group payoff between punishment and no-punishment conditions is provided.
- Model assumes evolutionary dynamics and may not capture short-run or one-shot experimental effects.
- Empirical data is pooled from three experiments with similar but not identical designs; some details (e.g., punishment information timing) vary.
- No analysis of communication, reward, or other institutional features beyond punishment.
