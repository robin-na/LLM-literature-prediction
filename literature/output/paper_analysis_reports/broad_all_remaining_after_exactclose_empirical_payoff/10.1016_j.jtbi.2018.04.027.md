## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- long-term payoff
- efficiency (probability of mutual cooperation as error probability approaches zero)
- defensibility (payoff not less than any co-player's)
- distinguishability (payoff greater than AllC co-players)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy memory length
- robustness to error

Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and computational, focusing on the construction and properties of strategies in repeated public goods games. The main outcomes are payoff-based (efficiency, defensibility, distinguishability), not behavioral rates like contribution or punishment frequency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with three players and implementation error, it is possible to construct deterministic memory-3 strategies that achieve mutual cooperation (efficiency) and defensibility (no player can be exploited), as well as distinguishability (outperforming unconditional cooperators). For n-person games, memory length at least n is required for such strategies. The strategies do not involve explicit punishment actions or costs, but use conditional cooperation and defection rules that can serve similar functions. The findings are theoretical and do not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling punishment mechanisms on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games, it is possible to achieve high efficiency through carefully designed deterministic strategies with sufficient memory, even in the presence of errors. However, it does not study the effect of explicit punishment mechanisms (such as costly punishment or sanctions) on efficiency, nor does it manipulate or compare games with and without punishment. Therefore, while the results are highly relevant for understanding the potential for cooperation and efficiency in repeated PGGs, they do not directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in experimental or empirical settings. The paper is most useful for understanding the theoretical limits of strategy design rather than the causal effect of punishment interventions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main analysis is for n=3, with generalization to arbitrary n. The results depend critically on player count, as memory length required for efficiency and defensibility increases with n.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is modeled as infinitely repeated (limit as T→∞), which is essential for the theoretical results on long-term payoffs and stationary distributions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player chooses to either contribute (c) or not (d); contributions are binary, not continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is defined as the multiplication factor ρ divided by n, with 1 < ρ < n.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is modeled as infinitely repeated; players know the game continues indefinitely.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players' strategies are conditioned on the full history of moves (memory-m), so in effect, players observe the relevant history of others' actions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled; the strategies use conditional cooperation and defection, not costly punishment.
- Findings are for infinitely repeated games with full knowledge of history and deterministic strategies, which may not generalize to finite or less transparent settings.
- The results do not address the causal effect of enabling or disabling punishment mechanisms on efficiency.
- The computational complexity of constructing successful strategies increases superexponentially with player count, making practical implementation for large n infeasible.
