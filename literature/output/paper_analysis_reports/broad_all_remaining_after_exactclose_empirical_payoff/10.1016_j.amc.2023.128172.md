The paper focuses on the evolutionary dynamics of strategies (cooperation, defection, bribery, use of external supervision) and the prevalence of corruption, not on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Payoff matrices are specified, but aggregate efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed or reported as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of an optional external supervision service can reduce corruption and promote cooperation, but its effectiveness depends on the cost of supervision and the mutation rates of players and enforcers. Lowering the cost of supervision is not always optimal: if the cost is too low, unnecessary supervision may occur, while if it is too high, corruption may persist. The initial fraction of honest enforcers and the relative mutation rates are critical: higher mutation rates for enforcers (relative to players) favor honest enforcement and trusting cooperation. In finite populations, the system may reach stable equilibria or exhibit persistent oscillations in the fractions of cooperators, defectors, and corrupt enforcers. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct implications for efficiency are not provided.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, mechanistic evidence about how punishment, corruption, and external supervision interact in a social dilemma context with bribery and enforcement. It does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff as a function of game design dimensions. However, it suggests that the effectiveness of punishment (via external supervision) is highly sensitive to the cost of supervision and the mutation rates of enforcers and players. The model is adjacent to public goods games, but is not a standard PGG, and the main outcomes are behavioral (strategy fractions), not efficiency. Thus, the paper is more useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators of punishment and corruption than for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player count (N) is explicitly manipulated in simulation experiments (N=10, 100, 1000) to study its effect on dynamics and equilibria.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run for a fixed number of time steps (T=1000, 2000, 5000 depending on N), but the model is evolutionary and does not have discrete rounds in the PGG sense.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (C), defect (D), or use external supervision (C_a).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via fines (f) for defectors and bribes (B) for corrupt enforcers; the cost of external supervision (a) is a key parameter. The cost to punishers is not directly analogous to PGG punishment cost, but the cost of engaging supervision is central.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the fine (f) and the ability of external supervision to detect and penalize corruption. The model explores how these parameters affect strategy dynamics.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cooperators who are cheated receive compensation (a form of reward) from enforcers in some cases. Positive incentives are included in the model.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation to cooperators is paid by enforcers; the cost is part of the model's payoff structure.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the compensation amount (fraction of fine f).

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a bribery/corruption game with pairwise interactions and external supervision.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of strategy fractions and corruption prevalence, not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- No direct empirical or experimental data; all results are theoretical or simulation-based.
- The model assumes a specific structure (one enforcer per pair, fixed payoff parameters) that may not generalize to all real-world or PGG-like environments.
- No analysis of communication, information feedback, or identification of punishers/rewarders.
- The effect of external supervision cost is context-dependent and may not generalize to other forms of punishment or institutional design.
