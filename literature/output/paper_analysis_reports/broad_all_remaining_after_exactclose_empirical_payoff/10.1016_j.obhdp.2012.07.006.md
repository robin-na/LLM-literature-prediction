# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The role of Social Value Orientation in response to an unfair offer in the Ultimatum Game
Authors: Karagonlar, G., & Kuhlman, D. M.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance/rejection of unfair offer
- self-reported emotions (anger, happiness, satisfaction, pleasure)
- attitudes toward rejection
- attributions about proposer motivation
- fairness perceptions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the binary accept/reject decision in a one-shot Ultimatum Game in response to an unfair offer. The study also measures self-reported emotions and cognitive attributions, but does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. No direct payoff-based outcome is analyzed beyond the accept/reject choice.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two lab experiments, the paper finds that Prosocials (as measured by SVO) are less likely to reject an unfair offer (20% of the pie) in the Ultimatum Game than Proselfs. This difference is not due to differences in fairness perceptions or expectations, but is mediated by more positive emotions and more positive attributions toward the proposer among Prosocials. The findings support the view that emotion regulation and cognitive reappraisal, rather than altruistic punishment, drive acceptance of unfair offers among Prosocials. The study does not manipulate or measure efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment in a public goods context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The Ultimatum Game is structurally different from public goods games, and the outcome of interest is the accept/reject decision rather than group payoff or efficiency. The findings are relevant for understanding individual differences in responses to norm violations and costly punishment, but do not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency. The study does, however, suggest that motivations for punishment (altruistic vs. spiteful) may differ by individual orientation, which could be contextually relevant for interpreting punishment effects in other games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a two-player (dyadic) Ultimatum Game: one proposer, one recipient.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; anonymity was emphasized.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The recipient's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rejection is a costly punishment: the recipient forgoes their own payoff to punish the proposer (both get zero if rejected). The cost is the recipient's own foregone payoff (2).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rejection reduces both players' payoffs to zero; the punishment is absolute (all-or-nothing).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told explicitly that the game is a one-shot interaction.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are anonymous to each other, identified only by code numbers; the proposer would know if their offer was rejected, but not the identity of the recipient.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, which is structurally different from public goods games and does not involve group efficiency or repeated interactions.
- The main outcome is the accept/reject decision, not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of an unfair offer, not as a separate punishment stage or peer sanctioning as in public goods games.
- The study does not manipulate or measure efficiency or payoff-based outcomes beyond the accept/reject choice.
- Emotions and cognitions are measured post-decision, which may reflect self-justification.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-person or repeated games.
