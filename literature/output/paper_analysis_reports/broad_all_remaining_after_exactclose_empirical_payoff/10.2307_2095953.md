## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The simulations show that the introduction of social control (punishment/sanctions) in a public goods environment can move a group from a low-efficiency, noncooperative equilibrium to a high-efficiency, cooperative equilibrium. Material sanctions (with moderate cost and linear production) are most effective at achieving and sustaining high efficiency, while moral sanctions are vulnerable to start-up problems unless the cost structure is highly favorable. Internalized (self-imposed) fairness norms can lead to even higher efficiency but risk overcooperation (wasteful excess contribution). The model demonstrates that sanctioning systems can be self-limiting: once high cooperation is achieved, the need for enforcement diminishes. The efficiency gains from punishment depend on the sanctioning regime's cost, production function, and the group's learning rate.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game can increase group efficiency, especially when the punishment system is not too costly and is effective at inducing compliance. The effect is moderated by the type of sanction (material vs. moral vs. internalized), the cost structure of enforcement, and the learning dynamics of the group. For prediction, the findings suggest that, all else equal, adding a reasonably efficient punishment mechanism to a PGG-like environment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude and sustainability of the effect depend on the details of the punishment technology and cost. The model also highlights the risk of overcooperation with internalized norms and the importance of self-limiting enforcement. The results are most directly applicable to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games with group sizes around 100, moderate jointness of supply, and no communication.

## Important Limitations
- The study is based on computer simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- Simulations assume homogeneous actors and fixed group size (N=100); effects of heterogeneity are not explored.
- Contribution and sanctioning choices are binary (all-or-nothing), not continuous.
- No communication or network structure is modeled; all interactions are group-level and anonymous.
- The model assumes no explicit knowledge of the number of rounds or endgame effects.
- Findings may not generalize to small groups, continuous contribution settings, or environments with communication.
- Theoretical model does not account for real-world complexities such as status differences, targeted punishment, or antisocial control.
