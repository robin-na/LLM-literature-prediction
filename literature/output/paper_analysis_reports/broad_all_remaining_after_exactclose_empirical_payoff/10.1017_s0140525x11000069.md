Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical and critical review, not an empirical study. It synthesizes findings from laboratory experiments (including public goods games with and without punishment), field studies, and ethnographic/historical evidence. It discusses both payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group earnings, aggregate payoffs) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation, punishment, norm enforcement).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that laboratory experiments with public goods games and punishment show that while punishment can increase cooperation rates, it often reduces group efficiency due to the destruction of resources. Efficiency gains from punishment are only observed under specific conditions, such as very long repeated interactions with the same group, and even then, the need for punishment diminishes as cooperation stabilizes. In field and ethnographic studies, there is little evidence for the use of costly, uncoordinated punishment to sustain cooperation; instead, low- or no-cost sanctions (gossip, ostracism, coordinated punishment) are prevalent and effective. The paper argues that the external validity of laboratory punishment experiments is limited, and that real-world institutions that sustain cooperation do so by reducing the cost of punishment, extending the time horizon, and improving information flow.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that enabling costly, uncoordinated punishment in public goods games is unlikely to increase efficiency except under narrow conditions (e.g., long repeated games with stable groups). In most laboratory settings, punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to resource destruction. In real-world or field-like settings, cooperation is more effectively sustained by low-cost, coordinated, or symbolic sanctions, not by costly punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the presence of costly punishment (especially if uncoordinated and not paired with communication or institutional support) should not be expected to reliably increase efficiency over the control condition, and may even reduce it. Game design dimensions that reduce the cost of punishment, allow communication, or enable coordination are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Laboratory public goods games typically use small groups (often 4-6 players), and the paper discusses how small group size facilitates monitoring and punishment. In field settings, larger groups require institutional solutions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is less positive as group size increases and monitoring becomes harder.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Longer repeated games (more rounds) allow punishment to be more effective at sustaining cooperation and can increase efficiency, but only when the horizon is very long (e.g., 50 rounds). In most cases, punishment does not improve efficiency.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) is discussed as a mechanism that, when combined with punishment, increases efficiency and cooperation. Communication alone or with punishment is more effective than punishment alone.
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. The concept of marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is discussed in the context of public goods and Prisoner's Dilemma games, but no specific values or manipulations are reported.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The cost to the punisher is central to the paper's argument. High punishment costs reduce efficiency and make costly punishment less viable in the field. Laboratory experiments show that punishment only increases efficiency when costs are low and/or the impact is high.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is discussed. When the cost/impact ratio is high (e.g., 1:3 or higher), punishment does not increase cooperation or efficiency. Low-cost, high-impact punishment is more effective.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Reward mechanisms are mentioned as alternatives to punishment, but not analyzed in detail.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The paper discusses the difference between one-shot, finitely repeated, and indefinitely repeated games. Subjects distinguish between these, and punishment is more effective in long repeated games.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Information about others' contributions and payoffs is discussed as important for monitoring and sustaining cooperation. Institutions that facilitate information flow are more effective.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Anonymity and the ability to identify punishers are discussed. In the lab, anonymity protects punishers; in the field, identification can lead to feuds and counter-punishment, reducing the effectiveness of costly punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical and critical review, not an original empirical or experimental study.
- It synthesizes evidence from multiple sources, but does not provide new quantitative data or direct experimental results.
- Some claims rely on the interpretation of existing literature, which may be selective or subject to debate.
- The paper focuses on the distinction between laboratory and field evidence, and may understate the diversity of laboratory designs or the potential for institutional innovation.
- No direct manipulation or measurement of all game design dimensions; some are discussed only in general terms.
