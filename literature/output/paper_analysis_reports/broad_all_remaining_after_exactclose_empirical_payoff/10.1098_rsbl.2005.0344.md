# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment and partner switching cause cooperative behaviour in a cleaning mutualism
Authors: Bshary, R., & Grutter, A. S.
Journal: Biology Letters
Year: 2005

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of preferred (prawn) vs. non-preferred (flake) food items eaten
- change in foraging behavior in response to punishment or partner switching
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the proportion of preferred vs. non-preferred food items eaten by cleaner fish after exposure to punishment (chasing) or partner switching (fleeing). No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported. The behavioral outcome is interpreted as a proxy for cooperation but is not a direct payoff-based outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that cleaner fish, when exposed to simulated punishment (chasing) or partner switching (fleeing) after eating their preferred food, learn to avoid the preferred food and instead eat more of the less preferred food. This behavioral shift is interpreted as evidence that punishment and partner switching can promote cooperative behavior in mutualistic interactions. The control group, which experienced no negative consequences, increased their preference for the preferred food. The findings support the idea that both punishment and the threat of partner switching can be effective partner control mechanisms, but the outcomes are measured in terms of behavioral change rather than direct payoffs or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that punishment and partner switching can alter behavior in a way that is consistent with increased cooperation, but it does not provide direct evidence about efficiency or group payoff as defined in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent to PGGs, focusing on dyadic mutualism rather than group contributions and payoffs. For prediction tasks about efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper suggests that punishment and partner switching can change behavior toward more cooperative outcomes, but does not quantify the effect on efficiency or provide data on group-level payoffs. Its main value is in supporting the behavioral mechanism by which punishment can promote cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The experiment is dyadic (one cleaner fish and one simulated client plate per trial), not a group setting.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each cleaner fish underwent multiple learning trials (six), plus initial and final tests. Not a repeated group game, but repeated dyadic interactions.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as chasing the cleaner fish for 1-2 seconds after eating the preferred food. This manipulation caused a behavioral shift toward less preferred food (interpreted as cooperation).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is dyadic and does not use a group or public goods structure; findings may not generalize to group-based PGG environments.
- No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (food choice) are reported.
- Punishment cost to the punisher is not modeled or measured.
- The simulated punishment and partner switching may not fully capture the complexity or magnitude of these mechanisms in natural or human settings.
- The study uses animal subjects (cleaner fish), which may limit direct applicability to human PGG-like environments.
