## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper provides a general theoretical framework for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms can be used to sustain efficient outcomes in repeated games with public monitoring. It shows that the set of sustainable payoffs is characterized by admissibility and joint rationality, and that explicit punishment phases (using approachability) can deter deviations. However, the efficiency achievable in equilibrium is limited by the monitoring structure: if deviations are not detectable or not attributable, efficiency may be strictly less than the social optimum. The paper gives explicit constructions of equilibrium strategies with main (cooperation), punishment, compensation, and reward phases, and proves that for sufficiently patient players, any payoff in the interior of the admissible and jointly rational set can be supported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the efficiency of repeated games with punishment, especially in settings with imperfect public monitoring. It provides a theoretical upper bound on efficiency as a function of the monitoring structure and the ability to implement punishment phases. The explicit construction of punishment and reward phases shows how punishment can be used to sustain cooperation, but also highlights that efficiency is limited by the possibility of undetectable or unattributable deviations. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the monitoring structure: when monitoring is sufficiently informative, punishment can support high efficiency; when it is not, efficiency is bounded away from the optimum. The results are general and apply to a wide class of repeated games, including but not limited to public goods games.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- Results depend critically on the structure of the public monitoring signal; in many practical settings, monitoring may be more limited or noisy.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as phases in equilibrium strategies, not as explicit costly actions; this may differ from standard public goods game punishment mechanisms.
- The model assumes infinite horizon (discounted repeated games) and patient players; results may not generalize to finitely repeated games or impatient players.
- The framework is general for repeated games, not specifically tailored to public goods games; some design dimensions (e.g., mpcr, all-or-nothing) are not directly addressed.
