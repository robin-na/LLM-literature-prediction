## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- index of cooperation (IC): fraction of initial states leading to full cooperation
- average per capita payoff
- resource abundance
- group payoff as a function of strategy mix
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of strategy types (CondC, ComD, Defector)
- frequency of honest vs corrupt enforcers
Outcome notes:
The main outcome is the 'index of cooperation' (IC), which is the fraction of initial conditions that lead to a fully cooperative equilibrium (all conditional cooperators). This is a direct function of group payoff and resource abundance, and is interpreted as a measure of successful management (i.e., efficiency). The model also tracks average payoffs and resource levels as a function of strategies and parameters.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that delegated punishment (enforcement) can support efficient, fully cooperative outcomes in common-pool resource games, but only if a sufficient fraction of enforcers are honest. If corruption is prevalent among enforcers, punishment loses its effectiveness, and the system converges to defection and resource overuse (low efficiency). The model exhibits bistability: depending on initial conditions (notably the initial fraction of honest enforcers), the system ends up either in full cooperation (high efficiency) or full defection (low efficiency). Key parameters moderating the effect of punishment include group size (larger groups make cooperation harder), resource growth rate (higher growth rates favor defection), and the speed of resource dynamics (slower resource response masks the benefits of cooperation and impedes efficiency). The cost of harvesting has a mixed effect. The model provides direct predictions about how these dimensions affect the likelihood of efficient outcomes under punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like resource games depends critically on the honesty of enforcers and several game design dimensions. If punishment is implemented via delegated enforcement and enforcers are honest, punishment can enable high efficiency (full cooperation). However, if enforcers are corrupt, punishment fails and efficiency collapses. The model shows that increasing group size, increasing resource growth rate, and slowing resource dynamics all reduce the effectiveness of punishment in achieving efficiency. These findings should inform predictions about treatment efficiency: punishment will only improve efficiency if the enforcement mechanism is robust to corruption and the group size and ecological parameters are favorable. The model is most directly relevant to settings with delegated (not peer) punishment and where corruption is possible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size (N) is a key parameter; increasing N from 2 to 18 is directly analyzed. Larger groups make cooperation and efficiency harder to achieve under delegated punishment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is determined by the resource growth rate (g), group size (N), and harvesting cost (c_h). The model analyzes how these parameters affect payoffs and cooperation.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost of hiring an enforcer (s) is a key parameter. Higher fines (A) and bribes (B) are also analyzed. Honest enforcement (punishment) can support cooperation and efficiency if not undermined by corruption.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the fine (A) and the honesty of enforcers. Higher fines suppress defectors more effectively, but only if enforcers are honest.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is implemented via delegated enforcement, not peer punishment; results may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- The model assumes that corruption is possible among enforcers, which may not apply to all real-world or experimental settings.
- No communication, reward, or information-sharing mechanisms are included.
- The model does not have a fixed number of rounds, so some experimental design features are not represented.
- The main efficiency outcome (index of cooperation) is based on the fraction of initial conditions leading to full cooperation, not average efficiency across all runs.
- Spatial structure and voluntary monitoring by harvesters are not modeled, though discussed as future directions.
