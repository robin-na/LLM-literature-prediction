The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (total/average payoff per treatment) and behavioral outcomes (cooperation/collusion rates, message types, punishment via threat/follow-through).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated three-player Bertrand game, the ability for all players to communicate (full network) leads to the highest group payoffs and cooperation rates. Treatments with only partial communication (either a leader or a pairwise link) do not improve payoffs over the no-communication baseline, and in some cases, payoffs are lower due to the use of punishment strategies (promise and threat) that are followed through after deviations. The structure of communication networks shapes both the strategies communicated and the realized efficiency: full communication supports more 'pure promise' messages and higher payoffs, while asymmetric or partial communication leads to more threats and lower efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that communication structure is a key moderator of the efficiency gains from communication/collusion in repeated games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated collusion games structurally similar to public goods games, the efficiency gains from enabling communication depend critically on the communication network structure. Full, symmetric communication among all players leads to the highest efficiency, while partial or asymmetric communication can result in lower efficiency than even the no-communication baseline, due to increased punishment and failed coordination. For prediction tasks, the presence and structure of communication (chat) is a key moderator: enabling chat among all players is likely to increase efficiency, but partial or asymmetric chat may not. The study does not include explicit monetary punishment or reward mechanisms, but does show that endogenous punishment via threat/follow-through in communication can reduce efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated games with communication as a design dimension.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use three players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 16 rounds; each round is a two-period game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Chat/communication is the main treatment variable: N0 (no chat), N1/N2 (partial chat), N3 (full chat). Only full chat increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/action choices are discrete: in each period, players choose between two options (A/B in period 1, Y/Z in period 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is two periods per round and that there are 16 rounds; rounds are independent and one is randomly paid.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe actions of others after each period; period 1 actions are revealed before period 2 choices.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game but a two-period, three-player Bertrand collusion game; results may not fully generalize to standard PGGs.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms; punishment is endogenous via strategy (threat/follow-through) rather than a separate costly action.
- Communication is restricted to a structured message space, not free-form chat.
- Only three-player groups and four communication structures are tested; results may differ with larger groups or other network structures.
- Each round is independent and only one is paid, which may affect incentives compared to repeated games with cumulative payoffs.
- The action space is discrete and limited (two options per period), which may not capture the full range of contribution behaviors in PGGs.
