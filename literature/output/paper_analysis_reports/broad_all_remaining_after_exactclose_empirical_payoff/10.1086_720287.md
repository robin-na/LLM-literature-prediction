# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Creation of Social Norms under Weak Institutions
Authors: Diekert, F., Eymess, T., Luomba, J., & Waichman, I.
Journal: Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- empirical expectations (beliefs about others' cooperation)
- normative beliefs
- voting to exclude (sanctioning behavior)
- effect of exclusion on subsequent cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates (binary contribute/defect), beliefs about others' cooperation, and use of the sanctioning mechanism. No direct analysis of group payoff, efficiency, or welfare is reported. The payoff structure is described, but outcomes are not aggregated or analyzed in terms of efficiency or total group earnings.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that providing social information about high or low cooperation rates only leads to stable differences in cooperation when a weak social sanctioning institution is present. With sanctioning, cooperation rates remain high if cooperation is emphasized and low if defection is emphasized; without sanctioning, cooperation declines over time regardless of the message. The sanctioning mechanism is a group vote to exclude a strategy from a small bonus, and is used infrequently. The effect of social information and sanctioning is stronger for participants with higher social proximity to others in their session. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on cooperation rates and beliefs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a (weak) social sanctioning institution is necessary for norm-based interventions (social information) to create stable high cooperation in repeated binary social dilemma games. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is close to a public goods game (binary prisoner's dilemma with group returns), and the sanctioning is a group-level exclusion from a small bonus, not direct costly punishment. The findings suggest that, in similar binary games with weak sanctioning, punishment can stabilize cooperation rates, but the effect on efficiency is not directly measured. The results are most useful for predicting behavioral (cooperation) outcomes rather than efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players in every round (three-player prisoner's dilemma).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Seven rounds per session: one initial round plus six repeated rounds, with perfect-stranger matching.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants; privacy ensured by shields.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each participant allocates all 4 points to either private or group account (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group account contribution yields 2 points to each of 3 players (total 6 points per contribution); MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5 (since each player gives up 4 points to contribute, and each gets 2 back).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctioning is via group vote to exclude a strategy from a 1-point bonus; no direct cost to the voter, but excluded players lose the bonus. The cost to the punisher is zero.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exclusion from a 1-point bonus per round, determined by majority vote on which strategy (cooperate/defect) is excluded. Effectiveness is low: the bonus is small relative to the main payoffs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told after the first round that there will be six additional rounds (total seven); the number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see a report on the allocation decisions of all group members and the total points earned by themselves.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (exclusion) is not personalized: participants are told which strategy is excluded and who is excluded, but not who voted for what. Anonymity is maintained.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; all main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, beliefs, sanctioning votes).
- The punishment mechanism is weak and not directly comparable to standard costly punishment in public goods games; it is a group-level exclusion from a small bonus, not direct deduction.
- The game is a binary (all-or-nothing) prisoner's dilemma, not a continuous public goods game; results may not generalize to continuous settings.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be inferred from this study, only on cooperation rates.
- The sanctioning institution is used infrequently, and its direct effect on payoffs is small.
- The study population is Tanzanian fishermen; external validity to other populations or contexts may be limited.
