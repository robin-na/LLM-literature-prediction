# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishing free-riders: How group size affects mutual monitoring and the provision of public goods
Authors: Carpenter, J. P.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution rates
- punishment expenditures
- punishment received
- change in contributions after punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates, punishment expenditures, and behavioral responses to punishment. Group payoff, efficiency, or welfare are not directly reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that allowing punishment in public goods games sustains high contribution rates, especially when participants can monitor and punish a larger fraction of the group. Increasing group size from 5 to 10 does not reduce contributions and may even increase them, contrary to standard theory. However, when the ability to monitor/punish is restricted (e.g., Single monitoring), contributions fall to levels similar to the no-punishment control. The effect of punishment is thus strongly moderated by the monitoring technology. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the high contribution rates in the Full and Half monitoring conditions suggest that efficiency would be higher with punishment enabled, especially when monitoring is not restricted.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, enabling punishment (especially with broad monitoring) increases contribution rates, and likely efficiency, relative to control. The effect is robust to group size (5 vs 10) and is strongly moderated by the monitoring fraction: punishment is only effective when players can monitor/punish at least half the group. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, predictions about efficiency must be inferred from contribution rates. The findings are most informative for settings where the main outcome of interest is contribution/cooperation, and less directly for efficiency or total payoff prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size was manipulated between 5 and 10. Larger groups did not have lower contributions; in some cases, contributions were higher in larger groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session lasted 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions were continuous (0-20 francs), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR was manipulated: 0.375 (low) and 0.75 (high). Higher MPCR led to higher contributions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost was nonlinear: e.g., 1 point cost 1 franc, 2 points cost 2 francs, up to 10 points costing 30 francs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the target's gross earnings by 10%, up to 100%.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards are discussed in literature review but not implemented in this experiment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told there would be 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw the individual contributions of all group members and a summary of their own and group earnings after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants saw how many points they received but not from whom.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; all main results are based on contribution rates and punishment behavior.
- Efficiency effects of punishment must be inferred from contribution rates, not directly measured.
- The punishment mechanism is nonlinear and may not generalize to all public goods game designs.
- The experiment uses a 'strangers' protocol (random reassignment each round), which may limit generalizability to repeated fixed groups.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
