# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment in a regulatory setting: Experimental evidence from the VCM
Authors: Anderson, L. R., & Stafford, S. L.
Journal: Journal of Regulatory Economics
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- group contribution
- compliance (contribution to public good)
- response to punishment probability and severity
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group contribution (amount contributed to the public good). The paper does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment, though it does describe the payoff structure and reports average earnings across all treatments. The analysis and regressions focus on contribution behavior, not on efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that introducing probabilistic punishment for free riding in a public goods game increases group contributions, and that increasing the expected cost of punishment (probability × severity) further increases compliance. Punishment severity has a larger marginal effect on contribution than punishment probability. In repeated games, past punishment can actually decrease subsequent compliance, possibly due to a 'lightning doesn't strike twice' belief. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than welfare or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard VCM public goods game, introducing and increasing the expected cost of externally imposed punishment increases contribution rates. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The findings are highly relevant for predicting the direction of contribution changes when punishment is enabled, and suggest that both punishment probability and severity matter, with severity being more influential. For efficiency prediction, the results imply that efficiency is likely to increase with punishment (since contributions increase), but the magnitude and any possible over-punishment effects on group payoff are not quantified.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 10 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject makes 20 allocation decisions (scenarios). In the One-Time treatment, all 20 are made at once; in the Repeated treatment, 20 rounds are played sequentially.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; subjects are seated in cubicles and instructed not to discuss decisions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects can allocate any amount (in dollars) from their $5 endowment between the group and private account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.20 (group account is doubled and split among 10 players: w=2/10=0.2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is externally imposed; cost to the punisher is zero (punishment is not peer-administered). The cost to the punished is proportional to the amount kept, multiplied by severity. The effect of increasing expected punishment cost (probability × severity) is to increase contributions.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via severity (multiplier on amount kept) and probability. Higher severity (effectiveness per unit) increases contributions more than higher probability.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 20 rounds/scenarios in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Repeated treatment, after each round, subjects are told the total group account amount and who has been selected for punishment. In the One-Time treatment, no feedback is given during decision-making.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Repeated treatment, subjects are told which player letters have been selected for punishment (identification letters are used).

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment; all main results are in terms of contribution rates.
- The experiment uses a laboratory student sample, which may not generalize to real-world regulatory settings.
- Punishment is externally imposed and costless to the punisher, which differs from peer punishment or endogenous sanctioning mechanisms.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency or welfare is not analyzed; possible negative effects of over-punishment on group payoff are not addressed.
- The study does not explore the long-term or dynamic effects of punishment beyond the 20-round experimental horizon.
