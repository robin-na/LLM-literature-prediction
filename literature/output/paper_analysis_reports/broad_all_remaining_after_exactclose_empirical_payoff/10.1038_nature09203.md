Outcome notes:
Theoretical and simulation results focus on the stationary distribution of strategies and the resulting average payoffs/incomes, as well as the stability of cooperation and punishment regimes. The paper explicitly discusses efficiency trade-offs between peer- and pool-punishment, and the effect of second-order punishment on group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both peer- and pool-punishment can emerge in voluntary public goods games, but their efficiency and stability differ. Peer-punishment is more efficient (higher average income) when second-order punishment is absent, but is less stable due to vulnerability to second-order free-riders. Pool-punishment, especially with second-order punishment, is more stable but incurs higher costs, leading to lower average group payoff. The model predicts that the introduction of punishment can increase cooperation but often reduces overall efficiency due to the costs of sanctioning, particularly for institutionalized (pool) punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed: it can stabilize cooperation but at a cost to total group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the type of punishment (peer vs. pool), the presence of second-order punishment, and whether participation is voluntary. Punishment can increase cooperation but often reduces efficiency due to its costs, especially for pool-punishment. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, one should expect that enabling punishment may not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it if punishment is costly and/or institutionalized. The presence of second-order punishment and voluntary participation are key moderators. The findings are directly relevant for mapping design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, punishment tech, player count, voluntary participation) to expected efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model and simulations use N=5 for group size in main figures, but the model is general for N >= 2.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Theoretical model is evolutionary and does not have explicit rounds, but the paper discusses that in repeated/longer games, peer-punishment can become cost-free and efficiency increases.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a binary choice: each player decides whether to contribute a fixed amount c > 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N; main figures use r=3, N=5, so MPCR=0.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Peer-punishment: cost per punishment is gamma=0.7; pool-punishment: cost to join pool is G=0.7. High punishment costs reduce efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer-punishment: fine per punisher is beta=0.7; pool-punishment: fine per pool-punisher is B=0.7. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical; results may not fully capture real human behavior.
- No explicit time horizon or finite rounds; evolutionary dynamics may differ from finite repeated games.
- No modeling of communication, reputation, or antisocial punishment.
- Assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contribution decisions.
- Does not specify how pool-punishment institutions are actually set up in practice.
- Assumes self-interested agents; does not model preferences for reciprocity or equity.
- No analysis of reward mechanisms.
- Findings may not generalize to compulsory participation settings.
