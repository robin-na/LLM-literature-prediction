Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the average payoff (social optimality), the state of the common resource (n), and the composition of the population (cooperators vs defectors). The paper explicitly analyzes when the system achieves the maximum possible group payoff (social optimum), and how this depends on the presence and intensity of punishment or reward.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both punishment and reward can prevent the tragedy of the commons in a dynamic resource game, but their effects on efficiency differ. Sufficiently strong punishment leads to a stable equilibrium with all cooperators and a fully replete resource, achieving the social optimum (maximum possible group payoff). In contrast, even with strong reward, some defectors persist, so the group does not reach the maximum efficiency. The model also shows that the effect of punishment or reward depends on the initial state and the intensity of the intervention, and that bistability (multiple possible long-run outcomes) can occur. The analysis is theoretical, using replicator dynamics and stability analysis, and is not based on empirical or experimental data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in dynamic resource games with feedback (and by extension, in public-goods-like environments), enabling punishment (with sufficient intensity) can increase group efficiency and achieve the social optimum, while enabling reward alone cannot fully eliminate inefficiency due to persistent defectors. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is a prisoner's dilemma with resource feedback, and where punishment or reward is implemented as a direct modification of payoffs. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment should be expected to increase efficiency more than enabling reward, especially when the goal is to reach the maximum possible group payoff. However, the model is theoretical and does not address practical implementation details or empirical constraints.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The population is assumed to be infinite and unstructured (well-mixed); results may not generalize to small groups or structured populations.
- Punishment and reward are modeled as direct modifications to payoffs, without explicit costs to the punisher or rewarder.
- The model uses continuous-time replicator dynamics, not discrete rounds as in most public goods experiments.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or linear public goods structure; the model is based on the prisoner's dilemma with resource feedback.
- No communication, information feedback, or identification of punishers/rewarders is modeled.
- The results depend on the assumption that punishment or reward can be made sufficiently strong; practical constraints on implementation are not addressed.
