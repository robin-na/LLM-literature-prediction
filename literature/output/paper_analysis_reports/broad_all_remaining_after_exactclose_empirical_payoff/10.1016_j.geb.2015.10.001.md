Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (group earnings relative to maximum possible), average earnings, and the frequency of mutual cooperation. Behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rate and punishment frequency are also reported and analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Allowing players to endogenously make themselves vulnerable to punishment (i.e., to enable the partner to punish them at a cost) increases cooperation and efficiency in a repeated prisoner's dilemma compared to a control with no punishment. The Gradual mechanism, where vulnerability can be increased stepwise and conditionally, leads to higher mutual cooperation and efficiency than the Leap mechanism, where vulnerability is chosen in one step. Efficiency gains are only observed when the vulnerability choice is endogenous and can be used as a signal; exogenous assignment of punishment possibilities does not improve and may even reduce efficiency. The effect is driven by the signaling value of voluntary vulnerability, not by the mere presence of punishment. The results suggest that the design of punishment mechanisms—especially the ability to signal intent and coordinate—critically moderates their effect on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling endogenous, voluntary punishment (where players can signal intent by making themselves vulnerable) increases efficiency in repeated prisoner's dilemma games compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is stronger when the mechanism allows gradual, conditional increases in vulnerability (i.e., more opportunity for signaling and coordination). The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of endogenous, voluntary punishment mechanisms—especially those allowing conditional, stepwise commitment—should be expected to yield higher efficiency than both no-punishment and exogenous punishment settings. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated prisoner's dilemma games, but the mechanism is adjacent to public goods games. The effect is contingent on the ability to signal intent; mere punishment possibility without signaling does not improve efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) prisoner's dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 50 rounds per session.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or direct communication allowed; only structured game actions and social history screen.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: Cooperate (C) or Defect (D).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 4 points per punishment; maximum possible punishment level is 50 points (set by the punished player).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact is set by the punished player's chosen vulnerability (0-50 points); cost to punisher is always 4 points per punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 50 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. A social history screen shows the 10 most recent completed rounds in the matching group, including possible punishment levels, actions, and punishment decisions (but not identities).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is pairwise and the punished player knows if the partner punished them, but identities are not revealed beyond the current pair.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a two-player prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to larger groups or standard PGGs.
- Punishment is endogenous and voluntary, with a unique signaling mechanism (vulnerability), which differs from standard exogenous punishment in PGGs.
- The social history screen provides rich feedback, which may not be present in other environments.
- No communication or chat is allowed; effects of communication are not tested.
- The design does not include a reward mechanism, so results are specific to punishment.
- The effect of group size, MPCR, and other PGG-specific parameters is not tested.
