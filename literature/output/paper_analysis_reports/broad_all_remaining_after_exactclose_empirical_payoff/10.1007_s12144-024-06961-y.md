# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The influence of social comparison on third-party altruistic behavior
Authors: Wang, X. M., Zhu, H. J., Zhao, Q. Y., & Wang, Y. R.
Journal: Current Psychology
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment rate
- compensation rate
- punishment amount (cost paid by third party)
- compensation amount (cost paid by third party)
- anger (moral emotion)
- empathy (moral emotion)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the rates and amounts of third-party punishment and compensation, as well as self-reported anger and empathy. No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. The 'amount' variables refer to the cost paid by the third party, not the net group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the direction of social comparison strongly influences whether third parties choose to punish violators or compensate victims in dictator-game scenarios. Upward comparison (feeling worse off) increases punishment, mediated by anger, while downward comparison (feeling better off) increases compensation, mediated by empathy. Public contexts increase the amount invested in compensation (especially for downward comparison), but do not change the basic choice pattern. The study does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so it does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about psychological and contextual moderators of third-party punishment and compensation in dictator-game settings, but does not report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. The findings are relevant for predicting when punishment or compensation will be chosen and how much will be invested, depending on social comparison and context. However, because the design is a third-party dictator game (not a public goods game) and does not report group efficiency or total payoff, it does not directly support prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. The evidence is most useful for understanding behavioral and emotional drivers of punishment/compensation, not for quantitative efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The dictator game task involves three roles: dictator (A), recipient (B), and third party (C, the participant). All studies use this three-player structure.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes decisions in three unfair distribution scenarios (6:4, 7:3, 8:2 splits), so there are three rounds per participant.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is allowed or mentioned; all decisions are made individually.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (punishment/compensation) is continuous: participants can choose any amount up to their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and compensation have a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio: for every 1 unit spent, the target (dictator or recipient) gains/loses 3 units.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 unit per 3 units deducted from the dictator.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (impact:cost).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation (rewarding the victim) is available as an alternative to punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation cost is 1 unit per 3 units added to the recipient.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation effectiveness is 3:1 (impact:cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are three rounds (three unfair allocations) in the task.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a third-party dictator game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant, so findings may not generalize to multi-player cooperation dilemmas.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual third-party behavioral and emotional outcomes are measured.
- The experimental paradigm focuses on fairness in economic distribution, not on repeated interaction or group cooperation.
- The public/anonymous context manipulation involves public posting of decisions, not direct peer observation or identification.
- The sample is limited to university students from a single university and cultural context.
- The study does not address the bystander effect or situations with multiple third parties.
- Generalizability to real-world or more complex social dilemmas is limited.
