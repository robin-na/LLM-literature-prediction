# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Palatable punishment in real-world social dilemmas? Punishing others to increase cooperation among the unpunished
Authors: Loukopoulos, P., Eek, D., Gärling, T., & Fujii, S.
Journal: Journal of Applied Social Psychology
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- choice between cooperation and defection
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate of cooperation (i.e., the percentage of participants choosing the cooperative option). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported, though the payoff structure is described and the link between cooperation and group earnings is discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that intermittent punishment (where only some group members are punished for defection in a given round) increases cooperation rates among those not directly punished, compared to a no-punishment baseline. This 'spillover effect' is robust to changes in the framing of the decision (individual vs. group focus) and to information about prior cooperation by others. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups are structured as six-person groups in the experiment, with participants always assigned to position 4 in the sequence.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant made 32 decisions (8 conditions x 4 repetitions), but each decision is a one-shot choice in a sequential protocol, not a repeated round-robin game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; decisions are made privately in computer booths.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: Option A (defection) or Option B (cooperation).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff structure is described: if all cooperate, each gets SEK 5,500; if one defects, defector gets SEK 7,000, others get SEK 5,000; if all defect, each gets SEK 4,500. This is not a standard linear PGG, but a sequential social dilemma with a stepwise payoff structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a cost for defection (SEK 1,000 or SEK 5,000) imposed on either the participant or another group member. The cost is not paid by another player, but is a fee imposed by the system.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective in that it increases cooperation rates when imposed, even when only some group members are punished (intermittent punishment).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of decision tasks (32), and each is a one-shot decision in a known sequence.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the choices of previous group members (sequential protocol), so they have summary information about prior cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff; no direct analysis of efficiency or welfare is reported.
- The game is a sequential social dilemma with a specific payoff structure, not a standard linear public goods game.
- Punishment is implemented as an externally imposed cost (fee), not as peer punishment or costly punishment by other players.
- Sample size is modest (48 participants), and all are university students.
- The experiment uses hypothetical payoffs, though bonuses are linked to performance.
- No analysis of heterogeneity in responses or subgroup effects beyond the main manipulations.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward-punishment interactions.
