## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by 5 players (focal player plus 4 neighbors) on a 100x100 spatial lattice. The local group size is always 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 100,000 Monte Carlo time steps, with results averaged over the last 10,000. The number of rounds is large and fixed.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed; players only know their own payoff each round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: players choose among pure strategies (cooperate, defect, punish, or abstain as loner).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. mpcr is r/N, with r varied (e.g., r=2.1, 2.3, 2.6, 3.0, 4.4, 4.7) and N=5. The effect of punishment is especially strong at low r (low mpcr).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (γ) is directly manipulated (e.g., γ=1, 0.5, 0.3, 0.1). Lowering γ increases the positive effect of punishment on efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (β) is fixed at 1.2 in most simulations; the effect of varying cost at fixed effectiveness is the main focus.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is mentioned in the introduction as a possible mechanism, but not implemented or analyzed in the model.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds is fixed and known in the simulation context.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players only know their own payoff; no information about others' contributions or payoffs is available.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical/simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes spatial structure (square lattice) and local interactions, which may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.
- Punishment is implemented as a fixed reduction in payoff with a fixed cost; real-world punishment may be more complex.
- Players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, which may not reflect human learning or imitation.
- No reward mechanism is implemented, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The model uses binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
