# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputation-based reciprocity in human-bot and human-human networks
Authors: Harrell, A., & Traeger, M. L.
Journal: Pnas Nexus
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- token-giving (number of tokens sent to alter)
- binary giving decision (give or not give token)
- perceptions of deserving help
- concern for reputation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: number of tokens given (continuous or binary), and survey measures of reputation concern and perceived deservingness. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The presence of bots in indirect reciprocity networks reduces human generosity, even when bots are programmed to be strong reciprocators. This reduction is driven by humans' reluctance to help bots, due to weaker norms about bots deserving help, not by a lack of reputational concern. Both 'reputational giving' (giving when observed by a third party) and 'rewarding reputations' (helping those with a reputation for helping) are less effective when bots are present. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the reduction in giving implies lower group welfare in hybrid networks compared to human-only networks.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of non-human agents (bots) in indirect reciprocity games reduces generosity and, by implication, group welfare. However, it does not manipulate or analyze punishment, nor does it report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. For prediction tasks about punishment effects on efficiency, this paper is only adjacent: it suggests that social context (e.g., presence of bots) can moderate the effectiveness of reciprocity-based mechanisms, but does not provide direct evidence about punishment or efficiency. The design and findings may be informative for understanding how social norms and recipient identity affect cooperation, but not for quantitative prediction of punishment effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chain reciprocity study: 4 agents per network (2 humans, 2 bots). One-shot giving: dyadic or triadic interactions, but always with a mix of humans and bots.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chain reciprocity: 11 rounds (intended 12, but valid data for 11). One-shot giving: 16 rounds (4 control, 12 RBR).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chain reciprocity: contribution is continuous (0-10 tokens). One-shot giving: binary (all-or-nothing, give or keep 1 token).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Tokens sent to another are doubled (multiplier = 2). In chain reciprocity, 4 players: MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5. In one-shot giving, dyadic: MPCR = 2/2 = 1.0.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is endogenous: participants can reward others by giving tokens, but there is no explicit reward mechanism (i.e., no separate costly reward action).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding (giving) costs the giver the token(s) they send; cost is 1 token per unit in one-shot, 1 per token in chain reciprocity.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token given is doubled for the recipient (reward impact per unit cost = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told in advance how many rounds there would be in the chain reciprocity study. In the one-shot giving study, all rounds are one-shot and order is randomized.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw what their alter had given to someone else before making their own decision, but did not see full group summaries or payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on indirect reciprocity (reputation-based giving) rather than punishment.
- No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; only behavioral giving outcomes are reported.
- The design is a close variant of public goods/indirect reciprocity games, but not a standard PGG or punishment game.
- Bots are programmed as strong reciprocators; results may differ with other bot strategies.
- Networks are small and fixed; real-world networks may be larger and dynamic.
- Participants are not told what happens to tokens given to bots, which may affect giving behavior.
