# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishing free riders: direct and indirect promotion of cooperation
Authors: Shinada, M., & Yamagishia, T.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount spent on punishment by third-party observers
- distribution of punishment across in-group and out-group cheaters
- cooperation rate in gift-giving game
- emotional responses (anger, unfairness, guilt) correlated with punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount of money spent on punishment (by third-party observers) and the distribution of this punishment toward in-group vs. out-group cheaters. The paper also reports on cooperation rates in the gift-giving game and emotional correlates of punishment. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that third-party punishment is group-based: cooperators in the gift-giving game punish in-group cheaters more severely than out-group cheaters, while noncooperators show the opposite pattern. Most punishment is directed at the least cooperative group members. Emotional responses (anger, unfairness, guilt) are correlated with punishment of in-group cheaters but not out-group cheaters. The design uses a gift-giving game with a public-goods-like structure, but does not directly measure or report group efficiency or total payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the behavioral allocation of punishment in group-structured social exchange games, especially regarding in-group vs. out-group targeting. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The design and findings are most useful for understanding the social and motivational determinants of punishment, not its impact on group welfare or efficiency. The punishment mechanism (costly to punisher, 3x impact on target) and the absence of feedback or repeated interaction are relevant for mapping to other designs, but the lack of efficiency data limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session is framed as 8 players (2 groups of 4), but only one is a real participant; the rest are simulated. The group size for the gift-giving game is 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. There are three rounds: two as trader (gift-giving), one as observer (punisher). The number of rounds is not known to participants until the last round.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (gift-giving) is continuous: traders choose how much of their 200 yen endowment to give (any amount from 0 to 200).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each yen given is doubled for the recipient (multiplier = 2). With 4 players, the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 for the recipient, but the structure is not a standard PGG; it's a chain-generalized exchange.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 yen to reduce the target's earnings by 3 yen (cost to punisher per unit = 1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 yen spent by punisher reduces target's earnings by 3 yen (3:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there will be several rounds, but only learn at the third round that it is the last round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Observers are shown how much each trader gave in the round. Traders are informed of the amount given by another trader in their group at the end of each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; traders are not informed until the end of all rounds whether they received punishment, and no information about the punisher's identity is given.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not report or analyze group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main results are behavioral (punishment allocation, cooperation rates, emotional correlates).
- The game is a gift-giving chain exchange, not a standard public goods game; mapping to PGGs is indirect.
- Only one real participant per session; all other players are simulated, which may affect ecological validity.
- Punishment is one-shot and cannot affect future behavior or reputation, limiting generalizability to repeated or strategic settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present, so findings are limited to punishment.
