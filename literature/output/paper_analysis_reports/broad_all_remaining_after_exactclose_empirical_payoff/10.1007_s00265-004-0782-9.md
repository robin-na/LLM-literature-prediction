# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strategic investment in reputation
Authors: Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M.
Journal: Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per player per round
- group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (proportion of players contributing in each round)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average payoff per player and cooperation rate. Payoff is directly reported and analyzed as a function of the experimental manipulation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment demonstrates that the possibility of reputation transfer between a public goods game and an indirect reciprocity game leads to higher cooperation and significantly higher average payoffs in the public goods game. When players' identities are consistent across both games (transferable name), they invest more in cooperation, resulting in higher group earnings. When identities are not transferable, cooperation and payoffs decline. The manipulation of reputation transferability is the key treatment; no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. The findings show that reputation incentives can substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that enabling reputation transfer between a public goods game and another social context (indirect reciprocity game) increases efficiency, as measured by average payoff per player. While the study does not manipulate punishment, it is highly informative for prediction tasks involving reputation mechanisms as an alternative to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds, information structure) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcomes are directly reported. For prediction, this paper supports the view that mechanisms increasing the salience or transferability of reputation can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, even in the absence of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main test phase consisted of 15 rounds (rounds 11-25), with additional introductory rounds. Players did not know the total number of rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was binary: each player could contribute €1.25 or not ('yes' or 'no' button).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each €1.25 contributed was doubled and divided among 6 players: MPCR = 2/6 = 0.333.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Indirect reciprocity game allowed players to reward others (donate €1.25 to give €2.00 to another). This is not a reward in the public goods game itself, but in the linked IR game.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the IR game, the cost to the donor was €1.25 per donation.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each €1.25 donated in the IR game gave €2.00 to the receiver (effectiveness = 1.6 per unit cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were not informed about the total number of rounds or the sequence of game types.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players could observe the complete history of the game (decisions, costs, and payoffs) on a large screen, but only under pseudonyms.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism was present; results pertain to reputation and indirect reciprocity, not punishment.
- The reputation effect is tested via linkage to an indirect reciprocity game, not within a single public goods game.
- The experiment uses pseudonyms and artificial identities, which may differ from real-world reputation dynamics.
- Players did not know the total number of rounds, which may affect endgame behavior.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment or to larger or smaller group sizes.
