# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: NORMS OF PUNISHMENT: EXPERIMENTS WITH STUDENTS AND THE GENERAL POPULATION
Authors: Bortolotti, S., Casari, M., & Pancotto, F.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average per-capita earnings (reported in Euros)
- per-period profit (tokens earned per period, Table 3, Result 2, Result 1)
- earnings drop from PGG-Standard to PGG-Punishment (Result 2)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- cooperation rate
- punishment assigned
- conditional cooperation
- prosocial vs antisocial punishment
- reaction to punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels and punishment behavior. Payoff-related outcomes (earnings, profit) are reported but not analyzed as primary outcomes. Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible) is not directly reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The opportunity for peer punishment increases cooperation among students but not among the general population. In the student sample, average contributions rise substantially when punishment is available (from 6.8 to 12.8 out of 20), while in the representative sample, contributions are slightly lower with punishment (from 9.1 to 8.9). The general population punishes as much or more than students, but punishment is more often directed at cooperators (antisocial punishment), and recipients are less responsive to punishment. As a result, punishment fails to increase cooperation or efficiency in the general population. Earnings per period decrease with punishment in both groups, but more so in the representative sample. The study highlights that the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency is highly dependent on the subject pool, with punishment only improving cooperation (and likely efficiency) among students.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated PGGs depends critically on the subject pool. In a standard 4-player, 8-round PGG with MPCR=0.5 and a punishment technology of 1 token cost to reduce 4 tokens from another, punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) among students but not among a representative general population. The main moderator is the prevalence of antisocial punishment and the responsiveness to punishment. For prediction, if the game is run with a student sample, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency; with a general population sample, punishment may not improve and could even reduce efficiency due to antisocial punishment and lack of response. The paper does not report efficiency directly, but the pattern of contributions and earnings suggests these conclusions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group had 4 players (N=4) in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG variant (with and without punishment) was played for 8 rounds, for a total of 16 rounds per subject.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were discrete but not all-or-nothing: subjects could choose to contribute 0, 6, 14, or 20 tokens out of 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (marginal per capita return) was 0.5 (a=0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 token per deduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point cost 1 token to the punisher and reduced the target's earnings by 4 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the number of rounds in advance (8 rounds per PGG variant).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects could observe individual contributions and earnings for each group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher's identity was not revealed; punishment was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible) is not directly reported or analyzed; only average earnings and contributions are provided.
- The order of games was not counterbalanced: all subjects played the no-punishment PGG first, then the punishment PGG, so order effects cannot be ruled out.
- The main outcome is contribution/cooperation, not efficiency or welfare; payoff outcomes are secondary.
- The study is limited to one region in Italy and may not generalize to other cultures or societies.
- The general population sample is stratified but not fully random; some non-students are present in the student sample.
- The punishment mechanism is fixed (1:4 cost:impact), so results may not generalize to other punishment technologies.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward or combined reward-punishment systems.
