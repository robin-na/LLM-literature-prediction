Outcome notes:
The main outcome is 'average group achievement' (η_G), which is the average fraction of groups that succeed in the collective-risk dilemma. This is closely related to efficiency, as it reflects the probability of group success and thus the preservation of the public good. Individual expected payoff (fitness) is also analyzed. Non-payoff outcomes include the prevalence of cooperation and the stationary distribution of population states.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in collective-risk dilemmas (threshold public goods games with risk of group failure), both reward and punishment policies improve group achievement (η_G) compared to no policy. Reward is particularly effective at initiating cooperation when the risk of failure is low, while punishment is more effective at sustaining high levels of cooperation, especially when the risk is high or the threshold for group success is stringent. The optimal policy is to use reward when the fraction of cooperators is low and switch to punishment when cooperation is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit formulas for the optimal switching point and shows that combining both policies synergistically yields the highest group achievement. The results are robust across a range of parameter values, including group size, population size, and policy efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (and/or reward) in threshold public goods games will increase group achievement (a proxy for efficiency) compared to a control without such mechanisms. The model allows for direct mapping of game design dimensions such as player count, group size, number of rounds (in evolutionary time), presence and efficiency of punishment and reward, and the structure of the public goods dilemma (threshold, risk). The findings suggest that punishment is most effective at maintaining cooperation once it is established, while reward is more important for initiating cooperation. The optimal policy is a dynamic combination of both. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that adding punishment (especially with sufficient budget and efficiency) will generally increase efficiency, but the effect size and optimality depend on the risk, threshold, and whether reward is also available. The model is theoretical, so empirical calibration may be needed for quantitative predictions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes institutional (not peer-to-peer) implementation of reward and punishment, with costs and impacts coming from a collective budget.
- The outcome measure (average group achievement, η_G) is a proxy for efficiency but is not identical to total group payoff or surplus.
- The model uses an evolutionary process rather than discrete rounds, which may differ from typical experimental public goods games.
- No consideration of communication, individual-level punishment/reward decisions, or information feedback to players.
- Assumes perfect implementation of policies and does not model enforcement difficulties or real-world frictions.
