The paper is a theoretical and comparative review. It discusses mechanisms (punishment, reward, reputation, ostracism) that maintain cooperation, especially in humans, and references experimental findings from the literature. It does not report new empirical data or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes. The focus is on behavioral mechanisms and evolutionary explanations.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that humans differ from other animals in their use of active enforcement mechanisms—especially punishment, reward, and reputation—to maintain cooperation and control free-riders. It highlights that in laboratory public goods games, punishment increases cooperation, but also notes the second-order dilemma of costly punishment. The review discusses the cognitive and social information mechanisms that enable these enforcement strategies in humans. However, it does not provide new empirical results or analyze efficiency or group payoff as outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative support for the idea that punishment mechanisms increase cooperation in public goods game-like environments, referencing experimental findings from the literature. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff effects, nor does it analyze how game design dimensions moderate these effects. It is useful for understanding the mechanisms and evolutionary context of punishment, but offers only indirect support for predicting efficiency outcomes from design features.
