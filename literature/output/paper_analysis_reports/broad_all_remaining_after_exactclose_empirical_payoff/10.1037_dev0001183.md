# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Third-Party Punishment Promotes Fairness in Children
Authors: Martin, J. W., Martin, S., & McAuliffe, K.
Journal: Developmental Psychology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of fair choices (choosing the fairer allocation in a forced-choice dictator game)
- references to fairness or personal benefit in free-response justifications
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the proportion of trials in which the participant chooses the fairer allocation. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. The study focuses on fairness behavior, not on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Third-party punishment (costly, by an adult experimenter) increases the rate at which younger children (ages 6-7) choose the fairer allocation in a forced-choice dictator game, compared to both a baseline and a reputation-only (observer) condition. The effect is not present in older children (ages 8-9), who already show high rates of fair choices. The mechanism appears to be direct feedback from punishment, not reputational concern. The study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, and the design is a dictator game rather than a public goods game or its direct variant.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that third-party punishment can increase norm-compliant (fair) behavior in a resource allocation game among children, especially those in a developmental window where fairness norms are emerging. However, the outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, and the game is a forced-choice dictator game, not a public goods game or a repeated social dilemma. Thus, the findings are only indirectly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The main value is as adjacent evidence that punishment can shape norm compliance in allocation tasks, especially for younger participants, but it does not provide direct evidence for efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a two-player forced-choice dictator game (one allocator, one recipient), with a third-party (experimenter) as observer/punisher in some conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 6 forced-choice trials.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication between participant and third party or recipient; participants and experimenters were told not to speak during the game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocation is forced-choice between two discrete distributions (e.g., 3/3 vs 4/2), not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly to the punisher (experimenter): tokens placed in the red box cannot be exchanged for Skittles. The exact cost per punishment is not numerically specified, but the cost is real and salient.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment removes the participant's tokens for that trial (participant receives nothing; recipient still receives their share). The impact is total loss of the participant's allocation for that trial.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the structure of the game and complete a fixed number of 6 trials; the number of rounds is known.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always administered by an identified adult experimenter (not anonymous).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a forced-choice dictator game, not a public goods game or repeated social dilemma, so findings are only adjacent to PGG environments.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings outcomes are reported; only behavioral (fairness) outcomes are measured.
- Punishment is administered by an adult experimenter, not by peers or group members, which may limit generalizability.
- The sample is children aged 6-9, so developmental effects may not generalize to adults.
- The number of rounds is small (6), and the effect of punishment is most pronounced in younger children.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The study does not manipulate or report on marginal per-capita return (mpcr) or other key PGG parameters.
