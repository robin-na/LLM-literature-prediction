# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reputation and social (dis)approval in feedback mechanisms: An experimental study
Authors: Lumeau, M., Masclet, D., & Penard, T.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount sent by Player A (proxy for trust)
- amount returned by Player B (proxy for trustworthiness)
- return rate of Player B
- group earnings (implied, not directly reported as efficiency)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and valence of ratings assigned
- impact of received ratings on subsequent behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: amount sent (trust) and return rate (trustworthiness). Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, but higher trust and trustworthiness imply higher group earnings. The study does not use the term 'efficiency' in the PGG sense, but discusses improvement in cooperation and market efficiency in a general sense.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both private and public feedback mechanisms increase trust (amount sent) and trustworthiness (return rate) in a repeated trust game compared to a baseline with no feedback. Public feedback (where ratings are observable to future partners) is more effective than private feedback (where ratings are only seen by the recipient) in increasing these behaviors. The difference is attributed to both a higher frequency of ratings and a stronger behavioral response to public ratings. When a cost is introduced for assigning ratings, the effects persist but are weaker, especially for private feedback. The study concludes that reputation mechanisms (public feedback) are essential for sustaining high levels of cooperation and implied efficiency in environments with imperfect information.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a feedback mechanism (a form of non-monetary sanction or reward) increases cooperation and implied efficiency in trust-game-like environments. Publicly observable feedback (reputation) is more effective than private feedback (social (dis)approval only). The results are robust to the introduction of a small cost for assigning feedback. For prediction tasks, the presence and observability of feedback mechanisms should be expected to increase efficiency, with public/reputational mechanisms having a larger effect than private ones. However, the results are from a trust game, not a PGG, so direct quantitative transfer to PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative direction is likely similar.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 8-10 subjects, always paired into groups of 2 (Player A and Player B) for the trust game. The game is strictly dyadic.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 20 periods (rounds) of the trust game.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; standard trust game protocol implies no chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (amount sent) is continuous: Player A chooses any amount from 0 to 10 units.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Amount sent by A is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is a trust game, not a PGG. No per-capita return as in PGG; the multiplier is 3 for the recipient.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In main treatments, assigning a rating (positive or negative) is costless. In a variant, assigning a rating costs 1 unit. The cost reduces the frequency and impact of ratings, but public feedback remains more effective.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/reward is non-monetary: a rating of +1 or -1, with impact via social (dis)approval (private) or reputation (public). Public feedback is more effective than private, indicating higher 'punishment tech' when observable.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Both positive (+1) and negative (-1) ratings are available; thus, both reward and punishment exist.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost structure is the same for reward and punishment: costless in main treatments, 1 unit cost in variant.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is a +1 rating, with impact via social approval (private) or reputation (public). Public feedback increases the effectiveness of reward.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is finitely repeated for 20 periods; subjects are informed of the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In Public Feedback, players see their partner's feedback profile (history of ratings and cumulative score). In Private Feedback, only the recipient sees their own ratings. No other summary information is provided.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game, not a public goods game; results may not transfer quantitatively to PGGs.
- Efficiency is not directly measured or reported; only behavioral proxies (trust, trustworthiness) are analyzed.
- Group size is always 2 (dyadic), not larger groups as in PGGs.
- No outside option or partner choice; all matches are random and compulsory.
- Feedback is non-monetary (ratings), not direct monetary punishment or reward.
- No direct measure of group payoff or efficiency ratio; conclusions about efficiency are inferred from behavioral outcomes.
