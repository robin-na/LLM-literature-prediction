Outcome notes:
The model tracks the evolution and equilibrium frequencies of altruistic and selfish strategies, including punishers and nonpunishers, under varying power asymmetries. While group-level productivity and average fitness are discussed, the main reported outcomes are the frequencies of behavioral strategies (altruism, punishment, selfishness) and the maintenance of altruism. Efficiency is discussed in terms of group productivity and the ability to maintain high levels of altruism, but explicit efficiency ratios (payoff relative to full cooperation) are not directly reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that punishment, whether selfish or altruistic, can sustain altruism in public goods games, but the efficiency of this outcome is contingent on power dynamics. Selfish punishment by a powerful minority can prevent the collapse into pure selfishness when power is highly asymmetric, but this comes at the cost of reduced group productivity compared to scenarios where altruistic punishment prevails. When power is more evenly distributed, altruistic punishment dominates, leading to higher levels of altruism and group efficiency. The model also shows that if selfish punishment is not possible, populations are more vulnerable to a collapse into selfishness under power asymmetry. Thus, the presence of punishment generally increases group-level cooperation and productivity, but the efficiency gain is maximized when punishment is altruistic and power is symmetric.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the symmetry and distribution of power. In environments with high power asymmetry, punishment may maintain some cooperation but at a lower efficiency due to the persistence of selfish punishers. In more egalitarian settings, punishment is more likely to be altruistic, leading to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with symmetric power and distributed punishment opportunities will see greater efficiency gains from punishment, while those with concentrated or asymmetric power may see only partial gains or even efficiency losses if selfish punishment dominates. The model also highlights the importance of the severity of punishment consequences and the ability to exclude selfish punishers in determining efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and agent-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Efficiency is discussed in terms of group productivity and average fitness, but explicit efficiency ratios (payoff relative to full cooperation) are not directly reported.
- The model assumes all-or-nothing contributions and does not allow for partial contributions.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included.
- Power asymmetry is modeled as a random assignment each generation, which may not capture all real-world dynamics.
- Findings may not generalize to empirical settings without further validation.
