# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: From spontaneous cooperation to spontaneous punishment - Distinguishing the underlying motives driving spontaneous behavior in first and second order public good games
Authors: Mischkowski, D., Glöckner, A., & Lewisch, P.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment investments (amount spent to punish)
- frequency of punishment
- contribution amount
- decision time for punishment and contribution
- affective states (negative affect, anger, upset)
- social value orientation (SVO)
- above-average contribution status
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment investments (amount spent to punish), decision time, and contribution amount. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. Payoff-related outcomes are not analyzed; the focus is on behavioral and psychological variables.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in both one-shot and iterated public goods games with a punishment option, individuals who decide to punish quickly invest more in punishment, especially if they contributed more than their peers and feel highly upset. The spontaneous punishment effect is not moderated by dispositional pro-sociality (SVO), but by situational factors (above-average contribution and negative affect). The study does not analyze or report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs, so it does not provide evidence on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides detailed evidence on the psychological and situational determinants of punishment behavior in public goods games, but does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. However, it does confirm that punishment is used more by above-average contributors and those experiencing negative affect, and that the cost-to-impact ratio of punishment is 1:4. The game design is a standard PGG with continuous contributions, four players, two rounds (Study 1) or one round (Study 2), no chat, and full information about others' contributions. The absence of efficiency or payoff analysis limits the paper's direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 2 rounds (iterated PGG); Study 2: 1 round (one-shot PGG).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-400 cents in 2 cent units), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (contributions are doubled and split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1: for every 1 cent spent, the punished loses 4 cents (cost-to-impact ratio 1:4).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4:1 (1 cent spent reduces other's payoff by 4 cents).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed in advance about the number of rounds (2 in Study 1, 1 in Study 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, participants are shown the contributions of all other group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Individual identifiers are maintained after the first round for reputation building, but full anonymity is assured. It is not explicitly stated whether punished players know who punished them, but the design suggests punishment is not anonymous within the group.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze group efficiency, welfare, total payoffs, or surplus; only behavioral outcomes (punishment, contribution, decision time, affect) are analyzed.
- No direct evidence is provided on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency in public goods games.
- Causal interpretation of the relationship between decision time and punishment is cautioned against; decision conflict may confound the observed effects.
- Affective measures are taken after the punishment decision, which may introduce justification or consistency bias.
- The findings are limited to lab settings with student samples and may not generalize to field or real-world environments.
