## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that peer-policing can sustain high levels of honest reporting in a decentralized community if and only if punishment is opportunistic—i.e., if those who police and catch cheaters are rewarded. When the benefit of catching cheaters and the punishment for being caught are both high (with the benefit exceeding the punishment), the population stabilizes with a majority of honest reporters and a minority of policers and cheaters. If punishment is purely altruistic (costly to the punisher), honesty cannot be sustained and the population converges to all cheating. The model also shows that reducing the cost of policing (e.g., by rewarding replication) or increasing the punishment for cheating can further increase the prevalence of honesty. However, the model does not directly report on group efficiency or total welfare, focusing instead on the behavioral dynamics of honesty, policing, and cheating.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment can sustain prosocial behavior (honest reporting) in social dilemmas, but only if the incentive structure rewards punishers. While the model is not a direct public goods game, it is structurally similar and thus informative for predicting when punishment will be effective in PGG-like environments. The key implication is that punishment will only improve group outcomes (e.g., honesty, cooperation) if the cost-benefit structure makes policing individually rational—i.e., if the reward for punishing exceeds the cost and the punishment for being caught cheating exceeds the benefit of cheating. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency outcomes. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of the incentive structure for the effectiveness of punishment.
