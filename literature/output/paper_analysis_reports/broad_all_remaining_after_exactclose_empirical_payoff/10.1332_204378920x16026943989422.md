## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper synthesizes evidence from experimental and real-world studies showing that a variety of factors—including financial incentives, reciprocity, fairness, reputation, group identification, and social norms—can increase cooperation in public goods games and similar social dilemmas. Punishment of free-riding and reward of cooperation are identified as effective mechanisms for increasing cooperation, especially when they are costly to the punisher/rewarder and when implemented by long-term partners. The review also notes that communication, group identity, and information about others' behavior can further enhance cooperation. However, the paper does not provide quantitative or qualitative findings on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a high-level theoretical synthesis of factors that promote cooperation in public goods games, including the role of punishment. It is useful for identifying which game design dimensions are likely to influence cooperation rates, but it does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling punishment is likely to increase cooperation, especially under conditions of group identification, communication, and when punishment is costly to the punisher. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff analysis limits its value for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not present new empirical data or meta-analytic synthesis.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are not directly analyzed or reported; the focus is on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation and punishment.
- Evidence for some dimensions (e.g., group size, number of rounds) is described as weak or mixed.
- The review aggregates findings from many studies but does not provide quantitative effect sizes or direct predictions for efficiency outcomes.
- Some game design dimensions (e.g., all-or-nothing, default contribution, reward effectiveness) are not addressed.
