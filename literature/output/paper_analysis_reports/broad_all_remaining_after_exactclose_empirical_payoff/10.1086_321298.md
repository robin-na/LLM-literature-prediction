## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proves that in repeated games of enforceable cooperation, only social norms (strategies that prescribe third-party punishment) can be evolutionarily stable. The presence of effective punishment is necessary for stability. Both efficient and inefficient social norms can be stable, but efficient (cooperative) norms are more robust to invasion and require a lower frequency to stabilize. The model predicts that, over time, groups are more likely to drift toward efficient, cooperative equilibria regulated by social norms, but inefficient, punishment-backed equilibria are also possible and robust. The findings are general for two-person repeated games and extend to sparse networks, provided third-party information is sufficiently available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the necessity of punishment (sanctions) for stable cooperation and efficiency in repeated social dilemmas. It shows that the presence of punishment mechanisms is a necessary condition for the evolutionary stability of cooperative, efficient outcomes, but does not guarantee efficiency: inefficient, punishment-backed equilibria can also be stable. However, more efficient equilibria are more robust to invasion and require a lower threshold to stabilize. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in a repeated game with enforceable cooperation will generally increase the likelihood of stable cooperation and higher efficiency, but the actual efficiency achieved may depend on the specific norm that evolves. The robustness of efficient equilibria to invasion is higher, so in the long run, efficiency is more likely with punishment enabled, but inefficient outcomes are possible. The results are most directly applicable to two-person repeated games, but the logic extends to larger groups and sparse networks if third-party information is available.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The main results are for two-person repeated games; extension to N-person public goods games is discussed only briefly and not formally analyzed.
- The model assumes full information about others' actions (informationally dense networks); results may not hold in settings with sparse or delayed information.
- The results require that the future is sufficiently important (high continuation probability or discount factor); they may not apply to finitely repeated games or games with low delta.
- The model does not address the effects of communication (chat), reward mechanisms, or default contribution framing.
- The model assumes that the population is sufficiently cognitively sophisticated to implement social norms (relevant for humans, but not all animals).
- The results are equilibrium (long-run) predictions and may not describe short-run dynamics or out-of-equilibrium behavior.
