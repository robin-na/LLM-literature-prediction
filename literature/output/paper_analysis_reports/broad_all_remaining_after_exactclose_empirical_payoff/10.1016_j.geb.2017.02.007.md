# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: At-will relationships: How an option to walk away affects cooperation and efficiency
Authors: Wilson, A. J., & Wu, H.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- efficiency (normalized group payoff relative to full cooperation)
- average group payoff
- activity rate (proportion of rounds with active relationships)
- termination/dissolution rates
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- punishment/termination frequency
- strategy type (e.g., lenient, forgiving, termination-based)
- conditional response to history
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is efficiency, defined as the normalized group payoff relative to the fully cooperative benchmark. The paper also reports cooperation rates, punishment/termination rates, and strategy types, but the primary focus is on efficiency and group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Introducing a termination (walk-away) option in repeated PD games increases cooperation and efficiency when the outside option is low, as termination is rarely used and serves as a credible threat. However, as the outside option rises above the in-relationship minmax, subjects begin to use termination more frequently as a punishment, leading to a sharp drop in efficiency. If the outside option increases further, efficiency rises again, as the cost of termination is less severe. Asymmetric division rules for termination payoffs have dramatic effects: if the party who terminates gets the higher payoff, almost all relationships dissolve immediately, resulting in very low efficiency. If the more cooperative party is rewarded, cooperation and efficiency are maximized. The paper provides direct evidence on how the structure and value of punishment options (termination) affect efficiency in repeated social dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment (here, the option to terminate a relationship) on efficiency is highly sensitive to the value and distribution of the punishment's payoff. When punishment is costly (low outside option), its mere availability increases efficiency, but if it becomes too attractive (high outside option), it is overused and efficiency drops. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and value of the punishment option, the symmetry/asymmetry of its payoff, and the underlying game structure (here, repeated PD with imperfect monitoring). The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of enabling punishment depends non-monotonically on the cost/benefit of punishment, and that institutional details (who gets what on punishment/termination) can have very large effects. For prediction, the control efficiency (without punishment) and the specific design of the punishment option are critical moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player repeated prisoner's dilemma games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are indefinitely repeated with a stochastic end (delta=0.8), implemented as blocks of 5 rounds with a 1/5 chance of ending after each block. Average number of rounds per supergame is not fixed but is determined by the stochastic process.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (C, high effort) or defect (D, low effort).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but in the PD stage game, joint cooperation yields $1.47$ per player, joint defection $1.15$. The marginal return to cooperation is positive but less than 1 (not a linear PGG).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as the option to terminate the relationship, with the cost determined by the outside option value (termination payoff). When the outside option is low, punishment is costly; when high, it is less costly or even beneficial.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the termination payoff: low outside option makes punishment severe (costly to both), high outside option makes it less severe. The impact per unit cost is not directly parameterized but is implicit in the design.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds in advance; the game ends stochastically after each block of 5 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the public signal (success/failure) and their own payoff each round. The design involves imperfect public monitoring.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental environment is a repeated two-player prisoner's dilemma with imperfect public monitoring, not a standard public goods game; generalizability to larger groups or linear PGGs may be limited.
- Punishment is implemented as a unilateral termination (walk-away) option, not as targeted costly punishment as in standard PGG punishment treatments.
- The outside option (termination payoff) is exogenously set and does not involve rematching or endogenous partner selection, which may differ from some real-world or networked environments.
- The study does not include reward mechanisms, only punishment via termination.
- The design is limited to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices, not continuous contributions.
- Findings on efficiency are specific to the structure and value of the termination option; other forms of punishment may yield different results.
