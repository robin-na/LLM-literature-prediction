## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both keeping and reversing social norms can promote cooperation and increase social welfare (average payoff) in indirect reciprocity games with punishment. The effect is moderated by the benefit-cost ratio: keeping rules are more effective at low benefit-cost ratios, while reversing rules are more effective at high benefit-cost ratios. Public reputation scenarios consistently support higher cooperation and welfare than private reputation scenarios. Punishment actions can help sustain cooperation, but their cost can reduce average welfare. The specific social norm in use (among 16 studied) determines whether the population reaches a cooperative or non-cooperative stable state, especially in private reputation settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the design of social norms and the structure of reputation (public vs. private) are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency (average payoff) in indirect reciprocity games. The results suggest that, in environments similar to pairwise donation games with indirect reciprocity, enabling punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency, but the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost of punishment and the social norm in use. Public reputation and appropriate social norms (keeping or reversing, depending on benefit-cost ratio) are important for maximizing efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to pairwise donation games, not standard public goods games, but the mechanisms are adjacent and informative for prediction.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent participates in θ=20 rounds per time step; simulations run for 4000 generations. No variation or analysis of round number effects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: agents choose C (cooperate), D (defect), or P (punish). No continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Equivalent to benefit-cost ratio b/c. Simulations vary b and c (e.g., b=5, c=3; b=9, c=3). Higher b/c generally increases cooperation and average payoff in public reputation, but effect is norm-dependent in private reputation.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is α=1 in main simulations. Increasing punishment cost (not systematically varied) would reduce average welfare. Punishment is costly and can reduce average payoff even as it increases cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is β=4 (fine to recipient per unit cost α=1). β is varied in Fig. 5C-D. Higher β does not increase cooperation in public reputation, and may slightly decrease average payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a pairwise donation game with indirect reciprocity, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- All results are from theoretical modeling and agent-based simulation, not empirical or experimental data.
- Population size, number of rounds, and other parameters are fixed in simulations; effects of varying these are not analyzed.
- No communication, reward, or group-level public goods are modeled.
- Punishment is modeled as a costly action with fixed cost and effectiveness; real-world punishment may be more complex.
- The effect of social norms is only explored for a specific set of 16 second-order rules; higher-order norms and other mechanisms are not studied.
