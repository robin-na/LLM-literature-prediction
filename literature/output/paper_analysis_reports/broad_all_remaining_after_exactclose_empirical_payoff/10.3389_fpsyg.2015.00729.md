# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do we harm others even if we don't need to?
Authors: Cacault, M. P., Goette, L., Lalive, R., & Thoenig, M.
Journal: Frontiers in Psychology
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contributions to public good (Project A)
- contributions to anti-social options (Projects B and C)
- in-group cooperation
- out-group aggression
- motives for contribution choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contributions to various projects (pro-social, anti-social, or both). The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as a primary outcome, nor does it provide explicit efficiency ratios. The focus is on behavioral patterns and motives, not on aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when participants are given the option to harm an out-group (even at a cost or with no additional benefit to their own group), a significant minority choose to do so, and this behavior is often motivated by a desire to benefit the in-group at the expense of the out-group. The presence of anti-social options (aggression) increases non-selfish contributions and stabilizes in-group cooperation over repeated rounds, compared to a standard public good game where cooperation declines over time. The effect is especially pronounced in later rounds. However, the paper does not analyze or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so it is unclear whether the increased cooperation offsets the welfare losses from aggression. The findings are relevant for understanding the behavioral complementarity between in-group cooperation and out-group aggression, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the availability of anti-social options (aggression) can increase in-group cooperation and non-selfish contributions in a public good game variant, especially in later rounds. However, because the anti-social options reduce the payoffs of out-groups and the paper does not report group efficiency or total earnings, it is not possible to determine whether the net effect on efficiency is positive or negative. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper suggests that behavioral responses to punishment-like options can increase cooperation, but may also introduce welfare-reducing behaviors. The design is adjacent to standard punishment mechanisms (since aggression is not used to enforce norms but to harm others), so caution is needed in transferring these findings to standard PGG punishment settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 3 players each in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game was played for 6 periods (rounds) with the same group composition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication was allowed between participants; strict enforcement of non-communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: participants could allocate any amount of their 30 MUs to the available projects.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. For Project A and B, 1 MU contributed is doubled (2 MUs) and split among 3 players, so MPCR = 2/3 ≈ 0.67.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Project C: 1 MU invested yields 0 to self, reduces victimized group by 1 MU (cost to punisher = 1 MU per 1 MU harm). Project B: 1 MU invested yields 2 MU to in-group (from victimized group), so cost is 1 MU for 1 MU harm, but also benefits in-group.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Project C: 1:1 cost-to-harm ratio. Project B: 1 MU invested both benefits in-group and harms out-group (1 MU harm per 1 MU invested, but also 2 MU to in-group).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was played for 6 periods (finite, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants saw the total number of MUs invested in each project by their group and, in the aggression condition, by how much the victimized group's payoff was reduced.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare outcomes, so the net effect of aggression on efficiency is unknown.
- The anti-social options (aggression) are not standard punishment mechanisms; they are not used to enforce cooperation or norms, but to harm an out-group, making the design only adjacent to standard PGG punishment treatments.
- The findings are based on a specific laboratory population (mainly university students in Switzerland), which may limit generalizability.
- The experiment does not manipulate or measure emotional state or group salience beyond minimal group assignment.
- The design does not allow for retaliation or strategic interaction between groups, which may affect the prevalence and impact of aggression.
