# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Fairness overrides reputation: the importance of fairness considerations in altruistic cooperation
Authors: Güney, S., & Newell, B. R.
Journal: Frontiers in Human Neuroscience
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rates of unfair offers (costly punishment)
- acceptance rates
- attribution of intentions
- fairness considerations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rejection rate of unfair offers in the Mini Ultimatum Game, interpreted as costly punishment. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The focus is on behavioral responses (punishment) and their psychological drivers (fairness, intentions, reputation), not on aggregate payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that rejection of unfair offers (costly punishment) in the Mini Ultimatum Game is primarily driven by fairness considerations, particularly the perceived intentions of the proposer. When intentions are not assessable, outcome fairness still motivates some punishment, but to a lesser degree. The opportunity for reputation building and future interaction (i.e., repeated, non-anonymous play) does not significantly increase costly punishment when fairness considerations are strong, but can increase punishment when intentions are ambiguous. The study does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the psychological drivers of costly punishment in ultimatum-like bargaining games are primarily fairness-based, not reputation-based, and that the presence of repeated interaction or reputation opportunities does not necessarily increase punishment or change behavioral outcomes when fairness motives are strong. However, because the study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment occurs, not its aggregate payoff consequences.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Mini Ultimatum Game played in dyads (2 players: Proposer and Responder). Experiment 2 sessions had 4 participants, but the game is always 2-player.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1a and 1b: one-shot (each participant played all four Mini UG variants, but each is a one-shot decision). Experiment 2: four rounds, with partner switching after two rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer; if rejected, both get zero.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: when the Responder rejects, both players get zero (Responder forgoes the offered amount).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is maximal: rejection sets both players' payoffs to zero.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1a and 1b: one-shot, so number of rounds is clear. Experiment 2: participants did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Experiment 2, after some rounds, decisions and outcomes were announced to all players (publicly after R2 and R4). In other rounds, outcomes were announced privately within pairs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Experiment 2, players' decisions were sometimes announced publicly, so punishment could be attributed to individuals.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Mini Ultimatum Game, which is structurally different from public goods games; results may not generalize to multi-player or continuous-contribution settings.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (rejection rates) are reported.
- Punishment is binary and maximal (rejection sets both payoffs to zero), unlike the graded punishment options in many public goods experiments.
- All participants are undergraduate students from a single university, limiting external validity.
- The study focuses on psychological drivers of punishment, not on aggregate consequences for group outcomes.
