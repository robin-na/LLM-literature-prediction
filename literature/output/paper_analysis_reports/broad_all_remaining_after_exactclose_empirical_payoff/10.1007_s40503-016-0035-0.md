# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Does corruption affect cooperation? A laboratory experiment
Authors: Campos-Vazquez, R. M., & Mejia, L. A.
Journal: Latin American Economic Review
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate to public good
- frequency of corruption (bribery, acceptance)
- punishment and counter-punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable in the public goods game is the contribution rate (percentage of endowment contributed). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The paper discusses cooperation and punishment effects in terms of contribution rates, not payoff-based efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that: (1) exposure to a high-corruption environment (low monitoring) reduces subsequent cooperation in a public goods game; (2) allowing a leader to centrally punish increases cooperation (contributions) compared to no punishment, with a stronger effect in low-corruption groups; (3) allowing group members to counter-punish the leader reduces cooperation compared to leader-only punishment. All findings are based on contribution rates, not on efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that punishment can foster cooperation, but its effectiveness is moderated by prior corruption exposure and is undermined by the threat of counter-punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that centralized punishment by a leader increases contribution rates in a standard linear public goods game, and that this effect is stronger in groups with less prior exposure to corruption. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified, and the findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling centralized punishment on cooperation (contribution rates), but not directly on efficiency. The results also highlight that the presence of counter-punishment can reduce the positive effect of punishment on cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods group consists of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game treatment is played for 5 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between players was not allowed; distance between players was sufficient to prevent communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players can contribute any amount from their 20 ECU endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (group project is multiplied by 2 and divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Leader pays 1 ECU to reduce another player's profit by 3 ECU (punishment cost = 1 per 3 impact). For counter-punishment, cost is 3 per 1 impact.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 ECU spent by leader reduces target's profit by 3 ECU. For counter-punishment, 3 ECU spent reduces leader's profit by 1 ECU.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is played for 5 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are informed of their own earnings and the contributions of their group's partners.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know that one group member is the leader with punishment power, but the leader is not explicitly identified to the group. Players know if they are punished, but not who punished whom (except that only the leader can punish).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff; no direct efficiency or welfare analysis is reported.
- The experiment uses a centralized punishment institution (leader-only punishment), not peer-to-peer punishment.
- The sample consists of first-year university students in Mexico, which may limit generalizability.
- The effect of punishment is only measured in the context of prior exposure to corruption (via a separate corruption game), which may interact with the results.
- The number of rounds is limited (5), which may affect the dynamics of cooperation and punishment.
- No analysis of the cost of punishment relative to group welfare or efficiency is provided.
