## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing adaptive feedback in punishment intensity—where punishment increases as defection rises and decreases as cooperation rises—can facilitate the evolution and maintenance of cooperation in public goods games. The system can exhibit bistability (converging to either full cooperation with low punishment or full defection with high punishment), stable oscillations between cooperation and defection, or stable full cooperation/defection depending on the average per capita incentive and other parameters. Notably, full cooperation can be achieved and maintained with only low-intensity punishment under certain conditions. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and numerical simulations of the replicator dynamics with feedback, not on empirical or experimental data. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so implications for efficiency must be inferred indirectly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that adaptive, feedback-based punishment can promote and stabilize cooperation in public goods games, sometimes with only low punishment intensity. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the results suggest that under certain parameter regimes, adaptive punishment could lead to high efficiency (if cooperation is high and punishment costs are low). However, the absence of direct efficiency analysis means that predictions about efficiency effects must be made cautiously and indirectly, based on the reported cooperation dynamics. The model is most relevant for settings with institutional punishment, adaptive feedback, and infinite well-mixed populations, and less directly applicable to finite, experimental, or empirical settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N is a model parameter; main text and Table 1 specify N=5 in numerical examples, but the model is general for arbitrary N.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is an infinite-horizon evolutionary game (replicator dynamics), not a finite-round repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: each player chooses to cooperate (contribute c) or defect (contribute nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is F/N, where F is the growth factor and N is group size. F=3, N=5 in numerical examples, so MPCR=0.6 in those cases.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is institutional: a total budget N*delta is used to punish defectors. The cost is modeled as an average per capita incentive delta, which is a key parameter.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is determined by the punishment intensity parameter b, which is dynamically adjusted via feedback. The model explicitly analyzes the effect of varying b.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are not directly analyzed; all results are in terms of cooperation frequency and punishment intensity.
- The model assumes infinite, well-mixed populations and institutional punishment, which may not generalize to finite or structured populations or to peer punishment settings.
- No analysis of stochasticity, learning, or bounded rationality; all dynamics are deterministic.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback beyond the adaptive punishment rule.
