# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment is sensitive to outside options in humans but not in cleaner fish (Labroides dimidiatus)
Authors: Deutchman, P., Aellen, M., Bogese, M., Bshary, R., Drayton, L., Gil, D., Martin, J., Pretot, L., Raihani, N., Santos, L. R., & Mcauliffe, K.
Journal: Animal Behaviour
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency/likelihood of punishment assigned
- moral wrongness ratings
- responsibility ratings
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable in both studies is the frequency or amount of punishment assigned in response to cheating, as a function of whether the cheater had alternative options (intent). No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. Human participants could spend money to punish, but the outcome measured is the amount spent, not the effect on group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that human participants are sensitive to the intent behind cheating when deciding to punish: they punish more when a partner chooses to cheat despite having a cooperative option. Cleaner fish, in contrast, do not show this sensitivity; male fish punish female partners at similar rates regardless of whether the female had a choice to cooperate or not. The main outcome is the likelihood or amount of punishment, not efficiency or group payoff. The human experiment is a novel economic game inspired by cleaner fish mutualism, not a standard public goods game, and does not report efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. Its main contribution is to show that human punishment is sensitive to intent (alternative options), while cleaner fish punishment is not. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant: it suggests that the psychological basis for punishment in humans includes sensitivity to intent, which may affect when and how punishment is used, but does not provide quantitative or qualitative evidence about efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human experiment: 3 players per group (Player 1, Player 2, Player 3). Cleaner fish: 2 fish per pair (male and female).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cleaner fish: 8 blocks of 4 conditions (32 trials per pair). Human: one-shot between-subjects design (single decision per participant).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players in either experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/cheating choices are discrete: in humans, Player 2 chooses between two options (all or nothing for self or equal split); in fish, female chooses which food item to eat (cooperate or cheat).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human: Each $0.01 spent reduces partner's endowment by $0.05 (punishment is costly). Fish: Punishment is aggressive chase, presumably costly in energy/risk, but not quantified.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human: Each $0.01 spent reduces partner's payoff by $0.05 (punishment effectiveness ratio 5:1). Fish: Punishment is direct aggression (chase), effectiveness not quantified.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available in either experiment; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human: One-shot game, participants know the structure. Fish: Number of rounds/blocks is fixed and repeated, but not described as hidden.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human: Participants are told about Player 2's choice and its consequences. Fish: Males observe female's choice and plate removal.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Human: Punishment is direct and the punished knows who punished (Player 1 punishes Player 2). Fish: Male directly punishes female partner.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is punishment behavior, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game; it is inspired by cleaner fish mutualism.
- No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be assessed.
- Sample size for the cleaner fish experiment is small (12 pairs), possibly underpowered to detect small effects.
- The human experiment is a one-shot, between-subjects design, limiting generalizability to repeated or group contexts.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- The ecological validity of the 'no-choice' condition for cleaner fish may be limited, as cheating is almost always intentional in the wild.
