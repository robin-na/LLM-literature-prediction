# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social background, cooperative behavior, and norm enforcement
Authors: Kocher, M., Martinsson, P., & Visser, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings in Part I and Part II (average earnings per group/community)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment assigned
- proportion of free-riders
- conditional contribution
- conditional punishment
- degree of anti-social punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution and punishment behavior. Earnings/payoff are discussed in relation to punishment costs, but efficiency (as a ratio to the social optimum) is not directly calculated or reported. Group earnings are compared across conditions and communities, but not normalized to efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper reports on one-shot public goods games with and without punishment, conducted in four South African high schools representing different socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. The introduction of punishment increased contributions significantly only in the white community, and only modestly or insignificantly in the others. Punishment was more frequent and severe in the African communities, leading to lower average earnings in the punishment condition for those groups. The study finds that social background variables (such as trust and household violence) explain much of the variation in cooperation and punishment. Overall, the efficiency impact of punishment is mixed: in some groups, punishment increases contributions but also reduces earnings due to the cost of punishment, sometimes resulting in no net efficiency gain.

## Prediction Guidance
For the prediction task, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in one-shot, three-player public goods games with MPCR=0.5 and a punishment technology of 1:3 (cost:impact) is highly context-dependent. In groups with low baseline contributions, punishment may increase contributions and potentially efficiency, but in groups with already high contributions, punishment may not increase cooperation and can reduce efficiency due to punishment costs. The presence of anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) can further reduce efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is a strong moderator: if baseline contributions are high, punishment may not improve and can even worsen efficiency. Social background and group norms are important moderators, but are not directly encoded in the game design dimensions. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will reliably increase efficiency, especially in one-shot or low-trust settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size was fixed at 3 in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each part (with and without punishment) was a one-shot game; no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication among participants was strictly forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous (integer between 0 and 20), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR was 0.5 (each unit contributed returns 0.5 to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost was 1 guilder per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point deducted 3 guilders from the punished player.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the game was one-shot in each part; instructions were clear about the structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributions in Part II, each group member was informed about the contributions of the other two members before the punishment stage.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; no information on who punished whom was revealed.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated or reported; only average earnings are discussed, and not normalized to the social optimum.
- The experiment is one-shot, so findings may not generalize to repeated or multi-round public goods games.
- The subject pool consists of high school students from four specific communities in Cape Town, limiting generalizability.
- Social background and trust are shown to be important moderators, but these are not encoded in standard game design dimensions.
- Anti-social punishment is present and may reduce efficiency, but its determinants are not fully explored.
- No direct analysis of the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency is provided.
