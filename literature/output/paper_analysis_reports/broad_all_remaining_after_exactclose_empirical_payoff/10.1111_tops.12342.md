## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in a partner-choice game where both agents can choose to search for better partners (look) or commit (not look), and can allow or disallow their partner to look, the only robust equilibrium is mutual looking and allowing. Commitment (strategic ignorance) does not evolve except when the cost of being abandoned is extremely high. The model directly analyzes the efficiency (expected payoff) of different equilibria and shows that, under most parameterizations, the efficiency of the 'punishment' (commitment-demanding) equilibrium is not higher and is not evolutionarily stable. The findings suggest that, in this class of games, enabling punishment-like mechanisms (demanding commitment) does not increase group efficiency and is not selected for, except in rare, extreme cases.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a theoretical counterpoint to the idea that punishment or commitment mechanisms generally increase efficiency in repeated social dilemmas. In the context of partner-choice games with bilateral temptation and the ability to condition on partner behavior, the model predicts that enabling punishment (demanding commitment or non-looking) does not robustly increase efficiency and is not evolutionarily stable. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in games with similar structure (bilateral temptation, partner choice, visible actions), enabling punishment or commitment mechanisms is unlikely to increase efficiency unless there are very large costs to being abandoned. The paper is most informative for adjacent settings rather than standard public goods games, but its payoff-based analysis is directly relevant to efficiency predictions.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The setting is adjacent to, but not the same as, standard public goods games; results may not transfer directly.
- Punishment is not modeled as a costly, scalable, or explicit mechanism, but as a refusal to enter a relationship with a looker.
- The model assumes one-shot interactions, with only limited discussion of repeated games.
- The model abstracts away from group-level enforcement, third-party punishment, and reputation effects.
- Results depend on the assumption that costs of being abandoned are not extremely high; in real-world settings, such costs may vary.
