Outcome notes:
The paper is a formal theory of reciprocity, not an empirical study. It discusses behavioral regularities and stylized facts from the literature, and presents a model predicting when people will reward or punish others based on perceived kindness or unkindness. It does not report new experimental or payoff-based outcome data.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops a psychological game-theoretic model of reciprocity, where individuals' utility depends on both material payoffs and the perceived kindness or unkindness of others' actions, which in turn depends on both outcomes and intentions. The model explains why people sometimes punish defectors or reward cooperators, even at a cost to themselves, and why the same material outcome can elicit different responses depending on the perceived intention behind it. The theory is shown to be consistent with a range of experimental findings, such as higher rejection rates for unfair offers in the ultimatum game, positive wage-effort relationships in gift-exchange, and conditional cooperation in public goods games. However, the paper does not provide new empirical data or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a theoretical framework for understanding when and why individuals will punish or reward others in social dilemmas, including public goods games. It highlights the importance of both outcomes and intentions in driving reciprocal behavior. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical estimates of how punishment affects efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper offers conceptual guidance on the psychological mechanisms underlying punishment, but does not supply direct evidence or parameter values for efficiency outcomes. Its value is mainly in informing the types of behavioral responses to expect under different game structures, especially regarding the role of intentions and available alternatives.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is developed for two-player games but is stated to be extendable to more players (see Appendix 2). Applications discuss both bilateral and multi-player games (e.g., public goods, market games).
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is applied to both one-shot and sequential (multi-round) games, including the centipede game and sequential prisoner's dilemma.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Some games discussed (e.g., ultimatum, best-shot) have discrete (all-or-nothing) choices; others (e.g., public goods, gift-exchange) have continuous choices. The model accommodates both.
- mpcr: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model notes that the propensity to cooperate increases in the marginal per capita return (mpcr) in public goods games, consistent with experimental findings.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model covers both rewarding and punishing as forms of reciprocity, but does not model explicit reward mechanisms as in some public goods experiments.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a formal theory and does not provide new empirical data or direct estimates of efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
- Punishment is modeled as a psychological response (reciprocal behavior), not as an explicit costly punishment mechanism as in public goods experiments.
- The model is not parameterized for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games.
- Many game design dimensions relevant for experimental public goods games (e.g., punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, feedback, anonymity) are not explicitly modeled.
- Findings are based on stylized facts and prior literature, not on new experimental manipulation or measurement.
