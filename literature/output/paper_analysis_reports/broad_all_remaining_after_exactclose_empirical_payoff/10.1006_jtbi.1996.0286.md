## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated games like the IPD and dominance games, the ability to punish can support the evolutionary stability of cooperative strategies, leading to higher efficiency (i.e., higher expected payoffs, closer to full cooperation) when the probability of continuation is high and punishment is sufficiently severe and/or effective. However, the effect of punishment on efficiency is parameter-dependent: if the probability of continuation is too low or punishment is too costly/ineffective, cooperation cannot be sustained and efficiency is low. The analysis also shows that there are many possible stable strategies, some of which are more efficient than others, and that the structure of punishment (e.g., how many rounds of mutual defection follow a defection) affects the range of stability and efficiency. In dominance games, punishment by a dominant can enforce resource control and higher efficiency for the dominant, but alternative equilibria exist where the subdominant prevails. In alternating PD, reciprocal strategies can be stable and efficient if the benefit-to-cost ratio is high enough. The findings are theoretical and based on formal analysis of payoffs and stability conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games depends on game design dimensions such as the probability of continuation (analogous to number of rounds), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the structure of the game (simultaneous vs. alternating moves, role asymmetry). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation, but only when parameters are favorable (e.g., high continuation probability, effective punishment). The results suggest that, for prediction tasks, one should expect punishment to improve efficiency in repeated games when the game is long enough and punishment is not too costly or weak, but that the effect can be neutral or negative if these conditions are not met. The paper also highlights that many different punishment strategies can be stable, so the specific implementation of punishment matters. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in repeated, two-player, payoff-based games with punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games analyzed are two-player repeated games (IPD, dominance game, alternating PD).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Games are repeated for a random number of rounds, with continuation probability ω. Higher ω (longer expected number of rounds) increases the range of stability for cooperative/punishing strategies and thus efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/action choices are binary (cooperate/defect, respect/steal, help/not help, punish/not punish).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (e.g., c in dominance game) is explicitly modeled. Lower punishment cost (relative to damage inflicted) increases the ability of punishment to support cooperation and efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (damage d to target per cost c to punisher) is explicitly modeled. Higher effectiveness (d >> c) increases the range of stability for punishment-based cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds; games end with probability 1-ω after each round (infinite horizon with discounting).

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- All games are two-player; results may not generalize to multi-player public goods games.
- The models assume perfect monitoring of actions and do not include noise in observation or private information.
- No communication, partner choice, or exit options are modeled.
- No explicit public goods game (Pgg) is analyzed; results are for repeated PD and related games.
- Reward mechanisms are not considered.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to be highly parameter-dependent; real-world applicability may be limited by the idealized nature of the models.
