## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- combined earnings of P and A (group welfare)

- income distributions over time
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and severity of punishment
- demand levels
- acceptance/rejection rates
- strategy types (latent class analysis)
- response to punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both payoff-related outcomes (combined and individual earnings, efficiency relative to maximum possible) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (punishment frequency, demand/acceptance, strategy types).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that when one player can punish another at low cost in a repeated extortion game, punishment is used frequently and harshly, especially as experience accumulates. The threat of punishment induces compliance with moderate demands, but actual punishment does not improve and may reduce future compliance. Over time, P's demands and income increase, while A's income decreases. The combined earnings of both players are about 80% of the maximum possible, indicating a notable efficiency loss compared to full cooperation. The results suggest that low-cost punishment leads to persistent, severe punishment and lower group efficiency, and that subject heterogeneity (different behavioral types) is important for understanding aggregate outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that in repeated asymmetric games with low-cost, high-impact punishment, the introduction of punishment reduces group efficiency compared to the maximum possible. The threat of punishment increases compliance with moderate demands, but actual punishment does not improve efficiency and may worsen it. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that when punishment is cheap and effective, efficiency is likely to be lower than in a fully cooperative scenario, and the presence of punishment can lead to persistent efficiency losses. The results are most directly relevant to asymmetric, dyadic games rather than standard symmetric public goods games, but the mechanisms (punishment cost, effectiveness, behavioral response) are informative for predicting efficiency in related environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by a pair (2 players: principal and agent).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair plays 15 rounds; the time horizon is common knowledge.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; terminals were partitioned to prevent communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (demand and punishment) choices are continuous within a range, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs the punisher 10 (Euro-cents) per use, regardless of the amount inflicted (up to 160). Low cost relative to possible damage.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: for a cost of 10, P can reduce A's income by up to 160. This high impact per cost is discussed as a key factor in persistent punishment and efficiency loss.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (15) is common knowledge; subjects know the time horizon.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the outcomes of their own pair's previous rounds (demands, acceptances, punishments), but there is no mention of summary information about other pairs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always from P to A within a fixed pair; identities are known within the pair, but not across pairs. No anonymity within the pair.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated dyadic extortion game with asymmetric punishment power, so findings may not generalize directly to symmetric PGGs.
- No control condition without punishment is included, so the direct effect of enabling punishment versus not enabling it is inferred rather than experimentally manipulated.
- The punishment is highly cost-effective (low cost, high impact), which may not reflect typical real-world or PGG-like environments.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The experiment uses only two players per group, limiting generalizability to larger group settings.
- Subject pool is university students from a single location, which may limit external validity.
