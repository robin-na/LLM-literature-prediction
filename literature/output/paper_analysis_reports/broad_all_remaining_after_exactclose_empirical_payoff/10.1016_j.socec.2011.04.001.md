# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Men among men do not take norm enforcement seriously
Authors: Boschini, A., Muren, A., & Persson, M.
Journal: Journal of Socio-economics
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- fraction of subjects/groups choosing to punish (play Y) in a sequential prisoner's dilemma punishment game
- fraction of subjects/groups choosing to reward (play X) in a reward game
- distribution of personality types (altruist, reciprocal, egoist) based on action profiles
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the fraction of subjects or groups who choose to punish a non-cooperator in a sequential prisoner's dilemma. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed. The study focuses on norm enforcement behavior (punishment) and its variation by gender and group context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the inclination to punish norm-breakers (interpreted as norm enforcement) is context-dependent: men among men are less likely to punish than women or men in mixed-gender settings. This result holds both for individuals and for groups. The difference is substantial (about 25% lower punishment rates for men-among-men) and statistically significant. However, the paper does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this study. The findings are relevant for understanding who enforces cooperation norms, but not for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods or similar games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-like environments. It does, however, provide informative evidence about the behavioral propensity to punish under different gender and group contexts in a sequential prisoner's dilemma. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the presence and composition of gender in groups may affect the likelihood of punishment being used, but it does not inform how this translates into efficiency outcomes. The game is not a standard public goods game, and efficiency is not measured or discussed. Thus, the paper is only indirectly relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment, and should be weighted accordingly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games were played by individuals, groups of two, or groups of three. Group size was a treatment variable in Experiment 2.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played three one-shot games: a simultaneous PD, a reward game, and a punishment game. Each is a single-shot (one round) game.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is mentioned or allowed. Groups made joint decisions but the process was not monitored.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/punishment choice is binary: choose X or Y. No continuous contribution or punishment levels.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher: 'no personal cost or gain associated with the punishment action.'
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented by choosing Y, which gives both players 100 (vs. X, which would give 100 to self and 600 to the other). Thus, punishment reduces the counterpart's payoff but does not affect the punisher's own payoff.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. A reward game is included as a separate treatment, where the counterpart is known to have cooperated.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding (choosing X in the reward game) is costly: the player can exploit the cooperator for higher own payoff by choosing Y.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding (choosing X) gives the counterpart 500 extra units compared to Y, at a cost to the rewarder.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the structure: each game is one-shot and the sequence of games is explained.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a sequential prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game or direct variant; results may not generalize to PGG settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are measured or reported; only behavioral outcomes (punishment frequency) are analyzed.
- Punishment is costless to the punisher, which differs from most public goods game punishment designs.
- The study focuses on gender and group context effects on norm enforcement, not on the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- External validity is limited; the experiment uses student subjects in a lab setting.
- No communication or feedback between players; group decision process is unobserved.
