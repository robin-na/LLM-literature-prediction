## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and public visibility of a punishment institution in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to control conditions without punishment. The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size n is a key parameter; larger groups increase the equilibrium fraction of opportunists and reduce the fraction of pool punishers, which can reduce efficiency. The effect is directly analyzed in the model.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is r/n, where r > 1 is the multiplier and n is group size. Higher rc-c increases the net benefit of cooperation, which reduces opportunism and increases efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is the tax amount gamma paid by pool punishers. Higher gamma increases the equilibrium fraction of opportunists (tax evaders) and reduces efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology is modeled via the fine beta per free rider and the functional form (volunteer's dilemma or linear). Higher beta (relative to c+gamma) increases deterrence, enabling pool punishment to be stable and increasing efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes evolutionary dynamics and infinite or large populations, which may not map directly to finite, repeated laboratory or field experiments.
- No explicit modeling of repeated rounds or finite time horizons; results are for long-run equilibria.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or more complex social structures.
- Assumes public visibility of the institution as a binary or parameterized variable, but real-world visibility may be more nuanced.
- Behavioral assumptions (e.g., opportunists' conditional cooperation) may not fully capture human psychology in experimental settings.
