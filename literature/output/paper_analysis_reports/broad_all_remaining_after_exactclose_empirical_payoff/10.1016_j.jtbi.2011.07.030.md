## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main examples use n=4; theory is general for n>=2. Group size is a parameter in all models and affects the threshold for incentives to be effective.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is for a one-shot (single round) public goods game. Repeated games are discussed as outside the scope.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed in the model; not part of the theoretical setup.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: x in [0, E]. Discrete (integer) strategies are used in simulations, but the main model is continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. mpcr = r/n; main examples use r=1.6, n=4, so mpcr=0.4. Theoretical results generalize to 1<r<n.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Institutional punishment is modeled as a fixed deduction (A) from the punished player; no explicit cost to the punisher (the institution).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the amount A deducted per punishment event; A is varied in the analysis.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is present in some treatments (IR, IRP) and absent in others (IP).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Institutional reward is modeled as a fixed bonus (A) to the rewarded player; no explicit cost to the rewarder (the institution).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is the amount A added per reward event; A is varied in the analysis.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot; all players know the structure.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes institutional (not peer) incentives, with no explicit cost to the punisher or rewarder (the institution).
- The game is one-shot (single round); repeated interactions and learning are not modeled.
- Contribution is continuous in the main model, with discrete strategies only in simulations.
- No communication, feedback, or summary information is provided to players.
- The model does not address the implementation or sustainability of institutional incentives in real-world settings.
- Results may not generalize to peer-sanctioning, repeated games, or settings with different information structures.
