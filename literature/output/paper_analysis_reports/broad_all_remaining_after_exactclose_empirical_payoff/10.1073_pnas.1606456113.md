## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated games where one player can gather information before cooperating or defecting, aversion to information gathering (i.e., a preference for 'blind' cooperation) can be sustained either by punishment (ending the game if the player looks) or by preferential interactions (continuing only with reliable partners). In homogeneous populations, punishment is required to enforce blind cooperation and thus higher payoffs. In heterogeneous populations, preferential interactions can achieve similar or greater efficiency without punishment. The model also predicts that if detecting information gathering is costly, punishment-based mechanisms break down in homogeneous populations, but preferential interactions remain effective in heterogeneous ones. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore positive in homogeneous populations but redundant or unnecessary in heterogeneous ones.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated cooperation games depends on population heterogeneity. In homogeneous populations, punishment (ending the game after information gathering or defection) is necessary to enforce cooperation and increase efficiency. In heterogeneous populations, preferential partner selection can achieve high efficiency without punishment. Thus, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, one should consider the degree of population heterogeneity and the possibility of preferential interactions. If the environment is homogeneous and punishment is strong, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency. If the environment is heterogeneous and allows for preferential interactions, punishment may have little or no additional effect on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The envelope game is a two-player repeated game (Player 1 and Player 2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is repeated for a random number of rounds, with continuation probability w (e.g., w=0.4), so the expected number of rounds is 1/(1-w).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (cooperation/defection) is a binary choice: cooperate or defect.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as ending the game, which costs both players future payoffs. The cost to the punisher (Player 2) is the loss of future rounds; the cost is discussed explicitly.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the ability to end the game and thus remove all future payoffs. The strength of punishment is a key parameter in the model.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated with a fixed continuation probability (w), so the end is stochastic but the structure is known.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (ending the game) is always attributable to Player 2, as there are only two players and the action is direct.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a two-player repeated binary cooperation game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize directly to multi-player PGGs.
- Population heterogeneity is modeled abstractly; real-world heterogeneity may be more complex.
- No explicit modeling of communication, reputation beyond the current game, or group-level dynamics.
- No reward mechanisms are considered, only punishment (ending the game).
