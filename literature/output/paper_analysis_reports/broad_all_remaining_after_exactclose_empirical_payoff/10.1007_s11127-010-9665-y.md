# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Power comes with responsibility-or does it?
Authors: Bolle, F., & Vogel, C.
Journal: Public Choice
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average income (percentage of endowment)
- allocator income
- non-allocator income
- group average income
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- forced contributions
- election outcomes
- self-reported motives
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is income (payoff), both for allocators and non-allocators, as well as group average. Contribution rates are also reported, but the focus is on income as a measure of efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Transferring responsibility to a single allocator who determines the contributions of all group members leads to higher average group income (efficiency) than the standard public goods game with voluntary contributions. This effect is observed both when the allocator is randomly assigned and when elected, though the difference between these modes is small. Over repeated phases, allocators become more selfish, reducing their own contributions, but group income remains higher than in the standard PG game. No direct punishment or reward mechanism is present, but the allocator's power to assign contributions functions as a form of enforced cooperation. The study does not find evidence for indirect punishment (allocators do not systematically penalize previous free riders).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a public goods environment, shifting from voluntary contribution to a regime where one player has the power to assign contributions (i.e., a strong form of centralized enforcement) increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the standard game. While this is not a punishment mechanism per se, it is adjacent to the prediction task because it shows that enforced contributions (even without explicit punishment costs) can improve efficiency. The findings suggest that, in settings where voluntary cooperation is inefficient, introducing a mechanism for enforced contributions (or strong centralized control) can increase group payoff. However, the absence of explicit punishment or cost for enforcement means the results are not directly transferable to standard punishment treatments, but they are informative for understanding the upper bound of efficiency gains from strong enforcement.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players in each game (randomly assigned from a pool of 6 per session).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. PG game: 10 rounds per phase; Allocator game: 1 round per phase. Each phase is repeated three times with new groups.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how many tokens (out of 20 or 100) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token contributed yields 0.5 to each player; with 3 players, MPCR = 0.5.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in the PG game (10 rounds per phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed about the contributions of the other group members in the previous round.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include an explicit punishment or reward mechanism; the allocator's power is not associated with a cost for enforcement.
- The allocator game is not a standard punishment treatment, so results may not generalize to settings with costly punishment.
- The effect of repeated interaction is limited by the use of new groups in each phase, reducing the potential for reputation or long-term reciprocity.
- The sample consists of university students in a laboratory setting, which may limit external validity.
- The study does not explore the effect of varying group size, MPCR, or other key parameters beyond the reported design.
