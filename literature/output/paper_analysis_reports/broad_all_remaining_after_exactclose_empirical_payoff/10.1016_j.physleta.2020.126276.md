# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Effect of habitat destruction on cooperation in public goods games
Authors: Yang, X. Q., Zhang, F., Wang, W. X., Zhang, D., Shi, Z. H., & Zhou, S. W.
Journal: Physics Letters A
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- institutional preference (punishment vs reward)
- social preference profile
- mismatch/match between institutional preference and assignment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution in the public goods game. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare directly. Some payoff data (individual payoffs) are shown in figures, but efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that introducing a weak, automatic punishment institution (where the lowest contributor in a group loses 1 yuan per round) does not significantly increase contributions compared to a control PGG or to a reward institution. There is no significant effect of matching or mismatching subjects' institutional preferences (punishment vs reward) with the institution they are assigned to. Subjects who prefer punishment tend to be lower contributors and have social preferences associated with anti-social or efficiency-reducing profiles. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff as a main outcome, and the punishment institution is designed to be strictly efficiency-reducing at the group level.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated linear PGG with weak, automatic, centralized punishment (punishment cost = 1, punishment tech = 1, imposed on lowest contributor), enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency compared to a control. The punishment is non-deterrent and strictly efficiency-reducing by design. Therefore, in similar game designs (weak, automatic, centralized punishment), one should not expect punishment to improve efficiency over the control. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is indirect and based on contribution rates and the institutional design. The findings are most relevant for settings with weak, automatic, non-peer punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment or stronger punishment regimes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per PGG session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG stage has 5 rounds; total of 10 rounds per subject (5 control, 5 with institution).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication among subjects was not permitted.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects allocate an integer amount (0-10 yuan) to the group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each unit contributed returns 0.5 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is automatic and centralized: the lowest contributor loses 1 yuan per round. No cost to other players; cost is imposed by the institution.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact is 1 yuan per round for the lowest contributor; no cost to others. The punishment is weak and non-deterrent by design.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward institution is present as a treatment arm: highest contributor gains 1 yuan per round.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is automatic and centralized: the highest contributor gains 1 yuan per round. No cost to other players; reward is imposed by the institution.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact is 1 yuan per round for the highest contributor; no cost to others. The reward is weak and non-deterrent by design.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the number of rounds in each stage (5 rounds per stage).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are informed of the total group contribution and their individual payoffs. In the institution stages, they are also told if they were punished/rewarded and by how much.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is automatic and centralized; no peer identification is possible. Subjects are told if they were punished/rewarded, but not by whom (since it is not peer-based).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. Efficiency is not directly reported or analyzed.
- The punishment and reward institutions are weak, automatic, and centralized, not peer-based or strategic.
- The punishment institution is strictly efficiency-reducing by design, so results may not generalize to deterrent or peer punishment settings.
- The subject pool is limited to male rural Chinese farmers, which may limit generalizability.
- Institutional preference is elicited via self-report, not incentivized choice.
- No direct analysis of group-level efficiency or welfare outcomes.
