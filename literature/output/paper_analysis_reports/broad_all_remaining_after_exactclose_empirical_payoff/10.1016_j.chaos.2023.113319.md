Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the evolution of cooperation, measured as the proportion of cooperators and excluders, not on group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. Payoff is used as a mechanism for strategy updating, but aggregate payoff or efficiency is not reported as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a social exclusion strategy with dynamic (state-dependent) costs into a spatial public goods game can substantially increase the average level of cooperation in the population. Higher probability of exclusion success and lower exclusion cost parameters both expand the parameter space where cooperation dominates. The Moore neighborhood structure (more neighbors per player) is more conducive to cooperation than the traditional neighborhood. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so the findings are about cooperation rates, not efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that dynamic-cost social exclusion (a form of punishment) can increase cooperation rates in spatial public goods games, especially with high exclusion probability and low cost. However, because the reported outcomes are cooperation rates rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects. The results suggest that, under similar game design dimensions, enabling effective punishment is likely to increase cooperation, which may correlate with higher efficiency, but this is not directly demonstrated. The effect of neighborhood size (player count per group) is also highlighted: larger groups (Moore neighborhood) facilitate higher cooperation. For efficiency prediction, this paper provides indirect support that punishment can be beneficial, but does not quantify efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Player count per group is manipulated via neighborhood structure: G=5 (traditional, 4 neighbors + self) and G=9 (Moore, 8 neighbors + self). Larger group size (Moore) leads to higher cooperation rates.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Simulations run for up to 10,000 or 50,000 Monte Carlo steps (MCSs) until steady state; not a repeated finite-round game as in experiments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: cooperators and excluders contribute one unit, defectors contribute nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied; mpcr is r/G. Higher r/G (weaker dilemma) increases cooperation rates.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Exclusion cost is parameterized by θ (variable part) and φ (fixed part). Higher θ increases cost and reduces cooperation rates.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the probability of exclusion success (β). Higher β increases cooperation rates.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main results are about cooperation rates.
- Findings are based on theoretical modeling and simulation, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes spatial structure and evolutionary updating, which may not generalize to all public goods game settings.
- No direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff; only indirect inference via cooperation rates is possible.
- No reward mechanism is considered, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
