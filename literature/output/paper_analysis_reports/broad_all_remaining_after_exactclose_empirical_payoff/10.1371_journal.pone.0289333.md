Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is which trait (green or blue) goes to fixation in the population, interpreted as a proxy for group identity maintenance or invasion. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus is reported. Fitness is used as an individual-level variable to determine reproduction, but group-level efficiency or payoff is not a reported outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The models show that increasing the sensitivity to mimicry (the ability to detect outsiders) is a much more effective way to maintain group-defining traits than increasing the cost imposed on detected mimics. Punishment mechanisms (altruistic punishment of in-group members who are insufficiently sensitive) can help maintain group traits, but their effect is less dramatic than that of detection sensitivity. The cost of punishment, even when high, does not strongly predict which trait will go to fixation. The models do not report group efficiency or payoff, but focus on trait fixation as a proxy for group integrity.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or surplus. However, it provides indirect theoretical support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in maintaining cooperation (or group integrity) depends more on the likelihood of detection than on the severity of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment mechanisms that increase detection or monitoring may be more effective than those that simply increase punishment cost. The findings are most relevant to environments where group identity or norm compliance is the main outcome, rather than efficiency or payoff.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use N=50 or N=100 individuals per generation, but the effect of player count is not analyzed as a moderator.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each simulation runs for 200 generations (rounds). The number of generations is fixed and not analyzed as a moderator.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (cost to detected mimics, and in Model 5, cost to punishers) is a key manipulated variable. Increasing punishment cost does not strongly increase the likelihood of in-group trait fixation.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is implicitly modeled as the fitness impact of being detected, but not varied independently from cost.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The number of generations (rounds) is fixed and known in the simulation setup.

## Important Limitations
- The models are not public-goods games and do not report efficiency, group payoff, or surplus; outcomes are based on trait fixation, not payoff.
- No direct empirical or experimental data; all results are from agent-based simulations.
- Fitness is used as an individual-level variable for reproduction, but group-level efficiency is not analyzed.
- No analysis of how game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds) moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Punishment is modeled as fitness cost, but the psychological or social mechanisms of punishment are not detailed.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included.
- Trait fixation is a proxy for group integrity, not for efficiency or welfare.
