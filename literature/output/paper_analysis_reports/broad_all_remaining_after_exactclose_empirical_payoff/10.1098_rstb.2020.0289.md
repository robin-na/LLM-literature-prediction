## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that both direct punishment and indirect, reputation-based tactics (gossip, ostracism) are important for maintaining cooperation and enforcing norms. Direct punishment is more effective at recalibrating offenders' behavior but is costly and risky, while indirect tactics are less costly and reduce retaliation risk but are less effective at changing behavior. The choice of tactic depends on factors such as the self-relevance of the offense, relationship to the offender, power dynamics, and emotional responses (anger vs. disgust). The paper highlights that indirect tactics are more prevalent in real-world settings than direct punishment as operationalized in laboratory public goods games. It also notes that punishment can sometimes reduce efficiency, and that the ecological validity of lab findings is limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. However, it offers theoretical context suggesting that the effectiveness and efficiency of punishment depend on its cost, the risk of retaliation, and the availability of alternative (indirect) sanctioning mechanisms. It cautions that lab-based punishment games may overestimate the prevalence and impact of direct punishment compared to real-world settings, where indirect tactics are more common. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of communication, relationship context, and the type of punishment available may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, but it does not provide quantitative or directly predictive evidence.
