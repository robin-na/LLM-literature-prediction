Journal: Physical Review E

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- strategy frequencies (fractions of defectors, cooperators, punishers, excluders)
- probability of defector extinction
- pattern formation and alliance dynamics
- critical cost thresholds for exclusion
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the evolutionary dynamics and survival/extinction of strategies (defectors, cooperators, punishers, excluders) in a spatial public goods game. Outcomes are reported in terms of strategy frequencies and extinction probabilities, not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. There is discussion of the community benefit and the ability to 'sweep out defectors', but no explicit calculation or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in spatial public goods games, the introduction of an excluder strategy (which monitors and excludes defectors at a cost) can lead to the elimination of defectors when excluders form alliances with other cooperative strategies (cooperators or punishers). This effect is robust even when the cost of exclusion is extremely high, provided the population structure allows for the formation of sufficiently large clusters of excluders and cooperators/punishers. The main outcomes are the extinction probabilities of defectors and the conditions for the survival of excluders, not direct measures of group efficiency or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that exclusion-based sanctions (a form of punishment) can be highly effective in structured populations, especially when combined with other cooperative strategies. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Instead, it suggests that under certain spatial and strategic conditions, sanctioning mechanisms can eliminate defection, which would likely increase efficiency, but this is not quantified. The findings are most relevant for understanding the qualitative impact of exclusion and alliances in spatially structured games, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run on square lattices of various sizes (e.g., L=60 to L=6000, with N=L^2 players), and also on random regular graphs with N=10^6. The number of players is a key parameter in the analysis of finite-size effects.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is evolutionary and proceeds in Monte Carlo steps (MCS), with outcomes measured after many rounds (e.g., 1000s of MCS). There is no fixed number of rounds as in a laboratory experiment, but the number of evolutionary steps is a key simulation parameter.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=1.44, 2, 3.8), and group size G=5, so MPCR = r/G. The value of r is a key parameter in the analysis.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost parameter gamma is varied (e.g., gamma=0.01, 0.6). The cost of exclusion (epsilon) is also varied, but this is for the excluder strategy, not classic punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized by beta (fine imposed on defectors) and gamma (cost to punisher). These are varied in the simulations.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or any direct payoff-based outcome; all results are in terms of strategy frequencies and extinction probabilities.
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- The exclusion mechanism is modeled as a separate strategy (excluder) rather than as an option available to all players, which may limit generalizability to standard punishment treatments.
- Findings are specific to spatially structured populations and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.
- Finite-size effects are significant and may affect the interpretation of results in smaller populations.
- No direct comparison to classic laboratory public goods experiments is provided.
