## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compares two institutional settings: PCS (partial compulsory solidarity), where the super-rich R can only donate to others but is not part of the public good game, and GCS (general compulsory solidarity), where R is included in the public good game and can also donate. GCS results in significantly higher contributions from all roles, higher average payoffs, and lower inequality than PCS. R's targeted donations (rewards) further increase cooperation, especially when R acquires costly information about others' contributions. However, the main driver of increased efficiency is the inclusion of R in the public good game, not the donation mechanism. There is no punishment or sanctioning mechanism; the closest is positive, targeted reward. The findings suggest that broad, non-discriminatory inclusion in public good provision is more efficient than relying on voluntary, targeted redistribution by wealthy outsiders.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that including all participants (even the wealthy) in the public good game increases efficiency and group payoffs, compared to a setting where the wealthy can only donate outside the game. While the study does not include punishment or sanctioning, it does show that positive targeted rewards (donations) can further boost cooperation, especially when donors can observe contributions. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that institutional design features that increase the scope of compulsory participation (i.e., including all in the public good game) are likely to increase efficiency, even in the absence of punishment. The absence of punishment means the findings are not directly transferable to punishment-enabled settings, but the mechanisms of reward and information are informative for understanding how efficiency can be increased by institutional design.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 periods per participant (2 phases of 12 periods each), with partner design within each phase.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants is strictly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 up to a maximum (20 ECU for P and M, 20 ECU for R in GCS). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 for all contributors in both PCS and GCS.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. R can make targeted positive donations (rewards) to other players after observing their contributions (at a cost).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit donated by R costs R one unit and gives one unit to the recipient.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is 1:1: each unit donated by R increases recipient's payoff by 1, costs R 1.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 12 periods per phase, and two phases.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants are informed about total contributions and their own payoff. R can purchase information about individual contributions.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only positive, targeted rewards (donations) are possible.
- PCS and GCS differ in more than one aspect (inclusion of R, number of contributors, efficiency of contributions), so effects are confounded.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with punishment or to standard PGGs without wealth heterogeneity.
- The design is not a standard PGG; it includes strong wealth asymmetry and a special role for the super-rich R.
- The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment cost or effectiveness, so cannot inform about punishment effects directly.
