## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in threshold public goods games relevant to climate governance, the presence of local (polycentric) sanctioning institutions leads to higher efficiency and more robust cooperation than global (centralized) institutions. Efficiency is measured by the average group payoff relative to the maximum possible. The model shows that local punishment institutions are more effective at sustaining cooperation, especially under high risk, and can prevent the collapse into full defection. Larger group sizes, higher threshold uncertainty, and high homophily among wealth classes reduce efficiency. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not empirical data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling local (polycentric) punishment institutions in public goods games will increase efficiency relative to global punishment or no punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in smaller groups and under high risk perception. The model directly analyzes efficiency and group payoff, making its results highly relevant for predicting the impact of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions. However, the results are theoretical and may need empirical validation for specific parameterizations.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size N is a key parameter; smaller N (smaller groups) lead to higher efficiency and cooperation. Larger groups make cooperation harder to achieve.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is modeled as a binary choice: cooperate (contribute cost c) or defect (contribute nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a threshold public goods game, not a linear MPCR game, but the cost-benefit structure is explicit: each cooperator pays cost c, and the group must reach a threshold M to avoid risk of loss. The effective 'return' is all-or-nothing based on threshold.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishers pay a cost (punishment tax, π_t) to fund the institution, which then fines defectors (π_f). The cost structure is explicit in the model.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the fine (π_f) imposed on defectors when the institution is active. The model compares local vs global punishment effectiveness.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players are assumed to observe the acts of others and their final or average outcome, but not the full process. This is part of the model's information structure.

## Important Limitations
- The results are entirely theoretical and based on evolutionary game theory models, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not account for real-world complexities such as network structure, communication, or enforcement limitations.
- Punishment is modeled as institutional (local or global), not as peer-to-peer or individually targeted.
- The model does not include reward mechanisms, only punishment.
- The analysis focuses on stationary distributions and long-run outcomes, not on short-term or dynamic effects.
- Parameter values (e.g., group size, risk, punishment cost) are chosen for theoretical exploration and may not map directly to real-world settings.
