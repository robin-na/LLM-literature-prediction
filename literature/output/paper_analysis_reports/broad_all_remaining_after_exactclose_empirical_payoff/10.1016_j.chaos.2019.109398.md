# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Awareness of wealth inequalities breeds animosity
Authors: Zhang, M., Ma, Y. J., Tao, Y., Wang, Z., Shi, L., & Wang, R. W.
Journal: Chaos Solitons & Fractals
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average final wealth level
- normalized difference in final wealth between groups
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation level (frequency of cooperation)
- frequency of punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rate and punishment frequency, but the paper also reports average final wealth (total earnings) and the wealth gap, which are closely related to efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in repeated PD games with costly punishment, the visibility of wealth information interacts with initial wealth inequality to affect both cooperation rates and average final wealth. When initial wealth is unequal, making wealth visible reduces cooperation and leads to lower average final wealth compared to when wealth is hidden. When initial wealth is equal, visibility has little effect on cooperation or final wealth. Punishment is available but rarely used, and its frequency does not significantly differ across treatments. Thus, in environments with inequality, hiding wealth information improves both cooperation and group payoff (final wealth), while visibility exacerbates inequality and reduces efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving efficiency in repeated PD or PGG-like games with punishment, this paper provides evidence that the visibility of wealth information is a strong moderator when initial endowments are unequal: making wealth visible reduces efficiency (average final wealth), while hiding it increases efficiency. The effect is not present when initial endowments are equal. The presence of punishment does not appear to moderate this effect, as punishment is infrequent and not significantly different across treatments. Thus, when modeling efficiency outcomes, consider both initial wealth inequality and information visibility as key moderators, especially in games with available but rarely used punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Pairwise (2-player) repeated PD games; each round is a random pairing.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is stochastic: after each round, there is a 75% chance of continuing. Players do not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose between discrete actions: cooperate, defect, or punish (no continuous contribution).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a discrete action with fixed payoffs: e.g., if a punisher (P) meets a cooperator (C), P gets -5, C gets -5; if P meets D, P gets -2, D gets -2; see payoff matrix.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the payoff matrix; both punisher and target lose points, but the cost/impact ratio is fixed by the matrix.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the total number of rounds; continuation probability is 75% per round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a pairwise repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a multi-player public goods game, so generalizability to group settings may be limited.
- Punishment is available but rarely used, so the findings may not generalize to environments with more frequent or impactful punishment.
- The main manipulation is visibility of wealth information and initial endowment inequality; other game design dimensions are not varied.
- The number of rounds is stochastic and unknown to participants, which may affect behavior compared to fixed-length games.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of reward and punishment cannot be assessed.
