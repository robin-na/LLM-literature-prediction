# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On the (in)effectiveness of rewards in sustaining cooperation
Authors: Vyrastekova, J., & van Soest, D.
Journal: Experimental Economics
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate efficiency (group payoff as % of maximum possible)
- average earnings
- CPR stage efficiency
- distance from social optimum
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of extraction tokens (contribution/withdrawal behavior)
- number of reward tokens sent/received
- correlation between extraction and rewards
- patterns of who rewards whom
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum), extraction behavior, and reward behavior. Efficiency is reported both for the CPR stage and for total payoffs. No punishment/sanctioning is present; only rewards are studied.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated CPR game with groups of 5 over 15 rounds, the introduction of net positive rewards (1:3 impact ratio) leads to significantly lower extraction (closer to the social optimum) and higher CPR-stage efficiency compared to both the unregulated baseline and the transfer reward (1:1) treatment. Transfer rewards do not affect extraction or efficiency. However, total group efficiency (including the reward stage) is similar across treatments, as the efficiency gains in the CPR stage with net positive rewards are offset by welfare losses from not using all reward tokens. The study demonstrates that the effectiveness of rewards in sustaining cooperation and improving efficiency depends critically on the reward's impact ratio.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of peer-based incentives in public-goods-like environments depends on the technology of the incentive: net positive rewards (where the recipient's gain exceeds the giver's cost) can increase efficiency, while transfer rewards (1:1) do not. Although the study uses a CPR game (public bad) rather than a standard public goods game, the structure is closely related. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of enabling a reward mechanism, the reward impact ratio (reward_tech) is a critical moderator. The absence of punishment means the results are not directly about punishment, but the logic may transfer to the design of positive incentives. For prediction, the control (no-reward) efficiency is not improved by transfer rewards, but is improved by net positive rewards in the CPR stage. However, total efficiency may not increase if the reward mechanism itself is not fully utilized.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 players per session; fixed throughout.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds per game (unregulated and regulated), for a total of 30 rounds per subject.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is mentioned; computerized and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (extraction) is discrete but not all-or-nothing; players allocate tokens between two options.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a linear PGG; CPR game with quadratic returns. Social optimum and Nash equilibrium are calculated and reported.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward mechanism is the main treatment variable; two types: transfer (1:1) and net positive (1:3).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward token costs 1 point to the giver in both treatments.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Two levels: 1:1 (transfer) and 1:3 (net positive). Only the 1:3 ratio increases efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is finitely repeated with a known number of rounds (15 per game).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the number of extraction tokens and payoffs of all group members each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player identities are scrambled each round; players can only base reward decisions on current round behavior, not on persistent identities.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a common pool resource (CPR) game (public bad) rather than a standard linear public goods game, so results may not transfer perfectly to PGG settings.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only rewards are studied, so direct evidence on punishment is absent.
- The reward mechanism is peer-to-peer and only two impact ratios (1:1 and 1:3) are tested; other ratios or institutional reward schemes are not examined.
- Total efficiency is not increased by net positive rewards due to welfare losses from unused reward tokens, which may limit the practical impact of the mechanism.
- Player identities are scrambled each round, preventing repeated-game reciprocity; this may affect the generalizability to settings with stable identities.
