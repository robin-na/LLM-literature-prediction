## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in public goods games with pool reward funds, allowing defectors to form their own reward funds (anti-social rewarding) causes a breakdown of cooperation and group efficiency, even when mechanisms to exclude non-rewarders (second-order sanctions) are present. Theoretical analysis shows that anti-social rewarding leads to stable equilibria with low or no cooperation, unless additional countermeasures are implemented. These include making pro-social reward funds more productive than anti-social ones, or making reward funds contingent on the actual production of the public good. Only under these conditions, and with sufficiently strong exclusion of non-rewarders, can high cooperation and efficiency be restored.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of anti-social rewarding mechanisms in public goods games with institutional rewards can dramatically reduce the efficiency gains from positive incentives, even when second-order punishment or exclusion is available. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling anti-social rewarding (or failing to prevent it) will likely result in much lower efficiency in the punishment/reward-enabled condition compared to control, unless the game design includes mechanisms that make pro-social rewards more effective or contingent on public good production. The findings are directly relevant for settings with institutional rewards, but less so for standard punishment-only designs. The results highlight the importance of considering the possibility of anti-social incentives and the structure of reward and punishment technologies when predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes infinite population size and well-mixed groups, which may not capture real-world heterogeneity or network effects.
- Punishment (negative incentives) is not modeled; only reward and exclusion (second-order sanctions) are considered.
- Exclusion (second-order sanctions) is assumed to be costless, which may not be realistic.
- The model focuses on binary contribution choices, though some discussion of continuous traits is included in the supplementary material.
- The results may not generalize to settings where anti-social rewarding is not possible or is prevented by design.
