# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effect of options to reward and punish on behavior in bargaining
Authors: Pevnitskaya, S., & Ryvkin, D.
Journal: Journal of Economics & Management Strategy
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average proposer payoff
- average responder payoff
- aggregate group payoff (efficiency)
- acceptance rate (as it affects realized payoffs)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- offer size
- acceptance decision
- frequency and magnitude of reward/punishment use
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoffs and efficiency (sum of proposer and responder payoffs, relative to the maximum possible). Behavioral outcomes such as offer size, acceptance, and use of reward/punishment are also analyzed, but the paper directly reports efficiency and payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that adding costly punishment and/or reward options for responders in ultimatum bargaining changes both proposer and responder behavior. When only reward is available, proposers make lower offers and efficiency drops. When only punishment is available, acceptance of low offers increases, and efficiency for low offers is higher than baseline. When both reward and punishment are available, both offers and efficiency are highest. The use of reward and punishment is rare and mostly symbolic, so efficiency losses from their direct use are minimal. The main efficiency effects come from changes in offer and acceptance behavior, not from the direct cost of sanctions. The results suggest that punishment options can increase efficiency in settings with low offers, while reward options alone may reduce efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence on how the availability and cost/effectiveness of punishment and reward options affect efficiency in a two-player bargaining environment. While not a public goods game, the findings are relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in asymmetric, bilateral settings with costly sanctions. The results show that costly punishment (with 1:1 cost-impact ratio) can increase efficiency by increasing acceptance of low offers, especially when offers are low, but that reward alone can reduce efficiency. The combination of both instruments yields the highest efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the presence of reward, the cost/effectiveness of punishment, and the baseline offer/acceptance norms. The findings are most transferable to settings with costly, direct, one-to-one punishment and reward, and less so to multiplayer or public goods environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played between two players: one proposer and one responder.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played a series of rounds (5-13 per session, average ~9-11), always with a new partner each round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between subjects during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Offers and responses are continuous (proposer chooses any split, responder can accept/reject, and if accepted, can choose any amount to reward/punish within limits). Not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: for every unit spent by the responder, the proposer's payoff is reduced by 1 and the responder's payoff is reduced by 1 (cost = 1, effectiveness = 1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (each unit spent reduces the proposer's payoff by 1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward is available in UR and UPR treatments; not available in U or UP.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is costly: for every unit spent by the responder, the proposer's payoff increases by 1 and the responder's payoff decreases by 1 (cost = 1, effectiveness = 1).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (each unit spent increases the proposer's payoff by 1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told the number of rounds in advance (see Table 1 for periods per session).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is based on a two-player ultimatum bargaining game, not a multiplayer public goods game, so generalizability to PGG-like environments is limited.
- Punishment and reward are costly and have a 1:1 cost-impact ratio, which is more conservative than many public goods experiments with higher effectiveness.
- The setting is asymmetric and bilateral, so findings may not transfer directly to symmetric, multiplayer, or repeated group settings.
- The use of reward and punishment is rare and mostly symbolic, so the main effects are indirect (via offer and acceptance behavior), not from direct sanctioning.
- No communication or reputation effects are present; results may differ in environments with communication or repeated interactions with the same partner.
- No default contribution or framing manipulations are tested.
