This is a theoretical review and framework paper. It does not report empirical outcomes, but discusses mechanisms and predictions for cooperation, reputation, and punishment in social dilemmas and related games.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that reputation systems can incentivize cooperation through two distinct mechanisms: 'pull' (where evaluators are motivated to cooperate with good actors for direct reciprocal benefit) and 'push' (where evaluators are motivated by normative pressure to cooperate with good actors to maintain their own reputation). It reviews how these mechanisms map onto different game-theoretic models (e.g., coordination games, indirect reciprocity) and discusses their implications for psychology, behavior, and the design of reputation-based interventions. The framework is used to interpret existing empirical findings and to generate predictions about when and how reputation will promote cooperation or punishment, but does not test these predictions directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. However, it offers a conceptual framework that may help interpret when and why reputation, cooperation, and punishment mechanisms (including those in PGGs) are likely to be effective, depending on whether incentives are primarily 'pull' or 'push'. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the structure of the game (e.g., whether it is a coordination game, whether social norms are salient, whether actions are observable) will moderate the effectiveness of reputation and punishment, but does not provide quantitative estimates or direct evidence for efficiency outcomes.
