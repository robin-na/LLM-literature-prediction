Macro-scale analyses discuss institutional complexity and economic development, but not in a controlled PGG-like environment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that institutions, including punishment mechanisms, can alter the payoffs in collective action problems such as public goods games, making cooperation more stable and frequent. The agent-based model demonstrates that when agents can collectively set punishment levels, cooperation can emerge and be maintained, with higher payoffs for cooperators and a reduction in free-riders. The model is illustrative and not based on empirical data. Macro-scale analyses show that institutional complexity and inclusivity are associated with higher economic development, but these are not tested in controlled PGG-like settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that introducing punishment mechanisms in public goods games can increase cooperation and, in model terms, improve payoffs for cooperators. However, it does not provide empirical estimates of efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment in controlled experimental settings. The agent-based model suggests that the ability to set and adjust punishment can help overcome collective action problems, but the findings are illustrative and not directly transferable to quantitative prediction tasks. The paper is useful for understanding mechanisms and for contextualizing the role of punishment, but not for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The agent-based model uses a fixed group size (population of agents), but the exact number is not specified in the main text. The model assumes group-level interaction, and the discussion notes that group size and complexity are important in institutional evolution.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The agent-based model is run over multiple generations (iterations), but not in the sense of repeated rounds as in a standard PGG experiment. The model tracks changes over time.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the model, agents have fixed strategies: they either always cooperate (contribute) or always free-ride (do not contribute). This is an all-or-nothing contribution structure.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model specifies that contributions to the public pot are doubled and redistributed, implying an MPCR of 2 divided by group size. The effect of punishment is discussed in terms of changing payoffs.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is explicitly modeled: punishers pay a cost, and free-riders pay a cost that is twice that amount. The level of punishment is set endogenously by the group.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the fine imposed on free-riders (twice the cost paid by punishers). The model explores the effect of varying punishment levels.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff in PGG-like environments.
- The agent-based model is illustrative and makes strong simplifying assumptions (e.g., fixed strategies, perfect foresight, no second-order free-rider problem).
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency as defined by group payoff relative to full cooperation.
- Macro-scale analyses are correlational and not based on controlled manipulation of punishment or other game design dimensions.
- No discussion of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback in the model.
