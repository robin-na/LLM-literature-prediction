Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the expected payoff to each strategy, with explicit algebraic conditions for when spiteful (costly harming) strategies outperform nonspiteful (do-nothing) strategies. The paper also analyzes the evolutionary dynamics (replicator equations) and equilibrium frequencies, but these are always tied to payoff differences.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated n-player games with an opting-out option, spiteful (costly harming) strategies can sometimes persist, but only under restrictive conditions: low cost-to-harm ratio, small group size, and specific opting-out rules. As group size increases, it becomes harder for spiteful strategies to achieve higher payoffs than nonspiteful strategies, and the basin of attraction for all-nonspiteful populations grows. The opting-out rule (how easily groups disband) is a key moderator: when homogeneous groups (all spiteful or all nonspiteful) are forced to disband, spite can persist more easily, but this is a somewhat artificial rule. The model is mathematically explicit about when spiteful strategies can dominate, and these conditions almost always correspond to lower group efficiency compared to all-nonspiteful populations. The analysis is directly about payoff and efficiency, not just behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public-goods-like n-player games with an opting-out mechanism, the introduction of costly punishment (here, spiteful harming) generally reduces group efficiency unless the cost-to-harm ratio is extremely low and the group size is small. As group size increases, the conditions for punishment/spite to persist become more restrictive, and group efficiency is more likely to be maximized when punishment/spite is absent. The details of the opting-out rule (e.g., whether homogeneous groups are forced to disband) can moderate this effect, but in most realistic settings, punishment/spite is not efficiency-enhancing. The model is not an exact PGG with standard punishment, but the payoff structure and evolutionary logic are closely related, and the results are directly about payoff/efficiency. Thus, the paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment/spite in large groups with standard cost structures will not increase efficiency, and may reduce it.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size n is a key parameter, varied systematically in the analysis. Larger n makes spite/punishment less likely to persist and reduces the conditions under which it can increase payoff.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Game is repeated for an infinite number of rounds (or until group disbands); not a finite repeated game. Results are for the equilibrium of the infinite-horizon process.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harming) choice is all-or-nothing: each player either harms (spiteful) or does nothing (nonspiteful).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Cost to the punisher (spiteful individual) per act is parameter C; lower C makes spite more likely to persist, but in all cases, spite reduces group efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is H/C; lower C/H (i.e., more effective punishment) makes spite more likely to persist, but does not increase group efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is about costly harming (spite), not costly helping (public goods provision), though the structure is closely related.
- Punishment is modeled as unconditional costly harming, not as targeted punishment of defectors.
- The opting-out rule is stylized and may not correspond to realistic group disbandment processes.
- No communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms are included.
- Results may not generalize to standard public goods games with targeted punishment or to settings with more complex social structure.
