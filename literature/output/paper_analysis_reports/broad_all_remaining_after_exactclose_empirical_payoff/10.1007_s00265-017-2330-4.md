## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that the coevolution of owner aggressiveness (defense) and intruder cautiousness (sensitivity to defense) can stabilize ownership of resources, even when defense is not directly effective at preventing theft. If intruders can observe or learn about owner aggressiveness and adjust their behavior accordingly, a deterrence effect emerges: owners become more aggressive, intruders become more cautious, and theft is reduced. This effect can occur even if defense is functionally equivalent to costly punishment with no direct preventive effect. The model demonstrates that variation in aggressiveness and the ability to respond to it are sufficient to trigger this feedback, leading to stable defense and low theft. However, the outcomes are primarily at the level of individual behavior and evolutionary stability, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (costly defense) can deter antisocial behavior (theft) and stabilize cooperation (ownership) even when direct preventive effects are weak or absent, as long as individuals can observe and respond to others' willingness to punish. However, the model is not a public goods game and does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its relevance to predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments is indirect: it suggests that the deterrence effect of punishment may be underestimated if only direct preventive effects are considered, and that variation in punishment propensity and observability can be important moderators. The findings are most useful for mechanism reasoning and for understanding the conditions under which punishment can stabilize cooperation, but do not provide quantitative predictions for efficiency outcomes in PGGs.
