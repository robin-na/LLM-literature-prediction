# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: REWARD AND PUNISHMENT AS INFLUENCE TECHNIQUES FOR ACHIEVEMENT OF COOPERATION UNDER INEQUITY
Authors: SCHMITT, D. R., & MARWELL, G.
Journal: Human Relations
Year: 1970

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of time spent on cooperation
- steady-state cooperation achieved
- use of reward and punishment strategies
- effect of communication on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: time spent on cooperative task, whether steady-state cooperation is achieved, and the use of influence strategies (reward, punishment, both, or neither). There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, though the design and discussion reference payoffs and their distribution.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a dyadic task with unequal cooperative payoffs, the ability to reward (transfer money) is much more effective than the ability to punish (subtract money) in inducing and sustaining high rates of cooperation. Groups where the reward strategy was used spent more than twice as much time cooperating compared to those using punishment or no strategy. Communication between subjects further increased the use of rewards and the likelihood of achieving steady-state cooperation, particularly among males. Punishment, when used alone, was generally ineffective in producing cooperation, and its use declined over time. The study does not report direct measures of group efficiency or total earnings, but the behavioral outcomes suggest that reward-based influence is more conducive to sustained cooperation than punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the effects of punishment and reward on cooperation in a setting adjacent to public goods games. The findings suggest that punishment alone is generally ineffective at inducing sustained cooperation, while reward (and especially the combination of reward and communication) is much more effective. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is dyadic, with asymmetric payoffs and the ability for only one player to reward or punish, which differs from standard PGGs. Thus, the main value for prediction is as a caution that punishment may not always increase cooperation or efficiency, especially when reward is also available and more salient.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups are dyads (2 players).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment is divided into timed segments (e.g., 50 minutes per segment), not discrete rounds. The task is continuous within each segment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Verbal communication is manipulated: not available in pre-talk and post-talk segments, available in the talk segment. Communication increases cooperation, especially among males.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cooperative task gives 1/10 cent to A and 7/10 cent to B per successful response; individual task gives 2/10 cent to each. Marginal per-capita return is asymmetric and not standard PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (B subtracts from A) costs B nothing per unit; only A loses money.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each press of the (-) button subtracts one count (1/10 cent) from A's total; B can subtract up to 80 cents per segment.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward (B gives to A) is available in key segments; reward is much more effective than punishment in inducing cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding costs B one count (1/10 cent) and gives A one count.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each press of the (+) button transfers one count from B to A.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the length of each segment (e.g., 50 minutes), but the task is continuous, not round-based.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject's panel shows both their own and the other's cumulative earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Only B can punish or reward A, so the source is always known.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses dyads with asymmetric payoffs and only one player able to punish or reward, which differs from standard public goods games.
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare are reported; outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates, strategy use).
- Punishment is costless to the punisher, which is not typical of most PGG punishment designs.
- The continuous, time-based task structure differs from round-based PGGs.
- Sex differences are pronounced, but the sample size for female groups is small and generalization is limited.
- Findings may not generalize to larger groups or to settings where both players can punish/reward.
