Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels and punishment behavior. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare is reported. The paper discusses the implications for efficiency and norm enforcement but does not provide explicit payoff or efficiency calculations.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that both second-party and third-party punishment increase contributions in a one-shot public goods game, with the effect size increasing as opportunities for third-party punishment and reciprocity increase. The order of mean contributions is: no punishment < second-party punishment < one-way third-party punishment < simultaneous two-way third-party punishment < sequential two-way third-party punishment. The study finds that third-party punishment is motivated by both indignation and group reciprocity, and that the presence of third-party punishment increases the expected cost of norm violation, leading to higher contributions. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (especially third-party punishment with reciprocity) increases contributions in a one-shot public goods game, relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on contribution rates, and suggest that the structure of punishment (second-party vs. third-party, reciprocity opportunities) is an important moderator. For efficiency prediction, the results imply that efficiency is likely to increase with punishment, but the magnitude cannot be directly inferred from the reported data.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group has 4 players; each session has two parallel 4-person groups (total 8 per session).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; only a single round per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each participant chooses how much of their 25 EMU endowment to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each EMU spent on punishment reduces the punisher's earnings by 1 EMU and the target's earnings by 2 EMUs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 EMU spent reduces target's payoff by 2 EMUs (punishment multiplier = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributions, participants are shown group total contribution, individual contributions of others, and their own gross payoff.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Single-blind anonymity: punishment is anonymous; punished players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
- All sessions are one-shot; results may not generalize to repeated games.
- The subject pool consists of college students, which may limit external validity.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting, which may not capture all real-world complexities.
