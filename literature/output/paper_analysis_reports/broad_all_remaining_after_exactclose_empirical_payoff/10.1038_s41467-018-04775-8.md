# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Dispositional free riders do not free ride on punishment
Authors: Weber, T. O., Weisel, O., & Gächter, S.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment expenditure
- frequency of punishment
- emotional responses (anger, etc.)
- beliefs about others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on contribution behavior, punishment behavior, and emotional responses. It does not report group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus. Payoff-based outcomes are not analyzed directly.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the availability of punishment leads dispositional free riders (DFR) to increase their contributions to the public good to levels similar to dispositional conditional cooperators (DCC). Both DFR and DCC are equally likely to punish non-cooperators, and their punishment expenditures and motives are similar, regardless of whether punishment reduces payoff inequality (3:1 ratio) or not (1:1 ratio). The presence of punishment thus increases cooperation among DFR, but the paper does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, focusing instead on behavioral outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a one-shot public goods game increases contributions among individuals who would otherwise free ride, and that both free riders and conditional cooperators are willing to punish non-cooperators. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation even among those predisposed to free ride, which is likely to translate into higher efficiency in similar game designs. The lack of direct efficiency data is a limitation for quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all public goods games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot (single round) public goods game in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.6 (multiplier) / 4 (players) = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 MU per punishment point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: In study 1, 1 punishment point destroys 2 MU (2:1 ratio). In study 2, either 3:1 or 1:1 ratio, randomly assigned.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly one-shot; players know there is only one round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown the individual contributions of each group member before the punishment stage.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; only behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, emotions) are analyzed.
- The experiment is one-shot, so findings may not generalize to repeated or dynamic public goods games.
- The sample consists of university students, which may limit generalizability.
- Reward mechanisms are not studied, so no evidence is provided on the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The study excludes participants who could not be classified as DCC or DFR, potentially omitting relevant behavioral types.
