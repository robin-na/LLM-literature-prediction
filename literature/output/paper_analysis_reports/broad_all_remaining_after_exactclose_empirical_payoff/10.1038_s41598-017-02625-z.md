Payoff is used as the basis for evolutionary success, but explicit group efficiency or welfare is not directly reported. Outcomes are primarily about the prevalence of cooperation and the role of commitment mechanisms, not direct group payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in one-shot games, the coexistence of proposers and acceptors can be necessary to keep defectors out of the population, especially when the cost of commitment must be paid before knowing if the other will accept (prior commitments). In repeated games, proposers alone can sustain cooperation, and the presence of acceptors can actually reduce cooperation by making it easier for defectors to invade. The introduction of an apology-forgiveness mechanism (where apologies after defection can restore cooperation) can be exploited by acceptors if the apology cost is too low, leading to lower cooperation. The results are framed in terms of evolutionary stability and the stationary distribution of strategies, with average payoff as the fitness criterion, but do not directly report group efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, but it does offer adjacent theoretical insights about how commitment mechanisms (which can be seen as a form of pre-emptive contract or compensation for defection) affect the prevalence of cooperation and the exclusion of defectors. The findings suggest that the design of commitment mechanisms (timing of costs, ability to recognize intentions, presence of apology-forgiveness) can strongly moderate the success of cooperation-promoting strategies and thus the average payoff in the population. However, because the model is based on the prisoner's dilemma and not a public goods game, and does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning as in PGGs, its direct predictive value for the downstream task is limited. The results are most relevant for understanding how alternative mechanisms to punishment (commitments, compensation, apology) might affect cooperation and payoff in related social dilemmas.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model uses pairwise (2-player) games throughout, both in one-shot and repeated settings.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both one-shot (single round) and repeated games (with high continuation probability, e.g., ω=0.9) are analyzed.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Communication is discussed as a way to know intentions in the RC model, but is not explicitly modeled as chat between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in the Donation game (prisoner's dilemma).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff matrix is parameterized by benefit b and cost c; marginal return is b-c for cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game structure (one-shot vs repeated) is known to players/strategies by construction.

## Important Limitations
- The model is based on the prisoner's dilemma (Donation game), not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- No explicit punishment or peer sanctioning mechanism is modeled; compensation for defection is not equivalent to punishment.
- Outcomes are primarily about the evolutionary stability and frequency of strategies, not direct measures of group efficiency or welfare.
- All games are pairwise (2-player); group size effects are not addressed.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are theoretical and simulation-based.
- No explicit analysis of the effect of game design dimensions on efficiency as defined in public goods games.
