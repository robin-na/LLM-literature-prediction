Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical/philosophical review of experimental and neuroeconomic studies, focusing on the psychological motives behind altruistic punishment and cooperation. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or other payoff-based outcomes, but rather discusses behavioral and motivational outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that while experimental economics and neuroeconomics have revealed robust patterns of altruistic punishment and cooperation in public goods and related games, these studies do not provide decisive evidence about the underlying psychological motives (altruism vs. egoism). Behavioral outcomes such as costly punishment of free-riders are well established, but the interpretation of these actions (e.g., as motivated by fairness, revenge, or self-interest) remains open. Neuroimaging studies (e.g., de Quervain et al. 2004) show reward-related brain activation during punishment, but this does not conclusively distinguish between altruistic and egoistic motives. The paper argues that more fine-grained experimental designs and controls are needed to advance the debate.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games or variants. It is useful for understanding the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment behavior, but does not report or synthesize data on group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers conceptual context but no quantitative or qualitative evidence about efficiency outcomes under punishment. It highlights the need for careful experimental design and the limitations of current evidence, but does not inform the mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency.

- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Rewarding/helping behavior is discussed in the context of dictator games and some PGG variants, but not as a systematically varied dimension.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. In punishment conditions, players are informed about others' contributions/investments before punishing. No systematic analysis.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a conceptual and philosophical review, not an empirical study; it does not report or synthesize efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- All outcome discussions are about behavioral and motivational variables (punishment, cooperation, emotion, neural activation), not efficiency or payoff.
- Game design dimensions are discussed only contextually, not systematically varied or analyzed for their effects.
- The paper highlights the limitations of current experimental and neuroeconomic methods for resolving questions about motives, and does not provide evidence for predicting efficiency under punishment.
