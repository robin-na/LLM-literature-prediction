The main dependent variable is the mean offer rate in a simulated ultimatum game. No direct analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff is reported. The study focuses on how punishment and social structure affect evolved offer rates, not on efficiency or surplus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a simulated ultimatum game, third-party punishment (altruistic punishment) does not generally lead to fair allocations, except in linearly structured populations where fair offers can evolve even without punishment. As the punishment multiplier (punishment impact per unit cost) increases, populations with spatial structure exhibit a sharp transition from low to very high offers, but these are not necessarily fair. The number of neighbors (social structure) strongly moderates both baseline offers and the threshold at which punishment induces high offers. The main outcome is the mean offer rate, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how punishment cost-effectiveness and social structure (number of neighbors) affect behavioral outcomes (mean offer rates) in an ultimatum game, which is adjacent to public goods games. It does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes from punishment in PGG-like environments. However, it suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in changing behavior is highly sensitive to the punishment cost/impact ratio and the social network structure. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the importance of considering punishment cost-effectiveness and group structure, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data.

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Population size is fixed at 625 (or 676 for hexagonal), but the main focus is on neighborhood size (number of neighbors per agent), not total player count per group.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations run for 30,000 generations, but each 'game' is a one-shot ultimatum game; repeated interactions are not allowed between the same agents.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or recognition is allowed between agents; model explicitly excludes chat or memory.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Offer and acceptance thresholds are continuous variables in [0,1]; not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is a continuous trait (pi in [0,1]); the punishment multiplier M determines the impact per unit cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (M) is systematically varied from 0 to 6.0; higher M leads to sharp transitions in offer rates, but not necessarily to fair or efficient outcomes.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents do not know or use round information; the simulation is evolutionary with fixed generations.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses an agent-based simulation of the ultimatum game, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG environments.
- The main outcome is mean offer rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or surplus is provided.
- Cultural, psychological, and real-world factors are explicitly excluded from the model.
- The model does not allow for communication, memory, or reputation effects.
- Reward mechanisms are not considered.
