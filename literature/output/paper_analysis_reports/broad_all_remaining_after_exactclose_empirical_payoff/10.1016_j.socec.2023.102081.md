# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: On the limits of hierarchy in public goods games: A survey and meta-analysis on the effects of design variables on cooperation
Authors: Billinger, S., & Rosenbaum, S. M.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The meta-analysis uses contribution rate (percentage of endowment contributed) as the main dependent variable. There is no direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as the primary outcome, though some studies in the review mention earnings or team production.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The meta-analysis finds that, among various design variables in hierarchical PGGs, only punishment reliably sustains higher contribution rates over time compared to baseline. The effect size for punishment is notably higher (d=0.75) than for other mechanisms. Rewards, monitoring, and other variables have positive but short-lived effects. The effect of punishment is stronger when it is free for the punisher than when it is costly. Group stability (partner matching), monitoring, and asymmetric information also increase cooperation, but not as durably as punishment. The analysis is based on contribution rates, not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in hierarchical PGGs increases and sustains contribution rates, which, in standard PGGs, is closely linked to higher efficiency. However, the meta-analysis does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so predictions about efficiency must be inferred from the contribution results. The effect of punishment is robust across studies and is stronger when punishment is free. Other design variables (rewards, monitoring, group stability) have positive but less durable effects. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, especially when punishment is not costly to the punisher, but the exact magnitude for efficiency must be inferred indirectly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (player count) is coded and analyzed as a moderator. Effects on cooperation vary: d=0.29 for 3, d=0.41 for 4, d=1.08 for 5, d=0.14 for 8. The effect of punishment is generally positive across sizes, but the magnitude varies.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Number of rounds (iterations) is coded and analyzed as a moderator. Effects on cooperation vary by number of rounds, with some evidence that longer games allow for more sustained effects of punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (costly vs. free) is coded and analyzed. Free punishment has a much larger effect on cooperation (d=1.29) than costly punishment (d=0.48).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward presence is coded and analyzed. Rewards increase cooperation (d=0.67 when present vs. d=0.40 when absent), but effects are short-lived compared to punishment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Information symmetry/asymmetry is coded and analyzed. Asymmetric information increases cooperation (d=1.17) compared to symmetric (d=0.32). This is about information on contributions and/or payoffs, not explicit summary screens.

## Important Limitations
- The meta-analysis uses contribution rate as the main outcome, not efficiency or group payoff, so effects on efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- Heterogeneity across studies in design details, protocols, and implementation may limit comparability.
- Some relevant studies could not be included due to lack of data or missing baselines.
- No direct analysis of marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment/reward effectiveness per unit cost, or communication effects.
- External validity may be limited due to the relatively small number of studies and lab-based settings.
