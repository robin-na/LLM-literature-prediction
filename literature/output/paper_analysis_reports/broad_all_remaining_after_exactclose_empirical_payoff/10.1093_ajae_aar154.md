## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that collective reputation mechanisms with local peer punishment can sustain higher efficiency (average payoffs, welfare) than individual reputation, as long as group size is not too small or too large and peer monitoring is present. Peer punishment deters shirking and allows for softer public punishment, increasing the duration of efficient cooperation. The optimal group size increases with the discount factor, the informativeness of public signals, and the quality premium, but decreases with the relative gain from shirking. Without peer monitoring, collective reputation is less efficient than individual reputation. Perfect global peer monitoring allows for arbitrarily large efficient groups. Mandatory traceability (which enables individual punishment) can reduce welfare by undermining group-based peer punishment. The model provides clear comparative statics and welfare implications for the design of collective brands and group-based sanctions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling peer punishment (local or global) in repeated group settings with imperfect public monitoring increases efficiency and group payoffs, compared to settings without such punishment. The model is not a literal public goods game, but is adjacent: it is a repeated moral hazard game with group-level payoffs, endogenous peer punishment, and collective reputation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment mechanisms (especially peer punishment) on efficiency, conditional on group size, monitoring structure, and information. The paper also provides comparative statics on how efficiency gains from punishment depend on group size, monitoring, and information, which can inform predictions about moderators. However, the model is theoretical and does not provide empirical effect sizes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (number of firms) is a key parameter; the model analyzes how efficiency and optimality depend on it. Efficiency is maximized at an intermediate group size: too small or too large groups are less efficient.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated (infinite horizon), and efficiency gains from punishment depend on the discount factor (patience/frequency of interaction). Higher discount factor (more rounds/future importance) increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort/contribution is binary: each firm chooses high or low effort (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: peer punishment is implemented by firms switching to low effort (collective shirking) in response to detected shirking. The cost is the loss of future payoffs, not an explicit cost per unit. Lower cost of punishment (i.e., more effective peer punishment) increases efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by how quickly and certainly peer punishment spreads (depends on information structure). More effective (faster, more global) peer punishment increases efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated and all players know this structure; the importance of the future (discount factor) is a key parameter. More future (higher discount factor) increases efficiency.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players (firms) observe public signals (summary of group performance) and, depending on information structure, may observe neighbors' efforts. More informative public signals increase efficiency.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the local peer monitoring structure, each firm can identify which neighbor shirked (perfect local observability). In global peer monitoring, all firms can identify all shirkers. This identification is crucial for peer punishment to be effective.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical effect sizes or direct experimental evidence.
- The setting is not a literal public goods game, but a repeated moral hazard game with group payoffs and endogenous peer punishment.
- Punishment is implemented as endogenous peer sanctions (collective shirking), not as explicit costly punishment actions.
- Assumes perfect local or global observability of effort among group members, which may not hold in real-world settings.
- No explicit modeling of communication, reward mechanisms, or continuous contribution choices.
- Assumes infinite horizon and rational, forward-looking agents.
