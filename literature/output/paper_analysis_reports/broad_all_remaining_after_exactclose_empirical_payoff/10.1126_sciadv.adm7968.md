# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The nasty neighbor effect in humans
Authors: Romano, A., Gross, J., & De Dreu, C. K. W.
Journal: Science Advances
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

- total earnings
- joint welfare
- resource wastage
- group bonus
- individual bonus
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment in attack

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are investments in attack/defense (which reduce joint welfare), trust/trustworthiness (which increase joint welfare), and actual payoffs (earnings). The paper explicitly analyzes how conflict investments reduce group earnings and joint welfare, and how trust/cooperation increases them.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that, contrary to standard expectations from in-group favoritism, people are more likely to waste resources in costly competition (attack and defense) with in-group members than with out-group members or strangers. This 'nasty neighbor effect' is observed in attacker-defender contest games and in a nested social dilemma with an attack option. The effect is robust across 51 countries, a field experiment in Kenya, and representative UK samples. The increased conflict investment with in-group members directly reduces efficiency and group earnings. The effect is not explained by a lack of in-group favoritism in cooperation, as trust and trustworthiness are still higher toward in-group members. Instead, the two phenomena coexist and are uncorrelated at the individual and country level. The nasty neighbor effect is stronger when individuals perceive greater resource scarcity or lower status within their group. In a nested public goods game with an attack option, participants assigned more costly deductions to lower-status in-group members, further reducing group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in contest or public-goods-like environments with an option for costly attack or punishment, the availability and use of such options can reduce group efficiency, especially when directed at in-group members. The findings suggest that enabling costly punishment or attack (even when it is not norm-enforcing but status-seeking or competitive) can decrease total group earnings and joint welfare, particularly under conditions of perceived resource scarcity or status competition. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the presence of a punishment/attack option, especially with high punishment effectiveness (e.g., 1:3 cost-impact ratio), is likely to reduce efficiency relative to a control without such options, even in the absence of explicit norm violations. The effect is robust across cultures and group compositions. However, the contest/attack framing is not a standard PGG, so direct quantitative transfer to classic PGGs with norm-enforcing punishment should be cautious.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main contest games are two-player (attacker-defender). Study 5 uses groups of 4 in a nested social dilemma. The effect is observed in both dyadic and small group settings.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants make multiple independent one-shot decisions (e.g., 27 attacker and 27 defender decisions in Study 1; 16 per role in Study 4; 1 per pairing in Study 2). No repeated interaction with feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants in any study.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment choices are continuous (0-10 MU in contest games, 0-5 MU in nested dilemma). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the nested social dilemma (Study 5), group pool and universal pool return 0.5 MU per MU contributed to each group member. In contest games, investments are lost (no return), so MPCR is effectively 0 for conflict investments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In contest games, each MU invested in attack/defense is lost to the investor. In Study 5, each deduction point costs 1 MU to the punisher. High cost relative to possible gain.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In Study 5, each deduction point reduces the target's earnings by 3 MU (1:3 cost-impact ratio). In contest games, attack can result in taking all non-invested MU from the defender if successful.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of decisions/rounds in advance; all are independent and one-shot.

## Important Limitations
- The main contest/attack games are not standard public goods games; they model costly competition rather than norm-enforcing punishment in a PGG.
- Punishment/attack is not conditional on norm violation but is used for status competition or resource acquisition.
- Findings may not generalize directly to classic PGGs with punishment for free-riding.
- All decisions are one-shot and anonymous; no repeated interaction or reputation effects.
- No communication or social context beyond group tags (nationality, ethnicity, minimal group).
- No reward mechanisms are present, so effects of positive incentives are not tested.
- The effect of punishment/attack on efficiency is always negative in these games by design, as all conflict investments are wasteful at the group level.
