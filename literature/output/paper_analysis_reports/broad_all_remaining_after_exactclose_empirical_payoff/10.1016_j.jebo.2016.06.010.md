Outcome notes:
The paper's main dependent variables are social surplus (welfare), cost of production, and allocation efficiency. It does not report on individual-level contribution or cooperation rates, but rather on market-level efficiency and allocation outcomes as a function of honesty and sanctions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that: (1) Increasing the fraction of honest sellers can reduce or increase social surplus depending on the initial prevalence of honesty and the elasticity of demand. When honesty is rare, more honesty can increase production costs and reduce welfare; when honesty is common, more honesty can improve efficiency. (2) Sanctions (punishment) are a more reliable tool for improving efficiency, as they do not distort the ranking of private and social costs. (3) The effects of honesty among buyers and sellers are asymmetric: honesty among sellers affects production efficiency, while honesty among buyers affects allocation efficiency. (4) Policies that increase individual compliance (e.g., moral suasion) may have no effect or even negative effects on market-level efficiency, depending on market structure and the distribution of honesty. (5) The relationship between individual-level compliance and market-level efficiency is complex and non-linear.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on efficiency is more reliably positive than the effect of increasing intrinsic motivation (morality/honesty). In environments analogous to public goods games with market entry/exit and endogenous prices, punishment that is uniform across agents improves efficiency by aligning private and social costs, while increasing the fraction of intrinsically honest agents can have mixed or even negative effects on efficiency, especially when honesty is rare. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment (extrinsic sanctions) is more likely to increase efficiency than relying on intrinsic motivation, and that the effect of punishment may depend on the distribution of compliance and market structure. However, the model is not a direct PGG or experimental result, so quantitative predictions should be made with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes a continuum of buyers and sellers, not a fixed small group as in standard PGGs. The number of agents is not manipulated but is a market-level parameter.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Sanctions are modeled as expected penalties (extrinsic punishment) for cheating, with cost to the cheater but not to a punisher. Increasing penalties reduces cheating and improves efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is captured by the penalty rate and detection probability; higher effectiveness (higher penalty or detection) increases efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model is not a direct public goods game or laboratory experiment, but a market with endogenous entry/exit and prices.
- Punishment is modeled as institutional (state) sanctions, not peer punishment as in many PGG experiments.
- The model assumes a continuum of agents and does not address small group or repeated interaction effects.
- No direct mapping to standard PGG parameters such as MPCR, group size, or round structure.
- No analysis of communication, reward, or other behavioral interventions beyond honesty and sanctions.
