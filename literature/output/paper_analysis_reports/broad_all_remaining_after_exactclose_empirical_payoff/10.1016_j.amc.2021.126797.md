Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the average payoff (efficiency) of the population, as a function of punishment cost, fine, and tax. The paper also reports on the stationary fractions of cooperators, defectors, and punishers, but these are interpreted in relation to group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that mercenary punishment (punishers who do not contribute but punish defectors) can promote cooperation and increase group efficiency in structured populations, even without an additional tax, provided the punishment cost is not too high and the fine is sufficiently large. Introducing a tax to support punishers expands the parameter space where defectors are eliminated and efficiency is high, but only if the punishment cost is significant. If the fine is too low, the tax can actually harm cooperators and allow defectors to coexist with punishers, reducing efficiency. There is an optimal punishment cost that maximizes average payoff. The model also reveals regions of cyclic dominance among strategies, leading to complex spatial dynamics.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment cost, punishment effectiveness (fine), and the presence of a tax to support punishment interact to determine group efficiency in a spatial public goods game. For prediction, it suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment) if the punishment cost is moderate and the fine is high, especially if a tax is used to support punishers when costs are high. However, if punishment is too costly or the fine is too low, efficiency may not improve or may even decrease. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with three strategies (C, D, P), and the results are sensitive to the exact parameter values of punishment cost, fine, and tax. The paper does not provide empirical data but offers detailed phase diagrams and payoff analyses that can inform predictions about the efficiency impact of punishment under varying game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are arranged on an L x L square lattice; each player interacts in 5-member groups (self + 4 neighbors). System sizes from L=200 to L=800 are used.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10^5 to 10^6 Monte Carlo steps; stationary (long-run) outcomes are reported.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is all-or-nothing: cooperators contribute c=1, defectors and punishers contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor r=3, group size N=5, so MPCR = r/N = 0.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (G_p) is systematically varied. Low cost allows punishers to dominate; intermediate cost maximizes efficiency; high cost leads to defector dominance.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine, beta) is systematically varied. Higher fine increases the region where efficiency is high and defectors are eliminated.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical/modeling; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- Findings are specific to spatially structured populations and may not generalize to well-mixed or real-world settings.
- Only three strategies are modeled (C, D, P), with punishers not contributing to the public good.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are considered.
- Results are sensitive to parameter choices (punishment cost, fine, tax) and system size (finite-size effects).
- Stationary (long-run) outcomes are reported; transient dynamics are not analyzed.
