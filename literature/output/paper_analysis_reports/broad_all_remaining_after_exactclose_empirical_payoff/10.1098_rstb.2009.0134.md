It discusses group welfare and average payoff in a qualitative sense but does not report empirical or quantitative payoff or efficiency outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that human cooperation at large scales is best explained by cultural group selection, where rapid cultural adaptation leads to persistent differences between groups. These differences, when linked to group-beneficial norms (such as those enforced by punishment and reward systems), can spread through group competition, imitation of successful groups, and selective migration. The authors posit that moral systems enforced by sanctions and rewards increase group welfare and the prevalence of prosocial motives. However, the paper is theoretical and does not present empirical data or experimental results on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or similar environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for why punishment and reward systems may support the evolution and maintenance of cooperation and group-beneficial norms, potentially increasing group welfare. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. Its value for prediction is mainly in supporting the plausibility of positive effects of sanctions on group-level outcomes, but it does not inform how specific game design dimensions or control efficiency moderate these effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Rewards are discussed as part of moral systems that enforce cooperation and increase group welfare, but not in a game design or experimental context.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not present empirical or experimental data on efficiency, payoff, or public goods game outcomes.

- The discussion of sanctions and rewards is general and not tied to specific parameterizations or mechanisms relevant to public goods games.
- No direct analysis of how game design dimensions moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
