# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Both Rewards and Moral Praise Can Increase the Prosocial Decisions: Revealed in a Modified Ultimatum Game Task
Authors: Wang, X. L., Han, J. H., Li, F. H., & Cao, B. H.
Journal: Frontiers in Psychology
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rate of self-benefiting (unfair) division
- frequency of prosocial (norm-compliant) decisions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the acceptance rate of unfair/self-benefiting offers in a modified ultimatum game. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The study focuses on behavioral compliance with fairness norms, not on aggregate payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that participants are less likely to reject unfair/self-benefiting offers (i.e., more likely to act selfishly) when doing so would result in a material loss (risk condition). When economic or moral rewards are provided for norm compliance, participants are more likely to reject unfair offers and act prosocially. The effect is stronger for economic rewards than for moral rewards, and is most pronounced for extremely unfair divisions. The study does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, focusing instead on behavioral compliance with fairness norms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. While it does not use a public goods game or analyze efficiency/payoff outcomes, it shows that the threat of material loss for norm compliance reduces prosocial behavior, and that rewards (especially economic) can restore norm compliance. The findings suggest that in environments where norm compliance is costly, punishment alone may not sustain cooperation, but rewards can help. However, because the outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, the paper's direct value for predicting efficiency under punishment is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a two-player (dyadic) interaction: participant and anonymous counterpart.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 120 trials per block (condition), with multiple blocks per study.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: accept or reject the proposed division.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Both economic and moral (praise) rewards are implemented as compensation for norm compliance in Study 2.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Economic reward is additional monetary payment; cost to experimenter, not to other players. Moral reward is a pictorial medal (no monetary cost).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Economic reward restores about 50% of the total remuneration; moral reward is symbolic. Both increase prosocial (norm-compliant) behavior, with economic reward more effective.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials per block (120), and the block structure is explained.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a modified ultimatum game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is implemented; only reward (economic or moral) is tested.
- The main outcome is behavioral (acceptance/rejection of unfair offers), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Laboratory setting with hypothetical or low-stakes monetary amounts may limit ecological validity.
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player or repeated public goods environments.
