## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This cross-country lab experiment uses a modified trust game with third-party punishment and reward. Third parties can spend up to 5 ECU per round to punish or reward the receiver, with a 1:1 cost-impact ratio. The experiment manipulates norm uncertainty by varying whether the third party is from the same country as the sender and receiver (same-country) or from a different country (cross-country). Third parties observe the sender's expectation about the receiver's return and use this as norm-relevant information. The main findings are: (1) third-party intervention (punishment/reward) is common and contingent on whether the receiver meets the sender's expectation; (2) both cooperation (transfers, returns) and efficiency (sender-receiver welfare, social welfare) are higher in the cross-country treatment; (3) third-party intervention is more responsive to norm-relevant information under higher norm uncertainty (cross-country); (4) the norm enforced by third parties shifts depending on context (trustworthiness in same-country, equal-sharing in cross-country). The presence and responsiveness of third-party punishment/reward increases group efficiency, especially when norm uncertainty is high.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment and reward in a trust-game-like environment increases efficiency (group welfare), especially when norm uncertainty is high (e.g., cross-group or cross-country settings). The effect is mediated by the responsiveness of third parties to norm-relevant information. The punishment mechanism here is costly (1:1 cost-impact), and the game is not a standard PGG but a trust game with continuous transfer and return choices. The findings suggest that, in environments with higher norm uncertainty or social salience, enabling punishment/reward can yield larger efficiency gains. The control (no punishment) condition is not directly present, but the same-country treatment with less responsive third-party intervention can serve as a lower bound. The results are most transferable to settings where third-party punishment is possible, norm cues are available, and the punishment/reward is not highly leveraged (1:1). For prediction, the presence of third-party punishment/reward, higher norm uncertainty, and norm-relevant information all increase the expected efficiency relative to control.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: sender, receiver, third party.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per session; one round randomly selected for payment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (transfer and return) choices are continuous (integer amounts within a range), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return for sender is 3 (tripling of transfer), but the game is a trust game, not a PGG. For the sender, each unit transferred is tripled for the receiver.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (third party) pays 1 ECU per unit of punishment or reward (up to 5 ECU per round).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is 1:1: each ECU spent by third party reduces or increases receiver's payoff by 1 ECU.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party can both punish and reward the receiver.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1 ECU per unit, same as punishment.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1: each ECU spent increases receiver's payoff by 1 ECU.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party observes all decisions (transfer, return, sender's expectation). Sender and receiver do not see third party's action or each other's payoffs beyond their own.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Roles are fixed (sender, receiver, third party), but groups are randomly rematched each round. No evidence that the identity of the punisher is revealed to the punished; anonymity is maintained.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game, not a standard public goods game, so direct transferability to PGG settings may be limited.
- There is no pure control condition without third-party punishment/reward; all treatments include the possibility of third-party intervention.
- Punishment and reward are both possible, but the cost-effectiveness is low (1:1), which may limit the generalizability to settings with higher punishment leverage.
- Norm-relevant information is always present (sender's expectation), so the effect of punishment/reward without norm cues is not identified.
- The cross-country manipulation changes both norm uncertainty and social salience, so the precise mechanism is not fully isolated.
- The sample is limited to Chinese and Dutch university students, which may affect generalizability.
- The game is played for 10 rounds with random rematching, which may not capture long-term or repeated group effects.
