## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. Exclusion-based punishment (TPR) is more stable and efficient, provided defectors cannot exploit cooperators. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency. Theoretical predictions are made for experimental settings, suggesting that people will avoid over-punishment and switch methods when possible.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not report new empirical data; its predictions are based on scenario analysis and references to existing experiments.
- Payoff matrices and efficiency claims are based on stylized models and may not capture all complexities of real experimental or field settings.
- The analysis assumes agents can switch between punishment methods, which may not be possible in all experimental designs.
- No direct analysis of some game design dimensions (e.g., chat, default contribution, reward mechanisms, knowledge of rounds).
