Journal: Journal of Theoretical Biology

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean payoff of harvesters
- mean payoff of enforcers
- group-level cooperation (sustained resource, i.e., forest health)
- welfare under different equilibria (cooperative vs. defective)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- fraction of honest/corrupt enforcers
- strategy composition dynamics
Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variables are the mean payoffs of harvesters and enforcers, and the equilibrium composition of strategies. The paper explicitly analyzes the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher group welfare) is sustained, and how this is affected by punishment, corruption, and information. Behavioral outcomes (cooperation/defection rates) are discussed, but always in relation to payoff and welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment (via hired enforcers) can sustain high efficiency (cooperation and group welfare) only if a sufficient fraction of enforcers are honest. If corruption is prevalent among enforcers, punishment fails and efficiency collapses. The system is bistable: both high- and low-efficiency equilibria are possible. Small rates of random exploration (mutation) among enforcers can tip the system toward either high or low efficiency, depending on whether honest or corrupt exploration is more common. Providing information about enforcer honesty (i.e., making corrupt enforcers identifiable) greatly increases the likelihood of high-efficiency outcomes. The model shows that interventions targeting enforcer honesty (education, incentives, transparency) are more effective for improving efficiency than interventions targeting harvesters directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency in public-goods-like games depends critically on the integrity of the punishment institution. If enforcers are honest and/or can be identified as such, punishment can lead to high efficiency. If enforcers are corrupt and cannot be identified, punishment may fail and efficiency may be low. The model suggests that game design dimensions that increase the likelihood of honest enforcement (e.g., transparency, information sharing, enforcer education) will increase the efficiency gain from punishment. The presence of bistability means that initial conditions and small biases (e.g., in enforcer behavior) can have large effects. For prediction, this paper implies that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the effectiveness and honesty of the punishment mechanism, and on whether players can identify corrupt enforcers.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is based on pairs of harvesters (2-player interactions), with a population of harvesters and enforcers. The authors note that larger groups are not modeled but could be an extension.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (cooperation) is binary: harvesters either cooperate (log legally) or defect (log illegally).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The benefit of cooperation (b) and cost (c) are explicit model parameters; the structure is equivalent to a donation game (special case of PGG).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented via a hired enforcer, paid a fee s by harvesters; the penalty A is imposed on defectors. The cost structure is central to the model's dynamics.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the penalty A (must be large enough to deter defection, A > b). The effectiveness is undermined if enforcers are corrupt.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Section 5 models the case where information about enforcer honesty (i.e., the ability to identify corrupt enforcers) is available. This greatly increases the likelihood of cooperative, high-efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Only pairs of harvesters are modeled; larger group interactions are not analyzed.
- The model assumes infinite, well-mixed populations (replicator dynamics), not finite or spatially structured populations.
- No communication, reputation, or direct peer punishment is modeled.
- No reward mechanisms are considered.
- Parameter values (e.g., costs, penalties, bribes) are not empirically calibrated.
- The model does not address the effects of repeated finite rounds or end-game effects.
