Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and analyzes the conditions under which full cooperation (maximal efficiency) can be sustained in repeated helping games with different ranking profiles and punishment regimes. The main outcomes are efficiency/welfare and the distribution of costs, not empirical rates of contribution or punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the structure of social interactions—specifically, the ranking profiles that determine the order in which players seek help—critically affects the ability to sustain full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) in repeated helping games. When punishment is available (either community or bilateral), full cooperation can be sustained if and only if incentive constraints are satisfied. These constraints are least restrictive (i.e., efficiency is most easily sustained) when expected costs are equalized across players (Latin square ranking profiles). Under bilateral punishment, even stricter symmetry (BFF) is required. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum discount factor required to sustain efficiency under each regime. Thus, the design of the interaction structure and the punishment regime jointly determine whether efficiency is achievable.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated helping games with sequential requests and costly helping. It shows that the ability of punishment to sustain efficiency depends on the structure of interactions (ranking profiles) and the punishment regime. When the structure equalizes expected costs (Latin square), punishment is most effective at sustaining efficiency. If the structure is unbalanced, punishment is less effective, and efficiency may not be sustainable. The results are theoretical but provide explicit conditions and formulas that can be mapped to efficiency predictions in similar repeated public-goods-like environments, especially where helping is sequential and punishment is either community-wide or bilateral. However, the model is not a standard PGG, so direct quantitative predictions for standard PGGs should be made with caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The model is defined for arbitrary n; results are derived for general n, with some results (BFF) only for even n. The number of players affects the critical discount factor and the feasibility of certain ranking profiles.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated (discounted repeated game). Results are for the infinite horizon case.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Helping is binary: a player either tries to help (1) or not (0) when asked.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is determined by the probability p of successful help, the value v, and the cost c. The model assumes v > c (helping is efficient).
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is modeled as the withdrawal of future cooperation (grim-trigger or bilateral trigger), not as a direct cost. The cost is the loss of future benefits, not an explicit punishment cost.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the threat of losing future cooperation (community or bilateral). The strength of punishment is endogenous to the equilibrium structure.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is infinitely repeated and this is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the identity of the player needing help and the effort choices of those asked for help (perfect monitoring).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In both punishment regimes, the identity of the punisher and the punished is known (either community or bilateral).

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The model is not a standard public goods game but a repeated helping game with sequential requests and binary helping decisions.
- Punishment is modeled as the withdrawal of future cooperation, not as a costly action with explicit cost and impact parameters.
- Results depend on the assumption of perfect monitoring and infinite repetition (discounted games).
- The mapping to standard PGG dimensions (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost/tech) is indirect.
- No analysis of stochastic or noisy punishment, or of limited information environments.
