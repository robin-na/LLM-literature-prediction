# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Implicit communication in the ultimatum game
Authors: Brunner, M., & Ostermaier, A.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- proposer earnings
- responder earnings
- total group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rates
- offer size
- Machiavellianism effects
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are rejection rates (punishment-like behavior), offer size (proposer cooperation), and earnings for each role and in total. Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but total earnings are reported and compared across conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated ultimatum game, disclosing responders' decisions to proposers (enabling implicit communication of disapproval/punishment) increases rejection rates for low offers. Proposers anticipate this and make higher offers, but not enough to fully offset the increased rejections. As a result, total group earnings (the sum of proposer and responder payoffs) are slightly lower with disclosure, though the difference is not statistically significant. The effect is most pronounced for moderately unfair offers. The study also finds that when offers are random (not chosen by proposers), disclosure reduces rejection rates, as responders avoid signaling envy. Overall, the main efficiency-relevant result is that punishment via rejection, when made salient through disclosure, does not increase group efficiency and may slightly reduce it.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that enabling punishment-like behavior (rejection of unfair offers) through increased transparency (disclosure) does not improve efficiency in a bargaining game adjacent to public goods games. Instead, it may slightly reduce efficiency, as the cost of increased rejections is not fully offset by higher offers. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in similar environments where punishment is enabled or made more salient, efficiency may not increase and could decrease slightly, especially if the punishment is costly and not perfectly anticipated by those being punished. The findings are most directly relevant to bargaining/ultimatum games, but the mechanism—costly punishment reducing group surplus—may generalize to PGG-like settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by 2 players (proposer and responder) in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 10 rounds (main text) or 20 rounds (instructions) of the ultimatum game; only one round was paid out.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or explicit communication was allowed; the experiment specifically blocks explicit communication to study implicit communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is discrete (0-10 ECU in whole numbers), and responder's choice is all-or-nothing (accept or reject).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection, which is costly to both players (both get zero if rejected). The cost is the foregone earnings for both proposer and responder.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (rejection) removes all payoffs for both players in that round, i.e., 100% effective per unit cost (all-or-nothing).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the total number of rounds in advance (10 or 20, depending on main text vs. instructions).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposers are told the outcome (accepted/rejected) and, in the disclosure condition, also the responder's decision and whether nature intervened. No summary statistics about others' behavior are provided.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the disclosure condition, proposers are told whether the responder accepted or rejected (i.e., who punished), enabling identification of the punisher within the pair (though not across rounds, as matching is anonymous).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to public goods games; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Efficiency is measured as total group earnings, not as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
- The punishment mechanism (rejection) is all-or-nothing and costly to both parties, which may differ from more targeted or less costly punishment in PGGs.
- The effect on efficiency is small and statistically insignificant; results may be sensitive to sample size or design details.
- No reward mechanism is present, so interaction effects between punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
