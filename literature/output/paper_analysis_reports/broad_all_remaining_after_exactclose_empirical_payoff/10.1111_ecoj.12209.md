# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: ODE TO THE SEA: WORKPLACE ORGANIZATIONS AND NORMS OF COOPERATION
Authors: Gneezy, U., Leibbrandt, A., & List, J. A.
Journal: Economic Journal
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust (trust game)
- trustworthiness (trust game)
- offers and minimal acceptable offers (ultimatum game)
- donation (charity game)
- contribution (public goods game)
- cooperation index (aggregate of above)
- coordination on efficient equilibrium (stag-hunt game)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: trust, trustworthiness, offers, donations, contributions, and coordination. The public goods game reports average contributions, but not group payoff, efficiency, or surplus. The cooperation index is a composite of behavioral measures. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Fishermen in group-based (sea) societies exhibit higher trust, trustworthiness, pro-sociality, and cooperation in economic games—including the public goods game—than those in individual-based (lake) societies. These differences are robust to controls for selection and other confounds. The study provides evidence that workplace organization (group vs. individual work) is a key driver of cooperative norms. However, the public goods game does not include punishment or reward options, and the main outcomes are behavioral (contributions, trust, etc.), not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative about the effect of group-based workplace organization on cooperation and contribution behavior in public goods games, but it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency or payoff outcomes. It does not manipulate or include punishment, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR) are reported and can be extracted, but the absence of punishment means this paper is not directly useful for predicting treatment efficiency with punishment enabled.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public goods game groups had 3 players. Other games had 2 players (trust, ultimatum, stag-hunt).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played only once (one-shot).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in the public goods game and other games were continuous (0-10 points), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point contributed to the public good increased each group member's payoff by 0.5 points. With 3 players, MPCR = 0.5.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games were one-shot; participants knew the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No feedback or summary information about others' choices was given during the games.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism was included in any game, so the paper cannot inform about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (contribution, trust, etc.).
- The study compares societies with different workplace organizations, not experimental manipulation of punishment or sanctions.
- The relationship between degree of group exposure and strength of cooperation norms is not explored (only all-or-nothing exposure).
