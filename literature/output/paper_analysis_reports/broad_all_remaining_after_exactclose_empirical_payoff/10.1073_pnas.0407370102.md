## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in models of indirect reciprocity with binary reputation, cooperation can be stably maintained if individuals' knowledge of others' reputations increases over time (e.g., with age or experience). Costly punishment—refusing to help those with a Bad score, even at the risk of one's own reputation—can support a stable equilibrium of cooperation, provided the information environment is sufficiently rich. If information is too scarce, defectors dominate and cooperation collapses. The model demonstrates that the efficiency (total payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) is higher when these conditions for stable cooperation are met, and that the presence of costly punishment is essential in the minimal model for resisting defectors. The findings are directly relevant to efficiency and payoff outcomes in social dilemmas with reputation and punishment-like mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment (in the form of costly refusal to help those with a Bad reputation) can increase efficiency in social dilemma environments, but only when the information environment allows for effective reputation tracking. The model is not a literal public goods game, but the mechanisms and payoff logic are closely related. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control only if players can reliably observe or infer others' reputations, and if the cost-to-benefit ratio of helping is not too high. The findings also highlight the importance of the number of rounds (or opportunities for information to accumulate) and the structure of information flow. However, the model does not address group size, communication, or some other public goods game design features directly.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical estimates of effect sizes or real-world parameter values.
- The game is dyadic and based on indirect reciprocity, not a literal public goods game; group-level dynamics may differ.
- Punishment is implemented as refusal to help, not as a separate costly action targeting defectors.
- No analysis of group size, communication, or explicit knowledge of the number of rounds.
- Assumes binary reputation and simplified information structure; real-world settings may be more complex.
