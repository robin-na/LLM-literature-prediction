# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Securing property rights: A dilemma experiment in Austria, Mexico, Mongolia, South Korea and the United States
Authors: Ahn, T. K., Balafoutas, L., Batsaikhan, M., Campos-Ortiz, F., Putterman, L., & Sutter, M.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per period
- efficiency gain (percentage of potential cooperative surplus achieved)
- production (tokens allocated to production)
- group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- allocation to theft
- allocation to private protection
- allocation to collective protection
- voting for mandatory collective protection
- abstention from theft
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (group earnings relative to social optimum), production, and theft. Voting behavior and allocations to protection are also analyzed. Contribution rate is not directly measured as in standard PGGs, but production and theft allocations serve a similar role.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper studies a property rights dilemma game in which subjects allocate effort among production, theft, and protection. Three institutional treatments are compared: (1) no collective protection (NCP, baseline), (2) voluntary collective protection (VCP), and (3) collective protection by majority vote (VOTE, mandatory if chosen). Efficiency, measured as group earnings relative to the social optimum, is lowest in NCP (24.6%), higher in VCP (36.6%), and highest in VOTE (48.0%). The VOTE treatment achieves the highest efficiency when the mandatory scheme is adopted, but this occurs in only 64% of opportunities. The efficiency gains from collective protection are robust across five countries, but the magnitude varies with national trust levels. The results show that institutional mechanisms for collective protection (analogous to public enforcement or taxation) can substantially increase efficiency in social dilemmas involving appropriation, but their effectiveness depends on both institutional design and cultural context.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving the effect of introducing a collective protection or sanctioning institution in a social dilemma with appropriation (theft), this paper provides direct evidence that such institutions increase efficiency, with mandatory (voted) collective protection yielding the largest gains. The efficiency increase from baseline to voluntary collective protection is modest but significant, while the increase to mandatory collective protection is larger but not maximal due to incomplete adoption and suboptimal implementation. The design dimensions most relevant are the presence and type of collective protection, the method of funding (voluntary vs. mandatory), and the cultural context (trust). While the game is not a standard PGG, the efficiency results are closely analogous and informative for settings where punishment or public enforcement is introduced. The findings suggest that enabling effective, enforceable collective protection (akin to punishment) is likely to increase efficiency, but the realized gains may be less than theoretical predictions due to behavioral and cultural factors.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 players in all treatments and countries.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 periods per session, divided into six 4-period phases.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; groups are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices (production, theft, protection) are continuous (allocation of 10 effort tokens per period among activities). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG; marginal returns to production are diminishing (see Table 2). Each token allocated to production yields a decreasing marginal number of wealth tokens (from 15 for the first token to 1 for the tenth).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (24) and the phase structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, subjects see their own and each group member's wealth from production, theft, and losses to theft. Cumulative stats are available at any time.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Group members have fixed letter identifiers. Theft is not directly attributed, but can sometimes be deduced if only one theft occurs in a period. No explicit punishment, but retaliation is possible based on observed stats.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a property rights dilemma with production, theft, and protection, so direct transferability to PGGs is limited.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanism is present; only protection (private or collective) against theft.
- Efficiency gains from collective protection are less than theoretical predictions due to incomplete adoption and suboptimal implementation.
- Cultural context (trust) significantly moderates the effect of institutions, limiting generalizability.
- The subject pool consists of university students in five countries, which may not represent broader populations.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may affect cooperation dynamics compared to settings with communication.
