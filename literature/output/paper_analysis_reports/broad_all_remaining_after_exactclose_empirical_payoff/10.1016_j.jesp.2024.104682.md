# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Ironic effects of prosocial gossip in driving inaccurate social perceptions
Authors: Grayson, S., Feinberg, M., Willer, R., & Zaki, J.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- gossip valence
- perceptions of group cooperativeness
- forecasted contributions
- own contribution choice
- cynicism
- trust
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the valence and focus of gossip, participants' perceptions of others' contributions, and their own contribution choices. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Contribution rate is used as a behavioral proxy, but efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game, participants overwhelmingly use gossip to highlight free-riders rather than cooperators, even when most group members are cooperative. When a new set of participants reads these gossip notes, they systematically underestimate the cooperativeness of the original group, becoming more cynical and less accurate in their perceptions. However, this increased cynicism does not translate into lower expectations for their own group's cooperation or reduced contributions in their own public goods game. There is some evidence that exposure to negative gossip polarizes behavior, increasing the likelihood of both full cooperation and full defection, but the average contribution is not significantly affected.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment or gossip on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. Instead, it provides evidence that gossip (as a reputational mechanism) is negatively skewed and can distort outsiders' perceptions of group cooperativeness, but does not affect actual contribution behavior in subsequent games. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that reputational mechanisms like gossip may increase cooperation (as shown in the referenced Feinberg et al., 2014 study), but the current paper's main contribution is to highlight the informational and perceptual side effects of such mechanisms, not their impact on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game group consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 6 rounds; Study 2: participants played a single round after exposure to gossip.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: players could choose any number of points (0-10) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point contributed was doubled and split among 4 players: MPCR = 0.5.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, group members' contribution decisions were shared among the group.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; it focuses on contribution rates and perceptions.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanism is present; only gossip as a reputational mechanism.
- The public goods game context is artificial and may not generalize to real-world social dilemmas.
- The main outcomes are perceptual (cynicism, accuracy of beliefs) and behavioral (contribution choice), not payoff-based.
- Null results for the effect of gossip on actual contribution behavior may be context-dependent and not generalizable.
