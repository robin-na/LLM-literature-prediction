## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that current enforcement (fines for defection) strongly increases the probability of cooperation in the first round of a match. However, exposure to enforcement in previous matches can decrease current cooperation in early games, because enforcement makes it harder to learn about the intrinsic cooperativeness of the group. Observing cooperation in the absence of enforcement is a stronger positive signal for future cooperation than observing cooperation under enforcement. The effect of enforcement on cooperation is dynamic and depends on the stage of learning in the group. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on cooperation rates and their determinants.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality evidence on how punishment (formal enforcement via fines) affects cooperation rates in repeated prisoner's dilemma games, which are adjacent to public goods games. The findings suggest that punishment increases cooperation in the short run, but may slow down the emergence of high cooperation in the long run by impeding learning about group values. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect evidence: if higher cooperation translates to higher efficiency in the analogous public goods context, then punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the short run, but the long-run effect may be muted or even negative if learning is important. The design dimensions (e.g., random rematching, repeated games, binary choices, fine size) are well specified and can inform parameter mapping, but the lack of direct efficiency data is a limitation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each match is a two-player (dyadic) repeated prisoner's dilemma.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each match is an indefinitely repeated game with a random continuation rule (probability of continuation δ=0.75 per round), expected length 4 rounds, but actual number of rounds per match varies.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choice is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a public goods game, but a prisoner's dilemma. Payoff matrix: (C,C)=40, (C,D)=12/60, (D,C)=60/12, (D,D)=35. The marginal return to cooperation is implied by the matrix structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as an exogenous fine (F=10) for defection; cost is not paid by another player but is an external enforcement (not peer punishment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed fine of 10 tokens per defection, subtracted from the defector's payoff. No cost to the punisher (since punishment is exogenous).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment (fines) is used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds in advance; matches use a random continuation rule (δ=0.75).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players receive full feedback at the end of each round about the actions taken within the current match. No information about partner's history in previous matches.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exogenous (not peer-administered), so there is no punisher to identify. Players know when a fine is imposed, but not by whom (since it is automatic).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. No direct analysis of efficiency or total earnings is reported.
- The game is a repeated prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game, so mapping to PGG efficiency is indirect.
- Punishment is implemented as exogenous fines, not peer punishment, so findings may not generalize to peer-sanctioned PGGs.
- The experiment uses random rematching and focuses on early vs. late games, which may not match all PGG environments.
- No analysis of heterogeneity by player type, group composition, or other moderators beyond enforcement and learning.
