# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Power and temptation cause shifts between exploitation and cooperation in a cleaner wrasse mutualism
Authors: Gingins, S., Werminghausen, J., Johnstone, R. A., Grutter, A. S., & Bshary, R.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of less-preferred food items eaten before eating a preferred item
- adjustment of foraging behavior in response to threat of early termination (sanction)
- comparison of foraging behavior between species and treatments
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the number of less-preferred items eaten before eating a preferred item, interpreted as a measure of cooperative versus exploitative behavior. No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Cleaner wrasse strategically adjust their foraging behavior in response to the threat of early termination (sanction) and temptation to cheat. When the threat of early termination is high and the temptation to cheat is low, cleaners behave more cooperatively (eat more less-preferred items, which is analogous to providing better service). When the temptation to cheat is high and the threat is low, cleaners exploit the situation (eat more preferred items). The non-cleaner control species does not adjust its behavior in response to these manipulations. The study provides evidence that simple partner control mechanisms (sanctions) can maintain cooperation in asymmetric interactions, but does not directly measure efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that the presence of a sanction mechanism (threat of early termination) can shift behavior from exploitation to cooperation, especially when the temptation to cheat is low. However, the outcomes are behavioral and not directly about efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that, in analogous public goods games, enabling a sanction mechanism may increase cooperation, but the effect on efficiency would depend on the cost structure and whether the sanction is costly or not. The study is most informative for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which sanctions can promote cooperation, but less directly informative for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The experiment is dyadic (one fish and one 'client' plate), modeling an asymmetric two-player interaction.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each fish experienced 28 trials (rounds), with each treatment combination repeated 7 times.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The effectiveness of the sanction (threat of early termination) is manipulated (fast vs slow plate removal), directly affecting the cheater's opportunity to exploit. Stronger sanctions (fast removal) increase cooperative behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or direct variant; it is a dyadic asymmetric mutualism model.
- Outcomes are behavioral (foraging choices) rather than direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- Sanction (early termination) is not costly to the punisher, so the findings may not generalize to costly punishment scenarios.
- The study uses animal subjects and artificial 'client' plates, which may limit generalizability to human PGG-like environments.
- No direct measurement of efficiency or welfare outcomes; only indirect inference about cooperation and exploitation.
