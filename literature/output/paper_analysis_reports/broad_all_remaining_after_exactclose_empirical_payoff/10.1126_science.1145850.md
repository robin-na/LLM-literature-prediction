# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Chimpanzees are rational maximizers in an ultimatum game
Authors: Jensen, K., Call, J., & Tomasello, M.
Journal: Science
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rates of offers
- proposer offer choices
- signs of arousal (displays, tantrums)
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral responses in a mini-ultimatum game: offer choices by proposers and rejection rates by responders. No explicit analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is conducted. Outcomes are about fairness sensitivity, punishment (via rejection), and maximizing behavior, not aggregate payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Chimpanzees, when tested in a mini-ultimatum game, do not systematically reject unfair offers, even when a fair alternative is available to the proposer. Proposers do not make fair offers, and responders accept almost all nonzero offers, only reliably rejecting offers where they receive nothing. This contrasts with human behavior, where unfair offers are often rejected as a form of costly punishment. The findings suggest that chimpanzees are rational maximizers and do not engage in costly punishment to enforce fairness. The study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral responses to fairness and punishment opportunities.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides limited direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it studies a mini-ultimatum game with chimpanzees and focuses on behavioral outcomes (offer choices, rejection rates) rather than efficiency or group payoff. However, it is informative in showing that, in the absence of a willingness to punish unfairness (i.e., when punishment is not exercised), there is no mechanism to enforce fair or cooperative outcomes. This suggests that, in environments where punishment is not used or is not costly, efficiency gains from punishment are unlikely. The findings are adjacent to the prediction task and may inform models about the necessity of actual punishment behavior for efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played between two individuals: one proposer and one responder.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played four games in a single session; the number of trials per game is not precisely specified, but the design is one-shot per game type with counterbalancing.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposers choose between two discrete, pre-set offers; responders accept or reject the entire offer (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as rejection of an offer, which is costly to the responder (they forgo their share). The cost is the loss of the responder's potential reward.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is maximal: rejection leads to both players getting nothing.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The structure of the session and the number of games are known to the experimenters; it is not explicitly stated whether chimpanzees know the number of games, but the design is not repeated or indefinite.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are not anonymous; each chimpanzee knows the identity of the other (adjacent cages, visible).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses chimpanzees, not humans, so direct generalization to human public goods games is limited.
- The game is a mini-ultimatum game, not a public goods game or a repeated game.
- No explicit analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is conducted; only behavioral outcomes are reported.
- Small sample size (11 chimpanzees) and all from a single social group.
- Players are not anonymous, and both roles are played by the same individuals, which may affect behavior.
