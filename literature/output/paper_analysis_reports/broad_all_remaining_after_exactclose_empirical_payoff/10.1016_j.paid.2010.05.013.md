Authors: Skatova, A., & Ferguson, E.
Journal: Personality and Individual Differences
Year: 2011

- average group payoff

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average social payoff (efficiency), with extensive reporting of the prevalence of each strategy. No human or animal data; all results are from agent-based simulations.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a spatial prisoner's dilemma with super cooperators (who both punish defectors and reward cooperators), the ability of speculative defectors to identify and cooperate with super cooperators (i.e., higher information level) generally increases the prevalence of super cooperators and raises average social payoff (efficiency), provided the cost of punishment/reward is not too high. When the cost is high, increasing information can instead destroy super cooperators. The alliance between speculative defectors and super cooperators can insulate super cooperators from exploitation by unconditional cooperators, further supporting cooperation and efficiency. The model demonstrates that the introduction of punishment/reward mechanisms and information about others' strategies can substantially improve group efficiency compared to the baseline with only unconditional cooperators and defectors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatially structured prisoner's dilemma environments, enabling punishment (and reward) mechanisms—especially when paired with higher information about others' strategies—can increase group efficiency, as measured by average social payoff. The effect is strongest when the cost of punishment/reward is moderate or low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games with similar structure, though the model is not a standard public goods game but a spatial PD with three strategies. The results suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment (with moderate cost and sufficient information) should increase efficiency relative to the control. However, if the cost of punishment/reward is high, the effect can reverse. The model's focus on spatial structure, information, and cost of punishment/reward provides indirect but informative guidance for analogous PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use a 2D lattice of size L x L, typically L=100, so 10,000 players; robustness checked for L=100, 200, 400, 1000. All players interact with 8 neighbors.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 30,000 Monte Carlo steps (MCS), with averages over the last 2,000 steps; also robustness checks with 1,000,000 MCS.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment/reward cost to super cooperators is parameter alpha (varied from 0 to 1). When cost is low or moderate, punishment increases efficiency; when cost is high, punishment can reduce efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is 'egotistic': cost-to-fine (reward) ratio is 1; punisher pays alpha, punished loses alpha, and punisher receives alpha. No variation in effectiveness per unit cost.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Super cooperators both punish defectors and reward cooperators. Reward is always present when super cooperators are present.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is the same as punishment cost (alpha), varied from 0 to 1.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness per unit cost is 1:1 (cost-to-reward ratio is 1).

## Important Limitations
- The model is a spatial prisoner's dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- All results are from agent-based simulations; no empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is modeled as 'egotistic' (punisher receives the fine), not as costly punishment where the punisher pays a cost without direct benefit.
- The model assumes perfect implementation of strategies and does not include noise or errors in action.
- Information level (q) is an exogenous parameter; in real settings, information acquisition may be endogenous or costly.
- No communication, reputation, or institutional mechanisms beyond local information and strategy updating.
