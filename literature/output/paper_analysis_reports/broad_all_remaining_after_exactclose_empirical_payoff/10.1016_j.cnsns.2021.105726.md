Title: The evolution of sectarianism

Journal: Communications in Nonlinear Science and Numerical Simulation

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- social efficiency (joint net gains from exchange)
- average payoff
- likelihood of egalitarian, sectarian, or conflict equilibria
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of coercive members
- strategy distributions (L, M, H)
- collective memory effects
- identity and favouritism dynamics
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the likelihood of different social contracts (egalitarian, sectarian, conflict), which are defined in terms of payoff efficiency and distribution. The model explicitly analyzes efficiency and group payoff, with social contracts corresponding to different efficiency levels.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the evolution of coercive (punishment-like) strategies in a repeated bargaining game can lead to either efficient egalitarian outcomes or inefficient sectarian contracts, depending on the relative costs and benefits of coercion and the structure of social learning. When the cost of coercion is low and potential gains from inegalitarian contracts are high, societies are more likely to settle into inefficient, conflictual, or sectarian equilibria. When both groups have similar coercive capacities and the cost of coercion is high, egalitarian and efficient outcomes are more likely. The model also shows that collective memory, identity politics, and initial asymmetries can tip the balance toward persistent conflict or sectarianism, reducing overall efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (coercion) on efficiency is highly context-dependent. If punishment is symmetric and costly, it can support efficient cooperation (egalitarian contracts). If punishment is asymmetric or the gains from inegalitarian outcomes are high, punishment can entrench inefficient, conflictual, or exploitative equilibria. The model suggests that game design dimensions such as the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the symmetry of player power, and the structure of social learning critically moderate the efficiency impact of punishment. However, the model is not a direct PGG or public goods game, but rather a repeated bargaining/coordination game with endogenous punishment-like coercion. Thus, its findings are most relevant for adjacent settings where punishment can be used to enforce either efficient or inefficient group outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model assumes two large subpopulations (sects), with group size not affecting equilibrium likelihoods in simulations. Exact player count is not specified, but group size is discussed as not critical to the main results.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model is a repeated game with ongoing interactions; number of rounds is not fixed but assumed to be large or infinite for evolutionary dynamics.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players choose among three discrete strategies (L, M, H), not continuous contributions. This is a discrete demand game, not all-or-nothing in the PGG sense, but not continuous either.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Cost of coercion (punishment) is a key parameter (σ_i) directly manipulated in the model. Higher punishment cost reduces the likelihood of inefficient sectarian contracts and increases the likelihood of efficient egalitarian contracts.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Effectiveness of coercion is modeled via the probability of successful coercion, which depends on the frequency of coercive members. Higher effectiveness (relative to cost) increases the likelihood of sectarian contracts if gains are high.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players have access to information about the strategies and payoffs of others via social learning and memory, but not explicit summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game or direct PGG variant, but a repeated Nash demand/coordination game with endogenous coercion.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are theoretical or simulation-based.
- No direct manipulation of most game design dimensions used in PGG experiments (e.g., MPCR, continuous contributions, explicit punishment/reward choices).
- Findings may not transfer directly to standard PGG settings, but are relevant for adjacent bargaining and coordination environments with punishment-like mechanisms.
- No analysis of communication, reward, or identification of punishers.
- Assumes large populations and evolutionary dynamics, which may not map directly to small-group lab experiments.
