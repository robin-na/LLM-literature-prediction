## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that models of reciprocal kindness, as formalized in psychological game theory (e.g., Rabin 1993, Charness & Rabin 2002, Falk & Fischbacher 2006), cannot explain mutually beneficial trust and trustworthiness in Trust Games—this is termed the 'Paradox of Trust.' The paper argues that trust and trustworthiness are better explained by a different form of reciprocity, namely reciprocal cooperation, where individuals are motivated to play their part in mutually beneficial practices conditional on others doing the same. The paper sketches how this approach can be applied to public goods games, but does not provide empirical results or efficiency data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative results that can be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. However, it is conceptually relevant for understanding the psychological underpinnings of cooperation and the limitations of certain reciprocity models. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that models focusing solely on rewarding/punishing intentions (reciprocal kindness) may not capture the mechanisms that lead to efficient cooperation in trust or public goods games. Instead, models of reciprocal cooperation may be more appropriate for predicting when mutually beneficial outcomes (i.e., high efficiency) can arise. The paper does not address specific game design dimensions or provide data on how punishment affects efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
