The paper explicitly analyzes when cooperation (and thus high group payoff) is evolutionarily stable under different punishment regimes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, allowing anti-social punishment (punishment of cooperators) prevents the evolution of cooperation in public goods games with costly punishment. In models where only pro-social punishment is possible, cooperation and punishment can co-evolve, leading to high group efficiency. However, when anti-social punishment is included, defecting strategies that punish others become dominant, and cooperation is not stable. This result holds across a wide range of parameter values and is robust to small behavioral errors. The findings highlight that the efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the absence of anti-social punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly contingent on the type of punishment allowed. If anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, as cooperation is not stable and defectors who punish others dominate. In contrast, if only pro-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of anti-social punishment as a design dimension is critical: if anti-social punishment is allowed, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment; if it is not allowed, efficiency gains are possible under the right parameter regimes.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation and punishment choices; results may differ with continuous strategies.
- Punishment is modeled as unconditional and strategy-based, not as a choice in response to observed behavior in a specific round.
- No communication, reputation, or higher-order strategies are included.
- The model does not include reward mechanisms or other positive incentives.
- The evolutionary dynamics are based on imitation or viability selection, not rational choice or learning.
- The results may not generalize to settings with more complex population structures, intergroup competition, or explicit group selection.
