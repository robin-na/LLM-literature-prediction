Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are simulation-based measures of total and individual gain/payoff, which are directly analogous to efficiency or group welfare in public goods games. Non-payoff outcomes such as number of fulfilled requests and cooperation frequency are also reported, but the focus is on payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Simulation results show that classical Tit-for-Tat fails in this asynchronous, stochastic setting, leading to deadlocks and low total gain. Generous Tit-for-Tat avoids deadlocks and achieves high group gain, but is vulnerable to exploitation by selfish participants. Altruistic and reputation-based strategies can effectively punish selfish participants, forcing them to cooperate to receive benefits. The best strategies (reputation-based and altruistic-reputation) allow cooperative participants to achieve near-optimal total gain, while selfish participants are excluded unless they match cooperative behavior. The findings are robust across variations in the number of cooperative/selfish participants and the probability of offering resources.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment-like mechanisms (retaliation, exclusion, reputation-based refusal) can substantially increase group efficiency in repeated resource-sharing games with asynchronous moves and stochastic cooperation. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment or exclusion in public-goods-like environments, especially when cooperation is probabilistic and not always feasible. The specific game structure differs from standard PGGs (asynchronous, resource lending/borrowing, not simultaneous contribution), but the payoff logic and the effect of punishment on group efficiency are closely analogous. The paper supports the prediction that enabling effective punishment or exclusion will increase efficiency, especially when selfish behavior is otherwise possible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 12 participants (8 cooperative, 4 selfish) as the main scenario; other group sizes are discussed for upper bound comparison.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 100,000 sequential requests (iterations), representing a long repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. At each iteration, a participant can offer one resource or none; requests are for one resource. The action is discrete and binary per request.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as refusal to lend resources (exclusion), not as a costly action. The cost is opportunity cost (foregone gain), not explicit expenditure.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: exclusion from resource sharing sharply reduces selfish participants' gain and increases group efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Games are played for a large, fixed number of rounds (100,000), but the effect of endgame knowledge is not analyzed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Participants track the history of offers/requests and compute reputations, which is analogous to showing summary information about others' behavior.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Participants know who refused their requests and can track individual histories, enabling targeted punishment/exclusion.

## Important Limitations
- The model is simulation-based and not tested with human subjects or real-world data.
- The game structure is adjacent to, but not identical with, standard public goods games: it is an asynchronous resource lending/borrowing game, not a simultaneous contribution game.
- Punishment is implemented as exclusion/refusal rather than costly punishment; there is no explicit cost to punishing beyond foregone gain.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or PGG multiplier; payoffs are based on lending/borrowing costs and gains.
- No communication or chat is modeled, and the effect of communication is not addressed.
- The effect of knowledge of the number of rounds (endgame effects) is not analyzed.
