Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are investment rates and payoff-based outcomes (profits, surplus). Non-payoff outcomes such as investment, agreement, and punishment behavior are also analyzed, especially to explain deviations from theoretical predictions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that ownership structure affects investment incentives and efficiency in public-good provision, but not always as predicted by theory. When the investor values the public good more, giving them ownership increases investment and group surplus. When the non-investor values the public good more, giving them ownership does not increase investment or surplus, because the investor-owner sometimes withholds the public good to punish the other party, reducing efficiency. When the possibility of such punishment is removed (by exogenously enforcing provision), investment and efficiency fall in line with theoretical predictions. Thus, the efficiency effect of ownership and the potential for punishment are intertwined, and the ability to punish by withholding provision can reduce efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of institutional design (ownership allocation) in public-good games depends on whether players can punish by withholding provision. In environments where the owner can withhold the public good as punishment, efficiency may be lower than predicted by standard theory, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is high. If the design disables such punishment (e.g., by enforcing provision), efficiency aligns with theoretical predictions. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of punishment opportunities (even if not explicit costly punishment) is a key moderator. The findings are most relevant for two-player, sequential, bargaining-based public-good games with binary investment and provision decisions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments are two-player games (player A and player B).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 16 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or free-form communication; all interactions are via structured bargaining offers.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Investment and provision decisions are binary (invest/not invest; provide/not provide).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is not explicitly named but can be inferred: in parameter constellation 1, investment cost I=95, total benefit vA+vB=140, so MPCR=140/95>1; in constellation 2, I=65, vA+vB=140, MPCR=140/65>1.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: owner can withhold the public good after bargaining breakdown, incurring a cost (forfeiting own benefit) to reduce the other's payoff. No explicit punishment cost parameter, but the cost is the owner's foregone benefit.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is effective: withholding provision after breakdown reduces the other player's payoff to zero. The 'technology' is binary and highly effective.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know there are 16 periods; one is randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the outcome of their own bargaining and investment decisions; no evidence of summary statistics about others' behavior across groups.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only two-player games; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Punishment is endogenous (withholding provision), not an explicit costly punishment mechanism as in standard PGG experiments.
- The design is a sequential investment and bargaining game, not a simultaneous-move PGG; findings may not transfer to standard PGG settings.
- All players are university students from a single institution, which may limit external validity.
- No explicit manipulation of communication, group size, or repeated group structure.
- The findings are most relevant for settings where the owner can unilaterally withhold the public good after bargaining breakdown.
