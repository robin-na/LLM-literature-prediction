# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation across multiple game theoretical paradigms is increased by fear more than anger in selfish individuals
Authors: Chierchia, G., Lesemann, F. H. P., Snower, D., & Singer, T.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- latent cooperation factor (across multiple games, including PGG)
- latent punishment factor (across multiple games, including 2nd/3rd party punishment)
- contributions in public good game
- punishment rates in punishment games
- trust/trustworthiness
- charitable donations
- dictator game giving
- ultimatum game offers/rejections
- common resource restraint
- helping in Zurich prosocial game
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are factor scores for cooperation and punishment, derived from behavior across a suite of economic games. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. Outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment, etc.), not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Induced fear, compared to anger, increases cooperation (as measured by a latent factor across multiple games, including the public good game) in individuals with selfish value orientations, bringing their cooperation levels up to those of prosocial individuals. No significant effect of emotion induction is found on punishment behavior. The effect is robust to controls for mood, risk attitudes, and other confounds. The study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so its findings are limited to behavioral outcomes rather than payoff-based outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that emotional context (fear vs. anger) can moderate cooperation in public-goods-like environments, especially among selfish individuals. However, because the outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, punishment) rather than efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a treatment (i.e., enabling/disabling punishment), but rather includes punishment games as part of a behavioral factor. Thus, while the paper supports the idea that context and individual differences can moderate cooperation and punishment, it does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper

The public good game and other games are standard multi-player games, but the exact player count for the PGG is not specified in the main text. The methods mention group sessions and standard paradigms, suggesting typical lab sizes (likely 4 for PGG), but this is not explicitly stated.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No mention of chat or communication; standard lab protocols and cubicle setup imply no communication between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in the public good game and other games are described as continuous (e.g., 'how much money to contribute'), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment games are included (2nd and 3rd party), but the exact cost structure (cost to punisher per unit) is not specified in the main text.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Standard public good and punishment games typically provide feedback on others' actions, but the main text does not specify what summaries were shown.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; all outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, punishment) rather than payoff-based.
- The effect of punishment as a treatment (i.e., enabling/disabling punishment) is not manipulated or analyzed; punishment is included as a behavioral factor only.
- Game parameters (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) are not fully specified in the main text.
- Findings on cooperation and punishment are based on latent factors across multiple games, not specifically on the public good game.
- No direct evidence is provided for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
