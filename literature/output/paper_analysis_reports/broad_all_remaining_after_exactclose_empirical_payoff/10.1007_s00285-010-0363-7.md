## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in finite populations, allowing individuals to reward contributors to the public good can initiate and maintain a significant minority of cooperators, even though the majority of the population is typically composed of non-cooperating shirkers. Reward is effective at jump-starting cooperation but is unable to stabilize it in the long run due to exploitation by second-order free-riders. The average group payoff (efficiency) is increased relative to pure defection, but full efficiency is not achieved. The effectiveness of reward is largely independent of group size when key parameters are scaled appropriately. The paper does not provide new results on punishment, but notes that punishment is more effective at stabilizing cooperation once established.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence on the effects of reward (not punishment) in public goods games. It shows that positive incentives can increase efficiency above the baseline of mutual defection, but do not achieve full efficiency due to the persistence of second-order free-riders. The findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, reward alone is insufficient to fully stabilize cooperation and maximize efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with reward enabled, but only indirectly inform predictions about punishment. The analysis of group size, MPCR, and reward parameters is informative for understanding how these dimensions affect efficiency with reward. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for cases where the treatment is reward (not punishment), and less directly informative for punishment-enabled games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size (M) is set to 100 in simulations; group size (N) is varied (N=2, N=5) to test effects on outcomes.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: individuals either contribute (cooperate) or do not (defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N, where r is the multiplication factor and N is group size. Values used include r=1.2 (N=2) and r=3 (N=5).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is the central treatment; individuals can reward contributors at a cost, providing a benefit to the recipient.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost (gamma) is a key parameter; values include gamma=0.05 (N=5) and gamma=0.2 (N=2).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness (beta) is a key parameter; values include beta=0.25 (N=5) and beta=1 (N=2). Reward is only effective if beta > gamma.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Reputation is modeled as a probability of learning whether co-players reward, which is a form of summary information. ## Important Limitations
- The paper does not model or analyze punishment directly; all results pertain to reward (positive incentives) only.
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are long-run stationary distributions, not finite-round or one-shot outcomes.
- No direct analysis of the effect of punishment on efficiency; only indirect discussion.
- No communication, chat, or social context beyond reputation is modeled.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices.
