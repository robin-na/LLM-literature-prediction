# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior
Authors: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S.
Journal: Theory and Decision
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average responder payoff
- average proposer payoff
- total group payoff (efficiency)
- session-level efficiency
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rates of unequal offers
- rate of equal offers
- switching behavior after punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are rejection rates (punishment), offer types (equal/unequal), and payoffs to each role. Efficiency is explicitly analyzed as total group payoff relative to maximum possible.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by two players (proposer and responder). Sessions have 16 participants, 8 in each role, but the game is strictly two-player.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays 8 rounds, always in the same role, with round-robin matching.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Proposer chooses between two discrete options: equal split (5,5) or unequal (8,2); responder can only accept or reject the unequal offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (rejection) is costly: both players get zero if the responder rejects the unequal offer. The cost to the punisher is 2 (foregone payoff from accepting 2), and the cost to the proposer is 8 (foregone payoff from being accepted).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: rejection reduces both players' payoffs to zero. The impact per unit cost is high, but the cost to the punisher is also high relative to their possible gain.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 8 rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the open response treatment, proposers are immediately informed of the responder's choice (punishment is visible and attributed to the specific responder). In the covered response treatment, proposers are not informed until the end, and cannot attribute punishment to specific responders.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated ultimatum minigame, so generalizability to multi-player public goods settings is limited.
- Punishment is binary and maximally costly (rejection leads to zero payoff for both), which may not reflect the range of punishment options in other environments.
- The group size is always two; effects in larger groups may differ.
- No reward mechanism is present, so interaction effects between punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- Efficiency effects are analyzed at the session level, but the number of sessions is small, limiting statistical power for some findings.
- Cultural and subject pool differences are controlled for, but may still influence results.
