# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Can short-term incentives induce long-lasting cooperation? Results from a public-goods experiment
Authors: Bruttel, L., & Friehe, T.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution to the public good
- punishment points assigned
- beliefs about others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average contribution to the public good. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus. Punishment behavior and beliefs are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment used a repeated linear public goods game with punishment, fixed groups of four, and 20 rounds. Treatments manipulated the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) and punishment cost for the first 10 rounds, then reverted to a common, less cooperative-friendly setting for rounds 11-20. High mpcr or low punishment cost increased contributions in rounds 1-10, but after reverting to baseline parameters, contributions in these groups declined rapidly, often below the control. The baseline (constant, moderate mpcr and punishment cost) showed stable contributions. The study finds no evidence that temporary strong incentives create persistent cooperation; instead, their removal can backfire. The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with punishment, temporary improvements in incentive structure (either via higher mpcr or lower punishment cost) do not produce lasting increases in cooperation once incentives revert to baseline. For the prediction task, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (or from temporarily improving punishment effectiveness or mpcr) is not persistent if the incentive is not maintained. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so its value for predicting efficiency is indirect and must be inferred from contribution patterns. The results caution against expecting lasting efficiency improvements from short-term incentive changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players (fixed partners).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session, with a parameter change after round 10.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous from 0 to 20 points (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. mpcr = alpha/4. Treatments: alpha=2 (mpcr=0.5) for HighReturn in rounds 1-10, alpha=1.2 (mpcr=0.3) otherwise. High mpcr increases contributions while in effect, but effect disappears after reverting to baseline.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (beta) manipulated: 0.25 (LowCost) vs 0.5 (Base/HighReturn) in rounds 1-10. Lower cost increases punishment and (weakly) contributions while in effect, but effect vanishes or reverses after cost increases.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1 per point (1 point reduces target by 1). Cost per point is beta. Lower beta = higher effectiveness per cost. Manipulated via beta.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there would be 20 rounds, with a possible parameter change after round 10.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw others' contributions, payoffs, and punishment assigned/received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: participant IDs were randomly reassigned each round to prevent reputation formation.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is average contribution, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total earnings, or surplus.
- The study only examines temporary changes in incentives (mpcr, punishment cost) and their removal, not the effect of enabling or disabling punishment per se.
- Results may not generalize to settings with communication, variable group size, or different payoff structures.
- Punishment is always available; there is no no-punishment control.
