# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Tend to Compare and Tend to Be Fair: The Relationship between Social Comparison Sensitivity and Justice Sensitivity
Authors: Zhen, S. S., & Yu, R. J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rates in ultimatum game (UG)
- save rates in social comparison game (SCG)
- self-reported satisfaction with outcomes
- correlations between justice sensitivity and social comparison sensitivity
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (accept/reject offers in UG, save/again in SCG) and self-reported satisfaction. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. The focus is on fairness, social comparison, and individual differences in sensitivity.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds a positive correlation between social comparison sensitivity and justice sensitivity, both in behavioral tasks (UG and SCG) and in self-report inventories. In the UG, participants are more likely to reject disadvantageous offers, and this tendency is associated with their behavior in the SCG. The results suggest that social comparison motives underlie fairness-related decisions, particularly the rejection of unfair offers. However, the study does not include a public goods game, does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a separate stage, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It uses a modified ultimatum game (dyadic, proposer-responder) and a social comparison game, focusing on individual differences in fairness and comparison sensitivity. There is no group interaction, no punishment stage as in PGGs, and no efficiency or group payoff data. The findings may be relevant for understanding motives behind rejection (which can be interpreted as a form of costly punishment), but the lack of group-level payoff outcomes and absence of a PGG or punishment-enabled/control design means this paper offers only indirect, limited support for the downstream prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The ultimatum game is dyadic (2 players: proposer and responder). The social comparison game is also dyadic (participant and partner).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 25 trials in each game (UG and SCG).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the UG, the responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer. In the SCG, the choice is save or again (keep or repeat).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told that one trial would be randomly selected for payment, and were instructed to treat each trial as if it could be the one that determines their payoff. The total number of trials (25) was known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown both their own and their partner's outcomes on each trial (pie chart display).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or any group interaction beyond dyadic bargaining.
- There is no explicit punishment or sanctioning stage as in PGGs; rejection in the ultimatum game is not analyzed as costly punishment in the PGG sense.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (accept/reject, save/again) or self-reported satisfaction.
- Sample size is relatively small and limited to student volunteers, which may affect generalizability.
- Participants were deceived to believe they were interacting with real partners, which may affect external validity.
- The findings are about individual differences in fairness and social comparison sensitivity, not about the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
