# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Why feed the Leviathan?
Authors: Guillen, P., Schwieren, C., & Staffiero, G.
Journal: Public Choice
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- frequency of public good provision (step-level reached)
- group success rate (provision rate)
- average group contribution
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- voting behavior (whether to keep or remove CSM)
- failure rates in reaching threshold
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency with which the group reaches the step-level threshold (i.e., public good is provided), which is a proxy for group success and efficiency but not a direct measure of total group payoff or efficiency ratio. Average group contribution is also reported. Individual payoffs are determined by the rules, but aggregate efficiency (total group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible) is not directly reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that a centralized sanctioning mechanism (CSM) in a step-level public good game leads to a dramatic increase in the rate at which groups successfully provide the public good (i.e., reach the threshold). In the baseline (no CSM), groups almost never reach the threshold after a few rounds. With CSM, the threshold is reached in nearly 90% of rounds. After CSM is removed, groups with prior CSM experience continue to reach the threshold at much higher rates than baseline groups (AR: 65%, VR: 48%, BL: 0% in rounds 8-14). The effect is attributed to an 'educational' or coordination-facilitating role of CSM. Voting on CSM removal does not improve post-removal outcomes compared to automatic removal. Failures to reach the threshold during the CSM phase predict lower post-CSM success. The CSM is costly (3 units per player per round), so while it increases the likelihood of public good provision, it also reduces per-round payoffs when active. The paper does not directly report efficiency ratios, but the increased provision rate implies higher group welfare relative to baseline.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a centralized punishment mechanism in a threshold (step-level) public good game increases the rate of successful public good provision, both during and after the punishment phase. The effect persists even after punishment is removed, supporting the idea of an 'educational' or coordination effect. The CSM is costly, so the net efficiency gain depends on the balance between increased public good provision and the cost of the mechanism. For prediction, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to substantially increase efficiency (proxied by provision rate) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially in step-level games with coordination problems. The effect is robust to whether removal is automatic or by vote. However, the paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must use provision rate as a proxy.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in fixed groups of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments have 14 rounds, with CSM present in rounds 1-7 and absent in 8-14 (or as decided by vote in VR).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants is explicitly forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is discrete (integer 0-10), but not all-or-nothing; players can choose any integer amount.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 12/31 ≈ 0.387 if threshold is reached; otherwise, contributions are lost.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. CSM costs 3 units per player per round (regardless of behavior); punished players (contribute <6) lose an additional 6 units.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is automatic: any player contributing <6 loses 6 units. No discretion or targeting.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the total number of rounds (14) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see their own contribution, the sum of group contributions, and whether they were punished.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly measured as total group payoff divided by maximum possible; instead, the main outcome is the frequency of public good provision (step-level reached).
- The CSM is costly, so while it increases provision rates, it reduces per-round payoffs when active; net efficiency effect depends on the balance between these.
- The game is a step-level (threshold) public good, not a standard linear PGG; results may not generalize to linear or continuous PGGs.
- Sample is limited to university students in Spain; external validity may be limited.
- No direct analysis of individual or group payoffs as a fraction of the social optimum.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only centralized punishment is considered.
