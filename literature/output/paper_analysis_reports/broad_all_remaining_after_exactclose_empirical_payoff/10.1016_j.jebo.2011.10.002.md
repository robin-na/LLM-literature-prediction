# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Credible communication and cooperation: Experimental evidence from multi-stage Games
Authors: Andersson, O., & Wengström, E.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (C actions in stage 1)
- message type sent/received
- stage 2 coordination (H actions)
- conditional cooperation based on communication
- use of punishment/reward strategies
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: cooperation rates in a two-stage game (prisoner's dilemma followed by coordination), and the effect of communication structure on these rates. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that allowing only pre-play communication leads to higher cooperation rates in the first stage (prisoner's dilemma) than allowing both pre-play and intra-play communication. Sending or receiving messages that signal cooperative intentions has a strong positive effect on cooperation when only pre-play communication is possible, but this effect is significantly weakened when intra-play communication is allowed. The results support the theoretical prediction that renegotiation opportunities (via intra-play communication) undermine the credibility of punishment threats and thus reduce the effectiveness of communication in sustaining cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (not payoff) effects of communication structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about the effect of communication structure (timing and frequency) on cooperation in multi-stage games with punishment/reward strategies, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the results suggest that communication structure can moderate the effectiveness of punishment/reward threats in sustaining cooperation, which may indirectly affect efficiency in similar environments. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits its direct applicability to efficiency prediction. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it uses a prisoner's dilemma plus coordination game, and punishment is implemented via equilibrium threats rather than explicit costly punishment. The main actionable insight is that more communication opportunities (especially intra-play) can reduce the effectiveness of pre-play communication in sustaining cooperation, likely due to renegotiation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played the two-stage game 8 times (8 rounds), with random rematching each round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Structured, non-binding (cheap talk) communication is allowed: pre-play only in treatment P, pre-play plus intra-play in treatment PI. No free-form chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: C (cooperate) or D (defect) in stage 1; H or L in stage 2.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward is possible via equilibrium selection (choosing H in stage 2), but not as a separate costly action.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 8 rounds; no mention of uncertainty about the number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, subjects learn the outcome of that stage (partner's action).

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a direct variant; it is a two-player, two-stage game (prisoner's dilemma plus coordination).
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanism; punishment/reward is implemented via equilibrium selection (threats to play less preferred equilibria).
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus; only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, message effects) are analyzed.
- Communication is structured and limited to pre-defined messages, not free-form chat.
- Results may not generalize to larger groups, continuous contribution settings, or standard public goods games with explicit punishment/reward options.
