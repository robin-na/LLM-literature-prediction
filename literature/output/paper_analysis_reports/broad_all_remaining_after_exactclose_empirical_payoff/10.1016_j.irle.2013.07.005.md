# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: First impressions are more important than early intervention: Qualifying broken windows theory in the lab
Authors: Engel, C., Beckenkamp, M., Glöckner, A., Irlenbusch, B., Hennig-Schmidt, H., Kube, S., Kurschilgen, M., Morell, A., Nicklisch, A., Normann, H. T., & Towfigh, E.
Journal: International Review of Law and Economics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- cooperation rate
- punishment assigned
- effect of first impressions on contribution
- effect of early punishment on contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good (i.e., cooperation/contribution rate). The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, but the contribution rate is closely related to efficiency in this context. Some discussion of payoffs is present in the design section, but all main analyses and results are about contributions, not payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the authors find that first impressions (i.e., the initial level of cooperation in the group) have a strong and lasting effect on subsequent contributions. The opportunity to punish freeriders (costly punishment) stabilizes cooperation and slows the decay of contributions over time, compared to games without punishment. However, the effect of early punishment (punishing in the first round) is small and fades over time, especially when punished players can retaliate. The main driver of sustained cooperation is good first impressions, with punishment playing a secondary, stabilizing role. The paper does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoffs, but higher contributions in the punishment condition imply higher efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard linear public goods games, enabling punishment increases and stabilizes contributions compared to no-punishment baselines, especially when initial group behavior is cooperative. While the paper does not report efficiency directly, the close link between contribution rate and efficiency in this design means the results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes. The effect of punishment is positive but depends on initial conditions; early punishment per se is less important than the overall opportunity for punishment. The findings are most applicable to 4-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGGs with standard punishment technology and no communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are repeated for 10 periods (rounds), announced in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player can contribute any amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each token contributed increases each group member's payoff by 0.4 tokens).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token per punishment point assigned (in linear 1:3 technology); in Fehr & Gächter (FG) technology, cost is also 1 token per point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness varies: in 1:3 technology, each point reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens; in FG technology, non-linear (see Fehr & Gächter 2000); also a 'severe' technology in some sessions.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (10) is announced in advance to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are informed about the contributions of the other group members. In punishment/counterpunishment treatments, feedback about punishment varies (own, others, all).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is targeted but anonymity is preserved; feedback is about amount received, not identity of punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome analyzed is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- No direct reporting of group earnings, welfare, or surplus.
- All experiments are in the lab with artificial stakes and no real-world consequences.
- No communication or social context beyond the experimental setting.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment and counterpunishment.
- All groups are of size 4 and play for 10 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- Punishment technologies vary across datasets, but the effect of these variations is not the main focus.
