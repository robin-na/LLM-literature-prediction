# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Human punishment is motivated by both a desire for revenge and a desire for equality
Authors: Bone, J. E., & Raihani, N. J.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of punishment assigned
- amount of punishment assigned
- whether punishment investment was tailored to equalize payoffs
- proportion of players choosing specific punishment investments
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on punishment behavior (frequency, amount, and targeting) in response to stealing and inequality, not on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The main dependent variables are behavioral (punishment choices), not payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment is motivated by both a desire for revenge (punishment occurs even when it cannot restore equality) and a desire for equality (when possible, punishment is tailored to equalize payoffs). Punishment is more likely when stealing creates disadvantageous inequality and when punishment is effective. However, the outcomes measured are behavioral (punishment frequency and amount), not efficiency or group payoff. The experiment is a two-player, one-shot, anonymous game with no communication, and does not use a public goods game or its direct variants.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about how punishment behavior is shaped by the effectiveness of punishment and the presence of inequality, but does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar multi-player settings. The findings may inform predictions about when punishment will be used and how it will be targeted, especially regarding the importance of punishment effectiveness (fee-to-fine ratio) and inequality, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not report efficiency or welfare, so its value for the prediction task is limited to qualitative insights about punishment motivation and targeting.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is strictly two-player (P1 and P2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; game is anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. P2's choice is all-or-nothing (steal $0.20 or do nothing); P1's punishment is discrete (0-4 points).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is $0.05 per point for P1 in both conditions; effectiveness (fee-to-fine ratio) is manipulated.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated: 1:1 (ineffective) vs 1:3 (effective) fee-to-fine ratio. More punishment when effective, especially to equalize payoffs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot; players know the game ends after one round.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. P1 is informed of P2's action (steal or not); no other summary information is provided.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is anonymous; P2 knows they were punished but not the identity of P1.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a direct variant; it is a two-player, one-shot, stealing and punishment game.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods contexts.
- The study does not analyze the effect of punishment on overall group outcomes or efficiency.
- Punishment is only studied in the context of negative actions (stealing), not in the context of voluntary contributions.
