Theoretical models focus on group payoff, fitness, and public good production as primary outcomes. Frequency of cooperators and coexistence are also analyzed, but as mechanisms or equilibrium properties rather than as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in nonlinear public goods games (which are more biologically realistic than linear ones), a mixed equilibrium with both cooperators and defectors can exist, and group payoff (efficiency) can be substantial and close to maximum even without assortment or repeated interactions. The effect of punishment or sanctions (host sanctions) is formally distinguished from partner fidelity feedback, with different evolutionary implications. The models show that the shape of the public good function (nonlinearity) is a key moderator of efficiency and cooperation, and that punishment/sanctions can maintain cooperation, but the resulting equilibrium is generally not fully efficient. The paper does not present new empirical data but provides a theoretical framework for interpreting efficiency and punishment effects in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions, especially the shape of the public good function (linearity vs. nonlinearity), group size, and cost/benefit ratio. It clarifies that punishment/sanctions (host sanctions) can support cooperation and increase group payoff, but the efficiency gain depends on whether the mechanism is true punishment (requiring commitment) or partner fidelity feedback (self-serving host response). The models suggest that in nonlinear public goods games, efficiency can be high even without punishment, and the marginal effect of punishment may be limited by the structure of the game. The paper is highly relevant for interpreting the likely efficiency effects of punishment in both linear and nonlinear public goods games, but does not provide direct empirical estimates.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Player count (N) is a key parameter in all public goods models discussed. The effect of player count on equilibrium efficiency is analyzed, especially in relation to the cost/benefit ratio and the shape of the public good function.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Theoretical models consider both one-shot and repeated games. The distinction is important for the effect of punishment/host sanctions, but most analysis focuses on one-shot interactions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Both all-or-nothing (volunteer's dilemma, step function) and continuous contribution (general nonlinear public goods) models are analyzed. The shape of the contribution function is a key moderator.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (r/N or c/b) is a central parameter in the models, affecting equilibrium efficiency and the existence of mixed equilibria.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is discussed in the context of host sanctions and commitment, but not parameterized in a standard PGG sense. The cost to the punisher (host) is a key consideration in the theory of host sanctions.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is discussed in terms of the host's ability to credibly commit to sanctions and the resulting effect on symbiont behavior.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward mechanisms are discussed as part of screening and contract theory, but not parameterized as in standard PGG experiments.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not present new empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed in biological and evolutionary terms, not in the precise parameterized sense of laboratory PGG experiments.
- The models assume infinite or large populations and do not address all real-world complexities (e.g., stochasticity, learning, bounded rationality).
- No direct predictions are made for specific experimental game designs; the results are general and qualitative.
- Some dimensions relevant to laboratory PGGs (e.g., chat, feedback, identification of punishers) are not addressed.
