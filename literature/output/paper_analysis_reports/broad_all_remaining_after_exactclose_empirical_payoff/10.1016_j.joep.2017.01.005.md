# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Better stuck together or free to go? Of the stability of cooperation when individuals have outside options
Authors: Gaudeul, A., Crosetto, P., & Riener, G.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

- average payoff in individual project

Non-payoff outcomes:
- exit frequency
- participation in public project
- beliefs about peer's effort
- beliefs about peer's participation
- drivers of exit (e.g., opportunity cost, exploitation aversion, fear of being left alone, failure in public project)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average individual payoff (efficiency/welfare). Non-payoff outcomes such as exit frequency and beliefs are analyzed as mechanisms and drivers.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment varied the ease of exit from a public project (barriers to exit) in a repeated, two-player, stochastic public-good-like game. When exit was easier (lower barriers), participants exited more frequently, but this did not increase efficiency or average payoffs. In fact, average individual welfare was lower in treatments with lower barriers to exit. The main reason was excessive exit, driven by pessimistic beliefs about the peer's likelihood of leaving, rather than accurate cost-benefit analysis. The study finds that exit is used both as a response to perceived exploitation and as a punishment for failure, but these mechanisms do not improve group efficiency. The findings suggest that making exit easier undermines group stability and reduces welfare, contrary to some theoretical expectations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-good-like environments with imperfect monitoring and stochastic outcomes, making exit easier (i.e., lowering barriers to exit) reduces efficiency and average payoffs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of exit options (a form of sanction or punishment via exclusion/self-exclusion) on efficiency. The results suggest that, unless exit is tightly controlled or costly, its availability will likely decrease group welfare due to excessive and inefficient use. For prediction tasks, the presence and ease of exit should be treated as a negative moderator of efficiency, even when the control (no-exit) game already has moderate efficiency. The study does not test classic costly punishment (e.g., deduction points), but its findings on exit as a sanction are highly informative for settings where exit or exclusion is a possible response to non-cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 32 periods (rounds) in total, with three different partners (8, 14, and 10 rounds per matching).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (effort) is continuous (0-10), but project choice (public vs. individual) is binary each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The probability of project success is a function of total effort: f(z) = sqrt(z/22). Payoff per success is fixed (20 or 16/24). Not a classic linear MPCR, but the marginal return to effort is shared and non-linear.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told the number of periods in each matching (8, 14, 10), and that there would be 32 rounds in total.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each period, participants saw their own and their peer's project choices, but not the peer's effort. They also saw their own history of choices and outcomes.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Exit is observable: after project choice, agents are informed of the peer's joining decision. However, all interactions are anonymous and there is no identification beyond the current peer.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not implement classic costly punishment (e.g., deduction points); exit is the only available sanction, so results may not generalize to other forms of punishment.
- The game is a two-player, stochastic, public-good-like environment with imperfect monitoring, which differs from standard linear PGGs with more players and deterministic returns.
- All interactions are in the lab with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The findings are most directly relevant to settings where exit or exclusion is the main sanction, not to environments with explicit punishment or reward mechanisms.
- The non-linear, probabilistic production function (success probability) differs from standard linear MPCR public goods games.
