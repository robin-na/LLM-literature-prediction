Title: Stern-judging: A simple, successful norm which promotes cooperation under indirect reciprocity
Authors: Pacheco, J. M., Santos, F. C., & Chalub, F. A. C. C.
Journal: Plos Computational Biology
Year: 2006

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

- average payoff per tribe
- maximized average payoff
- performance of norms in terms of group payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main outcome is the emergence and evolutionary stability of social norms (especially stern-judging) that maximize average group payoff in a model of indirect reciprocity. The model tracks average payoffs as a measure of evolutionary success, but does not report efficiency in the public goods game sense. Behavioral outcomes (cooperation, norm adoption) are also tracked.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in a multilevel evolutionary model of indirect reciprocity, the stern-judging norm—where helping good individuals or refusing help to bad individuals is rewarded, and the opposite is punished—emerges as the dominant and most robust social norm. This norm leads to maximized average payoffs for groups, indicating high efficiency in sustaining cooperation. The result is robust to different forms of group selection and inter-tribe conflict. The model suggests that simple, reputation-based punishment mechanisms are sufficient to promote and maintain high levels of cooperation and group welfare, even in the presence of errors and migration.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that simple, reputation-based punishment mechanisms (like stern-judging) can robustly increase group efficiency and cooperation in indirect reciprocity settings. While the model is not a public goods game per se, it is adjacent: it involves costly helping, reputation, and punishment via social norms. The findings suggest that, in environments where indirect reciprocity and reputation are salient, enabling punishment (in the form of social norm enforcement) is likely to increase group efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines. However, the model is abstract and does not manipulate most game design dimensions directly, so its predictive value is strongest for settings with similar indirect reciprocity structure and less for standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each tribe has 64 individuals; the model uses 64 tribes. All individuals interact with all others in their tribe.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each generation, every individual interacts once with every other in their tribe (single round per pair per generation); simulations run for 10,000 generations (9,000 burn-in, 1,000 data collection).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/helping is binary: donor chooses YES (help) or NO (refuse).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each help action costs 1 to donor, benefits recipient by b > 1. b is varied (e.g., b=2, b>=3). No explicit MPCR as in PGG, but analogous to b/c ratio.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as loss of reputation (bad reputation) for norm violations; this is not a direct payoff deduction but affects future payoffs via reputation. The effectiveness of this mechanism is central to the model's results.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of generations and rounds is fixed and known in the simulation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reputations are public and efficiently spread to all individuals in the tribe; all can observe the outcome of each interaction.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- The game structure is indirect reciprocity, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as reputation loss, not as direct costly punishment; no explicit cost to the punisher.
- Most game design dimensions are fixed or not manipulated; the model does not test the effect of varying these dimensions.
- No explicit analysis of efficiency as a ratio to full cooperation, though average group payoff is maximized.
- No human subjects or real-world data; findings are based on evolutionary simulations.
