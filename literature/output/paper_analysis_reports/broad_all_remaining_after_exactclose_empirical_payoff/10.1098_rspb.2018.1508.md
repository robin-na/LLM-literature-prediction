## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a population of initially non-cooperative individuals, a reputation system based on punishment is likely to emerge first and drive the initial evolution of cooperation. However, once cooperation is established, it is mainly sustained by a reputation system based on cooperative actions (social image). The model shows that cooperation rates are highest when both punishment-based and cooperation-based reputation systems are available. The emergence and stability of these systems depend on the costs of information acquisition (gossip) and the relevance of active gossip channels. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but focuses on cooperation rates and the use of reputation systems.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that punishment-based reputation systems can help establish cooperation in repeated social dilemmas, especially when information about others' behavior is costly to acquire. However, it does not provide direct evidence about efficiency or group payoff, nor does it use a standard public goods game (it uses a dyadic Prisoner's Dilemma with a punishment stage). The findings suggest that punishment may be most important for the initial emergence of cooperation, but less so for its maintenance. For prediction tasks about efficiency in public goods games with punishment, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly by highlighting the role of punishment-based reputation in overcoming initial defection.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N=100 in baseline; dyadic (2-player) interactions per round; group size is a parameter in simulations.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each individual has an expected m=30 interactions per generation; total rounds per generation is M = mN/2.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: contribute y or not (defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Public good multiplier r=1.6; in dyadic PD, each gets half the multiplied sum. Equivalent to MPCR = r/2 = 0.8.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher per act is k=0.2.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment inflicts p=0.5 on target per k=0.2 cost; effectiveness ratio p/k=2.5.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of interactions per generation is fixed, but individuals do not know the number of interactions that have occurred within a generation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players can acquire information about others' reputations via costly gossip; not automatic, but possible.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reputation for punishing is tracked and can be learned by others (via gossip), so punishment identity is available if information is acquired.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a simulation/theoretical model, not an empirical or experimental study.
- The game is a repeated dyadic Prisoner's Dilemma with a punishment stage, not a standard public goods game with group interaction.
- Outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates, strategy frequencies), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Information about others' behavior is acquired via a costly gossip mechanism, which may not map directly to lab or field PGG designs.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes; findings are only indirectly relevant for efficiency prediction tasks.
