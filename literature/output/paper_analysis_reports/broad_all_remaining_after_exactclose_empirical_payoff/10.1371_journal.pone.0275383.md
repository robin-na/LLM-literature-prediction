# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effectiveness of prosocial policies: Gender differences arising from social norms
Authors: Cabrales, A., Kendall, R., & Sánchez, A.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average speed (AS)
- earnings from belief elicitation
- driving choice payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- driving style choice (Fast, Slow, Auto)
- beliefs about others' choices
- personal normative beliefs
- normative expectations
- punishment fund contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: driving style choices and beliefs/norms. Average speed is a proxy for safety and negative externality, not a direct group payoff. Earnings are reported but not as the primary focus. Efficiency (as in public goods) is not directly measured.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that policy interventions (framing, exogenous punishment, endogenous punishment) do not reduce the overall average speed (a proxy for collective safety) compared to control. However, there are strong gender differences: policies reduce risky (Fast) choices and average speed among female participants, but increase them among males. This results in no net improvement in group-level outcomes and increases gender inequality in payoffs. The effect is mediated by differences in social norms and normative expectations. The most effective policy for changing female behavior is endogenous punishment, but male participants respond by increasing risky behavior, offsetting the gains. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff in the public goods sense, and the main outcomes are behavioral and norm-related.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment mechanisms (both exogenous and endogenous) on group-level efficiency or payoff in public-goods-like environments may be highly heterogeneous and context-dependent, especially along gender lines. In this specific driving game, punishment does not improve group-level efficiency (as proxied by average speed or safety) because positive effects for one subgroup (females) are offset by negative effects for another (males). The design is not a standard PGG, and efficiency is not directly measured, so the findings should be transferred with caution. The main implication for prediction is that punishment may not always increase efficiency, especially if there are strong heterogeneities in norm compliance or behavioral response. The study highlights the importance of considering subgroup responses and social norms when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sessions had 8-12 participants; N=10 is used in the game model and payoff calculations.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds was randomly determined between 17 and 25; participants did not know the final round in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (driving style) is a discrete choice among three options (Fast, Slow, Auto), not continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Endogenous, cost to punishers is £1 per contribution to the punishment fund; in Exogenous, punishment is imposed by the experimenter (no cost to participants).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Endogenous, each £1 contributed increases the fine by £2.5; total fine is number of contributors × £2.5. In Exogenous, fine is £4 with 25% probability.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the experiment would last between 18 and 25 rounds, but the exact number was randomly determined and not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the distribution of driving style choices in the previous round and their own guess about the distribution.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a driving game with negative externalities and discrete choices.
- Efficiency or group payoff (in the PGG sense) is not directly measured or reported; average speed is used as a proxy for safety, not for efficiency.
- Main outcomes are behavioral (choices, beliefs, norms), not payoff-based.
- Strong gender heterogeneity in response to punishment means group-level effects may not generalize to other contexts.
- Sample size per session is limited by group size; number of independent observations is relatively low.
- Findings may not transfer directly to standard PGGs or to environments without strong social norm heterogeneity.
