# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When Does Transparency Backfire? Putting Jeremy Bentham's Theory of General Prevention to the Experimental Test
Authors: Engel, C.
Journal: Journal of Empirical Legal Studies
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total earnings of contributors
- total earnings of supervisors
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- change in contribution after punishment
- sensitivity to others' contributions and punishment
- punishment received
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution level. Payoff-related outcomes (total earnings) are reported descriptively but not as the main focus of analysis. Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed; the focus is on contribution behavior and its response to punishment and information treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment manipulates the level of feedback about punishment and contributions in a standard linear public goods game with centralized punishment. Three feedback treatments (low, medium, high) vary the precision of information about others' contributions and punishment. The main finding is that full transparency (high treatment: individual-level information about both contributions and punishment) leads to significantly lower contributions than aggregate or less precise feedback. Information about punishment of others does not increase cooperation; instead, it can reduce it. Participants are more responsive to descriptive norms (others' contributions) than to injunctive signals (punishment). The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable, but reports total earnings descriptively.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with centralized punishment, increasing transparency about individual punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation (contributions), contrary to standard deterrence theory. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment with high transparency may not increase efficiency and could even reduce it, especially if the control (no punishment) game already has moderate contributions. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to contribution behavior. The design dimensions most relevant here are the feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), centralized punishment, and the linear PGG structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 contributors (type A) and 1 supervisor (type B), but only the 4 contributors make public goods decisions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each phase consists of 10 rounds; two phases per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; groups are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many of 20 talers to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4 (each taler contributed returns 0.4 to each of 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (supervisor) pays 1 taler to reduce target's payoff by 3 talers.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 taler spent destroys 3 talers for the punished).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds (10 per phase) is announced in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Feedback about others' contributions and punishment is manipulated across treatments: low (aggregate), medium (aggregate + punishment), high (individual-level). High transparency reduces contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the high treatment, players can identify which group member was punished and how much. This transparency reduces contributions.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution behavior, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not directly analyzed.
- The punishment institution is centralized (one supervisor per group), which may differ from decentralized punishment in other PGG studies.
- The supervisor benefits from the public good, which may affect punishment motives and external validity.
- The experiment uses a lab setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to field or policy contexts.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The effect of transparency is tested only in the context of centralized punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
