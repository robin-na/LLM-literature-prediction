## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not present new empirical data; its conclusions are based on synthesis and interpretation of existing studies.
- Some claims about the effects of communication, reputation, and coordination on efficiency are supported by limited or indirect evidence.
- The review notes that strong and precise experimental evidence for the interaction of punishment and indirect reciprocity, and for the role of anonymity in antisocial punishment, is still lacking.
- The paper does not systematically analyze the effects of all game design dimensions (e.g., MPCR, player count, all-or-nothing choices) and relies on generalizations from the literature.
