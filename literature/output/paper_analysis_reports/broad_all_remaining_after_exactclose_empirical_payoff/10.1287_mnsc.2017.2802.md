# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Dispute Resolution or Escalation? The Strategic Gaming of Feedback Withdrawal Options in Online Markets
Authors: Bolton, G., Greiner, B., & Ockenfels, A.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- market efficiency (realized gains from trade divided by maximum gains from trade)
- group payoff (buyer and seller payoffs)
- average quality delivered
- frequency of payment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- feedback given (positive/negative)
- make-good behavior
- feedback withdrawal
- retaliatory feedback
- altruistic punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (gains from trade), payment, quality, and feedback behavior. Efficiency is explicitly calculated as realized gains from trade divided by maximum possible gains. Feedback and punishment-like behaviors are also analyzed, but efficiency is a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In laboratory markets with two-sided moral hazard (both buyer and seller can defect), the introduction of a mutual feedback withdrawal option leads to more strategic negative feedback, less honest feedback, and lower efficiency (measured as realized gains from trade divided by maximum possible gains). The feedback system becomes less informative, and trust/trustworthiness are reduced. In one-sided markets (only seller can defect), the feedback withdrawal option does not significantly affect efficiency or the informativeness of feedback. The negative effect of FBW on efficiency is thus conditional on the presence of two-sided moral hazard and mutual withdrawal. The paper also finds that some buyers engage in 'altruistic punishment' by refusing to withdraw negative feedback even at a cost to themselves, partially mitigating the detrimental effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments structurally similar to public goods games with two-sided moral hazard and mutual punishment/retaliation options, introducing a mutual feedback withdrawal (punishment-like) mechanism reduces efficiency relative to a control without such a mechanism. The effect is not observed in one-sided environments. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of mutual, bilateral punishment/retaliation options in two-sided games is likely to reduce efficiency, even if the control game is already inefficient. The effect is mediated by strategic retaliation and the breakdown of reputation as an incentive for cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar two-sided, bilateral punishment/retaliation structures.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each matching group in the lab experiment consisted of 8 participants (4 buyers, 4 sellers), with random matching each round. The game is played in dyads (buyer-seller pairs), but the group size is relevant for matching and reputation effects.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 60 rounds, with analyses focused on rounds 11-50 to avoid learning and endgame effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants was allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (payment) by the buyer is all-or-nothing (pay 100 ECUs or not), but the seller's quality choice is continuous (0-100%).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each quality point costs the seller 1 ECU and benefits the buyer by 3 ECUs, so the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) for the group is 3 (to buyer) + (-1 to seller) = 2 per unit, but for the buyer alone, mpcr is 3.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented via negative feedback, which imposes a cost on future earnings (reputation effect), not a direct monetary deduction. The cost is empirically supported by prior and current studies.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is via the impact of negative feedback on future payoffs (reputation). The feedback withdrawal option allows for the removal of negative feedback, reducing punishment effectiveness.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed that the experiment consisted of 60 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were shown the number of positive and negative feedback ratings their counterpart had received in previous rounds before each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Feedback is given directly between trading partners, so the identity of the punisher (feedback giver) is always known to the recipient.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a standard public goods game but a bilateral trust/trade game with two-sided or one-sided moral hazard; results may not generalize to n-player public goods games.
- Punishment is implemented via reputation/feedback rather than direct monetary fines; the cost and effectiveness of punishment are mediated by reputation effects, which may differ from direct punishment in PGGs.
- The lab environment may not capture all complexities of real online markets, such as repeated interactions with the same partner, external enforcement, or platform-level interventions.
- The study does not manipulate or analyze the effect of group size beyond dyads; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- No explicit reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- The findings are most relevant for environments with two-sided, bilateral punishment/retaliation options and may not apply to unilateral or centralized punishment systems.
