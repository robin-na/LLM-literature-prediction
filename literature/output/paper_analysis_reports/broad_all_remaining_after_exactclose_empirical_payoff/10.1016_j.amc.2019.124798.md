The paper is primarily theoretical and review-based, focusing on mechanisms (language, communication) that facilitate cooperation, norm enforcement, and punishment in collective action problems. It discusses how these mechanisms can increase cooperation and reduce free riding, but does not report direct empirical results on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare in public goods games.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that language and symbolic communication fundamentally alter the possibilities for collective action by simplifying coordination, reducing the costs of monitoring and punishing free riders, and amplifying reputation and signaling effects. It reviews theoretical models and empirical studies (from both ethnography and experiments) showing that communication (including chat, gossip, and feedback) increases cooperation rates and the effectiveness of punishment in social dilemmas. However, it does not report direct measures of efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with and without punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that enabling communication (chat, language, gossip) in public goods games and collective action settings increases cooperation and the effectiveness of punishment, which should, in principle, improve group outcomes and efficiency. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it is not directly usable for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of communication and norm establishment as moderators of punishment effectiveness.
