# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Unfairness sensitivity and social decision-making in individuals with alcohol dependence: A preliminary study
Authors: Brevers, D., Noël, X., Ermer, E., Dabiri, D., Verbanck, P., & Kornreich, C.
Journal: Drug and Alcohol Dependence
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of offers accepted at each level of fairness (fair, medium-fair, unfair)
- rejection rates of unfair offers
Outcome notes:
The dependent variable is the acceptance/rejection rate of offers in the Ultimatum Game. No direct measure of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported, as the game is one-shot and played as responder only, with no real monetary payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Alcohol-dependent individuals are more likely to reject unfair offers in the Ultimatum Game compared to healthy controls, suggesting greater emotional reactivity to unfairness and reduced ability to make self-advantageous decisions in social contexts involving frustration. The study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, and the design does not include a punishment phase or a repeated public goods structure.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides limited direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar repeated social dilemmas. The Ultimatum Game is adjacent to the target environment, as rejection of unfair offers can be interpreted as a form of costly punishment, but the context is one-shot, dyadic, and does not involve group cooperation or repeated interactions. The findings may inform understanding of individual differences in punishment sensitivity, but do not provide evidence on efficiency or the effect of punishment mechanisms in public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game is played in dyads (2 players: proposer and responder), but only responder role is studied.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants played 54 single-shot trials, each with a new offer and a new (fictive) proposer; no repeated interaction with the same partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all offers are computer-mediated and anonymous except for a neutral face photo.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is operationalized as rejection of an offer, which is costly to both players (both get zero). The cost to the punisher (responder) is the foregone amount of the offer.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is maximal: rejection causes both players to get zero. No variation in punishment impact per unit cost.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials (54), but each is a one-shot with a new proposer; no repeated game structure.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, not a public goods game or repeated social dilemma, so findings may not generalize to group cooperation or punishment contexts.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings outcomes are reported; only acceptance/rejection rates are measured.
- All offers are hypothetical with no real monetary stakes, which may affect ecological validity.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of unfair offers, which is not directly comparable to punishment mechanisms in public goods games.
- No manipulation or variation of punishment cost or effectiveness; all rejections have the same consequence.
- No communication or reputation effects are present.
- Sample is limited to alcohol-dependent individuals and healthy controls, which may limit generalizability.
