## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical analysis shows that action-based punishment/reward policies are superior to outcome-based policies in generalized volunteer's dilemma settings (where more than one volunteer is needed and costs are private). Action-based policies can uniquely induce the efficient (second-best) outcome and prevent the no-participation equilibrium, while outcome-based policies always allow for a no-participation equilibrium, which is stable and potentially focal. The required level of punishment/reward under action-based policies is lower than under outcome-based policies. Combined policies that include punishment for non-participation can also rule out the no-participation equilibrium. The analysis is grounded in social cost minimization and group welfare, not just participation rates.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that punishment (when implemented as action-based or combined with action-contingency) will have a more positive effect on efficiency (group welfare, social cost minimization) than outcome-based punishment in public-goods-like environments where more than one participant is needed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (action-based vs. outcome-based) is a critical moderator: action-based punishment can uniquely induce efficient outcomes and prevent social traps, while outcome-based punishment may fail to improve efficiency if the no-participation equilibrium is selected. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in settings with binary (all-or-nothing) participation, private costs, and no coordination. However, the results are theoretical and based on a generalized volunteer's dilemma, which is adjacent but not identical to standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is parameterized by n (number of players), and comparative statics are discussed for different n. Example calculations use n=10.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a one-shot (single-round) simultaneous-move game; repeated or multi-round play is not analyzed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to participate (help) or not (A = {0,1}).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (sanction) is modeled as a per-capita payment S; the cost to the punisher is not explicitly modeled, as the policy is imposed by a planner.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the size of S and the policy structure (action-based vs. outcome-based). The impact per unit S is analyzed in equilibrium conditions.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewards (R) are included as possible policy instruments, both alone and in combination with punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is modeled as a per-capita payment R; the cost to the rewarder is not explicitly modeled, as the policy is imposed by a planner.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the size of R and the policy structure. The impact per unit R is analyzed in equilibrium conditions.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot; all players know the structure and that there is only one round.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- The setting is a generalized volunteer's dilemma (threshold public good with binary participation and private costs), which is adjacent but not identical to standard linear public goods games.
- Punishment and reward are implemented by a planner, not by peer assignment; costs to punishers/rewarders are not modeled.
- The model assumes one-shot, simultaneous-move play with no communication or coordination.
- Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is not defined, limiting direct comparability to standard PGGs.
- The results may not generalize to repeated games, games with communication, or settings with endogenous punishment/reward assignment.
