# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Retaliation or selfishness? An rTMS investigation of the role of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in prosocial motives
Authors: Müller-Leinss, J. M., Enzi, B., Flasbeck, V., & Brüne, M.
Journal: Social Neuroscience
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rate of fair/unfair offers in Dictator Game (as proposer) towards previously fair/unfair opponents
- punishment rate (unfair offers to previously unfair opponents)
- fairness rate (fair offers to previously fair opponents)
- rejection rates in Ultimatum Game (as responder)
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral responses (retaliation, fairness, selfishness) in economic games after rTMS manipulation, not on group or individual payoffs, efficiency, or welfare. Outcomes are rates of fair/unfair offers and punishment, not monetary efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Inhibiting the right DLPFC via rTMS increases both retaliation against previously unfair opponents and selfish behavior (less fairness) towards previously fair opponents in a Dictator Game following an Ultimatum Game. The manipulation did not affect rejection rates in the UG. The findings suggest the right DLPFC is involved in controlling selfish motives, but the design does not distinguish between retaliation and pure selfishness. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and is not a public goods game or direct punishment paradigm.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides limited direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent: it uses sequential UG and DG with role reversal and rTMS manipulation, focusing on individual-level retaliation/selfishness rather than group-level cooperation or efficiency. There is no manipulation of punishment institutions, costs, or group payoff structure as in PGGs. The main outcomes are behavioral (fair/unfair offers), not efficiency or payoff. Thus, the paper is not directly informative for the prediction task, except as context for the neural basis of retaliation/selfishness in economic games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played between two players (subject and virtual opponent). Four virtual opponents are used, but each round is dyadic.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game: 72 trials per subject (26 fair, 26 unfair, 20 computer offers); Dictator Game: number of rounds not explicitly stated, but appears to match number of opponents.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are via computer interface with virtual opponents.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is continuous: proposer chooses split of 10 Euros (e.g., 5:5, 4:6, etc.), not all-or-nothing.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of trials in each game (UG: 72 trials).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know which virtual opponent they are interacting with in each round (opponent's picture shown), so identity is known.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or a direct punishment/reward institution; it uses sequential UG and DG with role reversal and rTMS manipulation.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (fair/unfair offers, punishment rates).
- Punishment is operationalized as making an unfair offer in the DG, which is not costly to the punisher and increases own payoff, unlike standard punishment in PGGs.
- The design does not distinguish between retaliation and pure selfishness as motives for behavior after right DLPFC inhibition.
- All opponents are virtual; although participants are told they represent real people, this may affect ecological validity.
- No manipulation or reporting of key PGG dimensions such as MPCR, punishment cost, or group size beyond dyads.
