## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across 15 field sites and two economic games, the study finds no reliable evidence that perceiving a deity as moralistic (as measured by free-listing moral concerns) predicts increased cooperation (measured as coin allocations to a distant, anonymous co-religionist). Some sites show small positive trends, but overall effects are small, inconsistent, and not robust. The study also finds no robust interaction between perceived deity punitiveness, knowledge, and moral concern. The main outcomes are behavioral (coin allocations), not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that supernatural moral concerns do not play a direct, cross-culturally reliable role in motivating cooperative actions in these game contexts.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not use a PGG or a direct punishment mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. The games used (RAG and DG) are adjacent to PGGs but focus on individual allocation decisions rather than collective contributions or group outcomes. The study's main manipulation is the salience of supernatural moral concerns, not the presence or absence of a punishment institution. Thus, while the findings suggest that moralistic supernatural punishment beliefs do not reliably increase cooperation in these settings, they do not inform the effect of formal punishment mechanisms on efficiency in PGGs. The paper is best used as evidence that the mere salience of supernatural punishment (without institutionalized punishment options) does not robustly increase cooperative allocations in adjacent economic games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are two-player (SELF vs. DISTANT or LOCAL vs. DISTANT). Each participant allocates coins between themselves (or a local co-religionist) and a distant co-religionist.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a one-shot allocation (30 coins in RAG, 10 coins in DG), not repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; games are played individually and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/transfer is continuous: participants can allocate any number of coins between recipients.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; participants know the structure and number of allocations.

## Important Limitations
- The games used (Random Allocation Game and Dictator Game) are not public goods games and do not include a punishment or reward mechanism.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual allocation behavior is measured.
- The manipulation concerns the salience of supernatural moral concerns, not the presence or absence of a formal punishment institution.
- Results may not generalize to environments with explicit punishment or to standard PGGs.
- Substantial site-specific variation suggests that context matters and cross-cultural generalization is limited.
- The artificial nature of the games may not map onto salient local mental models or real-world cooperation dilemmas.
