Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the confrontation rate (i.e., frequency of confrontational vs cooperative choices) as determined by evolving gene profiles. Expected utility (EU) is calculated for the environment and compared before and after evolution, but the focus is on behavioral adaptation (confrontation/cooperation) rather than direct group payoff or efficiency as in standard PGGs. Average health and number of survivors are also reported, but not as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The simulations show that the confrontation rate in a population is highly sensitive to the magnitude of punishment (cost of conflict) and reward (resource availability). As punishment increases, confrontation rates decrease, and organisms become more cooperative. The adaptation is driven by the evolution of gene profiles that determine the probability of confrontation. The model also finds that memory (even a simple one-step memory) has a limited but significant effect on confrontation rates, especially as punishment increases. The study provides evidence that risk-taking and confrontation are shaped by both environmental parameters and genetic/cognitive factors, but does not directly measure efficiency or group payoff as in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that increasing the cost or effectiveness of punishment in a confrontation/cooperation environment reduces the frequency of confrontational (defecting) behavior and increases cooperation. While the model is not a public goods game, the findings suggest that in environments where punishment is more costly or effective, populations evolve to be more cooperative, which would likely increase efficiency in analogous PGG-like settings. However, the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff measures limits the ability to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes from design dimensions. The results are most useful for understanding the qualitative direction of punishment effects in related strategic environments.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 500 cycles (generations), with each organism participating in one pairwise game per cycle.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each decision is binary: confront or cooperate (all-or-nothing choice).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (cost of conflict) is a key manipulated variable, ranging from 1 to 100. Higher punishment reduces confrontation rates (i.e., increases cooperation).

Punishment is implemented as a direct cost to both parties if both confront, or to the loser in zero-sum games. The effectiveness of punishment is manipulated via its magnitude.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game but a pairwise confrontation/cooperation game, limiting direct applicability to PGG-like environments.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured; the main outcome is confrontation rate, with expected utility and health as proxies.
- Organisms are not conscious and do not plan ahead; adaptation is purely genetic and probabilistic.
- No communication, reputation, or social learning mechanisms are present.
- The evolutionary simulation abstracts away from many complexities of real social or economic environments.
