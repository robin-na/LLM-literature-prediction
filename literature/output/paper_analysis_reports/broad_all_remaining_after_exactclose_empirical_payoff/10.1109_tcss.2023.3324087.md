## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- fraction of data providers choosing high-quality data
- fraction of data users choosing to acquire data
- fraction of data regulators choosing to regulate
- evolutionary stable strategies (ESS) for strategy combinations
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the equilibrium fractions of each strategy (cooperation/defection) for each player type. The model is based on payoffs, but the reported results focus on behavioral strategy adoption, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the presence and magnitude of regulatory rewards/penalties (F) can shift the system toward cooperative equilibria, where all parties choose the socially desirable strategies (high-quality data provision, data acquisition, and regulation). The model provides explicit conditions under which full cooperation, partial cooperation, or defection equilibria are stable. Increasing the effectiveness or magnitude of regulatory punishment/reward expands the parameter space in which cooperation is stable. However, the main reported outcomes are the equilibrium strategy fractions, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings are useful for understanding how punishment/reward mechanisms can promote cooperation in multi-stakeholder environments similar to public goods games, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that increasing the strength or effectiveness of regulatory punishment/reward (analogous to punishment_tech and punishment_cost) can promote cooperative behavior among agents in a multi-stakeholder environment. While the model is not a standard public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff, it is informative for predicting that stronger, more effective punishment mechanisms are likely to increase cooperation rates. However, because efficiency is not directly analyzed, the paper should be used as indirect evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency, with caution regarding transfer to standard PGG settings.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model is continuous-time evolutionary dynamics (replicator equations), not discrete rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player chooses between two discrete strategies (cooperate/defect for each role).

- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Parameter F represents the magnitude/effectiveness of regulatory punishment/reward. Higher F increases cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Regulatory mechanism includes both reward and penalty (F) for providers and users.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost to regulator for rewarding/punishing is C3; reward/penalty magnitude is F.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a three-role evolutionary game with role-specific payoffs.
- Outcomes are reported as equilibrium strategy fractions, not as group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are theoretical or simulation-based.
- Infinite population assumption; not directly transferable to small-group PGG settings.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes; only indirect inference is possible.
