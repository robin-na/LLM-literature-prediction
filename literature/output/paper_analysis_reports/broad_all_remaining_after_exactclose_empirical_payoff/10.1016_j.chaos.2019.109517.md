The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare directly, but does provide payoff functions for each strategy and discusses the elimination of defectors as beneficial for the group.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that, in a spatial public goods game with both punishment and exclusion available, making exclusion the primary sanctioning mechanism (with punishers acting as pure cooperators when excluders are present) leads to a larger parameter region where cooperation dominates, compared to the reverse. The average frequency of cooperation is higher and the threshold for the emergence of cooperation is lower when exclusion leads. The results are robust across variations in the punishment fine, exclusion cost, and synergy factor. However, the paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the findings are based on behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates) rather than direct efficiency measures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a spatial PGG with both punishment and exclusion, exclusion as the leading sanctioning mechanism is more effective at promoting cooperation than punishment as the leading mechanism, across a wide range of parameter values. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The results suggest that game designs enabling exclusion (rather than punishment) as the primary sanction may yield higher cooperation rates, which may correlate with higher efficiency, but this is not directly demonstrated. The findings are most relevant for settings with spatial structure, multiple sanctioning options, and evolutionary updating.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; all results are based on cooperation rates and strategy frequencies.
- Findings are based on a spatial evolutionary simulation (square lattice); results may not generalize to well-mixed or other network structures.
- No empirical or experimental data; all results are from theoretical modeling and simulation.
- No reward mechanism is considered; only punishment and exclusion are compared.
- The model assumes agents can perfectly coordinate to avoid redundant sanctioning (i.e., only one sanctioning mechanism is active per group per round), which may not be realistic in practice.
