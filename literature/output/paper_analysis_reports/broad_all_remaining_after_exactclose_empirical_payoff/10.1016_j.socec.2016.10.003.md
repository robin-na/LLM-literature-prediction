# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effect of third party intervention in the trust game
Authors: Fiedler, M., & Haruvy, E.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total payoff (sum of payoffs per round)
- earnings per round
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount sent by proposer (investment)
- proportion returned by responder
- punishment/reward assigned by third party
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: amount sent, proportion returned, and third party actions. Aggregate payoffs are discussed but not as the primary outcome. Efficiency is not directly calculated or reported, but total payoffs are described in the instructions and can be inferred from the design.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a trust game, third party monitoring increases investment and return rates compared to no third party. Adding punishment or reward capabilities to the third party does not significantly increase aggregate cooperation or efficiency beyond monitoring alone, especially in random partner settings. In fixed partner settings, punishment may have a somewhat greater effect, but the difference is not large. Individual players are responsive to punishment and reward, but the overall aggregate effect is muted because the actual use of punishment/reward is infrequent and the absence of punishment/reward can itself act as reinforcement. The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, but the design allows for inference about total payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that in trust game environments (which are adjacent to public goods games), third party punishment does not substantially increase efficiency or aggregate payoff beyond what is achieved by third party monitoring alone, especially when partners are randomly matched. The effect of punishment is more pronounced in fixed partner settings, but still not dramatically higher than monitoring. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in similar trust/investment games, enabling punishment may not yield large efficiency gains over monitoring, particularly with random matching. However, the results may not transfer directly to public goods games, and efficiency is not directly measured.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: proposer (A), responder (B), and third party (C) in the main conditions; 2 players in the baseline.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) is continuous: proposer can send any amount from 0 to 10.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Amount sent by proposer is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is not a standard PGG MPCR; it is the trust game multiplier.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party can spend up to 5 L€ per target; each 1 L€ spent reduces target's payoff by 3 L€.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 L€ spent by third party reduces target's payoff by 3 L€).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward condition is a treatment arm: third party can reward by spending L€, which increases target's payoff by 3 L€ per L€ spent.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1 L€ per unit; effectiveness is 3:1 (1 L€ spent increases target's payoff by 3 L€).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (1 L€ spent increases target's payoff by 3 L€).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, participants receive feedback about their partners' actions and any punishment/reward received. However, third party's transfer to a player is only shown to that player and the third party.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the identity (role) of the third party who punishes or rewards them, but not the personal identity; roles are fixed and anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust (investment) game, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to PGG settings.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported as a primary outcome; only behavioral outcomes and payoffs per round are described.
- The third party is not affected by the payoffs of the other players, which may differ from some real-world or PGG-like sanctioning environments.
- Punishment and reward are administered by a third party, not by affected parties, which may limit comparability to standard PGG punishment treatments.
- The aggregate effect of punishment/reward is limited by the low frequency of their use; results may differ if punishment/reward is more frequent or mandatory.
