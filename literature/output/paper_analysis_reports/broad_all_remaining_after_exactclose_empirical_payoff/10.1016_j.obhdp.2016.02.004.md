# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The impact of personal responsibility on the (un)willingness to punish non-cooperation and reward cooperation
Authors: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- willingness to punish (frequency and size of punishment assigned)
- willingness to reward (frequency and size of reward assigned)
- felt personal responsibility
- felt entitlement
- felt external accountability
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and size of punishment/reward assigned to a target after observing cooperative or non-cooperative behavior in a common resource dilemma. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that people are more reluctant to punish non-cooperators when they feel personally responsible (i.e., when deciding individually), but this reluctance is attenuated when responsibility is shared in a group. This effect is specific to punishment and does not apply to rewarding cooperation. The effect is mediated by feelings of personal responsibility and entitlement, not by external accountability. The experiments use a common resource dilemma with sanctioning opportunities, but do not measure or report group efficiency or total payoffs. Thus, the findings are most relevant for understanding behavioral moderators of punishment willingness, not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to punish non-cooperation is higher when punishment decisions are made jointly by groups rather than by individuals, due to reduced personal responsibility and increased entitlement. However, since the study does not report efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or common resource games. The results are useful for predicting when punishment will be more or less likely to be used, but not for predicting the efficiency consequences of enabling punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the structure of the punishment decision (individual vs. group) may moderate the behavioral use of punishment, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 4 players per group; Experiment 2: 5 players per group; Experiment 3: 5 players per group. The number of players is fixed and reported for each experiment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experiment appears to use a single-shot (one-round) common resource task per participant, as only one harvest and one sanctioning decision are described.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants is allowed; participants are seated in separate cubicles and interact only via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvest) choices are continuous: participants choose how many chips to harvest (0-10). Sanctioning decisions are either binary (Experiment 1) or continuous (Experiments 2 and 3).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 4 players, 40 chips, chips left in pool are doubled and divided equally (MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5). Experiment 2 and 3: 5 players, 50 chips, same doubling and division (MPCR = 2/5 = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher: 'participants could administer the (negative and positive) sanction without any cost to themselves.'
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1; each decrement point reduces the target's payoff by €0.01. In Experiment 1, the fine equals the number of chips harvested (up to 10 chips, €0.10 each). In Experiments 2 and 3, up to 100 decrement points (€1.00 max).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available in Experiments 1 and 2 (participants can assign a bonus/increment points to cooperators). Experiment 3 focuses on punishment only.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is costless to the rewarder: 'participants could administer the (negative and positive) sanction without any cost to themselves.'
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1; each increment point increases the target's payoff by €0.01. In Experiment 1, the bonus equals the number of chips left in the pool. In Experiment 2, up to 100 increment points (€1.00 max).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the structure of the task and that there is a single harvest and sanctioning decision; the number of rounds is known and fixed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the harvest decision of Person C (the target of sanctioning). No mention of other summary information about all group members' contributions or payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 3 manipulates whether the punishment decision is public (accountable) or private (unaccountable). In accountable conditions, the number of points assigned and the explanation are made public to the group; in unaccountable, they are not.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes; all dependent variables are behavioral (punishment/reward assigned) or psychological (responsibility, entitlement, accountability).
- The experiments are single-shot (one round), so findings may not generalize to repeated or dynamic public goods or common resource games.
- Punishment and reward are costless to the punisher/rewarder, which differs from most real-world or standard experimental public goods games.
- The common resource dilemma is similar but not identical to a standard public goods game; the structure is a linear common pool resource with doubling of the remainder.
- The study focuses on the willingness to punish/reward, not on the effects of punishment/reward on cooperation or efficiency.
- All participants are university students from a single country, which may limit generalizability.
