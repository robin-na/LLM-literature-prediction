Outcome notes:
The paper reports on the frequencies and coexistence of strategies (cooperators, defectors, rewarding cooperators) in a spatial public goods game with reward. It does not report group payoff, efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. The main outcomes are behavioral (strategy prevalence and phase transitions), not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing costly reward in a spatial public goods game can promote cooperation, particularly when the synergy factor (MPCR) is low. However, the effect is non-monotonic: moderate rewards can be more effective than high rewards due to the emergence of cyclic dominance among defectors, cooperators, and rewarding cooperators. At high synergy factors, network reciprocity alone can sustain cooperation, and reward mainly determines which cooperative strategy prevails. The paper notes that, compared to punishment, reward is less efficient at promoting cooperation, especially because rewarding cooperators always pay a cost, making them vulnerable to second-order free-riders. The main reported outcomes are the stationary frequencies of each strategy and the phase diagrams of their coexistence, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the effect of reward (not punishment) on cooperation in spatial public goods games. It does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, it suggests that costly reward can promote cooperation under certain conditions, but is generally less effective than punishment. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of reward mechanisms and the importance of spatial structure, synergy factor (MPCR), and cost-benefit ratios in shaping cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only adjacent: it does not provide direct evidence on punishment or efficiency, but may inform expectations about the relative effectiveness of reward versus punishment and the importance of game parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player interacts with k=4 neighbors on a square lattice; each group has 5 players (focal + 4 neighbors). System size L=400-5000, but local group size is always 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 10^7 Monte Carlo steps; effectively infinite/steady-state evolutionary dynamics, not a fixed number of rounds as in human experiments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each cooperator (C or RC) contributes 1, defectors contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied (2.0, 3.5, 4.4); MPCR = r/(k+1) = r/5.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is the main treatment variable; presence and magnitude of reward are systematically varied.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Cost of reward (gamma) is varied; higher cost reduces effectiveness of reward in promoting cooperation.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward impact per unit cost (beta/gamma) is a key parameter; higher benefit-to-cost ratio increases effectiveness, but high rewards can also destabilize cooperation due to cyclic dominance.

## Important Limitations
- The paper studies reward, not punishment; no direct evidence on punishment effects.
- Outcomes are strategy frequencies and coexistence, not group efficiency, payoff, or welfare.
- Agent-based simulation in spatial (lattice) populations; results may not generalize to well-mixed or human experimental settings.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff.
- No human subjects; findings are theoretical/simulation-based.
