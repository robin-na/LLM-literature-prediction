# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Leadership with Imperfect Monitoring
Authors: Eisenkopf, G., & Walter, C.
Journal: Leadership Quarterly
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- aggregate contributions
- coordination (correlation of contributions)
- punishment frequency and intensity
- responsiveness to recommendations
- alignment between leader and follower contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are aggregate contributions (cooperation) and coordination (correlation of contributions). The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare directly, nor does it provide explicit ratios of realized to maximum possible payoffs. Punishment effects are analyzed in terms of their impact on contributions, not on efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that in a three-player, 20-round public goods game, punishment by a leader only increases contributions when the leader can observe individual follower contributions (individual monitoring). Under group monitoring (only aggregate info), punishment does not increase contributions compared to a simple recommendation. Leading-by-example increases coordination (alignment of contributions) but not aggregate contributions relative to recommendations. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. However, since contributions are higher with punishment under individual monitoring, it is likely that efficiency would also be higher in that condition, assuming standard PGG payoff structure. The effect of punishment is thus strongly moderated by the monitoring technology.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether the punisher can observe individual contributions (i.e., the 'punishment_tech' and monitoring dimension). If only aggregate (group-level) monitoring is available, punishment does not increase cooperation or likely efficiency. If individual-level monitoring is available, punishment increases cooperation and likely efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency or payoff data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution rates. Other design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, etc.) are clearly specified and match standard PGG setups. The absence of communication and rewards, and the use of continuous contribution choices, are also informative for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 3 players (A, B, C) in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per group in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player can contribute any amount from 0 to 100 points per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (each contributed point is multiplied by 1.5 and divided among 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For each point the leader uses to punish, the leader loses 1/3 of the points deducted from the punished follower (i.e., 3:1 punishment ratio: 1 point cost to leader removes 3 points from follower).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is 3:1: for each point spent by the leader, 3 points are deducted from the punished follower.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is not available in any treatment; only punishment is implemented.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told in advance that there are 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All players see their own payoff and, depending on monitoring condition, either aggregate group contributions (group monitoring) or individual contributions (leader only, individual monitoring). No summary of others' payoffs is provided.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always administered by the leader (player C), and followers know that only the leader can punish. There is no ambiguity about the source of punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes directly; all main results are in terms of contributions and coordination.
- No explicit calculation or reporting of the ratio of realized to maximum possible payoffs (efficiency) is provided.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency must be inferred from contribution rates, which may not fully capture payoff effects if punishment is costly.
- The study uses only three-player groups and a fixed MPCR, limiting generalizability to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- No reward mechanism is included, so results do not inform about reward or combined reward-punishment environments.
- All treatments are in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
