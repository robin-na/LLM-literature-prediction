# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social norms: Enforcement, breakdown & polarization
Authors: Balafoutas, L., Dimant, E., Gächter, S., & Krupka, E.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average points earned per trial
- total points earned
- group payoff (implied by sum of individual payoffs)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- number of cooperation choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average points earned (payoff) and cooperation rate. Payoff is directly reported and analyzed, including as a function of strategy and context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Participants learned to cooperate and achieved high average payoffs against both tit-for-tat and Pavlov strategies in a standard context. However, when the context signaled the computer's probability of cooperation (spinner), cooperation and payoffs were higher against tit-for-tat than Pavlov. When participants believed they were playing another person, cooperation and payoffs were higher against Pavlov than tit-for-tat. Thus, the effect of the 'punishment-like' feedback embedded in tit-for-tat and Pavlov depends on both the strategy and the context. The paper provides direct evidence on average payoffs (efficiency) under different strategy-context combinations, but does not manipulate or isolate explicit punishment mechanisms as in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about how feedback strategies (tit-for-tat, Pavlov) and context affect efficiency (average payoff) in repeated, alternating-move prisoner's dilemma games. While not a public goods game, the payoff structure and the embedded reinforcement/punishment mechanisms are closely related. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment-like feedback on efficiency is context-dependent: tit-for-tat (which punishes defection) increases efficiency in some contexts but not others, and Pavlov (which is less punitive) can also support high efficiency depending on context. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that the design of feedback/punishment and the social framing of the game can moderate the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms, even when control efficiency is known.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (participant vs computer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game consists of 100 alternating trials.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players; in the 'other player' context, participants believe they are playing a human, but no communication is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choices are binary: cooperate (Left) or defect (Right).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff matrix: both cooperate = 5 each; both defect = 2 each; one cooperates, one defects = 1 (cooperator), 6 (defector). Not a standard PGG, but the marginal return to cooperation is clear.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is 100 trials.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the computer's choice each round. In the 'other player' context, participants also see the other player's payoff matrix and points.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a repeated, alternating-move prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Punishment is not implemented as a separate, costly action but is embedded in the tit-for-tat and Pavlov strategies.
- All games are two-player and against a computer, not multi-player or fully human-human.
- Context manipulations (spinner, belief about opponent) may not map directly to standard PGG design dimensions.
- Sample sizes are modest, and some analyses are underpowered.
- No explicit manipulation of punishment cost or technology.
