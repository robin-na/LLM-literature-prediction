## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated prisoner's dilemma games with noise, cooperation is frequent and yields higher payoffs than defection when the benefit/cost ratio is high enough to support cooperative equilibria. Lenient and forgiving strategies (e.g., Tit-for-2-Tats, Grim2) are both common and successful in these settings. When only defection is an equilibrium (low b/c), defection dominates and is most profitable. The presence of noise increases the use of longer-memory, more lenient strategies. The paper provides detailed payoff matrices and shows that, in treatments with cooperative equilibria, the highest-earning strategies are lenient and forgiving. Social preferences (as measured by a dictator game) do not explain cooperation; strategic concerns do.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated prisoner's dilemma games with and without noise, especially as a function of the benefit/cost ratio and error rate. While not a public goods game, the repeated PD is closely related, and the findings on how game structure (payoff ratios, noise) affect the efficiency of cooperative vs. noncooperative strategies are directly relevant. The paper provides explicit expected payoffs for each strategy under different conditions, allowing for quantitative prediction of group efficiency. The results show that, when the game supports cooperative equilibria, efficiency is higher and cooperative strategies outperform defectors. The absence of explicit punishment/reward mechanisms means the findings are most relevant for environments where punishment is endogenous (via strategy choice) rather than via a separate punishment stage.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) repeated prisoner's dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are indefinitely repeated with a continuation probability (delta = 7/8), leading to a random number of rounds per interaction (average ~8 rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (pay cost to benefit other) or defect (do nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is b/c divided by 2 (since two players); b/c ratios of 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 4 are tested. Higher b/c supports more cooperation and higher efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the continuation probability (delta = 7/8) but not the exact number of rounds in advance; game length is random.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the actual move of their partner each round (not the intended move if error occurs); no aggregate summaries.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not fully generalize to multi-player or public goods settings.
- There is no explicit punishment or reward stage; all sanctions are endogenous via strategy choice.
- All games are dyadic (two players), so findings may not extend to larger groups.
- Noise is implemented as random action flips, which may differ from other forms of implementation error or monitoring noise.
- The study does not manipulate or analyze explicit punishment cost or technology, limiting direct applicability to settings with formal punishment mechanisms.
- The average number of rounds per interaction is relatively low (~8), which may affect learning and equilibrium selection.
