The main outcomes are the proportion of cooperation and the ability of different social norms to maintain cooperation under various conditions. Payoff is discussed in terms of mechanisms and model structure, but the reported results focus on cooperation rates, not group payoff, efficiency, or welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the leading eight social norms for indirect reciprocity can maintain high levels of cooperation in networked populations, even when reputation and strategy updating are local rather than global. However, the ability to sustain cooperation depends on the accuracy of reputation transmission, the speed of evolutionary updating, and the specific norm used. Norms that reward justified defection are less robust to noise and invasion by defectors. The main outcome is the proportion of cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. Its findings are adjacent: they show that certain reputation-based norms can sustain high cooperation in repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma games on networks, and that the robustness of cooperation depends on information and evolutionary parameters. For prediction tasks about efficiency in public goods games with punishment, this paper suggests that the structure of information and the harshness of social norms can affect the sustainability of cooperation, but it does not quantify efficiency or payoff outcomes. Use this paper for qualitative context about the importance of information structure and norm design, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Population size (N) is varied in simulations; larger populations make cooperation harder to sustain, especially under global evolution.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Simulations run for a maximum number of time-steps (T_max); repeated interactions are essential for the emergence of cooperation, but the exact number of rounds is not a main focus.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is a binary-choice Prisoner's Dilemma: cooperate (pay cost, give benefit) or defect (no cost, no benefit).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the PD, the benefit to the other (b) is 2, cost to self (c) is 1; so the marginal per-capita return for the recipient is 2, for the group is 2/2=1.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents may observe payoffs and reputations of their neighbors (local) or the whole population (global), depending on the model variant.

## Important Limitations
- The model is based on repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game or its direct variants.
- No explicit costly punishment or reward mechanisms; 'punishment' is via reputation and exclusion, not direct sanctions.
- Outcomes are reported as cooperation rates, not as efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Findings are based on agent-based simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes binary actions (cooperate/defect) and binary reputations (good/bad), which may not capture the full complexity of real-world cooperation or punishment.
- The study does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff outcomes relevant for the prediction task.
