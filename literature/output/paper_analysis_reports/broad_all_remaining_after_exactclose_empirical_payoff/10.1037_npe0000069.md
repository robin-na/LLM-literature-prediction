# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Emotions in a Repeated Cournot Duopoly Game: A Psychophysiological Experiment
Authors: Leppänen, I., & Hämäläinen, R. P.
Journal: Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- production quantity adjustments
- payoff changes (gains/losses relative to previous round)
- frequency of joint-optimum outcomes
- mean cooperation index
Non-payoff outcomes:
- emotional arousal (SCR)
- facial expressions (anger, disgust, positive affect via EMG)
- decision times
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are psychophysiological measures of emotion and behavioral adjustments in production quantity. Payoff outcomes are discussed in terms of changes (gains/losses) and frequency of joint-optimum (cooperative) outcomes, but efficiency or total group payoff is not the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that emotional responses are closely tied to reciprocal adjustments in a repeated Cournot duopoly game. When a player observes that the other did not cooperate (i.e., the other increased production), the player is more likely to make an upward adjustment (interpreted as punishment), and the size of this adjustment is positively correlated with emotional arousal. Disgust is associated with advantageous inequity (own one-sided gain), and positive affect is associated with mutual gains. The study provides evidence that emotions play a role in motivating reciprocal (including punitive) behavior in repeated social dilemmas, but it does not directly measure or report the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. The Cournot duopoly is adjacent to public goods games, and punishment is implemented as endogenous behavioral adjustment rather than as a discrete, explicit punishment option.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the emotional and motivational underpinnings of reciprocal (including punitive) behavior in repeated social dilemmas with continuous strategy spaces. It suggests that endogenous punishment-like responses (upward adjustments after defection) are emotionally motivated and can be costly for both players, potentially reducing efficiency. However, because the study does not manipulate explicit punishment mechanisms or report efficiency outcomes as a function of punishment, it is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The findings may be useful for understanding the psychological processes underlying endogenous punishment in adjacent game structures, but should not be used as direct quantitative evidence for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per game; duration not known to players in advance (indefinite horizon).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; players were anonymous and in separate rooms.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/production choice is continuous (integer 3-15); not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff function is x(a-x-y) with a=24; marginal returns depend on both players' choices, not a fixed MPCR as in standard PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: upward adjustment in production quantity after defection is costly for both players (reduces both payoffs). No explicit punishment mechanism or cost per se.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is limited: upward adjustment reduces both players' payoffs, not just the target's. No explicit punishment technology.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance (indefinite horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw a table with outcomes and own ideal outcomes from all previous rounds; payoff matrix was always visible except during wait stages.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players could infer the other's behavior from observed outcomes, but identities were anonymous; no explicit punishment, so no explicit identification.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a repeated Cournot duopoly, not a public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Punishment is endogenous (via production adjustment), not an explicit, discrete mechanism; no direct manipulation of punishment availability or cost.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not the primary outcome; the study focuses on emotional and behavioral responses, not on aggregate welfare.
- Sample size is moderate (22 pairs), and the experiment is conducted in a specific cultural and laboratory context.
- No analysis of how enabling or disabling explicit punishment affects efficiency, limiting direct applicability to the prediction task.
