# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Subjects in the lab, activists in the field: public goods and punishment
Authors: Dave, C., Hamre, S., Kephart, C., & Reuben, A.
Journal: Eurasian Economic Review
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate in VCM
- frequency of punishment (Nature node reached)
- terminal node choices in extensive form game
- role-based behavior (Activist vs Firm)
- inequity aversion and negative reciprocity measures
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates in the VCM and the frequency with which subjects choose to punish (i.e., reach the Nature node) in the extensive form game. Payoff outcomes are described in the design, but the analysis focuses on behavioral choices, not on group efficiency or total payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that both lab and field (activist) subjects respond to incentives in a public goods context with punishment. Activists are more likely to punish non-cooperative behavior, even when it is costly and probabilistic, and are more likely to settle at the Nash equilibrium when firms increase their public good contributions. Higher MPCR leads to higher contributions and more frequent punishment. The main behavioral drivers are inequity aversion and negative reciprocity. However, the paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff as an outcome, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly measured.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about how punishment is used in a public-goods-like environment, especially with a costly, probabilistic punishment mechanism. It shows that punishment is frequently used and is responsive to MPCR and fairness concerns. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is most useful for understanding behavioral mechanisms and the likelihood of punishment under different design parameters (e.g., MPCR, subject pool), but not for quantitative efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of four: two active (Activist, Firm) and two passive players in the VCM and stage game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds in the VCM; 10 repetitions of the stage game using the strategy method.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in the VCM are continuous (subjects allocate earned endowment), but in the stage game, choices are discrete (accept, demand, punish, etc.).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is manipulated at two levels: 0.30 (Low) and 0.75 (High). Higher MPCR increases contributions and likelihood of punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (boycott) costs the Activist a fixed 10 points if chosen.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic: with probability 0.75, the boycott succeeds and the Firm must increase contribution; with probability 0.25, it fails.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds in both VCM and stage game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see the endowments of others before making allocation decisions; payoffs and terminal node choices are revealed at the end of each round.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as an outcome; all main results are about contribution rates and punishment frequency.
- The punishment mechanism is probabilistic and costly, which differs from standard deterministic punishment in many PGG experiments.
- Field implementation uses bots for the Firm role, which may affect external validity for real-world interactions.
- The design is a hybrid of VCM and an extensive form bargaining/punishment game, not a standard repeated PGG with punishment.
- No direct analysis of how punishment affects group efficiency or surplus, limiting its use for efficiency prediction.
