## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in one-shot trust games, strategies that do not trust or reciprocate dominate, resulting in low average payoffs. When the game is repeated for more rounds (increasing the shadow of the future), conditional strategies that punish defection (e.g., grim trigger, hopeful) become viable and eventually dominate the population, leading to the emergence of a norm of trust and reciprocation and higher average payoffs. The transition from non-cooperation to cooperation occurs as the number of rounds increases beyond a threshold (typically r > 2 or 3). The stable outcome is a polymorphic population of conditional strategies supporting trust/reciprocation. The results are robust to initial conditions and to the inclusion of stochasticity in the evolutionary process.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated trust-game-like environments, the length of the repeated interaction (number of rounds) is a critical moderator of the emergence of cooperation and higher efficiency. When the game is one-shot or very short, efficiency remains low due to dominance of non-cooperative strategies. As the number of rounds increases, conditional strategies that implement punishment by withholding trust/reciprocation after defection can support the emergence of trust and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, in the absence of explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, repeated interaction alone can enable the emergence of efficient, cooperative outcomes if conditional strategies are available. The model does not directly manipulate or analyze explicit punishment cost or technology, but the logic of conditional strategies is analogous to endogenous punishment via future play. The results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated trust games as a function of the number of rounds and the available strategy set.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a population game where each player is assigned a strategy and plays against the population; the effective player count is large (continuum or large finite population). The model does not analyze variation in player count as a moderator.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The number of rounds (r) is a key manipulated variable. As r increases, conditional strategies supporting trust/reciprocation emerge and dominate, leading to higher average payoffs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (trust) is binary: sender either trusts (invests) or does not; receiver either reciprocates or does not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The sender's endowment is tripled if trusted (x becomes 3x), and the receiver can reciprocate by splitting or keep all. The effective MPCR for sender is 1.5 if reciprocated, 0 if not.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds per game (r) is known and fixed in the simulation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not an empirical or experimental study with human subjects.
- The game is a trust (investment) game with two roles (sender/receiver), not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to multi-player PGGs.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are modeled; punishment is endogenous via conditional strategies (e.g., grim trigger).
- The model assumes a large population and does not analyze the effect of varying player count.
- The strategy set is fixed and limited; results may depend on the available strategies.
- No communication, reputation, or network structure is included.
- Findings are theoretical and simulation-based; real-world behavior may differ.
