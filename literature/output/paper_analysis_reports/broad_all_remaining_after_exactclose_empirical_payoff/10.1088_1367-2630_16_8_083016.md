## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that probabilistic sharing of punishment duties in a public goods game can solve the problem of costly punishment and promote cooperation, even when punishment is expensive. In well-mixed populations, probabilistic sanctioning transforms the game into a coordination game with two stable equilibria (full cooperation and full defection). In structured populations, sharing punishment duties leads to high cooperation rates, but only if the severity and frequency of punishment are carefully balanced; both too little and too much punishment can reduce cooperation. The main reported outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. Theoretical payoff expressions are provided, and the analysis explains why intermediate punishment is optimal for cooperation, but efficiency is not directly measured or reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the way punishment is implemented (shared probabilistically or periodically) can have a major impact on cooperation rates in public goods games. While the main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, the derived payoff expressions and analysis of invasion dynamics suggest that intermediate levels of punishment severity and frequency can maximize group welfare by supporting high cooperation at lower cost. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the population and the sharing of punishment duties, are important moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, because efficiency is not directly reported, quantitative predictions about efficiency must be inferred indirectly from the cooperation rates and payoff formulas.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are reported.
- The main outcome is cooperation rate (fraction of cooperators), not group efficiency or total payoff; efficiency must be inferred indirectly.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or surplus.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback.
- Model assumes infinite or stationary rounds; does not address finite repeated games with known endpoints.
- Results may not generalize to all real-world settings due to idealized assumptions (e.g., lattice structure, imitation dynamics).
