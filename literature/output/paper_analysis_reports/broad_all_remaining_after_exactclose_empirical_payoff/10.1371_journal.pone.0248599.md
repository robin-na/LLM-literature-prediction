# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Strike one hundred to educate one: Measuring the efficacy of collective sanctions experimentally
Authors: Chapkovski, P.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per treatment
- mean contributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- compliance with threshold
- punishment behavior
- gender differences in contribution
- fairness perceptions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good. Average earnings are reported per treatment, but statistical analysis focuses on contributions, not efficiency or total group payoff. Efficiency (as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome) is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Collective sanctions are not effective at increasing cooperation or contributions in a public good game, and may even reduce them compared to individual sanctions, especially when peer punishment is available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions overall, but under collective sanctions, contributions are significantly lower than under individual sanctions with peer punishment. Average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions, but this difference is not statistically significant. There are notable gender differences: men contribute more under collective sanctions, women less. Perceptions of fairness mediate these effects, with collective sanctions seen as less fair, especially by women.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard PGG with 3 players, 15 rounds, continuous contributions, and a fixed threshold for external punishment, enabling collective sanctions (punishing the whole group for one member's under-contribution) does not improve efficiency or group payoff compared to individual sanctions, and may even reduce cooperation and contributions, especially when peer punishment is also available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions, but collective sanctions reduce this effect. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that switching from individual to collective sanctions is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, particularly in small groups with peer punishment. Gender composition may moderate effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per session, fixed for all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds per session, fixed for all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions are continuous (0-20 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (each token contributed returns 0.5 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: each deduction point costs punisher 1 token, reduces target's earnings by 2 tokens. External (central) punishment: 7 tokens deducted if threshold not met and detected.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Peer punishment: 1 token cost to punisher, 2 token loss to target (2:1 ratio). Central punishment: 7 token loss per detected violation (individual or group, depending on regime).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed in advance of the number of periods (15 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could see information about others' contributions and payoffs, as needed for peer punishment stage.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Identities of group members were not revealed; punishment was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (ratio of group payoff to fully cooperative outcome) is not directly analyzed; main outcome is contribution.
- Average earnings are reported but not statistically analyzed for treatment differences; focus is on contributions.
- Small group size (3 players) may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- Gender effects are observed but not the primary focus; sample is limited to university students.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment.
- No communication between players; may differ from real-world settings.
- Framing is neutral, which may not reflect real-world perceptions of authority or fairness.
