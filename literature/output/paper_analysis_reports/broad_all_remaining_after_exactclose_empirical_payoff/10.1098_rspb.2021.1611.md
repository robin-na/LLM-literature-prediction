# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait
Authors: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean average payment per participant
- punishment spending per round
- collective costs of punishment outweighing benefits of increased contributions (destructive punishment)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates over time
- punishment frequency and type (pro-social, anti-social, hypocritical)
- classification of social preferences (conditional cooperator, free-rider, other)
- change in contribution by immunity status
- punishment consistency across rounds
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates and punishment behaviors. The paper discusses that punishment is 'destructive' (costs outweigh benefits), but does not report group efficiency or total group payoff as a primary outcome. Some aggregate payoff information is provided (mean payment, punishment costs), but efficiency is not directly calculated or reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported as a primary outcome; conclusions about efficiency are based on statements that punishment is 'destructive' (costs outweigh benefits), not on explicit efficiency calculations.
- The experiment uses only student participants from Switzerland, which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- The number of rounds is limited (five), and the perfect-stranger matching protocol may not capture long-term dynamics.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment is available.
- The study focuses on the decoupling of punishment and cooperation, not on the full range of possible game design dimensions.
