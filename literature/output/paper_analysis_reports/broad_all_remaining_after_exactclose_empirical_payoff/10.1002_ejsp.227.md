# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperative, pure, and selfish trusting: Their distinctive effects on the reaction of trust recipients
Authors: Eilam, O., & Suleiman, R.
Journal: European Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Amount allocated to trust giver by trust recipient
- Distribution of allocations under different trust expectation conditions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount (out of 50 NIS) allocated to the trust giver by the trust recipient, under different expectation conditions. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The design is a one-shot allocation game, not a repeated public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that trust recipients allocate significantly less to trust givers who express selfish expectations than to those who express cooperative or no expectations. There is no significant difference between the cooperative and pure trust conditions. All allocations to the trust giver are above the minimum (preservation point) and below the equality point. The results are interpreted as evidence that explicit selfish expectations activate self-interest and reactance, leading to less cooperative responses, while cooperative or unstated expectations elicit more equal allocations. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it implement punishment as a game feature.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The design is a one-shot allocation game with no repeated interaction, no group structure, and no explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism. While the discussion references social sanctioning and norm enforcement, these are psychological constructs rather than implemented game features. The findings may be weakly informative for understanding how communicated expectations affect allocation behavior, but do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant is told they are paired with one other player (dyadic game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocation is continuous: Player 2 can allocate any amount between 0 and 50 NIS.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a single round and ends after the allocation.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or a repeated game structure; it is a one-shot dyadic allocation game.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual allocation decisions are measured.
- No punishment or reward mechanisms are implemented; punishment is discussed only as a psychological response.
- There is no control condition without a trust gesture (i.e., no dictator game baseline).
- Findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods environments.
