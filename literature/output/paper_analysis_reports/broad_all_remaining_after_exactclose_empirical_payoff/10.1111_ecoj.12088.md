# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Coordinating Resistance Through Communication and Repeated Interaction
Authors: Cason, T. N., & Mui, V. L.
Journal: Economic Journal
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rate of leader transgression (DAC)
- rate of successful joint resistance
- frequency of beneficiary resistance
- effect of communication on resistance
- effect of repetition on resistance
- mis-coordinated resistance rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: rates of leader transgression (DAC), rates of joint resistance, and the effect of communication and repetition on these behaviors. The paper does not report group efficiency, total surplus, or group payoff as a primary outcome, though it discusses surplus destruction in the game structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that repetition alone only modestly reduces leader DAC (transgression) rates, as leaders can punish beneficiaries who resist, deterring future resistance. Communication between responders, even when non-binding and private, substantially increases the rate of coordinated resistance and reduces leader DAC. The combination of repetition and communication is most effective at reducing DAC. The study also shows that hiding responder identities from the leader (so the leader cannot target challenging beneficiaries) increases resistance. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total surplus, or group payoff as a main outcome, so the direct effect on efficiency is not measured. The results are most relevant for understanding how communication and repeated interaction affect coordination in social dilemmas with endogenous roles and targeted punishment, rather than standard public goods games with peer punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the importance of communication and repeated interaction for coordinated resistance in social dilemmas with endogenous roles. While not a public goods game, the findings suggest that communication between potential punishers (or resisters) can be as important as, or more important than, repetition for deterring exploitative behavior. The results highlight that the ability to target punishers (e.g., identifying who punished) can undermine the effectiveness of resistance. For prediction tasks in public goods games, the main lesson is that communication channels and anonymity of punishment/reward can strongly moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by extension, efficiency. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes, but it does suggest that communication is a powerful moderator.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 3 players: 1 leader and 2 responders.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Treatments include one-shot (random matching), finite repetition (8 or 50 periods), and indefinite repetition (expected 8 periods per group).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Binary, private, non-binding communication between responders is a treatment variable. Communication increases coordinated resistance and reduces leader DAC.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder choices are binary: challenge or acquiesce (X or Y).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Challenging (resisting) is costly for responders, regardless of outcome. The cost is fixed in the payoff structure (e.g., see Figure 1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. If both responders challenge, DAC fails; if only one challenges, challenge fails and cost is incurred. The effectiveness of resistance is built into the game structure.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In finite repetition treatments, subjects know the number of rounds; in indefinite repetition, the continuation probability is known but the end is stochastic.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, all players see all actions and payoffs for their group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In most treatments, the leader can identify which responder challenged and can target them in future periods. In a special treatment, responder identities are hidden from the leader, which increases resistance.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a direct variant; it is a social dilemma with endogenous roles and targeted punishment.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (transgression and resistance rates), not efficiency, group payoff, or surplus.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency or total surplus is provided.
- Findings may not generalize to standard public goods games with peer punishment.
- The punishment mechanism is endogenous to the leader's targeting, not peer punishment as in PGGs.
- Reward mechanisms are not studied.
