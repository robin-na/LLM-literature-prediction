# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: A cue of kinship promotes cooperation for the public good
Authors: Krupp, D. B., Debruine, L. A., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- punishment assigned to free riders
- amount of punishment received
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution amounts and punishment behavior. Group efficiency or total payoff is not directly reported or analyzed, though contribution and punishment are closely related to efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that subtle cues of kinship (facial self-resemblance) increase cooperation in a public goods game with punishment. Participants contributed more to the public good as the number of self-resembling (kin-cue) group members increased, but did not change their punishment behavior toward free riders based on kinship cues. The experiment used a yoked control design to isolate the effect of kinship cues. The main outcomes are contribution and punishment levels; efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured. The findings suggest that kinship cues can promote cooperation, but do not alter punitive responses to free riding.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a standard public goods game with punishment, increasing perceived kinship among group members increases contributions but does not affect punishment rates. While efficiency is not directly measured, higher contributions generally imply higher efficiency, unless offset by excessive punishment costs. Since punishment rates did not increase, it is reasonable to infer that efficiency would likely increase with kinship cues. However, the paper does not manipulate or analyze any of the core game design dimensions (such as MPCR, punishment cost, or player count) except for the kinship cue. Thus, its direct value for predicting efficiency as a function of game design dimensions is limited, but it does suggest that social context (kinship cues) can moderate cooperation independently of punishment mechanics.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 4 players (1 participant + 3 ostensible others).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 4 rounds, each with a different group composition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; participants interacted only via the game interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous in whole-dollar increments from $0 to $10.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each $1 contributed is doubled and split among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: $1 spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by $3.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each $1 spent inflicts $3 penalty).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were 4 rounds and that one would be randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw the contributions of each group member and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could assign punishment to specific group members, but when they received punishment, they were not told which group member punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly report or analyze group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; only contribution and punishment behavior are measured.
- The only manipulated variable is the kinship cue (facial self-resemblance); core game design dimensions such as MPCR, punishment cost, or player count are not varied.
- The experiment uses preprogrammed strategies for other group members, not real interactive groups.
- The sample size is modest (40 experimental, 40 control), and participants are university students.
- The artificial laboratory setting and use of facial morphs may limit ecological validity.
