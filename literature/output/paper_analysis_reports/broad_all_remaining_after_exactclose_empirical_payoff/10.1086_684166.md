## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All models and analysis are for two-player (n=2) games only.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (hunt stag) or defect (hunt hare); the punishment strategy is a contingent binary action.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The payoff structure is parameterized by α, which determines the value of cooperation relative to defection; analogous to MPCR in public goods games.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to the punisher (r) is explicitly parameterized and varied in the analysis; higher punishment cost (when matched by high impact) increases the basin of attraction for efficient outcomes.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost (b, c, p) is explicitly parameterized; higher punishment effectiveness (punishee's cost relative to punisher's cost) increases the effect on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is limited to two-player (n=2) games; results may not generalize quantitatively to multi-player public goods games.
- The model is evolutionary and does not represent repeated games with explicit rounds or learning; it assumes strategy evolution via replicator dynamics.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented; all results are theoretical or simulation-based.
- No communication, reputation, or identification mechanisms are modeled.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are considered.
- The punishment option is modeled as a weakly dominated strategy, which may not correspond to how punishment is implemented in experimental or field settings.
