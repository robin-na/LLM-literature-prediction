# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inequality averse and compassionate blood donor: implication for interventions
Authors: Ferguson, E.
Journal: Vox Sanguinis
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- Payoff allocations to victim and perpetrator in 3PPC game (e.g., average amount spent on compensation or punishment, resulting allocations)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Preference for compensation, punishment, both, or doing nothing in 3PPC game
- Behavioral proxy for willingness to donate blood (taking information)
- Self-reported feelings (guilt, healthiness, likelihood to donate, perceived manipulation)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral choices (compensate, punish, both, or nothing) and a proxy for willingness to donate blood. Payoff allocations are described but not as a primary outcome; efficiency or group welfare is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Blood donors are more likely to choose compensation (directly helping the victim) over punishment (sanctioning the perpetrator) in a one-shot 3PPC game, while organ donors are more likely to choose punishment. The compensation and punishment options are both costly to the third party, and the game is designed so that each £1 spent on compensation or punishment has a 2x effect. The study does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, but describes how different choices affect the distribution of payoffs. A follow-up experiment finds that an intervention message based on advantageous inequality aversion increases non-donors' willingness to take information about blood donation, with some evidence that guilt-based appeals may have negative effects on current donors.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about individual preferences for compensation versus punishment in a third-party context, which is adjacent to public goods games with punishment. The design is a one-shot, three-player game with both punishment and compensation options, and the main outcomes are behavioral choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the presence of a compensation option may reduce the use of punishment, and that individuals' philanthropic identities (e.g., blood donor vs. organ donor) influence their preference for direct help versus norm enforcement. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in repeated public goods games, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited and indirect.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 3PPC game is played with three players: A (dictator), B (recipient), and C (third-party).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Decisions were made in private and anonymously; no chat or communication between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (compensation or punishment) is continuous in £1 units, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each £1 spent on compensation or punishment by Player-C has a 2x effect (i.e., increases B's allocation by £2 or reduces A's by £2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (Player-C) pays £1 to reduce perpetrator's (A's) allocation by £2.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2:1 (each £1 spent reduces A's allocation by £2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation (rewarding the victim) is available as an option.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder (Player-C) pays £1 to increase victim's (B's) allocation by £2.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 2:1 (each £1 spent increases B's allocation by £2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single round).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a one-shot (single round) third-party punishment and compensation game, not a repeated public goods game.
- The main outcomes are behavioral choices and willingness to take information, not efficiency or group payoff.
- Payoff allocations are described but not analyzed as efficiency or welfare outcomes.
- The context is third-party intervention in a dictator-like game, not direct contribution to a public good.
- Behavioral proxies (e.g., taking information) are not direct measures of actual donation behavior.
- No analysis of how punishment affects group efficiency or total welfare in a repeated or group context.
