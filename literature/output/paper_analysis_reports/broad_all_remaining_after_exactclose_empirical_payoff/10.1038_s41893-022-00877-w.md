## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and simulation-based; empirical validation is limited to qualitative comparison with a meta-study of experiments.
- The model assumes binary contribution choices and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- Only punishment institutions are modeled; reward mechanisms are not considered.
- The model does not endogenize the emergence of institution types or implementation rules, which are exogenously specified.
- The model does not include communication or chat between players.
- Experimental meta-study is limited to studies with repeated voting and play; single-shot studies are excluded.
