# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Effects of experimental situation on group cooperation and individual performance: Comparing laboratory and online experiments
Authors: Ozono, H., & Nakama, D.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- PGG contribution rate
- total amount of punishment used
- task performance (number of correct answers in individual tasks)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables for the group task are average contribution in the PGG and total punishment assigned. No explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. Individual task outcomes are number of correct answers in various tasks.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds no significant difference in PGG contribution or punishment behavior between laboratory and online settings among Japanese university students. The introduction of punishment does not significantly increase average contributions or maintain higher cooperation levels, though it may slow the decay of cooperation over rounds. In the online-worker sample, cooperation is lower and the effect of punishment is even weaker. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), not efficiency or group payoff. The paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely weak or negligible in these settings, especially with a punishment efficiency of 2 (punishment impact per unit cost).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with a punishment efficiency of 2, enabling punishment does not meaningfully increase average group cooperation or, by implication, efficiency, in either laboratory or online settings among Japanese university students. In a Japanese online-worker sample, punishment may even be associated with lower cooperation. The absence of direct efficiency or group payoff data limits quantitative prediction, but the behavioral results suggest that, under these design parameters, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is likely minimal or absent. The study also highlights the importance of sample heterogeneity and the specific punishment parameters (cost and effectiveness) in moderating punishment effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 participants in all PGG sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per PGG condition (with and without punishment).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between participants during the PGG; only allowed to ask experimenter questions via Zoom chat.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants choose how many of 20 points to contribute (not all-or-nothing).
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. If time limit is exceeded, full 20 points are automatically contributed (full contribution is the default on timeout).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total group contribution is multiplied by 1.5 and divided equally among 3 players; MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 point to reduce another member by 2 points; cost per unit punishment = 1.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2 (1 point spent reduces target by 2); authors note this is lower than the typical 3 and may explain weak punishment effects.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is available in the PGG; only punishment is implemented.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of rounds (10) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, each participant is shown all group members' contributions and the amount distributed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants see how much they were punished in total, but not by whom.

## Important Limitations
- No direct efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) are analyzed.
- Punishment efficiency (impact per unit cost) is lower than in many prior studies, which may limit generalizability.
- Sample is limited to Japanese university students and Japanese online-workers; cultural and incentive effects may not generalize.
- Laboratory and online settings may not fully capture differences in social distance, as lab participants were separated by partitions and had minimal interaction.
- Sample sizes are relatively small, and post hoc power analyses indicate low statistical power for some effects.
- Order of PGG with and without punishment is not counterbalanced, though prior work suggests this is not a major issue.
