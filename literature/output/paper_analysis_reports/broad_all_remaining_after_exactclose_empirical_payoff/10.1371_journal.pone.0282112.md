## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The agent-based model, using reinforcement learning and a social preference framework, closely replicates the experimental findings of [20] in a public goods game with endogenous punishment. For low cooperators, severe punishment increases contributions and can raise payoffs toward the social optimum, but mild punishment is ineffective and reduces payoffs due to its cost. For high cooperators, punishment (both mild and severe) increases contributions only slightly but reduces payoffs because the cost of punishment outweighs the marginal gain in cooperation. Middle cooperators benefit from mild punishment, which increases both contributions and payoffs. The model demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the underlying social preference structure of the group. Endogenous institution choice in the simulation leads agents to select the institution that maximizes their own payoffs, mirroring the learning observed in the experimental Sorted-Info treatment but not in the Sorted treatment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with known design parameters. The simulation results, validated against experimental data, show that the impact of punishment on efficiency depends on the group’s baseline cooperativeness: severe punishment can increase efficiency for low cooperators but is costly and can reduce efficiency for high cooperators. The model’s detailed reporting of payoffs under different punishment regimes, player types, and institution selection mechanisms makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings highlight the importance of considering both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the social preference composition of the group.

## Important Limitations
- The agent-based model is calibrated to reproduce aggregate patterns from a specific experimental design ([20]) and may not generalize to all public goods game settings.
- The simulation assumes homogeneous groups of agents with fixed social preference weights, which may not capture the full heterogeneity of human subjects.
- The model does not include intention-based preferences, trust, or reciprocity, which may be important in real-world cooperation.
- The simulation uses a much larger number of rounds (periods) than the experiment, which may affect learning and institution selection dynamics.
- No reward mechanism is considered, so findings may not transfer to settings with both punishment and reward.
