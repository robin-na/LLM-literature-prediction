Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average payoffs and cooperation rates in simulated populations under different social norms. The study uses computational evolutionary game theory to analyze which strategies (norms) are most successful, with success measured by long-run average payoff and evolutionary abundance. While the model is not a public goods game, it is a repeated donation game (binary choice, dyadic), which is adjacent to PGGs. Punishment is not implemented as a separate costly action, but some norms treat justified defection as 'punishment' in the reputational sense.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in well-mixed populations, even with complex (third-order) social norms, cooperation and high group payoff (efficiency) are difficult to sustain: defecting strategies (ALLD) are most abundant, and average payoffs are low. In group-structured populations, however, cooperation and high efficiency can evolve and persist, especially when third-order norms are available. The most successful norm (L1) treats cooperation as always good and defection as bad, except when defection is 'justified' (against bad recipients). This norm is robust to disagreements and errors in private assessment. The results highlight the importance of population structure and norm complexity for the evolution of efficient, cooperative outcomes. The model does not implement explicit costly punishment, but some norms functionally resemble punishment by allowing justified defection without reputational cost.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the population (well-mixed vs. group-structured) and the complexity of available social norms (second-order vs. third-order) are critical moderators of the effect of reputational 'punishment' (justified defection) on group efficiency. In well-mixed populations, enabling more complex norms or reputational punishment does not reliably increase efficiency; in group-structured populations, it can lead to high efficiency. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of group structure and the richness of the norm/strategy space are key predictors of whether enabling reputational punishment will improve efficiency. However, the results may not transfer directly to standard PGGs with explicit costly punishment, as the model is dyadic and punishment is reputational, not material.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Population size N=50 is used in main simulations; group size is also N=50 in group-structured models. Results for other group sizes are reported as qualitatively similar in SI Appendix.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for a large number of rounds (e.g., 10^6 donation games per simulation) to reach stationary state; the game is modeled as indefinitely repeated with stationary analysis.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each donor chooses to cooperate (give benefit b at cost c) or defect (do nothing); binary choice.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is b/c (benefit to recipient divided by cost to donor); main simulations use b=5, c unspecified but 0 < c < b. Varying b is shown to affect cooperation levels.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is modeled as indefinitely repeated; players do not know a fixed endpoint, as the analysis is stationary.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. All players observe every donation game and update their opinions; full information about actions is available to all (observation probability q=1 in main text).

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game but a repeated binary donation game (dyadic), so results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- Punishment is not implemented as a separate costly action; 'punishment' is reputational (justified defection), not material.
- All results are from computational simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes all players observe all actions (q=1), which may not hold in real-world settings.
- Payoff structure is fixed (b > c > 0), and only binary choices are allowed; no continuous contribution or richer action space.
- No communication, chat, or explicit group decision-making is modeled.
- Findings about the importance of group structure and norm complexity may depend on the specific evolutionary and imitation dynamics used.
