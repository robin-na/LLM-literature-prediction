# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: IMMATERIAL REWARDS AND SANCTIONS IN A VOLUNTARY CONTRIBUTION EXPERIMENT
Authors: Peeters, R., & Vorsatz, M.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per subject in each treatment (Euro)
- average group contribution (ECU)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution per period
- variance of group contributions
- probability of sending/receiving emoticons (smiley/frowny)
- effect of sending/receiving emoticons on next round's contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution rate. Payoff is reported as average earnings per subject per treatment, but efficiency (as a ratio to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed. No direct analysis of group efficiency or welfare; focus is on behavioral responses to immaterial (costless, non-monetary) feedback.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that allowing costless, immaterial feedback (smilies/frownies) does not significantly increase average group contributions or payoffs in a voluntary contribution public goods game. Under partner matching, feedback treatments show higher variance in group contributions, with some groups sustaining high contributions, but the average effect is not significant. Under stranger matching, feedback treatments do not outperform control and may even reduce contributions. The effect of feedback on future contributions is context-dependent: under partner matching, sending/receiving emoticons can reduce future contributions; under stranger matching, receiving a frowny can increase future contributions for some participants. Overall, immaterial feedback does not reliably improve efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that costless, immaterial punishment or reward mechanisms (such as emoticons) do not significantly improve efficiency or group payoff in standard public goods games, regardless of matching protocol. For prediction tasks, the presence of immaterial, non-costly, non-monetary punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency relative to control. The effect of such feedback is highly variable and context-dependent, with no robust positive effect on group payoff or efficiency. The findings suggest that, unless punishment or reward is material (i.e., has real payoff consequences), enabling such mechanisms is unlikely to improve efficiency in public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or free-form communication; only emoticon feedback allowed in feedback treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous in integer units from 0 to 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU per group member (MPCR = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (frowny) is costless to sender and recipient (immaterial).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment has no material effect; only informational/emotional (immaterial).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (smiley) treatment is present and costless/immaterial.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (smiley) is costless to sender and recipient (immaterial).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward has no material effect; only informational/emotional (immaterial).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (15) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all individual contributions within the group are shown (in random order), as well as own payoff.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Recipients see the number of emoticons received but not the identity of senders (anonymous feedback).

## Important Limitations
- Punishment and reward are immaterial (costless, non-monetary), so results may not generalize to settings with material sanctions or rewards.
- Primary outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not directly analyzed as a ratio to full cooperation.
- Effects of feedback are highly variable and context-dependent, with no significant average effect.
- Sample size per treatment may limit power to detect small effects.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with communication, material incentives, or different group sizes.
