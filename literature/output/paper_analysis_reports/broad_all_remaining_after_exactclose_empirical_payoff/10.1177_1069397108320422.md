Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and ethnographic, focusing on the evolutionary effects of social sanctioning (punishment and reward) in small-scale forager societies. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or any direct payoff-based outcomes. The main outcomes are the presence and types of social control, and their hypothesized evolutionary effects.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in small-scale, mobile hunter-gatherer societies, both punitive and positive social sanctioning are pervasive and target behaviors relevant to group cooperation, such as sharing, generosity, and suppression of dominance or cheating. Punishment (including ostracism and even capital punishment) is used to control antisocial behaviors, while positive sanctioning (reputation, social approval, preferential partnerships) rewards prosocial behaviors. The author theorizes that these mechanisms have had significant evolutionary effects, shaping both the prevalence of altruism and the development of conscience in humans. However, the findings are based on ethnographic coding and theoretical reasoning, not on experimental or quantitative analysis of efficiency or payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and ethnographic support for the idea that punishment and reward mechanisms are central to the maintenance of cooperation in small-scale societies, and that they have long-term evolutionary consequences. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar environments. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the plausibility of punishment as a mechanism for sustaining cooperation, but does not provide data or effect sizes relevant to predicting efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and ethnographic, not experimental or quantitative.
- No direct analysis or measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes.
- No manipulation or measurement of game design dimensions as in public goods experiments.
- Findings are based on ethnographic coding and theoretical reasoning, not on controlled experiments.
