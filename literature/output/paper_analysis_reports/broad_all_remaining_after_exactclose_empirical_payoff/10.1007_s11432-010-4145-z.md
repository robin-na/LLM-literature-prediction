## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated flow control games, the use of punishment strategies (including trigger, tit-for-tat, and finite punishment) can deter selfish deviations and sustain cooperation among users, leading to optimal or near-optimal system utility. In the absence of such mechanisms, the equilibrium is inefficient. The FCAR algorithm, which operationalizes finite punishment, is shown via simulation to effectively regularize user behavior and maintain high system utility, with only temporary utility losses during punishment. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the number of users and the length of punishment. In finitely repeated games, cooperation is harder to sustain, especially in the final stage, but rational punishment strategies can still maintain cooperation for most of the game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in repeated resource allocation games (adjacent to public goods games) increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the number of users, provided the punishment is sufficiently strong and the game is sufficiently long or indefinite. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency, especially when users are patient (high discount factor) and punishment is coordinated. The results are most directly applicable to repeated, continuous-action, non-anonymous settings with full information about others' actions and payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not based on human subject experiments.
- The setting is adjacent to, but not an exact public goods game; it is a flow control game with continuous actions and specific utility functions.
- Punishment is modeled as a coordinated reduction in utility, not as an explicit costly action by individuals.
- The analysis assumes full observability of actions and perfect coordination among punishers.
- Reward mechanisms are only mentioned, not analyzed.
- The results may not generalize to settings with private information, noisy monitoring, or limited ability to coordinate punishment.
