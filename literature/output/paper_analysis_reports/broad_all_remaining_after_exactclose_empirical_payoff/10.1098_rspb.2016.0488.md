## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, the evolutionary success of punishment, positive reciprocity, and partner switching depends on the number of rounds, group size, and the costs/effectiveness of punishment and switching. Punishment is favored in small and moderately sized groups, especially when punishment is efficient and its behavioral effect is long-lasting. Partner switching dominates in large groups or when the number of rounds is high, unless switching is costly. Positive reciprocity is rarely the dominant mechanism, except when punishment is less effective or more costly. The model does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff, but evolutionary success is determined by aggregate payoff, so the results indirectly inform about conditions under which punishment or other mechanisms would lead to higher average payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-like environments. It suggests that punishment is more likely to improve group outcomes (in terms of evolutionary success/payoff) in small or moderately sized groups, especially when punishment is efficient and its behavioral effect is long-lasting. In large groups or with many rounds, partner switching may be more effective. The findings are most relevant for adjacent settings to public goods games, especially repeated dyadic interactions, and should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to standard PGGs. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, but the evolutionary dominance of strategies is determined by payoff, so the results can be used to infer the likely direction of punishment's effect on efficiency under different design dimensions.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a theoretical evolutionary simulation, not an empirical or experimental study.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, cooperation rates), not explicit group efficiency or total payoff.
- The setting is an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (dyadic), not a standard public goods game; results are adjacent but not directly transferable.
- No explicit analysis of group efficiency or surplus; evolutionary success is based on individual payoff.
- No reward mechanism is modeled; only punishment, positive reciprocity, and partner switching are considered.
- No communication, summary information, or identification of punishers is modeled.
- Assumes pure strategies with limited memory (mostly one round), which may not capture all real-world strategic complexity.
