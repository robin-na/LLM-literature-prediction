# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Toward a (more) parsimonious account of the link between 'dark' personality and social decision-making in economic games
Authors: Hilbig, B. E., & Thielmann, I.
Journal: Judgment and Decision Making
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prosocial choice (binary: prosocial vs. antisocial decision in each game)
- association of personality traits (D, dark tetrad) with prosocial/antisocial choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is whether the participant made the prosocial choice in each game (coded 1 for prosocial, 0 otherwise). No payoff, efficiency, group earnings, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed. The focus is on individual-level behavioral choices and their association with personality traits.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across 10 large-sample, hypothetical studies using eight different economic games (including the Prisoner's Dilemma, Dictator Game, Ultimatum Game, Faith Game, Chicken Game, Stag Hunt, and Uncostly Retaliation Game), the common core of dark personality traits (D) consistently predicts antisocial choices (i.e., lower prosociality). Single dark tetrad traits also predict antisocial choices, but these effects are almost entirely accounted for by D. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment, reward, or efficiency outcomes, and does not include public goods games with or without punishment. The main contribution is to clarify the role of personality in prosocial/antisocial decision-making, not to inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. It does not include any games with a punishment or sanctioning phase, nor does it report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes. The findings are about individual differences in prosocial choice, not about institutional or design features such as punishment, reward, or their impact on efficiency. Therefore, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player games (dyads), as described in the methods for each game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) hypothetical decisions.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all games are anonymous and hypothetical.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Most games use binary (all-or-nothing) choices; some allow for limited discrete options, but all are coded as binary prosocial/antisocial for analysis.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot; participants know the game is a single decision.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the rules and possible payoffs, but do not see actual choices or payoffs of others.

## Important Limitations
- All games are hypothetical and not incentivized; this may affect the generalizability of findings to real-stakes environments.
- No public goods games or multi-player social dilemmas are included; all games are dyadic.
- No games include a punishment or reward phase as in PGGs with sanctions; the 'uncostly retaliation game' is not equivalent to a punishment phase.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed; only individual binary choices are studied.
- Samples are self-selected from a website focused on aversive personality, which may introduce selection bias.
- Findings are about personality predictors of prosocial/antisocial choice, not about institutional or design features such as punishment or reward.
