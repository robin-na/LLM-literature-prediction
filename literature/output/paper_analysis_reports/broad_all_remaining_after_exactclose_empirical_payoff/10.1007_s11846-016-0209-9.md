# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: What guides subjective performance evaluation: Incentive alignment or norm enforcement?
Authors: Gillenkirch, R. M., & Kreienbaum, H.
Journal: Review of Managerial Science
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- subordinate earnings (allocation of joint output)
- superior willingness to pay for information
Non-payoff outcomes:
- subordinate effort levels
- superior allocation decisions (reward/punishment of effort)
- information acquisition behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are effort levels chosen by subordinates, the superior's willingness to pay for information, and the allocation of joint output to subordinates. While these are related to payoffs, the study does not report group efficiency (total payoff relative to maximum possible), nor does it directly analyze welfare or surplus. The focus is on behavioral responses to subjective evaluation and information acquisition, not on efficiency per se.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that superiors are primarily motivated by norm enforcement: they pay more for information that allows them to reward high and punish low effort, and their allocation decisions reflect a willingness to punish norm violations. Subordinates increase their effort when they know their effort can be observed and evaluated. The study does not directly report group efficiency or total surplus, but higher effort levels in conditions with more informative evaluation suggest that group output and subordinate payoffs increase when norm enforcement is possible. However, the focus is on behavioral mechanisms (effort, information acquisition, allocation) rather than on efficiency as a formal outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that enabling punishment (via subjective evaluation and information acquisition) increases effort and likely increases group output in a team production setting with a free rider problem. However, it does not report efficiency or total payoff as a primary outcome, nor does it use a standard public goods game. The design is adjacent to PGGs: two subordinates choose effort (costly), output is the sum of efforts plus noise, and a superior (not a group member) allocates the output. The 'punishment' is implemented via allocation decisions, not explicit costly punishment. For prediction tasks, this study suggests that mechanisms enabling norm enforcement (punishment) can increase effort and likely improve group outcomes, but the lack of direct efficiency data and the non-standard game structure limit its direct applicability to PGG efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 participants: 1 superior and 2 subordinates. Only the 2 subordinates make effort decisions; the superior allocates output.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment lasted 16 rounds, but each group interacted for a single period (one-shot interaction per group composition). Information conditions were cycled within subjects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants was allowed; interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort choices were continuous: subordinates chose integer effort levels from 0 to 10.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 1: each unit of effort increases joint output by 1, which is then allocated between the two subordinates. However, output is also affected by random shocks.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via allocation: the superior can allocate less than a subordinate's contribution as a form of punishment. The superior pays to acquire information (costly), but does not incur a direct cost for punishing. The cost is in information acquisition, not in the act of punishing.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness depends on the superior's information: with more precise information, the superior can more effectively punish low effort. The 'technology' is the allocation mechanism plus information acquisition.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. The superior can also reward high effort by allocating more than a subordinate's contribution.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. No direct cost to the superior for rewarding; the only cost is in acquiring information. The superior must allocate the entire output.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness depends on information: with more precise information, the superior can more effectively reward high effort.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the number of rounds and the structure of the experiment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subordinates were informed about their own output and payoff, and received all information the superior had for her allocation decision. No mention of summary statistics about others beyond this.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not use a standard public goods game; instead, it is a team production game with a superior allocating output.
- Efficiency or group welfare is not directly reported or analyzed as an outcome.
- Punishment is implemented via allocation decisions by a third-party superior, not by peers, and not as explicit costly punishment.
- The setting is one-shot per group composition; no repeated interaction or reputation effects.
- External validity is limited due to the laboratory setting and artificial roles.
- The superior's only cost is in acquiring information, not in the act of punishing or rewarding.
- The study focuses on behavioral mechanisms (effort, information acquisition, allocation) rather than on efficiency or surplus.
