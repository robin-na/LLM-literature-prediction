# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The ecology of collective action: A public goods and sanctions experiment with controlled group formation
Authors: Ones, U., & Putterman, L.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- punishment direction (normal/perverse)
- persistence of contribution and punishment types
- response of contributions to punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on contribution levels, punishment behavior, and the persistence of behavioral types. It does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, though it discusses efficiency implications in the text.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment confirms that allowing punishment in a public goods game sustains high contribution levels over time, in contrast to the typical decay seen in standard VCMs without punishment. Punishment is mostly directed at low contributors, but a significant fraction is 'perverse' (aimed at high contributors), which can reduce efficiency. Grouping high contributors and strong punishers together leads to higher average contributions, while groups with perverse punishers contribute less. Individual tendencies to contribute and punish are persistent. However, the paper does not report group efficiency or total earnings, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency is not quantified.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment sustains high contributions in repeated public goods games, and that group composition (especially the presence or absence of perverse punishers) is a key moderator. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the findings are most useful for predicting contribution rates rather than efficiency per se. The qualitative discussion suggests that punishment can increase efficiency when perverse punishment is minimized, but can reduce efficiency when perverse punishment is common. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that punishment generally increases cooperation, but the efficiency effect depends on the nature of punishment (targeted vs. perverse) and group composition.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 25 periods per session, with groupings changing at specified intervals.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous (integer from 0 to 10), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each contributed unit returns 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 0.25 to the punisher for each 1 unit reduction in the target's earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 0.25 spent reduces the target's earnings by 1 unit (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (25) and the groupings schedule.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see the contributions of the other three group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are identified by letters (B-D), which are randomly reassigned each period to minimize vendettas; punishment is not fully anonymous but not fully identified.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare; all main outcomes are behavioral (contributions, punishment).
- Efficiency effects of punishment are discussed only qualitatively, not with quantitative data.
- The experiment does not include a no-punishment control, so the direct effect of enabling punishment on efficiency cannot be isolated.
- The presence of perverse punishment complicates the interpretation of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with different group sizes, MPCRs, or punishment parameters.
