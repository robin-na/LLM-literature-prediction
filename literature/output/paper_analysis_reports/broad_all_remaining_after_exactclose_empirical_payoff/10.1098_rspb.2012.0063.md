# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Female cleaner fish cooperate more with unfamiliar males
Authors: Raihani, N. J., Grutter, A. S., & Bshary, R.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- ratio of flake to prawn items eaten (proxy for cooperative behavior)
- frequency of cheating (eating prawn, the preferred food)
- frequency and intensity of male punishment (aggression/chasing)
- effect of partner familiarity on cooperation and punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: cooperation (feeding against preference), cheating, and punishment/aggression. No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are reported. The ratio of flake to prawn items is a proxy for cooperative behavior but not a direct payoff or efficiency measure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that female cleaner fish behave more cooperatively (feed more against their preference) when paired with unfamiliar males, who are more aggressive in punishing cheating than familiar males. Preventing male punishment reduces female cooperation. The effect of punishment on female cheating is short-lived, with no significant long-term effect. Male cooperative behavior is not affected by partner familiarity, likely because females do not punish males. The results suggest that in asymmetric interactions, harsher punishment from unfamiliar partners can induce greater cooperation from subordinates, even when familiarity would otherwise be expected to promote cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral proxies for cooperation and punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and severity of punishment can increase cooperative behavior in asymmetric dyadic interactions, even when the punisher is unfamiliar. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games. The findings are most relevant for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment increases cooperation, especially in settings with asymmetric power and punishment ability. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that enabling punishment (especially effective, low-cost punishment by dominants) can increase cooperation rates, but does not provide direct evidence about the impact on group efficiency or welfare.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments are conducted in dyads (pairs: one male, one female).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Multiple repeated trials per pair: 24 or 16 trials per condition, with at least 30 min between trials.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is aggressive chasing by males; cost to punisher is not quantified but discussed as a short-term cost.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is effective: male aggression reduces female cheating in the short term. Effectiveness is shown behaviorally, not as a numeric impact per cost.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always directly observed by the punished (aggression from the other fish in the pair).

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; only behavioral proxies for cooperation and punishment are reported.
- The experimental design is dyadic and asymmetric (male punishes female), not a public goods game or multi-player setting.
- Findings may not generalize to human or multi-player public goods games due to species differences and game structure.
- Punishment cost and effectiveness are not quantified in economic terms.
- No reward mechanism is present, so cannot inform about reward-punishment interactions.
