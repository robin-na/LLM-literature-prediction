The dependent variables are the amount of costly punishment assigned by Player B to Player A for fair and unfair choices. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that religious primes (subliminally presented) do not generally increase costly punishment of unfair behavior in a two-player punishment game. However, for the subset of participants who have previously donated to a religious organization, religious primes significantly increase the amount of punishment assigned to unfair choices. There is no effect of religious primes on punishment of fair choices, and no main effect of punishment primes or combined religion-punishment primes. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, focusing solely on punishment behavior as the outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and contextual moderators of costly punishment behavior, specifically the interaction between religious priming and prior religious commitment. However, it does not report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, nor does it use a public goods game or a multi-player social dilemma. The design is a two-player allocation and punishment game, which is adjacent to but not the same as a public goods game. The findings may inform predictions about the psychological triggers of punishment, but do not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The absence of efficiency or group payoff outcomes limits its direct utility for the downstream prediction task.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a two-player allocation and punishment game, not a public goods game or multi-player social dilemma.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings outcomes are reported or analyzed; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The findings are specific to the effect of religious and punishment primes on punishment behavior, not on cooperation or efficiency.
- The sample consists mainly of university students in Zurich, which may limit generalizability.
- The study uses subliminal priming, which may not generalize to real-world religious cues or contexts.
