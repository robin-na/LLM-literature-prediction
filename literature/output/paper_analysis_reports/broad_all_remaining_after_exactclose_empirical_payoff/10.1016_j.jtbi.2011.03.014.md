Outcome notes:
The paper is a review and synthesis of experimental and theoretical literature on public goods games with punishment, reward, and reputation mechanisms. It summarizes findings from multiple studies, reporting both payoff-based (efficiency, group payoff) and behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, reward).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that allowing punishment in public goods games reliably increases contributions and, over time, can increase group efficiency, especially when combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms. Punishment is more effective than reward alone in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. However, the costs of punishment can initially reduce efficiency, and only after repeated interactions does the net group payoff surpass the no-punishment baseline. When both punishment and reputation (indirect reciprocity) are available, efficiency is higher than with punishment alone, as the need for costly punishment is reduced. The review also notes that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its cost-effectiveness ratio and that the presence of reward does not always further increase efficiency unless it is combined with punishment in a way that targets norm violators.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency relative to control, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly. The efficiency gains are most pronounced when punishment is combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms, which reduce the frequency and cost of punishment needed. The review highlights the importance of game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward or reputation mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency boost from punishment is moderated by these dimensions, and that combining punishment with reputation mechanisms yields the highest efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The paper reviews studies with varying player counts (e.g., 4, 6, 8 players), but does not systematically analyze the effect of player count on efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Longer repeated interactions are necessary for punishment to yield net efficiency gains; short-run efficiency is often lower due to punishment costs.
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The public goods game is described as doubling the pool and redistributing, but specific MPCR values are not systematically analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is a key moderator; high punishment costs can reduce efficiency in the short run, but efficiency can increase in the long run if punishment is effective.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Higher punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) leads to higher contributions and efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward alone is less effective than punishment for efficiency, but combining reward and punishment can further increase cooperation.
- reward cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward cost is mentioned as a factor, but not systematically analyzed.
- reward technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward effectiveness is discussed in the context of direct and indirect reciprocity, but not systematically analyzed.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Some studies reviewed mention whether the number of rounds is known or unknown, which can affect cooperation, but this is not a primary focus.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players are typically informed about others' contributions, which is necessary for targeted punishment and reputation effects.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Players are often not able to track the identities of punishers over rounds, but can identify co-players within a round. The effect of punishment identification is not systematically analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a review and does not present new experimental data; its conclusions are based on synthesis of prior studies.
- Game design dimensions are discussed in aggregate, but not systematically varied or analyzed in a controlled way.
- Some claims about efficiency and moderators are based on indirect evidence or theoretical reasoning rather than direct experimental manipulation.
- The effect of player count, MPCR, and other design features is not systematically isolated.
- The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes for efficiency changes under different conditions.
