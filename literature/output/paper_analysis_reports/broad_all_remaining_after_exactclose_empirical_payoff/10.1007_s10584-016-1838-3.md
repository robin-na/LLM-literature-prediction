# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation studies of catastrophe avoidance: implications for climate negotiations
Authors: Hurlstone, M. J., Wang, S., Price, A., Leviston, Z., & Walker, I.
Journal: Climatic Change
Year: 2017

## Study Type
theory; empirical subtype=N/A; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of groups reaching threshold (catastrophe averted)
- contributions to public good
- pledges and proposals
- cooperation rates
- effect of communication, inequality, uncertainty, discounting, and reputation on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The reviewed experiments focus on behavioral outcomes such as the proportion of groups that reach a contribution threshold to avert catastrophe, cooperation rates, and the effects of various manipulations on these outcomes. Payoff or efficiency (in the sense of group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly analyzed, though group success in averting catastrophe is a related but not equivalent outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that cooperation in collective-risk social dilemma games is strongly affected by perceived risk, inequality, uncertainty about thresholds, intergenerational discounting, and the possibility of reputation-based rewards or punishments. High perceived risk, communication (pledges), intermediate targets, and reputation mechanisms can increase cooperation (i.e., the proportion of groups that avert catastrophe). Inequality and uncertainty about the threshold for catastrophe reduce cooperation. The paper discusses the need for credible enforcement mechanisms (punishment) in real-world climate agreements but does not review experiments that directly implement or vary punishment in the game. The main findings are about behavioral cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the importance of enforcement (punishment) mechanisms for sustaining cooperation in public-goods-like threshold games, but does not provide direct experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff. It is useful for understanding moderators of cooperation and the conceptual role of punishment, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that credible punishment mechanisms are likely to be important, especially under uncertainty, but does not provide data or effect sizes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reviewed experiments use group sizes of 5, 6, or 10; group size is discussed as a factor in cooperation, with larger groups facing more free-rider problems.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Most reviewed experiments use 10 rounds; some use 1 round. Number of rounds is reported but not manipulated as a main variable.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (pledges) is manipulated in some experiments (e.g., Tavoni et al. 2011), and increases cooperation.
- mpcr: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Games have a threshold structure, not a linear MPCR; some games double contributions (Milinski et al. 2006), but MPCR is not a main focus.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In Milinski et al. (2006), rewards are available in the indirect reciprocity game, and increase cooperation when linked to reputation.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In Milinski et al. (2006), reward cost is €1.50 for a €3 reward; not systematically varied.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward effectiveness is implied by the €1.50 cost for a €3 reward in Milinski et al. (2006); not systematically varied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Number of rounds is fixed and known in all reviewed experiments.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In most experiments, contributions are made public at the end of each round, which supports reputation and cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a review and synthesis, not a primary experimental study.
- No experiments reviewed directly manipulate or measure the effect of punishment (cost, effectiveness, or identification) on efficiency or group payoff.
- Main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, threshold reached), not efficiency or group payoff in the strict sense.
- Findings on enforcement and punishment are conceptual or based on analogy, not direct experimental evidence.
- The reviewed games are threshold public goods games (collective-risk social dilemmas), which are close but not exact analogues to standard linear public goods games.
