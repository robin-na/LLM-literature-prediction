## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average cheating probability (proxy for cooperation/defection)
- convergence to 'eventual correctness' (system always produces correct answers)
- convergence time to correctness
- percentage of simulation runs achieving correctness
- effect of punishment, aspiration, cost, and tolerance on convergence
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the probability of cheating (defection) and the system's ability to reach a state where all workers cooperate (eventual correctness). No explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. Convergence to correctness is a behavioral/system reliability outcome, not a direct payoff/efficiency measure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing punishment for cheating in a repeated master-worker (principal-agent) setting with reinforcement learning leads to faster convergence to a state where all workers cooperate (i.e., provide correct answers), compared to no punishment. However, punishment is not strictly necessary for eventual correctness if other conditions (such as sufficient reward relative to aspiration and cost) are met. The system is robust to high tolerance for defectors, but as group size increases, high tolerance can reduce the probability of achieving full cooperation. The main outcomes are the probability of cheating and the system's convergence to correctness, not direct measures of group efficiency or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, it shows that punishment reduces the average cheating probability and accelerates convergence to a fully cooperative state. In settings where efficiency is closely tied to the rate of cooperation (e.g., if only correct answers generate group benefit), these results suggest that punishment would increase efficiency, especially in the short-to-medium term. However, the absence of explicit payoff or efficiency data limits the direct applicability of these findings to the prediction task. The effects of group size, tolerance, and aspiration are also informative: higher group size and higher tolerance to defectors can undermine the effectiveness of punishment in achieving full cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Simulations vary the number of workers (e.g., 100, 2217, etc.). Larger group size makes convergence to correctness less likely at high tolerance levels.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations run for up to 1000 or 100,000 rounds. Convergence time is a key outcome, but number of rounds is not a treatment variable.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: workers either provide the correct answer (cooperate) or a wrong answer (defect/cheat).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a fine to the worker (W P_C), costless to the master. Simulations compare W P_C = 0 (no punishment) and W P_C = 1 (punishment equal to reward). Punishment accelerates convergence to correctness.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the size of the fine (W P_C). Larger fines increase the speed of convergence to correctness, but excessive punishment may deter participation.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward for correct answer (W B_Y) is always present and is a key parameter.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is paid by the master (cost W C_Y or W B_Y). The cost is always present and is a parameter in the model.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward size (W B_Y) is manipulated; higher reward relative to aspiration and cost is necessary for convergence to correctness.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated principal-agent (master-worker) game with binary (all-or-nothing) contribution and majority rule for outcome selection.
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported; outcomes are behavioral (cheating probability, convergence to correctness).
- Simulations, not experiments with human subjects; results may not generalize to real-world behavior.
- Punishment is modeled as costless to the punisher (master), which may not reflect real-world costs.
- Workers do not observe others' actions or payoffs; no social learning or network effects.
- Aspiration levels are fixed in most analyses; dynamic or heterogeneous aspirations are not fully explored.
- The effect of group size is only partially explored; very large groups may behave differently.
