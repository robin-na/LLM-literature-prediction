## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with rewards (where giving rewards is costly but beneficial to the receiver), the emergence of a social institution prescribing prosocial behavior (contribute and reward contributors) can lead to efficient public good provision, provided that rewards are only given to contributors and the network structure evolves quickly enough to allow exclusion of non-contributors. If rewards are also given to defectors, or if players update strategies too quickly, the system converges to low contribution and low efficiency. The model does not analyze punishment directly, but exclusion from rewards via network dynamics acts as an endogenous sanction. The findings are theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with reward mechanisms, especially in dynamic network settings. It provides strong theoretical evidence that reward-based institutions can achieve high efficiency if rewards are targeted only at contributors and if the network allows for exclusion of non-contributors. However, it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment, nor does it compare reward to punishment directly. For prediction tasks focused on punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that endogenous exclusion (a form of sanction) can substitute for explicit punishment, but does not quantify the effect of punishment per se. The model's results are most applicable to games with dynamic partner selection and reward, not to standard fixed-group punishment treatments.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Punishment is not modeled as a treatment; only rewards and endogenous exclusion via network structure are considered.
- The model assumes perfect observability of contributions, which may not hold in real-world settings.
- Findings may not generalize to fixed-group or non-networked public goods games.
- The model does not address the effect of explicit punishment mechanisms or compare reward to punishment directly.
- Parameter choices (e.g., reward cost, benefit, network evolution speed) may affect results and are not systematically varied.
