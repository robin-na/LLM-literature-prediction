- policy implementation willingness

Outcome notes:
The paper models and analyzes the evolution of cooperation and supervision strategies among government actors in emergency management, focusing on willingness to cooperate and implement policies. Payoff functions are constructed, but the main outcomes are behavioral (strategy selection, willingness) rather than aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical and numerical analysis show that: (1) Local governments are more likely to show strong willingness to implement emergency policies if the profit from doing so exceeds the increased cost; (2) To avoid all local governments selecting weak cooperation, the central authority (MWR) should increase penalties for weak willingness, making the penalty greater than the cost saved by weak cooperation. The model demonstrates how the strength of punishment and the cost/benefit structure affect the evolution of cooperation, but does not directly quantify efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that increasing punishment (penalties) for non-cooperation can increase the willingness to cooperate in multi-agent settings similar to public goods games. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report empirical results. The findings are most useful for understanding the mechanism by which punishment can shift equilibrium behavior toward greater cooperation, which may be associated with higher efficiency in analogous settings. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that higher punishment effectiveness and costliness of non-cooperation can promote cooperative equilibria, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency change.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model includes multiple local governments (at least 4: Henan, Hebei, Beijing, Tianjin) and a central authority (MWR), but does not systematically vary player count.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and continuous-time, not round-based, but the dynamic process is analogous to repeated or multi-stage games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Strategy choices are binary: strong or weak willingness to cooperate/implement policy.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The cost of punishment (penalty P, cost to MWR for supervision) is explicitly modeled and varied in the analysis. Increasing penalty for weak cooperation is shown to promote stronger willingness to cooperate.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is modeled as the impact of penalty P on local government willingness. Higher penalty effectiveness (relative to cost saved by weak cooperation) increases cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. There is a positive reward (increase in performance evaluation score, political achievement) for strong cooperation, but the focus is on penalties.
- reward cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Reward is modeled as an increase in political achievement (ε), but not as a direct cost to the rewarder.
- reward technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Reward effectiveness is present as the size of the increase in political achievement (ε), but not systematically varied.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Outcomes are behavioral (willingness to cooperate, strategy selection) rather than explicit efficiency or group payoff.
- The model is specific to emergency management among government actors and may not generalize directly to standard public goods games.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff.
- Parameter values are illustrative and not empirically calibrated.
