## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The agent-based model simulates a common good game in a residential complex, with agent strategies grounded in qualitative fieldwork. The model shows that in blocks with more defectors, common good production and payoffs collapse. Artificial experiments demonstrate that (1) introducing punishers in place of defectors, (2) increasing all agents' desirability for the common good, and (3) combining both interventions, all increase common good production and total payoffs. The most dramatic improvement occurs when both punishers are introduced and desirability is maximized, resulting in all agents adopting punishing strategies and achieving the highest payoffs and common good production. Thus, punishment mechanisms, especially when agents value the common good, can transform a low-efficiency, low-payoff equilibrium into a high-efficiency, high-payoff one.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (and increasing the number of punishers) in a public-goods-like environment can increase efficiency and group payoffs, especially when agents also value the common good. The model is not a standard PGG but is structurally similar, with continuous contributions, punishment, and payoff functions analogous to PGGs. The results suggest that, all else equal, introducing punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in settings where baseline cooperation is fragile or low. The effect is amplified if agents' intrinsic motivation (desirability) for the common good is high. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model uses 34 agents distributed across 4 blocks, matching the real residential complex. Player count is fixed per block and for the whole model.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model runs for 100 time ticks (rounds), with results averaged over 50 runs. Each tick is roughly equivalent to a week.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: agents can invest none to all of their endowment in the common good.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is agent-specific, based on desirability, but the overall MPCR is the mean of individual values. MPCR is a key parameter in the payoff function.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishers pay a cost of 1 unit of endowment to punish defectors. Artificial experiments manipulate the number of punishers, showing that more punishment increases efficiency and payoffs.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as effective in deterring defection; increasing the number of punishers leads to more cooperation and higher payoffs.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Agents sense the level of social pressure, which is a function of the number of more cooperative agents in their block, but do not see explicit summaries of others' contributions or payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is an agent-based simulation, not a real-world experiment; results depend on the assumptions and parameterization drawn from qualitative data.
- The game is a close variant of a public goods game but not a standard PGG; MPCR is agent-specific and desirability-driven.
- Punishment is implemented as a strategy type rather than as a choice available to all agents in each round.
- No direct communication, chat, or explicit information about others' actions is modeled, except via social pressure.
- Reward mechanisms are not considered, so the results do not address the effects of positive incentives.
- The model's findings are based on a specific real-world context (an Iranian residential complex) and may not generalize to all PGG-like environments.
