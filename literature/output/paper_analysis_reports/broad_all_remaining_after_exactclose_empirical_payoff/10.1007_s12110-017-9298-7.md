## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that rules promoting cooperation and using punishment can emerge from self-interested enforcement, not just from group-level selection. It predicts that the design and effectiveness of such rules depend on the distribution of enforcement power and the alignment of interests between enforcers and the group. The paper provides qualitative and historical examples but does not report experimental or quantitative findings on efficiency or payoff outcomes. It is relevant for understanding the mechanisms behind the emergence of punishment and cooperation rules, but does not directly inform predictions about efficiency changes in public goods games with or without punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and enforcement mechanisms may arise and be designed in social dilemmas, including public goods settings. It highlights the importance of power asymmetries, shared interests, and group cohesiveness in shaping the effectiveness and group-beneficial nature of punishment rules. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its value is in informing the types of mechanisms and moderators that may be important, rather than providing direct predictive evidence for efficiency changes.
