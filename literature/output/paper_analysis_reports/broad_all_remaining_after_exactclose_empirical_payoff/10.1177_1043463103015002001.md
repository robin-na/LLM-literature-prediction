## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment by strong reciprocators can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise unravel due to self-interest. Theoretical results show that with sufficient oblique cultural transmission and a non-trivial fraction of punishers, full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) can be a stable equilibrium. The paper also reviews experimental evidence showing that when punishment is allowed, group payoffs and cooperation rates are significantly higher than when punishment is not allowed. The model further predicts that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining efficiency depends on the cost and impact of punishment, the fraction of punishers, and the strength of cultural transmission.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control games without punishment, provided that punishment is not prohibitively costly and that a sufficient fraction of players are willing to punish defectors. The model and supporting evidence suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of game parameters, including group size and number of rounds, as long as punishment is effective and not too costly. The paper also highlights the importance of cultural and institutional context (e.g., norm internalization, oblique transmission) in sustaining prosocial behavior and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defection.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and relies on stylized models; empirical evidence is reviewed but not generated by the author.
- The models assume the existence of strong reciprocators and do not endogenize their emergence except through cultural transmission.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to depend on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, but real-world parameter values may vary.
- Cultural and institutional context (e.g., norm internalization, oblique transmission) is critical for sustaining cooperation, but these factors may be difficult to measure or manipulate in practice.
- The reviewed experiments are mostly lab-based and may not capture all complexities of real-world public goods dilemmas.
