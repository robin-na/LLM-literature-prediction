## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The chapter reviews evidence that, in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment opportunities (as in Fehr and Gächter 2000) leads to higher contribution rates and prevents the typical decay of cooperation seen in repeated games without punishment. It explains these results using models of social preferences, especially inequality aversion. However, the chapter does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes directly. The main findings are that punishment increases cooperation (contribution rates) and that social preference models can explain both altruistic and selfish behaviors depending on the context (e.g., presence of competition).

## Prediction Guidance
This chapter provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation, which is often associated with higher efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the chapter supports the qualitative expectation that punishment increases cooperation, but does not allow for precise prediction of efficiency changes. The evidence is most useful for understanding mechanisms and for qualitative directionality, not for quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Standard public goods game described with N=4 as an example; Fehr and Gächter (2000) referenced but not detailed for player count. Theoretical models and examples use N as a parameter.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Repeated games and decay of cooperation discussed; examples use 10 rounds and 'restart' procedures. No direct manipulation or analysis of round number effects on efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution is described as a continuous choice (how many tokens to contribute), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is described as alpha, with example alpha=0.4 for N=4 (so mpcr=0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as costly; the punisher pays a cost that increases with the amount of punishment. No quantitative details provided.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment reduces the punished player's payoff; the effectiveness per unit cost is not specified.

## Important Limitations
- The chapter is theoretical and review-based; it does not report new empirical data or direct analyses of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- All evidence about punishment effects is based on summary of prior literature (especially Fehr and Gächter 2000), not on new experiments or quantitative results.
- The main outcomes discussed are behavioral (contribution, punishment, rejection/acceptance), not payoff-based; efficiency effects are not directly analyzed.
- No quantitative details are provided for punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, or other key design parameters.
- No discussion of communication, reward mechanisms, or feedback/summary information in the public goods game context.
