# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Undermining trust and cooperation: The paradox of sanctioning systems in social dilemmas
Authors: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., De Cremer, D., & Wilke, H. A. M.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust in others
- cooperation (measured as number of chips contributed to the group)
- attribution of others' behavior to sanctioning system
- perceived motive of self-interest
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust (in others being internally motivated to cooperate) and cooperation (number of chips contributed). No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are reported. Cooperation is a behavioral outcome, not a direct payoff or efficiency measure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that sanctioning systems in public goods games can undermine trust in others' intrinsic motivation to cooperate. When the sanctioning system is removed, both trust and cooperation decrease, especially among participants with high initial trust. The negative effect on cooperation is mediated by the reduction in trust. The effect is stronger for large sanctions and when initial trust is high. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined from the reported results.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a punishment system can undermine trust and, after its removal, reduce cooperation, especially when initial trust is high. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on baseline trust levels and that the removal of punishment can have negative aftereffects on cooperation. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency outcomes. The findings are most useful for understanding behavioral mechanisms and potential moderators (such as initial trust and punishment size), rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies use groups of 4 participants in the public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each experiment consists of two rounds (phases): phase 1 and phase 2.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can give any number of chips (0-100).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each chip contributed is doubled and divided equally among 4 players. MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is imposed by the experimenter, not by peers. In Study 1, the two lowest contributors are fined EUR 5. In Study 2, small sanction is EUR 0.50, large sanction is EUR 5. In Study 3, the lowest contributor loses all chips.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed fine (EUR 0.50 or EUR 5) or loss of all chips, imposed by the experimenter on the lowest contributor(s). Not peer-administered; effectiveness is high (certain and automatic).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are two situations (A and B), i.e., two rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No feedback about others' decisions is given during the experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is imposed by the experimenter; it is not peer-administered, and punished individuals are not identified to others.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; only trust and cooperation (contribution) are measured.
- Punishment is imposed by the experimenter, not by peers, which may limit generalizability to peer punishment settings.
- The experiments use only two rounds (phases), so long-term effects of punishment are not assessed.
- No feedback about others' contributions is given, which may affect the dynamics of trust and cooperation.
- The findings are most relevant to the removal of punishment, not to the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency while it is present.
- The sample consists of university students in lab settings, which may limit external validity.
