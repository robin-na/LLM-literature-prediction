# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Fairness violations elicit greater punishment on behalf of another than for oneself
Authors: FeldmanHall, O., Sokol-Hessner, P., Van Bavel, J. J., & Phelps, E. A.
Journal: Nature Communications
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- endorsement rate of punishment option
- endorsement rate of compensation option
- endorsement rate of equity, accept, and reverse options
- differences in choice behavior between self and other conditions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequencies with which participants select different justice-restoration options (punish, compensate, equity, accept, reverse) in response to unfair offers. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; it focuses on behavioral preferences for punishment versus compensation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that, contrary to the assumption that punishment is the default response to fairness violations, individuals strongly prefer compensation over punishment when both options are available. This preference holds even when punishment is costless. However, when participants make decisions on behalf of others (third-party perspective), they are more likely to choose punitive options that both compensate the victim and punish the transgressor. The findings suggest that the context and available options significantly shape justice-restoration preferences. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its findings are not directly usable for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods or similar games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in environments where participants can choose between punishment and non-punitive justice-restoration options, punishment is not the preferred response to unfairness. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and the game is a modified ultimatum game rather than a public goods game. Therefore, while the findings suggest that the presence of alternative options may reduce the use of punishment, the paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods or similar games. Its main value is in highlighting that behavioral responses to punishment options are context-dependent and that efficiency predictions should consider the available choice set.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main game is a two-player (Player A and Player B) or three-player (Player A, B, C) sequential game, depending on the condition. Player A proposes a split, Player B (or C) chooses a response.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played multiple one-shot games (70 in lab, 40 in online), but each round is a one-shot interaction with a new partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all decisions were made anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is continuous (Player A chooses any split in whole dollar increments), and Player B's response is a choice among discrete options, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless in the main comparisons: the 'reverse' option allows the responder to both compensate themselves and punish the proposer at no additional cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment options vary in impact: 'punish' reduces Player A's payout to Player B's original offer; 'reverse' both compensates and punishes. The effectiveness per unit cost is not directly quantified, but the impact is described.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation is available as an option, which increases the responder's payout without reducing the proposer's payout (i.e., a reward/compensation mechanism).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Compensation is costless to the responder: choosing 'compensate' increases their own payout to match Player A's, enlarging the pie.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward/compensation is highly effective: choosing 'compensate' increases the responder's payout to match the proposer's, maximizing both players' monetary outcomes.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were aware of the number of trials/games they would play.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All decisions were made anonymously; players did not know the identity of the punisher or proposer.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a modified ultimatum game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant, so findings may not generalize to multi-player, repeated public goods settings.
- The main outcomes are behavioral preferences (endorsement rates) for punishment versus compensation, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Punishment and compensation are both costless in the main comparisons, which differs from most real-world or experimental public goods games where punishment is costly.
- The presence of multiple justice-restoration options (including compensation) may reduce the use of punishment compared to classic designs, limiting comparability.
- No analysis of how punishment or compensation affects group efficiency or total earnings is provided.
