## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of individual reward and punishment in the N-player trust game can promote the stable coexistence of investors and trustworthy trustees, which is interpreted as a promotion of trust. The size of the attraction domain for this coexistence increases with both the overall incentive strength and the share of incentives devoted to reward (as opposed to punishment). Reward is found to be more effective than punishment in promoting trust. The analysis is theoretical, using replicator dynamics, and does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the results imply that mechanisms increasing trust and trustworthiness can move the system toward more socially optimal states.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect theoretical support for the idea that introducing punishment (and especially reward) mechanisms can promote cooperation-like behavior in trust-based social dilemmas. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report quantitative efficiency outcomes for control vs. punishment-enabled conditions. The model is adjacent to public goods games, so its findings may be cautiously extrapolated to similar environments, especially regarding the positive effect of incentive strength and the relative effectiveness of reward over punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that increasing the strength and effectiveness of incentives (especially reward) is likely to expand the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes, but does not provide direct efficiency data.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The model is adjacent to, but not identical with, standard public goods games; it is based on the N-player trust game with three discrete strategies.
- Outcomes are reported in terms of evolutionary stability and attraction domains, not direct efficiency or group payoff.
- The model assumes an infinite well-mixed population and does not consider structured populations or finite group effects.
- No direct quantitative predictions of efficiency or group payoff are provided for control vs. punishment-enabled conditions.
