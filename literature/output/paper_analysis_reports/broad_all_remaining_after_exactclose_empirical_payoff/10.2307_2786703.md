# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: IS PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVE - COERCIVE STRATEGIES IN SOCIAL-EXCHANGE
Authors: MOLM, L. D.
Journal: Social Psychology Quarterly
Year: 1994

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- frequency of rewards received (net rewards)
- earnings (points earned, convertible to money)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of rewarding (compliance)
- frequency of punishing (retaliation)
- affective evaluations of partner
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency with which the subject rewards the partner (compliance), not group efficiency or total payoff. Net rewards (frequency of rewards minus punishments) and earnings are reported as secondary outcomes, but not as the primary focus. There is no explicit calculation of group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment is effective in increasing compliance (frequency of rewarding the punisher) when it is used frequently, contingently, and with high magnitude. The combination of high probability and high magnitude of punishment produces the highest compliance. Retaliation by the target does not increase with more frequent or stronger punishment, and negative affect toward the punisher is actually lower when punishment is strong and consistent, due to the resulting mutually rewarding exchanges. The effects are robust to the target's ability to retaliate and to variations in the probability and contingency of punishment. However, the main outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, but the frequency of compliance in a dyadic exchange. The results challenge classical exchange and bargaining theories that predict punishment provokes retaliation and reduces cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in dyadic reciprocal exchange settings with both reward and punishment options, frequent and contingent punishment increases compliance (rewarding behavior) by the target, without increasing retaliation or negative affect. However, because the primary outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, and the setting is not a public goods game or direct variant, the findings should be transferred to PGG-like environments with caution. The results suggest that, under conditions of high punishment frequency, contingency, and magnitude, punishment can be effective at increasing the target's prosocial behavior, but the implications for group efficiency in multi-person public goods games remain indirect. The paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment rather than direct efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment is conducted in a network of four actors, but the focal exchange is dyadic (subject and one simulated partner). Each subject interacts with two simulated partners, but the main analysis is on the dyad.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject participated in 200 exchange opportunities (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were via computer choices.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. On each round, actors could choose to reward, punish, or not act toward a partner; the amount per action was fixed, so the choice is discrete rather than continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher in terms of direct payoff; the only cost is the opportunity cost of not acting toward the alternative partner.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per action is fixed and manipulated as 'high' or 'low' punishment power (e.g., 7 or 3 points per action).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding (adding points to partner's earnings) is always available as an action.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is costless to the rewarder; adding points to the partner does not reduce the subject's own earnings.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward impact per action is fixed and manipulated as 'high' or 'low' reward power (e.g., 7 or 3 points per action).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the total number of exchange opportunities (200) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects were informed of their own choices and their partners' choices (reward, punish, or no action), and their point totals were updated.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew which partner (of the two) rewarded or punished them each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental setting is a dyadic reciprocal exchange network, not a public goods game or direct variant; findings may not generalize to multi-person PGG environments.
- The primary outcome is behavioral (compliance/rewarding), not group efficiency or total payoff; efficiency is not explicitly measured or reported.
- Punishment and reward are costless to the actor (except for opportunity cost), which differs from standard PGG designs where punishment/reward typically have direct costs.
- All partners except the subject are computer-simulated and follow programmed strategies, which may limit ecological validity.
- The study does not report group-level outcomes or efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, limiting direct applicability to the prediction of efficiency in PGG-like games.
