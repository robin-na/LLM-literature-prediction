# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Changing Social Norm Compliance with Noninvasive Brain Stimulation
Authors: Ruff, C. C., Ugazio, G., & Fehr, E.
Journal: Science
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount transferred by Player A (voluntary norm compliance)
- amount transferred by Player A in presence of punishment (sanction-induced norm compliance)
- difference in transfer between punishment and baseline rounds
- beliefs about fairness, expected anger, and expected punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: transfer amounts in baseline (voluntary) and punishment (sanction-induced) rounds, and the difference between them. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the threat of punishment increases norm compliance (higher transfers) in a modified dictator game, and that noninvasive brain stimulation of the rLPFC can causally modulate this effect. Anodal tDCS (increasing excitability) increases sanction-induced compliance (larger transfer difference between punishment and baseline rounds), while cathodal tDCS (decreasing excitability) reduces it. The effect is stronger in social (human) punishment contexts than in non-social (computer) contexts. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so its findings are limited to behavioral compliance rather than payoff-based outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment opportunities increases norm compliance in a dictator-like game, and that this effect is modulated by neural mechanisms. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The behavioral results (increased compliance under punishment) are consistent with the broader literature, but the lack of payoff data limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round involves two players: Player A (decision maker) and Player B (potential punisher).
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The experiment is described as having multiple rounds, but the exact number of rounds is not specified in the main text.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Interactions are described as anonymous and via computer terminals; no chat or communication is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (transfer) is continuous: Player A decides how much to transfer from their endowment.
- punishment cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Player B can reduce Player A's MUs in punishment rounds, but the cost structure for punishment is not specified in the main text.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Interactions are anonymous; Player A does not know the identity of Player B.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a modified dictator game, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player or public goods settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral compliance is measured.
- Punishment cost and effectiveness details are not specified, limiting comparability to standard PGG punishment designs.
- All participants are female, which may limit generalizability.
- The number of rounds and other design details are not fully specified in the main text.
