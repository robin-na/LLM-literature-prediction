## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, unlike standard social dilemma games (such as the public goods game), the adversarial game studied here can have multiple equilibria, including both efficient (no-crime, high-payoff) and inefficient (high-crime, low-payoff) outcomes. The presence of punishment (reporting and conviction) transforms the game from a pure social dilemma into a coordination game, where the effectiveness of punishment depends on the collective willingness to report. The Informant strategy (defectors who also report) is critical: under imitation dynamics, any presence of Informants is sufficient to reach efficiency, but under best response dynamics, Informants are necessary but not sufficient. The model shows that too severe punishment or high retaliation risk can undermine the emergence of efficient equilibria. Thus, the design of punishment institutions and the social context (e.g., support for reporting) are crucial moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like adversarial games is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism, the available strategy set (especially the presence of Informants), and the evolutionary dynamics. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under certain parameter regimes: moderate punishment severity, low retaliation risk, and the availability of strategies that combine defection and punishment. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will always improve efficiency; instead, the effect is conditional and can be negative or null if punishment is too harsh or if key strategies are unavailable. The results are most directly applicable to adversarial games with crime and reporting, but the mechanism insights may transfer to public goods games with similar punishment structures.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented in this paper.
- The game is an adversarial crime/punishment model, not a standard public goods game, so direct transfer to PGG settings may be limited.
- The model assumes a large population with random matching; real-world or experimental settings may differ.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to parameter choices (punishment severity, retaliation risk, etc.), which may not map directly to typical PGG parameters.
- The model does not include communication, reward mechanisms, or information feedback, which may be present in other experimental designs.
- The evolutionary dynamics considered (best response vs. imitation) may not capture actual human learning or adaptation in experiments.
