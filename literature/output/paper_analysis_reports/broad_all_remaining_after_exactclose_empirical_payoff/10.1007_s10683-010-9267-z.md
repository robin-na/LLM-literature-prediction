## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that voluntary costly feedback mechanisms can sustain high efficiency and trust/trustworthiness, even when feedback is not automatic. Allowing sellers to see buyers' feedback-provision histories (FbHist) increases feedback provision but does not improve efficiency or trustworthiness compared to the baseline where this information is not available (NoHist). In fact, in high feedback cost conditions, efficiency and trustworthiness are weakly or significantly lower when sellers can see buyers' feedback-provision histories. Sellers use this information to target buyers who do not leave feedback, reducing their own trustworthy behavior toward these buyers. The main efficiency outcome (proportion of successful trades) is reported for all treatments, showing that the addition of feedback-provision histories does not increase and may decrease efficiency, especially in later rounds and at higher feedback costs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated trust/trade game with voluntary costly feedback, adding a mechanism that allows sellers to see buyers' feedback-provision histories does not increase efficiency and may reduce it, especially when feedback is costly. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment or sanctioning mechanisms that allow for targeted discrimination based on feedback history may not always improve efficiency, particularly if the baseline efficiency is already high. The results are most relevant for environments where feedback is voluntary and costly, and where the punishment mechanism is indirect (via reputation and future trade decisions) rather than direct monetary punishment. The findings caution against assuming that more detailed reputation or sanctioning mechanisms will always improve group efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 12 subjects, with fixed roles as buyer or seller (6 buyers, 6 sellers per session).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 45 rounds, divided into three 15-round subsessions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Buyers choose whether to buy (all-or-nothing), and sellers choose whether to ship (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each successful trade generates a surplus of 15 points for both buyer and seller (buyer pays 35 for item worth 50, seller pays 20 to produce, receives 35).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is indirect via feedback; buyers pay a cost (C=2 or C=5) to provide feedback, which can harm sellers' future outcomes. Higher feedback cost reduces efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is via reputation: feedback affects future sellers' willingness to ship. The 'technology' is the ability to leave feedback that is visible to future partners. When sellers can see buyers' feedback-provision histories, they can target reticent buyers, reducing trustworthiness and efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the number of rounds in each subsession and the number of subsessions.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both buyers and sellers see the seller's transaction summary and history (number of sales, feedbacks, etc.). In FbHist, sellers also see buyers' feedback-provision history.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust/trade game rather than a standard public goods game, so results may not fully generalize to PGG environments.
- Punishment is indirect (via reputation and future trade) rather than direct monetary punishment.
- The group size is fixed at 12 (6 buyers, 6 sellers), and roles are fixed, which may limit generalizability.
- Feedback is always truthful and cannot be faked, which may not reflect real-world settings.
- The study only examines first-order feedback-provision histories, not more complex reputation systems.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
