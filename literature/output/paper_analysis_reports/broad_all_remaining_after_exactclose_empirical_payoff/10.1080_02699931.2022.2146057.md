# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The carrot and the stick: How guilt and shame facilitate reciprocity-driven cooperation
Authors: Bica, A., & Cretu, R. Z.
Journal: Cognition & Emotion
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rates of fair and unfair offers
- proposed amounts to fair and unfair partners
- perceived fairness ratings
- reciprocity (positive and negative)
- effect of guilt and shame induction on behavior
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral outcomes in the Ultimatum Game: acceptance rates, proposals, and reciprocity patterns. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a primary outcome. Some financial loss is discussed in the context of punishment/rejection, but not as a formal efficiency or welfare measure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Inducing guilt in participants led to more generous offers to fair partners (positive reciprocity), while inducing shame led to higher rejection rates of unfair offers and lower offers to unfair partners (negative reciprocity). Both emotions influenced fairness-related decision-making, but via different mechanisms. The study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral responses (acceptance, proposal amounts) in the Ultimatum Game. The findings suggest that moral emotions can facilitate cooperation, but the pathways differ: guilt promotes generosity toward fair actors, while shame motivates punishment of unfairness.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the behavioral mechanisms (reciprocity, punishment, reward) that can affect cooperation in economic games, but does not directly inform predictions about efficiency or group payoff in public goods or punishment-enabled games. The Ultimatum Game is adjacent to public goods games, and the punishment here is endogenous (rejection of unfair offers) rather than a separate costly punishment mechanism. The findings may inform expectations about how emotional states or social context could moderate the impact of punishment or reciprocity on cooperation, but do not provide quantitative or direct evidence for efficiency outcomes under different game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played against two anonymous partners (one fair, one unfair) in a two-player Ultimatum Game setting.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 10 rounds as responder and 10 as proposer with each partner, for a total of 40 rounds (20 per partner, 2 partners).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players; all interactions were anonymous and computer-mediated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (offer) is continuous: proposers choose any split of $20; responders accept or reject (all-or-nothing on acceptance).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: rejection of an offer results in zero payoff for both players. The cost is the foregone earnings for both proposer and responder.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is binary and maximal: rejection eliminates all payoffs for both players in that round.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward is endogenous: proposers can make generous offers, which is interpreted as positive reciprocity (rewarding fair partners).
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Cost of reward is the amount of money the proposer gives up by making a generous offer.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Reward is direct: the amount offered to the partner is the reward; effectiveness is the monetary value transferred.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the structure and number of rounds in the experiment.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to public goods games; punishment is endogenous (rejection) rather than a separate costly punishment mechanism.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; all outcomes are behavioral (acceptance, proposals, reciprocity).
- Sample is limited to college students, limiting generalizability.
- Cultural factors in the expression of guilt and shame are not addressed.
- Potential anchoring effects from the order of partner presentation cannot be ruled out.
- Ecological validity is limited by the artificial nature of the online, anonymous, computer-mediated task.
