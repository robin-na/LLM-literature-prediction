# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Costly punishment across human societies
Authors: Henrich, J., McElreath, R., Barr, A., Ensminger, J., Barrett, C., Bolyanatz, A., Cardenas, J. C., Gurven, M., Gwako, E., Henrich, N., Lesorogol, C., Marlowe, F. W., Tracer, D., & Ziker, J.
Journal: Science
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of costly punishment (rejection rates in ultimatum game, punishment rates in third-party punishment game)
- minimum acceptable offer (MAO) in ultimatum and third-party punishment games
- maximum acceptable offer (MXAO) in ultimatum game
- mean offers in dictator game (behavioral altruism)
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral measures of costly punishment and altruism (e.g., rejection rates, willingness to punish, offers in dictator game). It does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes. The closest related outcome is the 'income-maximizing offer' (IMO), which is an indirect measure of how punishment affects proposer payoffs, but not group efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that costly punishment (both second-party and third-party) is a widespread human behavior, but its prevalence and intensity vary substantially across societies. In all populations, more unequal offers are punished more frequently, but the threshold for punishment and the willingness to punish differ. There is a strong positive correlation between a population's willingness to punish and its average altruistic behavior (as measured by dictator game offers). The findings support theories of gene-culture coevolution in human cooperation. However, the paper does not use public goods games and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its direct relevance to predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments is limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that costly punishment is a robust and variable feature of human behavior across societies, and that it is positively associated with altruism. However, because the experiments are ultimatum and third-party punishment games (not public goods games), and because efficiency or group payoff is not reported, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. The main implication is that the effect of punishment on cooperation-related behaviors is likely to be highly variable across populations, and that willingness to punish is linked to local norms of altruism. The paper does not provide direct evidence on how punishment affects efficiency or payoff in repeated or group-based public goods settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game: 2 players; Third-party punishment game: 3 players; Dictator game: 2 players. All games are one-shot and not group-based.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between players was explicitly prevented before and during the games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Offers in the ultimatum and third-party punishment games were restricted to 10% increments of the stake, i.e., discrete but not strictly all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the third-party punishment game, punisher pays 10% of the stake (20% of their allocation) to reduce the target's payoff by 30% of the stake.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 10% of stake paid by punisher reduces target's payoff by 30% of stake (3:1 impact/cost ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; players know there is only one round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are anonymous; players do not know the identity of punishers or other players.

## Important Limitations
- The experiments are not public goods games; they use ultimatum, third-party punishment, and dictator games, which differ in structure and group dynamics.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral measures of punishment and altruism are analyzed.
- All games are one-shot and do not capture repeated interaction or dynamic effects relevant to many public goods environments.
- The findings on punishment and altruism may not generalize to multi-player, repeated, or public goods contexts.
- Variation in punishment is shown to be large across populations, but the paper does not identify the causal factors behind this variation.
