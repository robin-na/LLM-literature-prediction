## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proves that in repeated common interest games (with no discounting), the mere presence of cooperative (nondraconian) strategies in the set of possible strategies does not guarantee efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcomes in equilibrium. However, if the set of possible strategies includes at least one 'draconian' strategy (i.e., a strategy that is willing to punish noncooperation to the maximum extent, such as min-maxing the opponent forever after a deviation), then all Nash equilibria are efficient. The paper also shows that this result is robust to certain computability restrictions and discusses the limits of these results under discounting and other technical assumptions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated games with a structure similar to public goods or common interest games, the availability of sufficiently severe punishment (i.e., the ability to impose maximal punishment at low or no cost) is both necessary and sufficient for punishment to induce efficient outcomes in equilibrium. If the game design allows for draconian punishment strategies, then enabling punishment should lead to high efficiency, regardless of the control efficiency. If only mild or 'cooperative' punishment is possible, efficiency gains are not guaranteed. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted games, but the logic is relevant for understanding the role of punishment severity and credibility in other settings.

## Important Limitations
- The results are theoretical and apply to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted repeated games; generalization to more players or finite/discounted settings is discussed but not formally established.
- The model assumes that punishment can be imposed at no or negligible cost to the punisher (i.e., min-maxing is feasible and credible); in real-world or experimental settings, punishment may be costly and less effective.
- The results do not address stochastic or noisy environments, or games with incomplete information.
- The analysis does not cover public goods games with more than two players or with linear payoff structures (i.e., standard MPCR public goods games).
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are theoretical.
