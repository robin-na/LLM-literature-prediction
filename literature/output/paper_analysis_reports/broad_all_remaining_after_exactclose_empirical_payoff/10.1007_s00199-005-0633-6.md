# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inferring repeated-game strategies from actions: evidence from trust game experiments
Authors: Engle-Warnick, J., & Slonim, R.
Journal: Economic Theory
Year: 2006

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average per round payoff
- movement toward equilibrium payoffs
- proportion of supergames fit by inferred strategies
Non-payoff outcomes:
- inferred repeated-game strategies (e.g., grim trigger, tit-for-tat, end-game defection)
- frequency of strategy types over time
- best response dynamics
- conditional cooperation/defection
Outcome notes:
The paper's main dependent variables are the inferred strategies used by players in repeated trust games, but it also reports average per-round payoffs and their evolution over time, especially in relation to equilibrium payoffs. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated, but average payoffs and their movement toward cooperative or non-cooperative equilibrium are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study uses a novel inference method to map observed actions in repeated trust games to underlying strategies. In indefinite games (with a continuation probability of 0.8), many subjects use harsh trigger strategies that punish non-cooperation, supporting cooperative outcomes and higher payoffs. Over time, the proportion of play consistent with these strategies increases, and average payoffs move toward the cooperative equilibrium. In finite games (5 rounds), strategies with end-game defection dominate, and payoffs decline toward the non-cooperative equilibrium. The paper provides evidence that punishment-like strategies (grim trigger) are effective in sustaining cooperation and higher payoffs in indefinite games, but not in finite games.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment (in the form of harsh trigger strategies) in improving efficiency or payoffs depends strongly on the game horizon: indefinite repetition with sufficient continuation probability enables punishment to sustain cooperation and higher payoffs, while finite repetition leads to unraveling and lower payoffs. The trust game is not a standard PGG, but the findings are likely to transfer to other repeated social dilemmas with similar strategic structure. The presence of punishment strategies is associated with more positive efficiency effects in indefinite games, but not in finite games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (Player A and Player B) trust games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Finite games: 5 rounds; Indefinite games: random length with continuation probability 0.8 (average ~5.1 rounds). The effect of game horizon is central: indefinite games support cooperation via punishment, finite games do not.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are anonymous and via choices.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: Player A chooses Send (S) or Don't Send (D); Player B chooses Keep (K) or Return (R).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If Player A sends, the endowment is doubled (from $0.40 to $0.80) and given to Player B, who can return $0.60 to each or keep $1.20. The marginal per-capita return is not directly calculated, but the structure is described.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a harsh trigger (permanent defection after non-cooperation), which is highly effective in sustaining cooperation in indefinite games.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In finite games, players know there are 5 rounds. In indefinite games, players do not know when the game will end (continuation probability 0.8). This difference is central to the results.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Players observe the actions of their counterpart in each round, but no mention of additional summary information.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not a separate action but is implemented as a strategy (e.g., switching to defection). Players know their counterpart's actions but not their identity across supergames (random rematching).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated trust game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as a strategy (grim trigger), not as a costly, explicit punishment action.
- Payoff-based efficiency is not directly calculated; only average per-round payoffs and their movement toward equilibrium are reported.
- All games are two-player; effects in larger groups may differ.
- No explicit manipulation of punishment cost or effectiveness; only the presence of punishment strategies is studied.
- No communication or chat; results may differ with communication.
- No explicit reward mechanism is present.
