# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Corruption, social judgment and culture: An experiment
Authors: Salmon, T. C., & Serra, D.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- propensity to offer bribes
- propensity to accept bribes
- propensity to act corruptly (offer or accept bribe)
- propensity to send messages of disapproval or approval
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: whether subjects offer or accept bribes, and whether third parties send messages of disapproval. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total welfare, or aggregate payoffs is reported. The game is a one-shot bribery/corruption game with negative externalities, not a repeated public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that increasing social observability and allowing for social judgment (informal sanctions in the form of approval/disapproval messages) reduces the likelihood of corrupt behavior (offering or accepting bribes) among participants who identify with low-corruption cultures, but not among those who identify with high-corruption cultures. The effect is driven by differences in the likelihood of social disapproval: participants from high-corruption cultures are less likely to disapprove of corrupt acts. The Victim Knows treatment (where the victim is informed but cannot judge) has little effect. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, and the design is a one-shot, role-based bribery game rather than a repeated public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the effect of informal social sanctions (social judgment) on norm-violating behavior in a one-shot, negative-externality game. While not a public goods game, it is relevant for understanding the limits of informal punishment/sanction mechanisms, especially their dependence on cultural context. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of informal sanctions in reducing norm violations (and thus potentially increasing efficiency) is highly contingent on prevailing social norms and expectations of disapproval. However, because the game is not repeated, does not feature voluntary contributions, and does not report efficiency or group payoff, its direct predictive value for public goods game efficiency is limited. It is most informative for settings where informal social sanctions are the main enforcement mechanism and where cultural heterogeneity is a concern.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played in groups of 3: citizen, public official, and other member of society.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot; each subject plays only one round in one treatment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; all interactions are anonymous except for the possibility of sending approval/disapproval messages in the Social Judgment treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (bribe offer/accept) is binary: offer/accept or not.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Social Judgment treatment, sending a message (approval/disapproval) is costless; there is no formal punishment with material cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is informal: approval/disapproval messages (happy/neutral/frowny face) with no material consequence, only social signaling.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Approval messages (happy face) can be sent in Social Judgment treatment, but no material reward.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sending approval (reward) messages is costless.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is purely social (approval message), no material effect.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot; no uncertainty about number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Social Judgment treatment, all 'other members of society' observe the choices of all citizens and officials in the session.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or repeated social dilemma; it is a one-shot bribery/corruption game with negative externality.
- No direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; only behavioral outcomes (bribe offer/accept, disapproval messages) are analyzed.
- Punishment and reward are purely informal (social messages) and have no material consequences.
- The effect of punishment/reward is highly contingent on cultural background, limiting generalizability.
- No analysis of dynamic effects, learning, or repeated interaction.
- The sample is university students in the US, with self-reported cultural identification.
