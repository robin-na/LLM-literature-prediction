## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that experimental evidence from public goods games challenges the strong free-rider and sanctioners' dilemma assumptions of rationalist theory. It highlights that individuals often cooperate and punish defectors even at a cost to themselves, and that mechanisms such as communication, framing, trust, and legitimacy can enhance cooperation. Punishment (including decentralized, third-party, and symbolic forms) is shown to be effective in sustaining cooperation, especially when perceived as fair. Outcasting (exclusion) and endogenously chosen rules (consent) are also identified as effective mechanisms. However, the paper does not report new experimental results or provide direct measures of efficiency; rather, it draws on the literature to inform theoretical debates in international law.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and related mechanisms (communication, framing, trust, legitimacy) can increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in public goods games and similar environments. It is useful for understanding which design dimensions (e.g., presence of punishment, communication, framing, endogenous rule choice) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct empirical evidence on efficiency outcomes, so it should be used as a source of mechanism-based priors and qualitative guidance rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical synthesis and does not report new empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency and group payoff outcomes are discussed only indirectly and qualitatively; no quantitative estimates are provided.
- The review draws on experimental literature, but external validity to international law and real-world state behavior is acknowledged as limited.
- Some mechanisms (e.g., outcasting, endogenous law) are discussed based on experiments in national or individual contexts, not international law.
- No direct analysis of how specific game design dimensions quantitatively moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
