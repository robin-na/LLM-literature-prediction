## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or meta-analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Most evidence is synthesized from studies focused on behavioral outcomes (punishment, cooperation) rather than direct efficiency or group payoff.
- Findings about moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) are often based on indirect or mixed evidence.
- The review highlights the complexity and context-dependence of punishment effects, but does not provide quantitative predictions for efficiency.
- Some referenced studies use related but non-PGG games (e.g., Ultimatum Game, Dictator Game), which may limit direct applicability.
