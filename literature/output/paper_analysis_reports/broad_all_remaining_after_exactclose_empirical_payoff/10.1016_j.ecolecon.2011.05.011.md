The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (tokens), resource state, and frequency of rule breaking. Non-payoff outcomes such as effort, cooperation, and trust are also analyzed, but efficiency and group earnings are central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing externally imposed rules with probabilistic sanctions (confiscation of yield upon detection) in a CPR field experiment in fishing villages leads to only modest improvements in group efficiency and earnings. The effect is significant only when the quota rule is chosen and followed, but in most groups, rule breaking remains common and efficiency gains are small or absent. The context—such as economic necessity, lack of trust in authorities, and low legitimacy of rules—plays a major role in limiting the effectiveness of sanctions. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms, as implemented here, do not reliably increase efficiency in real-world resource-dependent communities, except under specific conditions of rule choice and compliance.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in field settings with resource-dependent participants, the introduction of rules with probabilistic sanctions (punishment) does not reliably increase efficiency in public-goods-like games. Efficiency gains are observed only when the group chooses and complies with a quota rule; otherwise, rule breaking and contextual barriers (e.g., lack of trust, economic necessity) undermine the effect of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment mechanisms alone is not sufficient to ensure higher efficiency—contextual factors and the type of rule matter. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) is only modestly higher, and only in some cases. Game design dimensions such as the absence of communication, the probabilistic and relatively weak punishment, and the lack of participant buy-in to rules are important moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group in the experiment consisted of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 20 rounds (10 rounds per stage: baseline and rule stage).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly banned during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Effort choices were discrete: low or high effort (0, 1, or 2 units). Not strictly all-or-nothing, but not continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by the payoff table, but the exact MPCR value is not explicitly stated. Payoff structure is provided in Table 1.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as confiscation of yield if caught breaking the rule (with 1/6 probability). The cost to the punisher is not explicit; punishment is external/automatic, not peer-administered.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: if caught, the entire harvested amount is confiscated. Probability of inspection is 1/6 per round. Collective losses from fines are 20-27.3% of group payoffs per round if a rule is broken and detected.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (10 per stage) was known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants received information about the state of the resource and group outcomes after each round.

## Important Limitations
- Punishment was implemented as an external, probabilistic sanction (confiscation by inspection), not as peer-to-peer punishment; results may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- No communication was allowed between participants, which may limit coordination and the effectiveness of rules.
- The legitimacy and acceptance of rules were low, as rules were externally imposed and not crafted by participants.
- Contextual factors (economic necessity, lack of trust, weak institutions) strongly limited the effect of punishment; results may not generalize to other contexts.
- The study focuses on field settings with high resource dependence and may not be representative of laboratory or less resource-dependent populations.
- The punishment mechanism had a relatively low probability of detection (1/6), which may have limited its deterrent effect.
