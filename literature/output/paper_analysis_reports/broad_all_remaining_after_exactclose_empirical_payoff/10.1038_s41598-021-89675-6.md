# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Moral labels increase cooperation and costly punishment in a Prisoner's Dilemma game with punishment option
Authors: Mieth, L., Buchner, A., & Bell, R.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability of cooperation
- probability of moral punishment
- probability of hypocritical punishment
- probability of antisocial punishment
- punishment bias
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: cooperation rate and types of punishment. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment is reported. Some descriptive statistics on final account balances are given, but not as a function of punishment vs. no-punishment conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that moral framing (using labels like 'I cooperate' and 'I cheat') increases the likelihood of both moral and hypocritical punishment compared to neutral framing ('A' and 'B'). In Experiment 1, where only participants could punish, moral framing increased punishment but did not affect cooperation. In Experiment 2, where both participants and partners could punish, moral framing increased both punishment and cooperation. Antisocial punishment and general punishment bias were not affected by framing. The results suggest that the effect of moral framing on cooperation is contingent on the presence of punishment. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes as a function of punishment, so its direct relevance to efficiency prediction is limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and framing of punishment options can increase both punishment behavior and, under certain conditions, cooperation in a Prisoner's Dilemma game. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it uses a two-player Prisoner's Dilemma with a punishment stage, but the findings about behavioral responses to punishment and framing may be informative for predicting when punishment will increase cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) in similar environments. The results suggest that punishment is more likely to increase cooperation when it is symmetric (i.e., both players can punish), and that moral framing amplifies this effect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is a two-player (dyadic) Prisoner's Dilemma in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 20 one-shot rounds, each with a different partner (no repeated interaction with the same partner).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff matrix: mutual cooperation = 10 cents each, mutual defection = 0, unilateral defection = 20 (defector), -10 (cooperator). Not a standard PGG MPCR, but the payoff structure is fully specified.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 cent spent by punisher reduces partner's payoff by 10 cents (cost-to-impact ratio 1:10).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 cent spent reduces partner's payoff by 10 cents (punishment tech = 10).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds (20), as the design is described as 20 one-shot rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see both their own and their partner's choices and payoffs, as well as updated account balances.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always attributed to the participant (or to the partner in Experiment 2); the interface makes clear who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a two-player Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game; findings may not fully generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment is reported.
- All interactions are one-shot (no repeated games with the same partner), limiting insight into long-term or dynamic effects.
- Partners are computer-controlled, not real human participants, which may affect ecological validity.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency or welfare is not measured or discussed.
- The study focuses on the effect of moral framing and punishment on behavior, not on payoff-based outcomes.
