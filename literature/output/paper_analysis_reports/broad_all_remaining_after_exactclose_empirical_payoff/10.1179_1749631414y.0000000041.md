Journal: Environmental Archaeology

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- storage efficiency (cooperative and household)
- probability of shortage avoidance
- potential for surplus generation
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average probability to cooperate
- emergence and persistence of cooperation
- effect of social norms (reciprocity control) on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The model tracks the evolution of cooperation and the probability of shortage avoidance, which is a proxy for group welfare, but does not directly report group payoff, total earnings, or efficiency as defined in public goods games. Surplus generation is discussed but not as a primary quantitative outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that cooperation in food storage is most likely to emerge and persist when (1) cooperative storage is at least as efficient as household storage, (2) there is moderate to high dependency on storable food, and (3) social norms regulating access to the cooperative stock are neither too strict nor too lax. Too strict enforcement (high reciprocity requirement) can undermine cooperation by excluding agents who fail to contribute due to bad luck, while too lax enforcement allows free-riding to undermine the common stock. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff in the public goods sense, but shows that cooperation increases the probability of avoiding shortages and, under certain conditions, can lead to surplus. The findings suggest that moderate sanctioning (via access control) is optimal for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff in a public goods game with explicit punishment, it models a closely related common-pool resource dilemma where access to the common resource is conditional on past cooperation (a form of sanction). The results suggest that intermediate levels of sanctioning (not too strict, not too lax) maximize cooperation and system performance (in terms of shortage avoidance and potential surplus). For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment or exclusion mechanisms that are too harsh may reduce efficiency by discouraging recovery from bad luck, while too little enforcement allows free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of storage efficiency and dependency on the resource, which may map onto MPCR and baseline efficiency in public goods games. However, direct quantitative predictions of efficiency with and without punishment are not available from this model.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 100 agents (households) as the default, but the model is general for finite N.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 100,000 ticks; learning occurs in generations of 50 ticks.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and agent-based, not empirical or experimental; results are simulation-based and not validated with real-world data.
- No explicit public goods game or direct measurement of group efficiency or payoff as in standard PGG experiments.
- Punishment is modeled only as exclusion from the cooperative stock based on past cooperation, not as costly punishment or peer sanctioning.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or mapping to standard PGG parameters.
- No communication, reputation, or observation of others' actions; agents learn only from their own outcomes.
- Findings are qualitative and mechanistic, not quantitative predictions of efficiency with or without punishment.
