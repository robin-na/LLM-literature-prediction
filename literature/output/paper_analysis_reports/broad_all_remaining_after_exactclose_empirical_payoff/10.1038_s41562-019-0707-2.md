# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions
Authors: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S.
Journal: Nature Human Behaviour
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency
- conditional punishment preferences
- cooperation rate
- anger ratings
- self-reported motivations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment decisions (unconditional and conditional), types of punishment preference (coordinated, independent, anticoordinated, non-punisher), and cooperation rates. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Some discussion of payoff structure and Nash equilibrium, but no empirical efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players: each group consists of two Punishers and one Target.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game: each group plays a single round of the public goods game and punishment stage.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: each player chooses to either 'cooperate' (contribute) or 'defect' (not contribute).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cooperation yields 2 MU to each of 3 players (total 6 MU per cooperation), so MPCR = 2/5 = 0.4 (since defecting yields 5 MU to self, 0 to others; cooperating yields 2 MU to all).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 MU per punishment; Target loses 3 MU per punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 MU cost to punisher results in 3 MU loss to target (punishment impact per unit cost = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; the number of rounds is clear and fixed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are shown the Target's PGG decision (cooperate or defect) before making punishment decisions. No mention of other summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers know their own and the other punisher's role, but the Target does not know which punisher assigned punishment. Punishers do not know each other's PGG decision.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report or analyze group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined.
- The experiment is one-shot, so long-run or repeated-game effects of punishment are not addressed.
- No direct comparison between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions is provided.
- The sample is from Amazon Mechanical Turk (US participants), which may limit generalizability to other populations or laboratory settings.
- The design is highly stylized and minimal, which may limit ecological validity.
