## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes an infinitely large, well-mixed population, which may not capture dynamics in finite or structured groups.
- All incentives are institutional (centralized); peer-to-peer punishment and reward are not analyzed.
- The model assumes compulsory payment for the incentive institution, which may not reflect voluntary participation or second-order free-riding in real settings.
- No explicit modeling of rounds, time horizon, or learning beyond evolutionary dynamics.
- No communication or information feedback is modeled.
- The results may not generalize to settings with adaptive or dynamic incentive schemes beyond the static or simple adaptive cases discussed.
