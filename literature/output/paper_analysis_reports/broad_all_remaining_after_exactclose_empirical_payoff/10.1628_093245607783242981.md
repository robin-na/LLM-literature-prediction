Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and focuses on the formation of cooperative tendencies and social trust, defined as the proportion of cooperative players. While payoffs are modeled, the main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, trust), not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when punishment institutions are more effective at detecting and punishing defection, more individuals choose to invest in cooperative tendencies, leading to higher social trust (i.e., a higher proportion of cooperative players). However, the average level of intrinsic cooperative tendency among cooperators is lower, since extrinsic incentives substitute for intrinsic motivation. The model predicts multiple equilibria depending on the distribution of investment costs, and highlights the potential for both high- and low-trust equilibria. The relationship between institutional punishment and cooperation is complex: stronger institutions can crowd in more cooperators but may reduce intrinsic motivation. The paper does not provide direct predictions or analysis of group efficiency or total payoff, but the results are relevant for understanding how punishment institutions affect the prevalence of cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that increasing the effectiveness of punishment institutions (e.g., higher probability of detection or stronger sanctions) increases the proportion of cooperative players in a prisoners' dilemma-like environment. While it does not directly model public goods games or report efficiency outcomes, it suggests that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation rates, which may translate into higher efficiency in related environments. However, the paper also warns that reliance on extrinsic punishment may reduce intrinsic motivation over time. For prediction tasks, the main implication is that enabling or strengthening punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency), but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The model is most relevant for settings where the main outcome is the prevalence of cooperation rather than group payoff.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model uses a continuum of agents, but the game is a two-player prisoners' dilemma. The effect of group size is not analyzed.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Both one-shot and finitely repeated games are analyzed. The effect of repetition is discussed in terms of reputation and cooperation, but not in terms of efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The contribution choice is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Payoff parameters (g, d, l) are specified, which determine the incentives for cooperation, analogous to MPCR in PGGs.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is modeled as institutional (probability q of detection and zero material return for defectors), not as a cost paid by other players. The cost is implicit in the effectiveness parameter q.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is parameterized by q (probability of detection and sanction). Higher q increases social trust (proportion of cooperators).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In repeated games, the number of rounds (T) is finite and known.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental results.
- The model is based on the prisoners' dilemma, not the public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed; the main outcome is the proportion of cooperative players (social trust).
- Punishment is modeled as an institutional mechanism (probability of detection and sanction), not as peer punishment with explicit costs and impact.
- No analysis of communication, information feedback, or other design features common in experimental PGGs.
- No direct quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes.
