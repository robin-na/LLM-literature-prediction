Payoff-related outcomes such as fitness, wealth, and P&L are reported as part of the evolutionary dynamics, but the paper does not directly report efficiency as the ratio of realized group payoff to the maximum possible group payoff. Instead, it uses fitness and wealth as proxies for evolutionary success.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The simulation model, based on the structure of standard public goods games with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost: 1 MU spent removes 3 MUs from target), shows that when agents evolve under disadvantageous inequity aversion, they develop a stable, empirically-matched propensity to punish defectors (k≈0.25). This level of punishment is sufficient to sustain high cooperation and maximize group fitness/wealth. The model finds that disadvantageous inequity aversion is evolutionarily stable and dominates other preference types, leading to higher average wealth, lower volatility, and longer agent lifetimes. While the model does not report efficiency in the strict sense (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible), it provides strong evidence that punishment under disadvantageous inequity aversion increases group welfare and evolutionary success compared to self-regarding or non-punishing populations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in a standard public goods game with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment leads to higher group welfare and evolutionary success, provided agents have disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model is calibrated to match empirical punishment levels and shows that these levels are sufficient to sustain high cooperation and maximize group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar game designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff/welfare) relative to the control. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative efficiency ratios, so the evidence is most useful for qualitative or directional prediction, especially in settings matching the model's design parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model and the empirical experiments it is calibrated to use n=4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Empirical experiments used 6 or 10 rounds; the simulation runs for thousands to millions of periods to reach evolutionary equilibrium.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses m in [0,20] MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. mpcr = 0.4 (g=1.6, n=4); each MU contributed returns 1.6 MUs to the group, split equally.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 MU to reduce target's payoff by 3 MUs (punishment efficiency factor r_p=3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 MU spent removes 3 MUs from target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Empirical experiments had a fixed number of rounds (6 or 10), but the simulation is evolutionary and does not model player knowledge of game length.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, individual contributions are revealed to all group members before punishment decisions.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a simulation/theory study, not a direct empirical test of efficiency outcomes.
- Efficiency is not reported as the ratio of realized group payoff to the maximum possible; instead, fitness, wealth, and P&L are used as proxies.
- The model is calibrated to n=4, mpcr=0.4, and a specific punishment cost/tech; generalization to other parameterizations is not directly tested.
- No reward mechanism is considered; only punishment is modeled.
- No communication or reputation effects are included.
- The model assumes agents can only punish those who contributed less than themselves (no antisocial punishment).
- The evolutionary simulation abstracts away from short-term strategic behavior and memory effects.
