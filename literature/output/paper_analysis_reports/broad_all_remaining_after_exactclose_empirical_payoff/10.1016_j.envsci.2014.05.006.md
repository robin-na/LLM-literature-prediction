# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: What can economic experiments tell us about institutional change in social-ecological systems?
Authors: Rommel, J.
Journal: Environmental Science & Policy
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- market entry rate (proportion of participants who choose to enter the market and trade)
- conditional entry (proportion of participants who condition entry on others' behavior)
- punishment frequency (proportion of observers who punish, amount spent on punishment)
- requested compensation for trade
- moral perception of trading
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is market entry (whether participants choose to trade, thus causing a negative externality). The study does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare in the sense of public goods games. The closest outcome is the rate of market entry, which is interpreted as a proxy for moral behavior (foregoing profit to avoid negative externality).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that allowing third-party punishment of traders (who cause negative externalities) leads to a significant reduction in market entry rates compared to the baseline, especially for sellers. About 86% of observers are willing to punish, spending on average more than half their endowment to do so. The conditional entry treatment also reduces market entry, as about a quarter of participants condition their entry on others' behavior, reflecting norm conformity. The main outcome is the proportion of participants who choose to trade (i.e., cause the externality), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings show that punishment can enforce social norms and reduce harmful market behavior, but the study does not measure efficiency as defined in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of costly punishment reduces the frequency of norm-violating behavior (trading that causes negative externalities) in a large market setting. However, because the outcome is market entry rate (not group efficiency or total payoff), the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games. The punishment mechanism is effective at deterring harmful behavior, especially among sellers, and most observers are willing to punish at a cost. For prediction tasks, the main signal is that enabling punishment reduces the rate of norm violation, but the mapping to efficiency or group payoff in standard PGGs is indirect. The market structure, one-shot design, and externality to a third party differ from standard PGGs, so caution is needed in transferring quantitative effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each market consists of approximately 300 sellers and 300 buyers (large market, one-shot).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment is one-shot; each participant makes a single decision (no repeated rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Entry decision is all-or-nothing (enter or not); if entering, participants submit a bid/ask (continuous).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: each €1 spent by the observer reduces the trader's payoff by €3. Observers have a €3 endowment for punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: €1 spent by observer reduces trader's payoff by €3 (punishment multiplier = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single decision).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed about the structure and consequences of the market, but do not see real-time summaries of others' choices.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; traders are matched with an observer, but identities are not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or direct variant; it is a market with negative externalities, so efficiency is not defined as in PGGs.
- The main outcome is market entry (behavioral), not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The setting is one-shot, not repeated, so dynamic effects are not captured.
- The externality is imposed on a third party (UNICEF donation), not on other participants.
- No direct measurement of group efficiency or surplus; only indirect inference from entry rates.
- No reward mechanism is present, so cannot compare punishment to reward.
- The mapping from market entry rates to efficiency in PGGs is indirect and may not generalize quantitatively.
