The primary dependent variable is the mean contribution (level of cooperation) in the population after 10,000 generations. Payoff is used as a mechanism for evolutionary updating, but group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff are not directly reported as outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in agent-based simulations of a continuous prisoner's dilemma, cooperation (measured as mean contribution) does not evolve in the absence of both punishment and social structure. When only one of these is present, cooperation also does not evolve. However, when both social structure (local interactions on networks) and the option for costly punishment are present, populations can evolve to high levels of cooperation, depending on the strength of punishment and the network structure. The transition from defection to cooperation requires stronger punishment as network density increases. Scale-free networks are an exception, showing little or no response to punishment. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome, so direct implications for efficiency are not available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of both local social structure and effective punishment mechanisms is necessary for the evolution of high cooperation rates in continuous-contribution social dilemmas. However, because the outcome is mean contribution rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. The results suggest that, in structured populations (except scale-free networks), enabling punishment can dramatically increase cooperation rates, which would likely increase efficiency, but the paper does not quantify this directly. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that punishment is only effective in structured (non-well-mixed) populations, and that the effectiveness of punishment depends on network density and punishment strength.

Punishment cost per unit is c_k (agent trait, [0,1]); punishment impact is c_k * M, where M is the punishment multiplier (varied from 0 to 6, and up to 5000 in some tests). Higher M (punishment effectiveness) leads to more cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment multiplier M is the effectiveness per unit cost; higher M leads to more cooperation in structured networks.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents do not have memory or knowledge of future rounds; each game is one-shot and anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is mean contribution (cooperation rate), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The model is a simulation, not an empirical or experimental study.
- Payoff is used for evolutionary updating, but not reported as an outcome.
- The game is a two-player continuous prisoner's dilemma, not a standard multi-player public goods game.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or surplus is provided.
- Results may not generalize to human behavior or real-world settings without further empirical validation.
