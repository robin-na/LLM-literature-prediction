# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punitive justice serves to restore reciprocal cooperation in three small-scale societies
Authors: Fitouchi, L., & Singh, M.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency and type of punishment (victim, kin, third-party)
- frequency and type of compensation or restorative payments
- frequency and type of mediation or reconciliation procedures
- relationship between kinship/interdependence and punishment/compensation
- escalation to feuding or breakdown of cooperation after non-payment
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on ethnographic and case-level analysis of punitive justice, compensation, and mediation in small-scale societies. Outcomes are primarily behavioral and institutional (who punishes, how, and under what circumstances), not measured in terms of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Some discussion of breakdown of cooperation or escalation to feuding is present, but not quantified as efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three small-scale societies, punitive justice is primarily restorative and dyadic, not group-norm-enforcing. Third-party punishment is rare, and most interventions are aimed at mediating disputes and restoring relationships. Compensation to victims is common and often proportional to harm, especially among the Mentawai and Nuer. Kinship and interdependence reduce the likelihood or magnitude of punishment/compensation. When costs are not imposed on offenders, cooperation often breaks down and feuding may result. The paper does not use public goods games or measure efficiency, but provides evidence that punishment and compensation are more about restoring dyadic cooperation than increasing group-level efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the function of punishment in environments that are adjacent to public goods games: real-world small-scale societies with reciprocal obligations and norm violations. It suggests that, in such contexts, punishment is not primarily about increasing group efficiency or enforcing group-level cooperation, but about restoring dyadic relationships and preventing escalation. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment may depend on whether the context is more about dyadic reciprocity or group-level cooperation. However, because the paper does not use experimental PGGs or report efficiency or payoff outcomes, its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes in PGGs is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment and compensation often involve real costs (e.g., livestock, goods, violence), but not quantified as in PGGs. Some discussion that high-status individuals may have lower punishment costs.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Effectiveness of punishment is discussed in terms of restoring relationships and deterring future harm, not as a technical impact per unit cost.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Punishment and compensation are almost always public and known to all parties; anonymity is not a feature.

## Important Limitations
- The study is based on ethnographic and retrospective case analysis, not controlled experiments or public goods games.
- No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; outcomes are behavioral and institutional.
- No manipulation of game design dimensions such as player count, rounds, MPCR, or punishment parameters.
- Findings may not generalize to experimental PGGs or environments with different social structures.
- Data for some societies (e.g., Kiowa) are based on interviews conducted decades after the events, raising concerns about recall bias.
- Nuer analysis is qualitative and lacks quantitative data.
