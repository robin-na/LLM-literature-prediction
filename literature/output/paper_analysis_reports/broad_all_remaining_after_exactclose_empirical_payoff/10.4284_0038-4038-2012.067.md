# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Emotions, Sanctions, and Cooperation
Authors: Joffily, M., Masclet, D., Noussair, C. N., & Villeval, M. C.
Journal: Southern Economic Journal
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average contribution
- punishment points assigned/received
- payoff function described
Non-payoff outcomes:
- skin conductance response (SCR)
- self-reported emotional valence
- change in contribution after punishment
- punishment behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are emotional measures (SCR, valence) and contribution behavior. While the payoff function is described and group-level contributions are reported, efficiency or total group payoff is not directly analyzed as a primary outcome. The effect of punishment on contributions is the main behavioral focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions, with groups converging to near-full cooperation. Emotional responses (measured by SCR and self-reported valence) are closely linked to both cooperation and punishment: positive emotions are associated with higher contributions, and negative emotions in response to free riding predict punishment behavior. Receiving punishment leads to negative emotions and increased subsequent contributions by free riders. The study provides strong evidence that punishment opportunities can sustain high cooperation, but the main outcomes are emotional and behavioral (contribution rates), not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and a punishment stage with convex costs and anonymous assignment, the introduction of punishment opportunities leads to a large increase in average contributions (from about 9.3 to 19.2 out of 20). While efficiency is not directly reported, the near-maximal contributions in the punishment condition imply that group efficiency approaches the social optimum. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no rewards, anonymous punishment, full feedback) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar lab PGG designs. However, the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff reporting is a limitation for precise quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players, fixed membership for 20 rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; explicit prohibition in instructions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: 0–20 ECU per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 0.4 (group account multiplied by 1.6, split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is convex: 1 point costs 1 ECU, 2 points cost 2 ECU, 3 points cost 4 ECU, etc. (see Table 1).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces target's payoff by 10%, up to 100%.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told there are 20 periods; game length is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see total group contribution and each member's individual contribution (in random order).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; IDs are scrambled each round to prevent identification and retaliation.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported or analyzed as a primary outcome; only average contributions are presented.
- The sample size is relatively small (12 groups, 6 per treatment).
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with undergraduate students, which may limit generalizability.
- Emotional measures (SCR, self-reported valence) are the main focus, not economic efficiency.
- No analysis of heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness across different parameterizations (e.g., different MPCRs, punishment costs).
