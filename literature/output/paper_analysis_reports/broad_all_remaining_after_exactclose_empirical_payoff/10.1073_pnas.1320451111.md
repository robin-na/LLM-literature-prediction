# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Third-party punishment increases cooperation in children through (misaligned) expectations and conditional cooperation
Authors: Lergetporer, P., Angerer, S., Glätzle-Rützler, D., & Sutter, M.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average tokens earned per player
- net surplus per player
- expected payoff from cooperation vs. defection
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- beliefs about partner's cooperation
- beliefs about likelihood of punishment
- actual punishment frequency
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the average payoff (tokens earned) per player in each treatment, with explicit calculation of net surplus. Cooperation rates and beliefs are also reported, but efficiency/payoff is a primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that the threat of third-party punishment in a one-shot PD with children increases cooperation rates from 25% (control) to 58% (punishment treatment). This leads to an increase in average payoffs from 2.49 tokens (control) to 2.98 tokens (punishment), a 20% improvement in efficiency. The effect is not due to frequent actual punishment (which is rare), but rather to children's (over-)expectation of punishment and to conditional cooperation based on higher expected partner cooperation. The effect is robust across age cohorts (7-11 years).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a two-player, one-shot PD with children substantially increases efficiency, even when actual punishment is rare. The effect is mediated by beliefs and conditional cooperation. For prediction, the presence of a third-party punishment mechanism (with a strong, binary punishment technology) in a simple, anonymous, one-shot, two-player game with no communication and known payoffs can be expected to increase efficiency by a substantial margin (here, ~20%). The effect is robust to age (within 7-11), and is not dependent on actual punishment frequency. The design is adjacent to a PGG, but the payoff structure and mechanism are closely related.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is a two-player (dyadic) prisoner's dilemma, with a third-party observer in the punishment treatment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (send both tokens) or defect (keep both tokens).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player has 2 tokens; sending both tokens doubles their value for the partner. If both cooperate, each gets 4 tokens. MPCR is 1 (each token sent yields 2 tokens to the other, but the sender loses 1).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher (third party) pays 1 token to punish; this destroys all of the defector's earnings from the PD.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is binary and maximal: 1 token cost to the punisher eliminates all of the defector's earnings (up to 4 tokens).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is not available in the experiment, but the possibility of third-party reward is discussed as a future research direction.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; the number of rounds is clear and known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are not informed about the partner's choice before making their own decision. After the experiment, outcomes are revealed after a delay. No summary information is provided during decision-making.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know the identity of the third-party punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a one-shot, binary-choice prisoner's dilemma, not a standard multi-player public goods game; results may not generalize to larger groups or repeated settings.
- Punishment is third-party, not peer punishment, and is binary and maximal; other punishment technologies may yield different effects.
- Actual punishment is rare, so the efficiency effect is driven by beliefs and conditional cooperation, which may differ in other populations or with different information structures.
- The study is conducted with children aged 7-11; results may not generalize to adults or younger children.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment is studied.
- Delayed payment (3 months) may affect children's valuation of payoffs, though the authors check for effects of patience.
