Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good (in RWF), interpreted as a measure of cooperation. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported. The paper does not report efficiency or welfare outcomes, nor does it analyze group payoff as a share of the social optimum.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that communication spillovers between villages (i.e., when participants in one village can talk to those in a neighboring village who have already played) lead to substantial increases in contributions to the public good. The effect is robust to different matching strategies and distance thresholds. In the second round, when some villages played alternative versions of the public goods game (including punishment, reward, or risk), the positive effect of these treatments on contributions was only observed in villages not exposed to communication spillovers. The main implication is that communication between sessions can significantly increase cooperation, potentially biasing estimates of treatment effects in field experiments. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so its direct relevance to predicting efficiency is limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that communication (even unstructured, informal communication between sessions) can increase cooperation in public goods games. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that allowing or enabling communication (or failing to prevent spillovers) may increase contributions, which could translate into higher efficiency if all other game parameters are held constant. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, and only analyzes contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a main treatment, so it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, highlight the importance of controlling for communication and spillovers when interpreting the effects of any treatment (including punishment) in field experiments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public goods game session involved 12 participants per village.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each village played two rounds: the first round was identical for all, the second round involved either a repeat or an alternative version (punishment, reward, or risk).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication was not permitted during the game, but the paper studies the effect of communication spillovers between sessions (i.e., between villages). The main finding is that such communication increases contributions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could decide how much of their 400 RWF endowment to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.25: contributions are multiplied by 3 and divided among 12 players.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. One of the alternative versions in round 2 included a reward mechanism, but the paper does not analyze its effect in detail.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. The paper does not report or analyze efficiency or total group earnings.
- Punishment and reward are only present in one of the alternative versions in round 2, and their effects are not separately analyzed or reported.
- The study focuses on communication spillovers, not on the effect of punishment or reward mechanisms.
- Findings may not generalize to settings where communication between sessions is not possible or is tightly controlled.
- The mechanisms behind increased cooperation are inferred indirectly; communication content was not observed.
