The paper is a review of neural mechanisms underlying altruistic punishment, focusing on cognitive and affective processes. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes. Behavioral outcomes discussed are mainly about the decision to punish, neural activation, and psychological processes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews evidence that altruistic punishment is driven by neural processes related to inequity aversion, cost-benefit analysis, and social reference framing. It highlights the involvement of brain regions such as the insula, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex in these processes. The review draws on studies using ultimatum, dictator, and trust games, but does not focus on public goods games or report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding the psychological and neural basis of punishment decisions, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods game-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is useful for understanding the cognitive and neural mechanisms that may underlie punishment behavior, which could inform mechanistic models, but it does not report or synthesize payoff-based outcomes or moderators relevant to efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers only indirect, mechanistic context and does not inform quantitative or qualitative predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The cost to the punisher is a central concept in the review, discussed as a key variable in the decision to punish, but not manipulated or analyzed for its effect on efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The impact of punishment per unit cost is discussed in the context of cost-benefit analysis, but not quantitatively analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical review and does not report new empirical data or meta-analytic synthesis.
- It does not analyze or report efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes.
- The review focuses on neural and cognitive mechanisms underlying punishment, not on the effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency in public goods games.
- Most evidence is drawn from ultimatum, dictator, and trust games, which are adjacent but not identical to public goods games.
- No direct evidence is provided for the effect of punishment on efficiency or its moderators in public goods game-like environments.
