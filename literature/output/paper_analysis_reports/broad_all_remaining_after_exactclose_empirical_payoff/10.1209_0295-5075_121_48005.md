## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both punishment and partner switching (opportunistic rewiring of social ties) can support the evolution of cooperation in a networked prisoner's dilemma. When partner switching is infrequent, strong punishment is necessary to suppress defectors. When partner switching is frequent, cooperation dominates even with weak or no punishment. The mechanism is that defectors sever ties with punishers and preferentially connect to cooperators, which increases cooperators' opportunities to be imitated and thus proliferate. Average payoffs by strategy are reported, showing that cooperators and punishers can outperform defectors, but the main outcome is the frequency of each strategy. The results suggest that social fluidity (partner switching) can substitute for punishment in maintaining cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report group efficiency or total welfare directly, it shows that both punishment and partner switching can increase the prevalence of cooperation and reduce defection, and that average payoffs for cooperators and punishers can exceed those of defectors. The findings suggest that in networked games with opportunities for partner switching, the marginal benefit of adding punishment to increase efficiency may be reduced if partner switching is already frequent. The results also highlight the importance of the punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the fluidity of social relationships (analogous to partner switching) as moderators of punishment's effect. However, because the main outcomes are strategy frequencies and not group efficiency, the evidence is most useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators rather than for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The study is a theoretical agent-based simulation, not an empirical experiment with human subjects.
- The main outcomes are strategy frequencies, not group efficiency or total welfare, so direct quantitative predictions of efficiency are not possible.
- The game is a prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game, so results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Partner switching is modeled as opportunistic link adaptation in a network, which may not correspond directly to real-world or experimental partner switching.
- No reward mechanism is included; only punishment and partner switching are studied.
- Payoff outcomes are reported by strategy, not as group-level efficiency or surplus.
