# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Hidden Paths from Morality to Cooperation: Moral Judgments Promote Trust and Trustworthiness
Authors: Simpson, B., Harrell, A., & Willer, R.
Journal: Social Forces
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trustworthiness (amount returned in trust game)
- trust (amount sent in trust game, partner choice)
- self-perceived moral identity
- perceived morality and trustworthiness of others
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral measures of trustworthiness (amount returned in a trust game), trust (amount sent/partner choice), and self/other perceptions of morality and trustworthiness. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three experiments, the authors show that (1) making moral judgments of others' immoral behavior increases subsequent trustworthiness in a trust game (measured by amount returned), (2) this effect is mediated by increased self-perceived moral identity, and (3) observers trust and prefer to interact with strong moral judges, perceiving them as more moral and trustworthy. The behavioral outcomes are individual-level trust and trustworthiness, not group-level efficiency or payoff. The design does not include public goods games, explicit punishment, or sanctioning mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its findings are adjacent: it shows that making moral judgments (not material punishment) can increase trustworthiness in trust games, and that observers trust moral judges more. However, there is no manipulation of punishment, no public goods game, and no group payoff or efficiency outcome. The results may be relevant for understanding how non-material social judgments can affect prosocial behavior, but they do not inform the quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Study 1 and 3 use groups of 4-6 or 4-5, but the trust game is always dyadic (Sender-Receiver). The group size is not a manipulated variable for the trust game outcome.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. All trust games are one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; all interactions are anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Trust game decisions (amount returned or sent) are continuous, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In the trust game, the amount sent is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is not a PGG; the marginal return is not per capita but to the receiver.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants are told the game is a single round (one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants are shown information about others' moral judgments (in Study 3), but not about others' payoffs or contributions.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use public goods games or any group-level cooperation dilemma; all behavioral measures are from dyadic trust games.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; 'moral judgment' is a private evaluative act, not a material sanction.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; all outcomes are individual-level trust/trustworthiness or perceptions.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment, group-level cooperation, or repeated interactions.
- The effect of moral judgments is only tested in domains with moral consensus; effects may differ in contested moral domains.
