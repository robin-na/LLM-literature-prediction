Payoff outcomes are discussed in terms of expected payoffs for strategies, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported. Theoretical and simulation results focus on the prevalence and stability of cooperation under different partner choice regimes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing the option to leave one's partner in repeated prisoner's dilemma games (i.e., partner choice) leads to a substantial increase in average cooperation rates across a wide range of benefit-to-cost ratios and continuation probabilities. This is because partner choice enables endogenous assortment: cooperators can avoid defectors by leaving, which stabilizes cooperation. Theoretical analysis and agent-based simulations both show that, with partner choice, the population spends more time in cooperative states and escapes from universal defection more quickly. The paper compares strategies that punish by defecting (grim trigger) to those that punish by leaving, and finds that punishing by leaving is more robust to invasion and more likely to support cooperation. Although the main outcome is cooperation rate, higher cooperation implies higher average payoffs, so efficiency is indirectly improved by partner choice.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that enabling partner choice (the ability to leave and rematch) in repeated prisoner's dilemma games increases cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff. While the setting is not a public goods game per se, the mechanism is closely related, and the results are likely to transfer to PGG-like environments where partner choice is possible. The paper does not study costly punishment or explicit sanctions, but rather 'punishment' by leaving. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling partner choice should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no partner choice, especially in repeated dyadic games. The results are most directly relevant when the game is dyadic, repeated, and partner choice is the main intervention.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players per game). This is fixed by model construction.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are repeated with a continuation probability (delta), i.e., indefinite horizon with geometric stopping. Number of rounds is stochastic, not fixed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Action space is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Equivalent to benefit-to-cost ratio (b/c) in the prisoner's dilemma; b is varied from 1 to 6 (or 7), c is normalized to 1.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know the exact number of rounds; the game ends stochastically with continuation probability delta.

## Important Limitations
- The model is limited to dyadic repeated prisoner's dilemma games; results may not fully generalize to multi-player public goods games.
- Punishment is implemented as leaving (partner choice), not as costly punishment; findings may not transfer to settings with explicit costly punishment or peer sanctions.
- Payoff outcomes are discussed in terms of expected payoffs and cooperation rates, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported.
- Simulations use infinite or large populations and assume well-mixed matching; real-world social structure or network effects are not modeled.
- No communication, reputation, or information about others' past behavior is allowed; the effect of these features is not addressed.
- No errors in strategy execution are modeled; robustness to noise is not analyzed in this paper.
