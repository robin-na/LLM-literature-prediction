## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that the enforcement of the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention is best understood through a combination of rational-choice and institutional approaches. It highlights the collective action problem inherent in international anti-bribery efforts, likening it to a public goods game with free-rider issues. The rational-choice approach explains 'under-enforcement' as a result of insufficient incentives and ineffective centralized monitoring or sanctioning. The institutional approach emphasizes the role of domestic institutional contexts and 'unconscious enforcement' by agencies like the US SEC, whose evolving missions and regulatory environments lead to increased enforcement over time. The paper suggests that decentralized monitoring involving private sector whistleblowers and inter-country competition may be more effective than centralized mechanisms. However, it does not provide empirical data or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual and theoretical context for understanding how punishment, monitoring, and institutional design might affect cooperation and compliance in public-goods-game-like environments, especially at the international treaty level. However, it does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff effects of punishment or monitoring mechanisms. Its relevance to predicting efficiency in experimental public goods games is limited to its discussion of collective action, monitoring, and sanctioning as mechanisms, rather than direct evidence. It may inform mechanism design or the interpretation of monitoring and punishment variables, but does not support direct prediction of efficiency outcomes.
