## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in a donation game with institutional punishment, the evolution of cooperation and pay-it-forward strategies is strongly affected by the cognitive model of the players. Under EUT, only strong punishment (high fine or high probability) can sustain cooperation, and pay-it-forward strategies are not stable. Under PT, even weak or infrequent punishment is sufficient to deter free-riding, because players overweight small probabilities of being punished. This leads to the emergence and stability of pay-it-forward strategies and higher average payoffs (efficiency) in the population. The model shows bi-stability, with both cooperative and non-cooperative equilibria possible depending on parameters and initial conditions, but overall, punishment increases efficiency more under PT than under EUT.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games (here, a donation game) will increase group efficiency, especially when players are subject to prospect-theoretic biases. The effect is more pronounced when punishment is mild or infrequent, as real human players may overweight the risk of being punished. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the payoff-based outcomes are directly analyzed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (even with low probability or low severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, particularly if the control game has low efficiency due to free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of psychological factors (probability weighting) in moderating the effect of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes a large, well-mixed population; two players are randomly selected for each game. There is no fixed group size as in standard PGGs.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and continuous-time; players are selected infinitely many times. There is no fixed number of rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to help (pay cost c) or not (pay nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Equivalent to b/c ratio; b > c is assumed. In numerical examples, b=5, c=1, so mpcr=5 for the recipient (but not a standard PGG).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players (except free-riders) pay cost d to maintain the punishment institution. d=0.05 in examples. Higher punishment cost makes cooperation harder to sustain.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is institutional: defectors are fined s with probability δ. s is varied (e.g., s=5.6, 5.1, 4.6, 3). Higher s increases efficiency by deterring free-riding.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a donation game (pairwise helping), not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs.
- Population is assumed infinite and well-mixed; no group structure or finite group size.
- Punishment is institutional and probabilistic, not peer-to-peer.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are modeled.
- Findings depend on the assumption that players follow prospect theory; real human behavior may differ.
