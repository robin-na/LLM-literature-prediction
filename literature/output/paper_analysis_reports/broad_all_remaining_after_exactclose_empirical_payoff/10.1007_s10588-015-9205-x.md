## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The theta-pi model demonstrates that both punishment (regulative institutions) and empathy (normative institutions) can increase the efficiency of economic interactions by expanding the set of transactions that are mutually beneficial and deterring predation. The model provides explicit conditions under which markets are efficient (all cooperate), collapse (all defect or abstain), or are path-dependent/oscillatory. Punishment (pi) is especially effective in high-temptation, low-sucker-loss environments, while empathy (theta) is more effective when losses from being cheated are high. When some actors are exempt from punishment (elites), efficiency declines and can collapse if the exempt group is too large. Overlapping institutions and partial coverage can lead to complex dynamics, including cycles of cooperation and predation. The model quantifies the marginal value of increasing institutional strength and provides phase diagrams for different transaction types.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (increasing pi) in a public-goods-like environment will increase efficiency, especially when the temptation to defect is high and the cost to victims is not overwhelming. The model clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its universality and the structure of payoffs, and that partial or selective enforcement can undermine efficiency. The findings suggest that, all else equal, increasing the probability or effectiveness of punishment will shift the equilibrium from market collapse or low efficiency to high efficiency, provided the institutional coverage is broad. The model also highlights that empathy-based mechanisms can substitute for punishment in some environments, but are less effective when temptation is high and losses are low. For prediction tasks, the model's phase diagrams and threshold conditions can be mapped to game design dimensions such as punishment cost, effectiveness, and universality, supporting the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency in public-goods-like games.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically validated; no experimental or observational data are presented.
- The model is dyadic (two-player) and does not generalize to multi-player or group public goods games.
- Repeated interactions, learning, and dynamic adjustment are not explicitly modeled; the analysis is equilibrium-based.
- Punishment is modeled as a probability of being caught by an impersonal enforcer, not as peer punishment or costly punishment with explicit cost/impact tradeoffs.
- No explicit mapping to standard public goods game parameters (e.g., MPCR, group size, contribution levels) is provided.
- Empathy (theta) is modeled as a fixed parameter; heterogeneity and distributional effects are only partially explored.
- The model does not address communication, information, or other social mechanisms beyond institutions.
- The model assumes symmetric payoffs and does not address more complex or asymmetric interactions.
