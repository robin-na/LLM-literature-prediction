The paper is entirely theoretical and focuses on the set of payoff profiles (efficiency, welfare) that can be supported by a new class of punishment strategies in repeated games. No behavioral or non-payoff outcomes are analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that in repeated games, including social dilemmas and Cournot oligopoly, AFP strategies—where punishment is finite and asymmetric—can sustain any feasible and individually rational payoff profile when players are sufficiently patient. The punishment phase is designed so that nondeviators are always better off than the deviator, and after a finite number of periods, cooperation resumes. This approach is less restrictive than some standard equilibrium refinements and can support efficient outcomes (e.g., cartel profits) even in cases where other concepts (like WRP) cannot. The results are illustrated with examples including the prisoner's dilemma and Cournot oligopoly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that finite, asymmetric punishment strategies can sustain efficient, cooperative outcomes in repeated games, even when standard trigger or minimax punishments are not credible or too harsh. For the prediction task, it suggests that the design of the punishment phase (finite duration, asymmetry favoring nondeviators) can be critical for supporting high efficiency, especially as the number of players increases or when renegotiation-proofness is problematic. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide direct quantitative predictions for specific experimental public goods games, but they do indicate that efficiency can be maintained with appropriately designed punishment even in large groups or complex environments.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- Results assume infinite repetition and perfect monitoring, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- Punishment is implemented as a phase with lower payoffs for the deviator, not as a costly action or explicit punishment mechanism as in many public goods experiments.
- No quantitative predictions for efficiency changes in finite, noisy, or imperfectly monitored environments.
- Does not address the effects of communication, default framing, or marginal per-capita return (mpcr) directly.
