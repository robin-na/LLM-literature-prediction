The main behavioral outcome is theft (tokens invested in theft), but the paper also reports group and individual earnings, and explicitly discusses efficiency as total group earnings. Voting and exclusion behavior are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Exclusion (ostracism) as a punishment mechanism reduces theft in a social dilemma game with production and theft choices. Individuals who are excluded and then re-admitted steal less than before exclusion, indicating a disciplining effect. However, exclusion also leads to retaliation (excluded members cast more exclusion votes) and stigmatization (those previously excluded are more likely to be excluded again, even after controlling for their behavior). Comparing treatments with and without exclusion, total group earnings (efficiency) are very similar, indicating that the efficiency gains from reduced theft are offset by the costs of exclusion (e.g., lost production from excluded members, cost of voting). The effect of exclusion on efficiency is thus mixed: it reduces anti-social behavior but does not systematically improve group welfare. Heterogeneity in productivity does not significantly affect theft or efficiency. The findings are robust across homogeneous and heterogeneous groups.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that introducing exclusion (ostracism) as a punishment mechanism in a social dilemma with theft reduces anti-social behavior (theft), but does not systematically increase efficiency (total group earnings) compared to a control without punishment. The efficiency effect is mixed: for some subgroups (high-productivity members), earnings may increase slightly, but for others (low-productivity members), earnings decrease. The net effect on group efficiency is negligible. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in similar games (with exclusion as punishment, majority voting, and one-period exclusion), enabling punishment may not reliably increase efficiency, even if it reduces anti-social behavior. The game is not a standard PGG, so direct transfer to PGGs should be cautious, but the evidence is informative for adjacent social dilemmas with exclusion-based punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments have 15 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (allocation) is continuous: players allocate 10 tokens between production and theft each period.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Voting to exclude costs 1 ECU if the vote is successful (i.e., if the exclusion occurs).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exclusion for one period: excluded members cannot participate in allocation or voting and receive a small fixed income (5 ECU) for that period.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 15 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, players see an information table with each member's earnings from production, theft, and losses to theft, but not who stole from whom.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players do not know who voted to exclude whom; exclusion is by majority vote, but individual votes are not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a social dilemma with production and direct theft, so results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as exclusion (ostracism) for one period, not as monetary deduction; effects may differ from standard monetary punishment.
- Efficiency effects are measured as total group earnings, but the costs of exclusion (lost production, fixed income for excluded members, cost of voting) may offset gains from reduced theft.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects; external validity to real-world or field settings is limited.
- No reward mechanism is present, so interaction effects between punishment and reward cannot be assessed.
- The effect of exclusion on efficiency is mixed and may depend on parameterization; in this setting, net welfare effects are negligible.
