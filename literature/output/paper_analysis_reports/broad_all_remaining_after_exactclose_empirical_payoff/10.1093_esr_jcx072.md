## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Using transaction data from the Silk Road 1.0 cryptomarket, the study shows that sellers with more positive reputation (five-star ratings) can increase their prices and sell items more quickly, while negative ratings have the opposite effect. The effect sizes are economically meaningful: a 10-fold increase in five-star ratings increases item price by 6.8% and sales per day by 14.7%, while a 3-fold increase in non-five-star ratings decreases price by 4.7% and sales per day by 18.7%. The results support the hypothesis that reputation systems can substitute for formal legal enforcement in promoting cooperative, mutually beneficial exchange.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that reputation mechanisms can increase efficiency and cooperation in market-like environments even when formal punishment or legal sanctions are absent. While the setting is not a public goods game or direct variant, the findings are relevant for predicting efficiency in environments where informal reputation-based incentives substitute for formal punishment. The results suggest that, in the absence of formal punishment, well-designed reputation systems can yield higher efficiency and market success. However, the paper does not directly manipulate or analyze punishment, so its predictive value is indirect and most relevant for settings where reputation is the main incentive mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and does not manipulate punishment, reward, or other institutional features, so causal inference is limited.
- The setting is a cryptomarket for illegal drugs, which differs in important ways from laboratory public goods games or controlled experiments.
- No direct measure of group efficiency or total welfare; outcomes are item price and sales per day, interpreted as proxies for market success and cooperation.
- No analysis of group size, round structure, or other key public goods game dimensions.
- The default five-star rating may inflate reputation scores, potentially biasing results.
- The study does not analyze the cost or technology of giving ratings (reward or punishment), nor does it examine the effect of communication or identity disclosure.
