# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Politeness and Compassion Differentially Predict Adherence to Fairness Norms and Interventions to Norm Violations in Economic Games
Authors: Zhao, K., Ferguson, E., & Smillie, L. D.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount spent on third-party punishment
- amount spent on third-party recompensation
- dictator allocations
- correlations between personality traits and game behaviors
- frequency of punishment/recompensation
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral outcomes (punishment, recompensation, dictator allocations) and their relation to personality traits. It does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus. The only monetary outcomes are individual-level allocations and expenditures, not group-level efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that politeness and honesty-humility predict fair allocations in the dictator game, while compassion predicts third-party recompensation (helping the victim) and, to a lesser extent, third-party punishment (punishing the offender). Most third-party punishers are 'prosocial' (they also behave fairly as dictators), and both punishment and recompensation increase with the degree of unfairness observed. However, the study does not analyze or report group efficiency, total welfare, or surplus, and the game structure is not a public goods game but a dictator/third-party punishment/recompensation design.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it offers insight into the psychological predictors of punishment and recompensation behaviors in third-party games, it does not report group-level efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze public goods game design dimensions such as MPCR, player count, or rounds. Its findings are only indirectly relevant for understanding the motivations behind punishment and helping behaviors, not their impact on group efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games involve three players: dictator (A), recipient (B), and third-party (C).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant made decisions for a single round per game (dictator game and one third-party game). The third-party games used the strategy method, so participants indicated their response for each possible dictator transfer, but only one scenario was payoff-relevant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment/recompensation choices were continuous in integer increments (not all-or-nothing). Dictator could allocate 0-5 points (out of 10) in one-point increments; third-party could spend 0-5 points (out of 5) in one-point increments.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a public goods game, but the punishment/recompensation multiplier is 3: each point spent by the third party reduces (punishment) or increases (recompensation) the target's payoff by 3 points.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 point to reduce the dictator's payoff by 3 points (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point spent reduces the target's payoff by 3 points).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. A reward/recompensation mechanism exists in the third-party recompensation game: third party can spend points to increase the recipient's payoff.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder pays 1 point to increase the recipient's payoff by 3 points (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (each point spent increases the recipient's payoff by 3 points).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game structure and that each decision was a one-shot or single-round event.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party observers saw the dictator's allocation to the recipient before making their own decision. No further summary information is described.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All interactions were anonymous; players did not know the identity of the punisher or rewarder.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or a close variant; it uses dictator and third-party punishment/recompensation games.
- No group-level efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- The findings are about individual behavioral responses (punishment, recompensation) and their psychological predictors, not about the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
- No variation or analysis of key public goods game design dimensions such as MPCR, player count (beyond 3), or repeated rounds.
- The sample is from MTurk and may not generalize to other populations.
- The strategy method is used for third-party decisions, which may differ from direct-response methods.
