# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Propensities to engage in and punish corrupt behavior: Experimental evidence from Australia, India, Indonesia and Singapore
Authors: Cameron, L., Chaudhuri, A., Erkal, N., & Gangadharan, L.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- propensity to offer bribes
- propensity to accept bribes
- propensity to punish corrupt behavior
- amount of bribe offered
- amount of punishment assigned
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: rates of bribery, acceptance, and punishment, and the amounts chosen. The paper distinguishes between welfare-enhancing and welfare-reducing bribe treatments, but does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as a primary outcome. Some discussion of welfare is present in the design, but not as a measured outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the propensity to punish corrupt behavior varies more across cultures than the propensity to engage in corruption. Indian subjects are most likely to offer and accept bribes and least likely to punish, while Indonesian subjects are least likely to engage in corruption and most likely to punish. The manipulation of the bribe's welfare impact (welfare-enhancing vs. welfare-reducing) only significantly affects behavior in Australia, where higher harm reduces punishment rates and increases bribery. In other countries, this manipulation has little effect. The main outcomes are behavioral (bribery and punishment rates/amounts), not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence on how punishment opportunities and the cost/harm structure of norm violations affect the willingness to punish and engage in norm-violating behavior in a corruption context. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs: it is a three-player, one-shot, sequential-move game with endogenous costly punishment, but not a repeated or group-based public goods game. The main value for prediction is in understanding that punishment rates and their deterrent effect are highly culture-dependent and may not always translate into higher efficiency, especially when punishment is costly and the harm from norm violation is high. The manipulation of the harm from corruption (welfare-enhancing vs. welfare-reducing) shows that increasing the harm can sometimes reduce punishment and increase norm violation, due to the increased cost to the victim. This is relevant for predicting when punishment will be effective in improving efficiency, but the lack of direct efficiency data limits quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is always played with three players: firm, official, citizen.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is strictly one-shot; each subject plays only once.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions are made individually and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (bribe) and punishment choices are continuous within a range, not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: each unit of punishment reduces the citizen's payoff by 1.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of punishment reduces the firm and official's payoff by 3 units (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot; subjects know this in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Citizens observe the choices of the firm and official before deciding on punishment. No mention of additional summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is not anonymous within the trio; the punished know who punished them (the citizen).

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a public goods game or a repeated game; it is a one-shot, three-player corruption game, so findings may not generalize to standard PGGs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported as dependent variables; only behavioral outcomes (bribery and punishment rates/amounts) are measured.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff cannot be directly inferred from the reported results.
- Cultural and institutional context may moderate punishment and corruption behavior in ways not captured by game design dimensions alone.
- The use of loaded language ('bribe', 'punishment') may affect external validity relative to more neutral or abstract experimental designs.
