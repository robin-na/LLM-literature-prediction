## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional punishment can evolve and be sustained when leaders (kings) can punish subjects for low tribute, especially when subjects are intelligent (accurate learners) and update their strategies quickly relative to kings. Strong punishment leads to high tribute payments but reduces subject payoffs and overall efficiency, except in the idealized case where punishment is never actually used. The model predicts that confusion or slow learning among subjects can collectively benefit them by undermining the effectiveness of coercive punishment. Increased global awareness (subjects learning from other kingdoms) destabilizes coercion and can collapse institutional punishment. The model is robust to changes in population size and punishment strength bounds.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment, when used coercively in asymmetric settings, can increase compliance (tribute payment) but generally reduces group efficiency and aggregate payoffs compared to non-punishment or noncoercive equilibria. For prediction tasks, the model suggests that enabling punishment in similar asymmetric games will likely decrease efficiency unless compliance is perfect and punishment is never actually used. The effects are moderated by learning dynamics (intelligence, update speed) and the degree of information subjects have about other groups. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is imposed by a central authority and the 'public good' is tribute to that authority, rather than a symmetric PGG.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model uses n=10 or n=100 subjects per kingdom, and N=10 kingdoms. Population size is varied and shown to have little effect on qualitative results.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Model is iterated over many rounds (e.g., 10^6 or 10^7 strategy updates), representing ongoing evolutionary dynamics.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (tribute) is continuous: subjects choose p in [0,1].
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: king pays s*(1-p) to inflict same cost on subject. Cost per unit punishment is 1:1.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 1:1 (cost to king = cost to subject). Strength of punishment (s) is varied.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Global awareness (subjects learning from other kingdoms) is explicitly modeled and shown to undermine coercive punishment and increase subject payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is always imposed by the king; identity is always known (asymmetric, institutional punishment).

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is an asymmetric tribute game with a single punisher (king) and multiple subjects.
- No provision of public goods or group benefit; tribute is transferred to the king, not multiplied or shared.
- No possibility of rebellion, revolution, or removal of the king is modeled.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanisms are considered.
- Assumes perfect knowledge of payoffs and strategies except where learning errors are explicitly modeled.
- Does not model communication, negotiation, or endogenous institution formation.
