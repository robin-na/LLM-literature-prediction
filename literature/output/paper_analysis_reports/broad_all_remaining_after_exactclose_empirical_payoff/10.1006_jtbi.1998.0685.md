## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the Assessor strategy, which incorporates a punishment-like mechanism (reprobation) based on observed or reported selfish behavior, achieves higher fitness (i.e., greater group efficiency or payoff) than standard reciprocal strategies like Tit-for-Tat or Observer Tit-for-Tat when competing against always-defect strategies. This advantage is robust across both unstructured and family-structured populations, provided certain thresholds of cooperation frequency are met. The Assessor strategy is especially effective when individuals can observe or be informed about others' reprobation of selfish behavior, allowing them to withhold cooperation from defectors more efficiently. The model predicts that such punishment-like mechanisms can support higher levels of cooperation and group payoff than reciprocity alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment or sanctioning mechanisms (here, reprobation) in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency/payoff relative to control conditions without such mechanisms. The findings are most directly relevant to iterated prisoner's dilemma settings, which are adjacent to public goods games, and suggest that the ability to observe and act on others' punishment signals is a key moderator. For prediction tasks, the results imply that, all else equal, introducing a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, especially when players can observe or learn about others' punishment actions. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide quantitative effect sizes or empirical validation.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model is based on pairwise iterated prisoner's dilemma, not multi-player public goods games, so direct quantitative transfer to PGG settings may be limited.
- Punishment is modeled as reprobation (withholding cooperation) rather than explicit costly punishment, so the cost structure may differ from standard PGG punishment treatments.
- No quantitative effect sizes or predictions for real-world or laboratory settings are provided.
- The model assumes perfect or near-perfect information transmission and memory, which may not hold in practice.
