# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How many bad apples does it take to spoil the whole barrel?: Social exclusion and toleration for bad apples
Authors: Kerr, N. L., Rumble, A. C., Park, E. S., Ouwerkerk, J. W., Parks, C. D., Gallucci, M., & van Lange, P. A. M.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to group account
- cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable in both experiments is the level of cooperation or contribution to the public good. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported, though the structure of the game allows inference about these outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games, the presence of even a single 'bad apple' (defector) can sharply reduce cooperation among other group members when there is no threat of social exclusion. However, when group members can be excluded for uncooperative behavior (i.e., there is a credible threat of social exclusion), participants are much less likely to follow the bad apple's example, and cooperation remains high unless a majority of the group are defectors. This deterrent effect of exclusion is strong in small groups (4-5 people) but disappears in larger groups (8 people), where exclusion threats do not increase cooperation. The dependent variable is contribution/cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff, but the findings imply that exclusion threats would increase group efficiency in small groups.

## Prediction Guidance
For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling a social exclusion mechanism (a form of non-monetary punishment) in small public goods games (4-5 players) increases cooperation and would likely increase efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is degraded by the presence of defectors. However, the effect of exclusion threats disappears in larger groups (8 players), suggesting that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment via exclusion is moderated by group size. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so predictions must be inferred from cooperation rates. The exclusion mechanism studied is not monetary punishment but social ostracism, so results may not generalize to all punishment technologies.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Experiment 1 uses 5-person groups; Experiment 2 compares 4-person and 8-person groups. The effect of exclusion threat is strong in small groups but absent in larger groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: single round (with expectation of multiple rounds, but only one decision measured). Experiment 2: 40 rounds, number of rounds not disclosed in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Experiment 1, high exclusion threat condition includes a group discussion (like in a chat room) about the investment game and everyone's decisions. No chat in low threat or in Experiment 2.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1 and 2 use continuous contribution public goods games (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: group account is doubled and divided among 5 (MPCR = 0.4). Experiment 2: group account is doubled in 4-person groups (MPCR = 0.5), multiplied by 4 in 8-person groups (MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is via social exclusion (ostracism), not monetary. The threat of exclusion is effective in small groups but not in large groups.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: participants led to believe there would be multiple rounds, but only one measured. Experiment 2: number of rounds (40) not disclosed in advance to avoid end-play effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the allocation decisions of other group members (preprogrammed feedback) in both experiments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In high exclusion threat conditions, participants' names and decisions are visible to the group, and exclusion is by group vote. In low threat, choices are anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is cooperation/contribution, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency effects must be inferred.
- Punishment is implemented as social exclusion (ostracism), not monetary punishment; results may not generalize to other punishment forms.
- The effect of exclusion threats disappears in larger groups (8 people), limiting generalizability to large-scale public goods problems.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings.
- Experiments use preprogrammed feedback and deception about group composition, which may affect external validity.
- Participants are university students in lab settings; real-world generalizability may be limited.
