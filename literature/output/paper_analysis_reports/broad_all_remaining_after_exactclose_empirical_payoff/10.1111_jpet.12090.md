## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the long-run efficiency of a team trust game with coordinated punishment depends critically on the institutional parameters of punishment: the effectiveness of coordinated punishment (damage inflicted if enough punishers act), the cost of punishment, and the strength of peer punishment (peer pressure on non-punishers). If the effectiveness of coordinated punishment is high enough (damage parameter λ ≥ 0.5) and peer pressure is strong (peer punishment cost to defectors is high relative to cost of coordinated punishment), then the only stable long-run outcome is a fully cooperative, efficient culture with no observed punishment (because the threat is credible). If these conditions are not met, the system converges to a non-cooperative, inefficient culture. The model also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment, lowering its cost, or increasing peer pressure all make the efficient outcome more likely. The results are robust to the dynamics of preference transmission, and the model provides explicit boundaries for the basins of attraction of efficient vs inefficient cultures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling effective, coordinated punishment (with sufficient effectiveness and/or low enough cost) and strong peer punishment will increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, even when the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the mechanisms (threshold punishment, peer punishment, endogenous preference evolution) are highly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The explicit parameter boundaries (e.g., λ ≥ 0.5 for effectiveness, peer punishment cost vs coordinated punishment cost) can inform quantitative predictions about when punishment will be effective. The model also highlights that if punishment is not sufficiently effective or peer pressure is weak, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency. The results are most directly applicable to trust/investment games with team-based punishment, but the logic is transferable to PGG variants with threshold or coordinated punishment.

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost per punisher is z if only one punishes (ineffective), z/2 if both punish (effective). Lower cost of coordinated punishment increases efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- The game is a team trust/investment game, not a standard public goods game, though it is closely related.
- Punishment is modeled as coordinated (threshold) punishment, which may differ from individual punishment in standard PGGs.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) investment decisions, not continuous contributions.
- Preference evolution is modeled via cultural transmission, which may not capture all real-world dynamics.
- No communication or information feedback is modeled.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment and peer punishment are considered.
