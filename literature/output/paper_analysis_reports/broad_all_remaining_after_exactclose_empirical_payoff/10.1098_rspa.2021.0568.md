## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in finite-population PGGs, the expected total cost to an institution of achieving a minimum cooperation level via punishment or reward can be exactly calculated and optimized. The cost efficiency of punishment versus reward depends on the required cooperation threshold: for low cooperation targets, reward is more cost-efficient; for high targets, punishment is more cost-efficient, with the threshold typically around 50% cooperation. The cost functions exhibit phase transitions as the intensity of selection changes. The analysis provides explicit formulas and algorithms for determining the optimal incentive size and minimal institutional cost, given the game parameters, population size, and selection intensity. The results are validated by numerical simulations and apply to both the PGG and the donation game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, theoretically grounded formulas for predicting the efficiency (cost-effectiveness) of institutional punishment (and reward) in finite-population PGGs, as a function of player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, selection intensity, and the required cooperation level. It shows that the efficiency of punishment depends critically on the required cooperation threshold and the selection intensity. For prediction tasks, the paper enables calculation of the minimal institutional cost (and thus efficiency) for any specified game design, provided the relevant parameters are known. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the institution can set the punishment/reward level to achieve a target cooperation rate, and where the main outcome of interest is the total cost to the institution (i.e., efficiency in terms of budget spent per unit of cooperation achieved). The findings are less directly applicable to settings where individual payoffs or group welfare (rather than institutional cost) are the main outcome, but the cost formulas can be mapped to group efficiency if the institutional cost is subtracted from total group payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes a well-mixed, finite population and one-shot (non-repeated) games; repeated interactions are not considered.
- Only binary (all-or-nothing) contribution choices are modeled; continuous contribution levels are not analyzed.
- Institutional incentives are assumed to have fixed efficiency (cost equals impact, a = b = 1); more complex or variable efficiency is not considered.
- The analysis assumes the small mutation limit for tractability, which may not capture all real-world evolutionary dynamics.
- The model does not address who pays for the institutional incentives or the sustainability of the institutional budget.
- No communication, feedback, or identification of punishers/rewarders is modeled.
- The main outcome is institutional cost to achieve a cooperation target, not group welfare or individual payoffs per se.
