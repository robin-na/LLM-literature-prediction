# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Incentivizing cooperation against a norm of defection: Experimental Evidence from Egypt
Authors: Hassan, M., Amin, E., Mansour, S., & Voigt, S.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution (points per round)
- change in contribution after receiving/sending messages
- frequency/type of messages sent
- self-reported emotions (pride, shame)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is individual contribution to the public good. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare is reported. Some summary statistics (means, medians) of contributions are provided, but efficiency or group payoff is not explicitly calculated or compared between conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that (1) pride primes can increase high contributors' willingness to contribute, but with a lagged effect, and (2) appeal messages (religious, patriotic, social harm, or custom) sent by high contributors to low contributors increase the recipients' subsequent contributions by 2–5 points on average. However, most high contributors choose not to send appeals, and there is no direct monetary punishment or reward. The study does not report group efficiency or total group payoff, so the effect of these interventions on efficiency is not directly assessed. The interventions are non-monetary and adjacent to punishment, as they are costly to the sender but do not reduce the recipient's payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that non-monetary, costly appeals can increase individual contributions in a public goods game, especially in a context with a strong norm of defection. However, because the interventions do not reduce the recipient's payoff and group efficiency is not reported, the findings are only indirectly relevant for predicting the effect of monetary punishment on efficiency. The design is most informative for settings where costly communication or social sanctions (not payoff-reducing punishment) are available. The results suggest that such appeals can increase contributions, but the effect on efficiency remains unquantified. For prediction tasks focused on monetary punishment, this paper provides only adjacent evidence.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is unannounced; after round 8, each round has a 30% chance of being the last. Actual sessions ranged from 9 to 11 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No free-form chat; in some treatments, high contributors can send pre-defined or custom appeal messages to low contributors at a cost.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many of 20 points to contribute each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sending an appeal message costs the sender 1 point; there is no monetary punishment for the recipient.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Appeal messages are costly to the sender (1 point) but have no direct payoff effect on the recipient; they are not monetary punishment.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No monetary reward mechanism is present; only non-monetary appeals and pride primes.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is not announced in advance; after round 8, each round has a 30% chance of being the last.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are shown their own income and the total group contribution for the round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Appeal messages are sent from specific high contributors to specific low contributors, but the identity of the sender is not revealed; messages are not anonymous, but sender identity is not made explicit.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare; only individual contributions are analyzed.
- No monetary punishment or reward mechanisms; interventions are non-monetary and adjacent to punishment.
- Sample is limited to Egyptian university students, which may limit generalizability.
- Number of rounds is variable and not known to participants, which may affect behavior.
- Small sample sizes in some treatments.
- Emotions (pride, shame) are measured only at the end of the experiment, not per round.
- No long-term or post-experiment follow-up to assess durability of effects.
