## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, contrary to the predictions of standard economic theory, groups can achieve higher efficiency and avoid the tragedy of the commons when allowed to communicate or implement costly punishment. Both lab and field experiments show that these mechanisms increase group earnings and reduce overuse of resources. The review also notes that centralized sanctioning mechanisms may be more effective and sustainable than peer punishment. However, the effectiveness of punishment and other interventions is context-specific, depending on factors such as group size, communication, trust, and cultural background. The paper also discusses the phenomenon of 'crowding out,' where externally imposed rules can sometimes reduce performance compared to voluntary agreements.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments generally increases group efficiency or earnings, especially when compared to baseline games without punishment or communication. It also highlights that the effect of punishment is moderated by contextual factors such as group size, communication, and the nature of the sanctioning institution (peer vs. centralized). While the review does not provide quantitative estimates, it offers strong qualitative evidence that punishment mechanisms, when available, tend to improve efficiency outcomes, but the magnitude and sustainability of these effects depend on the specific game design and social context.

## Important Limitations

- Quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff effects are not provided; the evidence is qualitative.
- Contextual moderators (e.g., group size, culture, ecological complexity) are discussed but not systematically analyzed or quantified.
