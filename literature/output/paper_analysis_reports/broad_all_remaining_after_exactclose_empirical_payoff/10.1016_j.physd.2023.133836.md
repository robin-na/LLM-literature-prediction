## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a collective risk dilemma (CRD) model, local exclusion (ostracism) is more effective than local punishment in promoting group achievement (i.e., the fraction of groups that reach the collective target), especially at low risk levels. Local sanctioning institutions (both punishment and exclusion) are more effective than global ones. The advantage of exclusion over punishment is most pronounced when the risk of collective failure is low. When risk is high, both exclusion and punishment lead to high group achievement. The results are based on theoretical analysis and simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not on direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff, but group achievement is a closely related outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in threshold public goods environments with risk of collective loss (CRD), enabling local punishment or exclusion mechanisms increases group achievement (a proxy for efficiency) compared to no sanctions, with local exclusion (ostracism) being especially effective at low risk. The results suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment (especially local, exclusionary forms) is likely to increase efficiency or group success, particularly when the risk of failure is not too high. However, the findings are based on group achievement rather than explicit efficiency ratios, and are derived from theoretical models rather than empirical data. The model parameters (e.g., group size, cost of exclusion, risk level) are specified, allowing some mapping to prediction dimensions.

- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Exclusion (ostracism) is modeled as confiscation of all endowment of defectors if threshold is met; punishment is a cost imposed. Exclusion is found to be more effective than punishment in promoting group achievement.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main outcome is group achievement (fraction of groups reaching the target), not explicit efficiency or total group payoff.
- The model uses a threshold public goods structure (CRD), which differs from standard linear PGGs.
- Punishment and exclusion are implemented as institutional (not peer-to-peer) mechanisms.
- No analysis of the effect of varying group size, cost parameters, or other dimensions beyond the main parameter set.
- No consideration of communication, repeated rounds, or information feedback as in many experimental PGGs.
- Assumes perfect exclusion (ostracism never fails), which may not generalize to all real-world settings.
