# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Mill ownership and farmer's cooperative behavior: the case of Costa Rica coffee farmers
Authors: Hopfensitz, A., & Miquel-Florensa, J.
Journal: Journal of Institutional Economics
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- willingness to implement punishment mechanism
- effect of punishment on contribution
- side selling behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good (out of 10 units). No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported, though the structure allows inference about efficiency. Some discussion of real-world side selling as a behavioral correlate.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that, in a public good game, Costa Rican coffee farmers selling to cooperatives do not contribute more than those selling to private mills when paired with their own group. However, cooperative farmers contribute significantly less when paired with private mill farmers, while private mill farmers' contributions are unaffected by partner background. When a punishment mechanism (penalizing lowest contributors, with partial redistribution to the group) is available, contributions by cooperative farmers increase substantially, especially when paired with private mill farmers (over 100% increase in contribution). The effect of punishment on private mill farmers is negligible. The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff, but the structure of the game allows inference that group welfare would increase with higher contributions under punishment, especially for cooperative farmers in mixed groups.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism in a public good game can substantially increase contributions, especially among cooperative farmers interacting with out-group partners. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the effect on efficiency must be inferred from the increase in contributions. The punishment mechanism is costly (1 unit per participant), and only a fraction of the fine is redistributed, so efficiency gains may be less than the increase in contributions. The design is a one-shot or single-decision public good game with 4 players, MPCR 0.375, and a punishment mechanism that is not peer-to-peer but an impartial rule penalizing the lowest contributor. The findings are most informative for predicting contribution changes (and thus likely efficiency changes) when punishment is enabled in similar settings, especially for groups with strong in-group/out-group distinctions. The lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation for precise prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each public good game is played with 4 players (participant plus 3 others, whose strategies are drawn from previous sessions).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each decision is a one-shot (single round) public good game; participants make several independent decisions with different partner backgrounds and punishment conditions, but each is a single decision without feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; decisions are made individually and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 10 units (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.375 (each unit contributed is multiplied by 1.5 and divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment mechanism costs 1 unit per participant to activate; the penalty for lowest contributors is not precisely specified but is proportional to deviation from group average, with 1/4 of collected fines redistributed to the group.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is an impartial rule: those contributing less than the group average lose some units, with 1/4 of fines added to the public good. The exact penalty per unit deviation is ambiguous to participants.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is implemented. Some of the punishment fine is redistributed to the group, but there is no explicit reward for high contributors.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know each decision is a single-shot game; the structure is explained in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No feedback or summary information about others' contributions is given between games; all decisions are made without feedback.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented by an impartial rule, not by individuals; no identification of who is punished or who triggers punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency effects must be inferred.
- The punishment mechanism is not peer-to-peer but an impartial rule penalizing the lowest contributor, which may not generalize to other punishment designs.
- The cost and effectiveness of punishment are not precisely specified to participants, and only a fraction of the fine is redistributed.
- Participants self-select into the punishment mechanism, which may affect generalizability.
- The sample is not randomly assigned to cooperative or private mill groups, raising selection concerns.
- The study uses hypothetical partner strategies and a questionnaire format due to logistical constraints, not a fully interactive real-time experiment.
