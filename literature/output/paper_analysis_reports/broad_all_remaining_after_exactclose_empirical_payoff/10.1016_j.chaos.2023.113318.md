## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that external incentives (subsidies/carbon credits) can promote cooperation and improve environmental quality in a spatial public goods game with environmental feedback, but the effect is not always positive. Higher subsidies increase the competitiveness of cooperators (both those who reinvest in the public good and those who use credits for personal gain), but can also create conditions where defectors survive by exploiting improved environments, leading to coexistence or even dominance of defectors. The use of incentives to promote cooperation is thus sensitive to the balance between environmental repair and degradation rates, the structure of incentives, and the spatial dynamics of strategy clusters. The model does not include punishment or sanctions in the standard sense, but the 'second-order social dilemma' (where some cooperators act as free-riders on others' altruism) is analogous to the problem of sustaining costly punishment. The main outcomes are cooperation rates and environmental quality, with payoffs determined by these variables.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of external incentives (akin to rewards) on cooperation and group welfare in spatial public goods games with environmental feedback. It suggests that the effect of such mechanisms on efficiency or payoff is highly context-dependent: increasing the incentive can improve cooperation and welfare in some parameter regions, but can also reduce them in others due to complex spatial and strategic dynamics. The findings caution against assuming monotonic positive effects of incentives and highlight the importance of considering environmental feedbacks and the possibility of second-order free-riding. However, the model does not include punishment or sanctions as defined in the prediction task, so its direct applicability to punishment effects is limited. The results are most relevant for reward-based or subsidy-based interventions in spatial, environmentally-coupled PGGs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment or sanctioning mechanisms are not modeled; only external rewards/subsidies are considered.
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as the ratio of group payoff to the fully cooperative benchmark; outcomes are mainly cooperation rates and environmental quality.
- The model assumes a spatially structured population (square lattice) and may not generalize to well-mixed or other network structures.
- Individual heterogeneity, communication, and more realistic behavioral factors are not included.
- The model's findings are specific to environmental feedback PGGs with external rewards, not standard PGGs with punishment.
