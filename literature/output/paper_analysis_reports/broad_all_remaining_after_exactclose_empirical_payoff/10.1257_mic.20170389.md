# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Trust the Police? Self-Selection of Motivated Agents into the German Police Force
Authors: Friebel, G., Kosfeld, M., & Thielmann, G.
Journal: American Economic Journal-microeconomics
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trust (amount sent in trust game)
- trustworthiness (amount returned in trust game)
- investment in reward and punishment as third party
- norm enforcement type (reward, punish, both, antisocial, none)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: trust, trustworthiness, and willingness to invest in costly reward and punishment as a third party. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Police applicants in Germany are more trustworthy (return more in the trust game) and invest more in costly third-party punishment and reward than comparable high school students. The difference in trust is explained by risk preferences, but the difference in trustworthiness and norm enforcement remains significant after controlling for covariates. The willingness to punish and reward is measured as the average amount invested in these actions as a third party in a trust-game-based scenario. The study provides evidence for positive self-selection of intrinsically motivated, norm-enforcing individuals into the police force. However, the study does not report on group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs, and thus does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that individuals who self-select into enforcement roles (police applicants) are more willing to engage in costly punishment and reward to enforce cooperation norms. However, the experimental design is not a public goods game or a repeated social dilemma, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding the motivation to punish and enforce norms, but do not provide direct evidence on how punishment affects efficiency or group payoffs in public-goods-game-like environments. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used as indirect evidence about the types of individuals who may be more likely to use punishment, rather than as evidence about the efficiency consequences of punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust game: 2 players (A and B); reward-and-punishment game: 3 players (A, B, C).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both games are one-shot (single round), not repeated.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/transfer choices are discrete: A can send €0, €50, or €100; B can return €0 or a fixed amount depending on A's transfer.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Transfer from A is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is not a standard PGG; marginal per-capita return is not directly comparable.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment (or reward) point costs the punisher (player C) €1 and changes the target's payoff by €2.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each €1 spent reduces the target's payoff by €2.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available: player C can allocate positive points (reward) as well as negative points (punishment).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward point costs the rewarder (player C) €1 and increases the target's payoff by €2.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: each €1 spent increases the target's payoff by €2.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the relevant choices of others for their own decisions (e.g., player C sees A and B's choices), but no summary statistics or history is shown.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All interactions are anonymous; players do not know the identity of those who punish or reward them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or repeated social dilemma; it uses a trust game and a third-party punishment/reward game.
- No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (trust, trustworthiness, norm enforcement), not efficiency or group payoff.
- The study compares two subject pools (police applicants vs. high school students) rather than manipulating punishment as a treatment within a game.
- Findings may not generalize to public-goods-game-like environments or to the effect of punishment on efficiency.
