# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Second-order beliefs in reputation systems with endogenous evaluations - an experimental study
Authors: Greiff, M., & Paetzel, F.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- mean contribution
- payoff matrix and average earnings reported
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates
- evaluation (reputation) scores
- conditional cooperation
- reaction to partner's and own evaluation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution (behavioral), not efficiency or group payoff. Payoff structure is described and average earnings are reported, but efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that in a repeated two-player public good game, providing information about both one's own and the partner's evaluation (reputation) increases contributions compared to control and to the treatment where only the partner's evaluation is shown. When only the partner's evaluation is available, contributions do not increase significantly relative to control. The effect is explained by the role of second-order beliefs in conditional cooperation. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but higher contributions in the BOTH-INFO treatment imply higher group payoffs. There is no punishment or costly sanctioning; evaluations are costless, non-binding signals.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is informative for predicting the effect of reputation-based information (not costly punishment) on contribution rates in repeated two-player public good games. It suggests that simply providing information about others' evaluations is insufficient to increase cooperation, but providing both own and partner's evaluation can increase contributions. However, because the study does not include a costly punishment mechanism, its direct relevance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for settings where social image or reputation feedback (not material punishment) is the main intervention. For prediction tasks involving costly punishment, this paper provides only adjacent evidence.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (groups of 2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 periods (rounds) in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: 0, 1, 2, or 3 (not all-or-nothing, but not continuous).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contributed unit yields 3 to the group (1.5 per person), so MPCR = 1.5.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see their partner's contribution and evaluation after each round.

## Important Limitations
- No costly punishment or material sanctions; evaluations are costless and do not affect payoffs directly.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed as an outcome; only contribution rates are reported.
- Only two-player groups; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- No analysis of how evaluation information interacts with actual payoff-based punishment or reward mechanisms.
- The reputation system is noisy and endogenous, which may differ from standard public goods game punishment designs.
