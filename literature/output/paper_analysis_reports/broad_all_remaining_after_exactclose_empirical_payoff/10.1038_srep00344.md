Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the sustainability of the punishment institution, operationalized as the punishment pool balance (Σ), which is a direct function of group payoffs and costs. The paper also reports on the stationary fractions of each strategy, which are related to but not the same as efficiency. Contribution rates are not the primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. If second-order free-riders are not punished sufficiently, the punishment institution cannot be sustained, and efficiency remains low. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player interacts with k=4 neighbors on a square lattice, so each group has 5 players. The lattice size varies (L=200 to 1600), but the local group size is always 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is run for up to 10^6 full Monte Carlo steps to reach stationary state; effectively infinite/steady-state rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each cooperator or punisher contributes 1, defectors contribute 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Synergy factor r > 1 is varied; MPCR = r/(k+1) = r/5. Higher r (MPCR) makes cooperation and sustainable punishment more likely.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishers pay cost β=1.0 per round to the punishment pool. The fine γ (imposed on defectors) and δγ (on second-order free-riders) are varied. The cost to punishers is fixed, but the effectiveness (fine) is varied.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the fine γ per unit cost β. The ratio γ/β is a key parameter; higher γ (for fixed β) increases the likelihood of sustainable punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The study is a theoretical/modeling paper, not an empirical experiment; results may not fully generalize to real human behavior.
- The model assumes unconditional, population-wide (institutional) punishment, which may not capture all features of real-world or experimental peer punishment.
- The spatial structure (square lattice) may not reflect all relevant social network structures.
- No communication, reputation, or reward mechanisms are included.
- The model assumes infinite or very long time horizons (steady-state), which may differ from finite-round experimental settings.
- The discontinuous phase transition and strict elimination of second-order free-riders may be an artifact of the model's assumptions.
