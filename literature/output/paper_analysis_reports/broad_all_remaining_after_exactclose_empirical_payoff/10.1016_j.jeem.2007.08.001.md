## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The analysis shows that the threat of the harshest possible group punishment (worst perfect equilibrium) is sufficient to deter defection, even when individual actions are not observable. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes unobservable individual actions and only aggregate outcomes are observable, which may not match all experimental or real-world settings.
- Punishment is symmetric and group-based; the model does not address individual-targeted punishment or settings where the defector can be identified.
- No communication, reward, or other institutional features are modeled.
- The model assumes continuous (not all-or-nothing) contribution/harvest choices.
- The results depend on the ability to implement sufficiently harsh (costly) group punishment, which may not be feasible or credible in all real-world settings.
- The model assumes deterministic resource dynamics and perfect observation of aggregate stock; stochasticity and measurement error are not considered.
