# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Watching Eyes Triggers Third-Party Punishment: The Role of Emotion Within the Eyes
Authors: Li, M. P., Shangguan, C. Y., Shi, H. Q., & Lu, J. M.
Journal: Frontiers in Psychology
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of third-party punishment decisions
- effect of eye cues (watching eyes) on punishment
- effect of emotional valence of eye cues (positive/negative) on punishment
- gender differences in punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency/probability of third-party punishment decisions. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. Participants' monetary payoffs were not actually linked to their decisions, as all received a fixed payment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that displaying images of eyes (vs. control images) increases the frequency of third-party punishment in a modified dictator game, and that negative (angry) eyes further increase punishment compared to positive (happy) eyes. The effect is robust across varying degrees of unfairness in the dictator's offer. The outcome measured is the rate of punishment decisions, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that social cues of observation and negative evaluation can increase norm enforcement via punishment, but do not address the impact on group efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that social cues (watching eyes) and their emotional valence can increase the frequency of third-party punishment in a dictator game context. However, it does not provide data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it uses a third-party dictator game with costly punishment, but the outcome is behavioral (punishment frequency) rather than payoff-based. The findings may be useful for understanding psychological moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three roles: Proposer (A), Recipient (B), and Third Party (C). Only the third party is a real participant; A and B are virtual. The effective player count per group is 3, but only one real decision-maker per trial.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 72 trials per participant (2 blocks of 36); Experiment 2: 300 trials per participant (5 blocks of 60).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party's punishment choice is binary: punish (pay 15 coins, dictator loses 45) or not punish (no cost).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs the third party 15 coins per act.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment costs the punisher 15 coins and reduces the dictator's payoff by 45 coins (3x cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of blocks and trials; the structure is explicit.

## Important Limitations
- The outcome measured is the frequency of punishment, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- The third-party dictator game is adjacent to, but not the same as, a public goods game.
- Only punishment (not helping or rewarding) is available as a third-party action.
- Participants' actual monetary payoffs were not linked to their in-game decisions; all received a fixed payment.
- The emotional valence manipulation (positive/negative eyes) did not include a neutral control in Experiment 2.
- Only two emotions (happiness and anger) were tested for eye cues.
- All other players (A and B) are virtual; only the third party is a real participant.
