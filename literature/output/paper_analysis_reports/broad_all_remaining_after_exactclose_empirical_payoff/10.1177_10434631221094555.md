# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Verbal interaction in a social dilemma
Authors: Adams, Z., Ludwiczak, A., Sharma, D., & Osman, M.
Journal: Rationality and Society
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group contribution to public good (number of squeezes contributed to group pot)
- difference in group squeezes between rounds
Non-payoff outcomes:
- consensus-building language in chat
- alignment scores in chat
- register and stancetaking in communication
- promises, questions, imperatives, comments in chat
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the change in group contributions to the public good between rounds, which is a behavioral measure of cooperation, not a direct measure of efficiency or group payoff. The study does not report efficiency (payoff relative to maximum possible) or total group earnings, but does report group contributions and their change after chat. The focus is on how the quality and style of communication (consensus, alignment, register) affect subsequent cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the quality of communication—specifically, consensus-building language and alignment in chat—significantly predicts increases in cooperative behavior in a repeated public goods game. Groups with higher consensus scores in chat showed larger increases in contributions to the group pot in the next round. The analysis also shows that certain registers (gameshow, familial, socialist) are associated with more successful cooperation, while business and political registers are associated with less success. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that allowing chat/communication between rounds in a public goods game can increase cooperation, but the effect depends on the quality and style of the communication. However, it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is only indirectly informative: it supports the importance of communication (chat) as a moderator of cooperation, but does not address punishment or efficiency directly. It is most useful for understanding the role of chat/communication as a game design dimension.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. There were 2 rounds of the public goods game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. All groups had an online chat between rounds; the quality of chat (consensus, alignment) was found to increase cooperation.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group contribution was multiplied by 1.5 and divided among 5 players, so MPCR = 1.5/5 = 0.3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were two rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Depending on condition, participants saw either (1) intentions and group total, (2) actions and group total, or (3) group total only.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism was present; the study does not address the effect of punishment on efficiency or cooperation.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not reported; only group contributions (cooperation) are measured.
- The time between chat and Round 2 varied between groups (2-6 days), which may have affected the impact of chat on behavior.
- Case study approach for chat analysis may limit generalizability.
- Individual differences (e.g., personality) are not controlled for and may influence results.
- Findings about chat/communication may not generalize to settings with punishment, reward, or other institutional features.
