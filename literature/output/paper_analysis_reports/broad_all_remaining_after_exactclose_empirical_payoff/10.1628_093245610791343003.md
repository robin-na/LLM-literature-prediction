## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is built on a 'miniature social reciprocity game' with groups of 4 players, divided into two pairs for the public goods stage.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is iterated over many periods (discrete time), with random rematching each round. The number of rounds is not fixed but the process is ongoing.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to contribute all or none of their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.75 (contributions are multiplied by 1.5 and split among 2 players in each pair).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each act of punishment costs the punisher 10 EMUs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each act of punishment reduces the target's payoff by 20 EMUs.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the contribution stage, all four players are informed of all players' choices before punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: 'agents never know who had, or had not, punished whom.'

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- Findings are most directly applicable to small groups (4 players) with binary contribution and specific punishment parameters; generalization to other settings may be limited.
- The model does not consider positive rewards or reputation mechanisms.
- The model assumes random rematching and no repeated interaction within groups, which may differ from some real-world or experimental settings.
- The model does not address the effects of communication or more complex social structures.
