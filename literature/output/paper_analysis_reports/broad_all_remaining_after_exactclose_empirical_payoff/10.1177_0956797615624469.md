# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Looking Under the Hood of Third-Party Punishment Reveals Design for Personal Benefit
Authors: Krasnow, M. M., Delton, A. W., Cosmides, L., & Tooby, J.
Journal: Psychological Science
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned by third parties
- dictator allocations to recipients
- inferences about dictator valuation of others
- correlations between allocations, inferences, and punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are punishment behavior (how much punishers spend to punish), dictator allocations, and inferences about valuation. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that third-party punishment is driven by the punisher's inference that a dictator who mistreats a recipient is also likely to mistreat the punisher. When punishers have information about how they themselves are treated, this information dominates their punishment decisions, and information about third-party treatment is largely ignored. The results support a deterrence-based account of punishment motivation. The study does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-like games. Its findings are relevant for understanding the psychological motivations for punishment, especially the conditions under which punishment is likely to be deployed, but it does not address whether punishment increases or decreases group efficiency. The design is a modified dictator/third-party-punishment game, not a standard public goods game, and no efficiency or total payoff outcomes are reported. Thus, it offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task focused on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: groups of 3 (dictator, recipient, punisher); Study 2: groups of 4 (dictator, 3 punishers/recipients).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game in both studies; no repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (dictator allocation) is discrete but not all-or-nothing: multiple increments allowed (e.g., $0, $2.50, $5, $7.50, $10 in Study 1; $0.10 or $0.50 in Study 2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: Punishers spend up to $5 in $1 increments; each $1 spent reduces dictator's earnings by 20% of their allocation. Study 2: Punishers spend up to $0.50 in $0.10 increments; each $0.10 spent reduces dictator's earnings by 20%.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each $1 (Study 1) or $0.10 (Study 2) spent reduces dictator's earnings by 20% of their allocation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot; no uncertainty about number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are informed of dictator allocations to recipients; no mention of additional summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; dictators do not know the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a modified dictator/third-party-punishment game, not a public goods game or direct variant.
- No efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported or analyzed.
- The main outcomes are punishment behavior and inferences about intentions, not payoff-based outcomes.
- The game is one-shot and anonymous, which may not generalize to repeated or more interactive settings.
- Findings are about psychological motivation for punishment, not about the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
