## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that intensified institutional punishment (in the form of policing during France's state of emergency) reduced anti-social peer punishment (anti-Muslim threats and actions) but had significant anti-social side effects, such as a high proportion of innocent people subjected to house searches and arrests. The author conceptually links these dynamics to public goods game theory, distinguishing between peer and institutional punishment, and discusses the legitimacy, proportionality, and transparency of institutional punishment. However, the paper does not provide experimental or quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it analyze a public goods game or close variant directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides conceptual context for the distinction between peer and institutional punishment and discusses possible crowding out of anti-social peer punishment by institutional punishment. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or variants. It does not report on the game design dimensions or control/treatment efficiency relevant for the downstream prediction task. Its main value is in highlighting potential anti-social side effects of institutional punishment and the importance of legitimacy and proportionality, but it does not inform quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.
