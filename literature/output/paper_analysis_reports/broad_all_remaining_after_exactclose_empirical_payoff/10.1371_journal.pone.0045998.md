Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral responses (switching, preferences, attack cessation) rather than direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. The discussion links these behaviors to the potential for public goods and the effectiveness of punishment, but does not report direct payoff or efficiency outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that individual blennies differ markedly in their foraging strategies, including how they respond to punishment by victim fish. Some blennies are more likely to switch victim species after being punished, while others are not. Laboratory experiments show that punishment can reduce the likelihood of attack or cause switching, but these effects are highly individual-specific. The paper discusses how only certain patterns of blenny response to punishment (specifically, switching to another species) would generate a public good for the victim group, while other patterns would not. However, the study does not measure group efficiency or payoff directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, mechanistic insight into how punishment might or might not generate public goods in a natural system, depending on the behavioral rules of the punished party. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a way that would allow direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting that the effect of punishment on public good provision (and thus efficiency) may depend on the behavioral heterogeneity of the punished agents and the structure of the interaction (e.g., whether punishment causes switching to other species or not).

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly measure efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; all outcomes are behavioral.
- The system is a naturalistic host-parasite interaction, not a controlled public goods game; mapping to PGG dimensions is indirect.
- Sample sizes for some laboratory experiments are small (e.g., n=3 blennies in experiment 3).
- No manipulation of key game design dimensions such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost.
- No direct comparison of group outcomes with and without punishment; only individual-level behavioral responses are measured.
