The paper does not report empirical behavioral outcomes, but analyzes the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher group payoff/efficiency) is favored.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper shows that in n-player repeated prisoners' dilemma games with non-rivalrous public goods, direct reciprocity alone can support the evolution of cooperation (and thus high group efficiency), especially as group size increases. This holds even when individuals sometimes make mistakes or have imperfect information, provided they use sufficiently optimistic conditional strategies. The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not include punishment or reward mechanisms. The main outcome is the condition under which cooperative strategies are selectively favored over defection, which is directly tied to group payoff and efficiency. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated efficiency values, but gives analytic thresholds for when cooperation is favored.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public goods games with non-rivalrous benefits, increasing group size can make cooperation (and thus high efficiency) more likely, even in the presence of mistakes or imperfect information, as long as players use optimistic conditional strategies. However, the paper does not model punishment, so it does not provide direct evidence for the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. Its results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in control (no-punishment) conditions as a function of group size, information, and error, but not for the effect of punishment. The paper is useful for understanding baseline efficiency in repeated public goods games with direct reciprocity, but not for predicting the incremental effect of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size n is a key parameter; the paper analyzes how increasing n affects the evolutionary success of cooperation. For non-rivalrous goods, larger n makes cooperation more likely (less stringent conditions for cooperation to be favored).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Games are repeated with a continuation probability delta; more rounds (higher delta) make cooperation more likely. The number of rounds is not fixed but determined by a geometric distribution.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model allows both binary (all-or-nothing) and continuous contribution strategies. Some analyses focus on binary strategies (TFT_a), others allow continuous (e.g., strategy R).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The benefit-to-cost ratio (b/c) is the key parameter; higher b/c (i.e., higher MPCR) makes cooperation more likely. For non-rivalrous goods, b is not divided by n.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds is not fixed; games continue with probability delta each round (indefinite horizon). Players do not know the exact number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Information structure is a key variable: models consider both perfect and imperfect information about others' past behavior. More information (i.e., seeing others' actions) makes cooperation more likely.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment and reward mechanisms are not modeled; the paper cannot inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency.
- All results assume non-rivalrous public goods; findings do not generalize to standard rivalrous public goods games.
- Efficiency outcomes are not reported as explicit numerical values, but as analytic thresholds for when cooperation is favored.
- The model assumes infinite or large populations and weak selection; real-world dynamics may differ.
- The effect of relatedness, heterogeneity, and more complex strategies is not fully explored.
