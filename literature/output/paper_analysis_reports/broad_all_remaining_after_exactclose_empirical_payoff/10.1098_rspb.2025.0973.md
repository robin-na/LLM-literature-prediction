Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the evolutionary frequencies of different strategies (cooperation, defection, reciprocity, anti-reciprocity) in agent-based simulations across different game-theoretic environments. Payoff is used as the basis for evolutionary success, but group efficiency or total welfare is not directly reported. The focus is on the conditions under which reciprocity (which includes punishing defectors) evolves, not on group payoff or efficiency per se.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Reciprocal matching strategies, which reward cooperation and punish defection, are more likely to evolve and persist in competitive socio-ecologies where the risk of exploitation is high (e.g., Chicken/Hawk-Dove/Attack-Defend games) than in cooperative ones (Stag Hunt). In cooperative environments, unconditional cooperation dominates, while in competitive environments, anti-reciprocity (mismatching) and defection are more successful. The success of reciprocity increases with the probability of repeated interaction. The paper does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff, but the evolutionary success of reciprocity implies it is adaptive in competitive settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment (as part of reciprocal strategies) in promoting cooperation depends on the competitiveness of the environment and the risk of exploitation. While it does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, it suggests that punishment (as part of reciprocity) is more likely to be adaptive and prevalent in competitive, high-risk environments. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency may be more positive in competitive, high-exploitation-risk games (e.g., Chicken, Hawk-Dove) than in cooperative games (e.g., Stag Hunt). However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations use fixed-size populations (e.g., N=240, with robustness checks at N=60, 120, 240), but the games themselves are 2-player (dyadic) repeated games.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Number of rounds is stochastic, determined by a geometric distribution with continuation probability delta (0.6 to 0.99), so expected number of rounds is 1/(1-delta).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in all games.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Agents do not know the exact number of rounds; games end probabilistically with continuation probability delta.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses agent-based simulations and theoretical models, not empirical or experimental data.
- The games modeled are 2-player repeated games (Stag Hunt, Chicken, Attack-Defend, Prisoner's Dilemma), not public goods games.
- There is no explicit punishment or reward mechanism with associated costs; punishment is implemented as reciprocal defection.
- Group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are not directly reported; outcomes are evolutionary frequencies of strategies.
- The set of strategies is limited (e.g., no Generous Tit-for-Tat, Grim Trigger, Win-Stay-Lose-Shift, or generalized reciprocity).
- Population structure, multi-level selection, and more complex social environments are not modeled.
