# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do reputation feedback systems really improve trust among anonymous traders? An experimental study
Authors: Masclet, D., & Pénard, T.
Journal: Applied Economics
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff
- total earnings
- welfare
- Player A's payoff
- Player B's payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment (amount sent by Player A)
- return (amount returned by Player B)
- frequency and type of ratings (positive/negative/none)
- probability of evaluating partner
- probability of negative evaluation
- determinants of rating behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are investment and return in a repeated trust game, but the paper also reports average payoffs and analyzes the effect of feedback (punishment/reward) systems on both behavior and earnings.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that adding a reputation feedback system (where players can assign costly positive or negative ratings to their partner after each round) to a repeated trust game significantly increases both trust (amount sent) and trustworthiness (amount returned), resulting in higher average payoffs for both roles. The simultaneous rating system, which prevents players from knowing their partner's rating before making their own, produces the highest efficiency and welfare, as it minimizes strategic and retaliatory rating behavior. Sequential systems, where players can condition their rating on their partner's action, are less effective, and a system that prevents one side from punishing (as in the eBay-inspired treatment) does not improve and may even reduce efficiency. The results are robust across multiple sessions and are supported by both behavioral and payoff data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment/reward (reputation feedback) system in a repeated trust game increases efficiency (average payoff) compared to a control without such a system. The effect is most pronounced when the feedback system is designed to prevent strategic and retaliatory use (i.e., simultaneous, secret ratings). The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment/reward system with moderate cost and effectiveness, especially when implemented in a way that minimizes strategic retaliation, should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control. The results are directly relevant for trust-game-like environments and are likely to transfer to PGG-like settings with similar feedback mechanisms, though the mapping is not exact. The paper also provides evidence on the moderating role of feedback system design (timing, information structure) on the efficiency effect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 participants, paired into 5 pairs (Player A and Player B). The game is two-player (dyadic) per round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 20 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all interactions are via the experimental interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) and return choices are continuous between 0 and 10 (for A) and 0 and 3*amount received (for B), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The amount sent by Player A is tripled (multiplier = 3), and then Player B decides how much to return. Since the game is dyadic, the effective MPCR for A is 1.5 (if B returns all), but the structure is not a standard PGG.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Leaving a rating (positive or negative) costs 1 unit (1/10th of the endowment) to the rater; the rated player does not incur direct cost/benefit, but ratings affect reputation and future behavior.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each negative rating reduces the recipient's feedback profile by 1 point; positive rating increases by 1. The impact is via reputation, not direct payoff. The effectiveness is shown by increased trust and payoffs.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players can assign both positive (reward) and negative (punishment) ratings, both at the same cost.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Cost to assign a positive (reward) rating is 1 unit, same as for negative rating.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each positive rating increases the recipient's feedback profile by 1 point; effect is via reputation and future behavior.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game has 20 periods (finite, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players are shown their partner's feedback profile (cumulative ratings) at the start of each period; this is a key mechanism for the effect of the feedback system.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Players know which partner assigned the rating in the current round, as ratings are exchanged within the dyad. The timing and information structure (simultaneous vs sequential) is manipulated and shown to affect efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated trust game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant; mapping to PGG environments is adjacent but not exact.
- The feedback system combines both punishment and reward (positive and negative ratings), so effects cannot be attributed to punishment alone.
- Punishment and reward are implemented via reputation (feedback profile), not direct monetary deduction from the target.
- The cost and impact of ratings are fixed and may not generalize to other cost/impact structures.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- The effect of feedback system design (timing, information structure) is shown to be important, but only a limited set of designs are tested.
- No direct manipulation of group size beyond dyads; results may not generalize to larger groups.
