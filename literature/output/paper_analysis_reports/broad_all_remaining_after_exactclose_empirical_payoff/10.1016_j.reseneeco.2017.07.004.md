# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation in watershed management: A field experiment on location, trust, and enforcement
Authors: Ansink, E., Tesfaye, A., Bouma, J., & Brouwer, R.
Journal: Resource and Energy Economics
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff (ETB) by treatment and location
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount invested by trustor (trust)
- amount returned by trustee (trustworthiness)
- self-reported actual investments in soil conservation (field behavior)
- demographic and contextual variables
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust (amount invested in the trust game), trustworthiness (amount returned), and self-reported actual investments in soil conservation. Payoff is reported as average ETB earned, but efficiency (as a share of maximum possible group payoff) is not directly analyzed. The trust game is not a standard public goods game, but an investment/trust game with two roles and pairwise matching.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The enforcement (punishment) treatment, which penalizes trustees for not returning at least the amount sent by trustors, increases the amount returned but does not increase trust (investment by trustors). There is no significant effect of enforcement on average payoffs. Framing the game in terms of soil conservation reduces trust. Trust (as measured in the game) is a strong predictor of actual soil conservation investments in the field. The study finds no evidence that enforcement increases cooperation or efficiency in this context, and suggests that enforcement may crowd out trust. The trust game is not a public goods game but is related as a social dilemma with externalities.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that introducing a punishment/enforcement mechanism in a trust game does not increase trust or average payoffs, and may crowd out intrinsic motivation for cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in trust-game-like environments with high baseline trust, adding punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce voluntary cooperation. However, the game is not a standard public goods game, and efficiency is not directly measured as a share of maximum possible group payoff. The enforcement mechanism is probabilistic and only applies to the trustee role. The findings are most relevant for settings where trust and reciprocity are central, and less directly transferable to standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played in pairs (2 players per game: trustor and trustee), with random rematching and role reversal.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays both roles (trustor and trustee) in one session; not a repeated multi-round game in the standard sense.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed during the game; strict silence enforced.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (investment) is continuous: trustor chooses any amount from 0 to 10 ETB.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Amount sent by trustor is tripled (multiplier = 3); in pairwise game, this is the effective marginal return for the trustee.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In enforcement treatment, if trustee returns less than amount sent, a fine equal to the shortfall is imposed with 50% probability. The cost is paid by the trustee; no cost to the trustor.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fine equal to the difference between amount sent and amount returned, imposed with 50% probability (coin flip).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the structure of the game and that they will play both roles; the game is not repeated over multiple rounds with the same partner.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust (investment) game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to PGGs.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as a share of maximum possible group payoff; only average payoffs are reported.
- The enforcement (punishment) mechanism is probabilistic and only applies to the trustee role, not to all players.
- Sample size is limited (80 subjects), and some treatment groups are small.
- Actual field behavior (soil conservation) is self-reported, not experimentally incentivized.
- Cultural and contextual factors (high baseline trust, homogeneity) may limit generalizability.
