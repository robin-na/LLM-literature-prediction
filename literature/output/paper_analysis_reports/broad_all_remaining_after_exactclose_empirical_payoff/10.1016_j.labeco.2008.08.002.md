Outcome notes:
The paper is a theoretical analysis of optimal contracts for collaboration under repeated interaction, focusing on efficiency, welfare, and payoff outcomes. Non-payoff outcomes such as effort, collaboration, and peer sanctioning are discussed as mechanisms, but the main results are about efficiency and welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a repeated game with voluntary team formation, optimal contracts may use high-powered team incentives and low-powered individual incentives to exploit peer monitoring and reputation effects. This can make collaboration sustainable and efficient even with weak complementarities, overcoming the free-rider problem present in static settings. However, when the opportunity to collaborate is frequent and agents are patient, the optimal contract may under-incentivize individual effort, leading to inefficiency when collaboration is not possible. Thus, repeated interaction and peer sanctions can both improve and harm efficiency, depending on parameter values (discount factor, frequency of collaboration opportunities, degree of complementarities).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling peer sanctions (punishment via reputation loss and exclusion from collaboration) in repeated public-goods-like environments depends on the structure of incentives and the frequency of collaboration opportunities. When peer sanctions are possible, efficiency can increase by overcoming free-rider problems, but if individual incentives are weakened too much to make peer sanctions more effective, efficiency can decrease when collaboration is not possible. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not unconditionally positive: it depends on whether the contract design balances team and individual incentives, the patience of agents, and the frequency of opportunities for collaboration. The model is adjacent to public goods games, with peer sanctions implemented via exclusion and reputation rather than explicit costly punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- Punishment is implemented via peer sanctions (reputation loss, exclusion from collaboration), not explicit costly punishment as in standard PGG experiments.
- The main model is for two agents; extension to larger groups is only discussed.
- The model assumes binary (all-or-nothing) effort and infinitely repeated interaction.
- The principal's ability to observe is limited to outcomes and mode of production, not effort.
- The model does not include communication or chat between agents.
- The frequency of collaboration opportunities and the patience of agents are key parameters, but their real-world values are not empirically estimated.
