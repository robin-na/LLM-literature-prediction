# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Intergroup Conflict and Intra-Group Punishment in an Experimental Contest Game
Authors: Abbink, K., Brandts, J., Herrmann, B., & Orzen, H.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- contest expenditures (group and individual level)
- rent dissipation (ratio of total resources spent to value of prize)
- material losses (total points lost relative to equilibrium)
- total group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment frequency and allocation
- heterogeneity in team member contributions
- responsiveness to others' behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contest expenditures and rent dissipation, which are direct measures of efficiency/welfare. Non-payoff outcomes such as punishment behavior and contribution heterogeneity are also analyzed, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a group contest game, the introduction of within-group punishment opportunities causes a large increase in unproductive conflict expenditures and a severe reduction in efficiency. In the baseline (no-punishment) group contest, expenditures are already well above equilibrium, but with punishment, they rise even further: in the last five rounds, contest expenditures are 119% higher than without punishment, and total material losses (including punishment costs) are 869% of the Nash equilibrium level, with rent dissipation exceeding 100% of the prize. Punishment is used primarily to target low contributors, and its presence reduces heterogeneity in team member contributions, but the net effect is to discipline members into even more wasteful over-investment. The results directly contradict the efficiency-enhancing effects of punishment observed in standard public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that in contest games with public-good-like intra-group sharing, enabling within-group punishment leads to a large decrease in efficiency, with group payoffs and welfare falling far below both the equilibrium and the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across rounds and is not offset by any increase in group earnings. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment in a group contest environment should be expected to reduce efficiency, even if punishment increases contributions or reduces free-riding. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group size, number of rounds, the presence and cost/effectiveness of punishment, and the contest structure (lottery contest for a rival prize with intra-group sharing). Control efficiency (without punishment) is already low, but enabling punishment makes it much worse.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Treatments include both individuals (1:1), mixed (1:4), and teams (4:4); main punishment result is for 4:4 (teams of 4). Larger group size (teams) is associated with higher contest expenditures and lower efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Main treatments are 20 rounds; one treatment (4:4/40) uses 40 rounds. The punishment treatment (4:4P) is 20 rounds. No evidence that longer horizon reverses the negative efficiency effect of punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants; all interaction is via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (contest token purchase) is continuous: any integer amount between 0 and 1,000 per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but the contest structure is such that the marginal return to investment is determined by the lottery contest function. For each team member, the expected marginal return is the increase in probability of winning (proportional to tokens invested) times the prize (1,000 points per member).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each deduction token assigned costs the punisher 1 point and reduces the punished member's balance by 3 points. Punishment is costly and its cost/effectiveness is explicitly described.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point cost to punisher yields 3 point loss to target (3:1 ratio). This is fixed in the design.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the total number of rounds in advance (20 or 40, depending on treatment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed of each team member's investment and the total investment of the rival party. Team members are identified by a constant number, but identities are not revealed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: participants know how much they were punished and by whom (by team member number), but not the real identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The contest game is not a standard public goods game: contributions are wasteful (rent-seeking), and higher contributions reduce efficiency, unlike in PGGs where contributions increase efficiency.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory with anonymous, one-shot groups and no communication; real-world conflict may involve communication, leadership, and other social factors.
- Punishment is only possible within teams, not between teams; the effects of inter-group punishment are not studied.
- The prize is modeled as perfectly nonrival within the group, which may not generalize to all real-world conflict settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The findings may not generalize to environments where contributions are productive (as in standard PGGs) or where punishment can be targeted across groups.
