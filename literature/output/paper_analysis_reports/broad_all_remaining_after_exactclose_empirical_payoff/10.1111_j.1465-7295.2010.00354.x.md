# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: PEER EVALUATIONS AND TEAM PERFORMANCE: WHEN FRIENDS DO WORSE THAN STRANGERS
Authors: Corgnet, B.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- team performance (joint profits as % of maximum possible)
- average earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- self-reported motivation
- self-reported satisfaction
- peer assessment allocation choices (equality/equity norms)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is team performance, measured as the ratio of actual joint profits to the maximum possible. The study also reports average earnings. Non-payoff outcomes include survey-based measures of motivation and satisfaction, and analysis of peer assessment allocation rules.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that using peer evaluations to allocate team profits in a real-effort team task leads to lower team performance (measured as a percentage of maximum possible profits) than an equal split rule. This effect is statistically significant and is stronger in teams where members are acquaintances or friends. The mechanism appears to involve a crowding-out of intrinsic motivation and increased negative reciprocity when peer evaluations are used among friends. The majority of peer assessments follow equality norms, but a significant minority use equity (contribution-based) norms. The results suggest that peer evaluation mechanisms may not always improve efficiency, especially in contexts with strong interpersonal relationships.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in small real-effort team settings with face-to-face communication and high social proximity, introducing a peer evaluation mechanism for allocating payoffs (a form of endogenous sanction/reward) can reduce efficiency compared to a fixed equal split. This is contrary to standard public goods game findings where punishment or reward options often increase efficiency. The negative effect is especially pronounced when team members are friends or classmates. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of introducing peer-based sanction/reward mechanisms on efficiency is context-dependent: in environments with high social ties and communication, such mechanisms may reduce efficiency relative to control. The findings are most relevant for small teams with real-effort tasks, face-to-face interaction, and endogenous allocation of payoffs via peer assessment.

- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Team members work side by side and can observe each other's effort; no explicit summary statistics, but high observability.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a real-effort team task rather than a standard public goods game, so results may not generalize to standard PGG settings.
- Punishment is not present; only endogenous allocation of rewards via peer evaluation is studied.
- Teams are small (3 members), and effects may differ in larger groups.
- Face-to-face communication and high observability may not be present in all PGG-like environments.
- The negative effect of peer evaluation on efficiency may depend on the presence of strong social ties; results may not generalize to anonymous or low-social-tie settings.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or standard PGG payoff structure.
