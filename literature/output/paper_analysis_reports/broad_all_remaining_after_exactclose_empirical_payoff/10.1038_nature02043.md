## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that altruistic punishment (costly punishment of norm violators) is a robust phenomenon in laboratory experiments, including public goods games and third-party punishment games. It reviews evidence that, in the absence of punishment, cooperation in public goods games typically decays over repeated rounds, but that the opportunity for punishment can sustain higher levels of cooperation. The paper also discusses the limits of altruism, noting that higher costs of punishment or contribution reduce the frequency of these behaviors. While the paper provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the importance of punishment in sustaining cooperation, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff effects from punishment interventions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that enabling punishment in public goods games and similar environments can sustain higher cooperation and deter free-riding, especially in repeated or group settings. However, it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it systematically analyze how game design dimensions (such as player count, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is most useful for contextualizing the likely direction of punishment effects (i.e., more positive for cooperation and, by implication, possibly for efficiency), but it does not provide parameter estimates or direct outcome data for model training or calibration.
