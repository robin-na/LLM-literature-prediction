## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper proposes that punishment and policing behaviors are maintained when the inclusive fitness benefits to the punisher outweigh the costs, and when these benefits are greater than those from bystanding. The framework is applied to a range of biological and social systems, showing that power, relatedness, and reproductive options drive variation in who punishes. In systems with strong power asymmetries, dominants are more likely to punish; in systems with high relatedness and low individual reproductive prospects, policing is more likely to be corrective and group-beneficial. The paper highlights that the same inclusive fitness logic can explain punishment across diverse domains, but does not provide empirical data or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a conceptual framework for understanding the conditions under which punishment is likely to emerge and be maintained in social groups, emphasizing the roles of power, relatedness, and reproductive options. However, it does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. For the downstream prediction task, the paper may inform mechanistic expectations about when punishment is likely to be effective or who will implement it, but it does not supply data or direct evidence to calibrate efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions.
