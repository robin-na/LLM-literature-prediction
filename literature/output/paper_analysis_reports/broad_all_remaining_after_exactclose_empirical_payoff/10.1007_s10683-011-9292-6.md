## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in a linear public goods game with peer punishment, subjects' punishment behavior is not consistent with the instrumental model (which would predict strategic substitution and lower total punishment in sequential settings). Instead, punishment is largely expressive: individuals punish even when they expect others to do so, and the aggregate punishment is not minimized. The study provides evidence against the view that peer punishment is used instrumentally to increase group welfare or efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on the behavioral patterns and motivations for punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in this public goods game design, the presence of peer punishment does not necessarily lead to efficiency-maximizing or welfare-improving outcomes, because punishment is not used instrumentally to deter free-riding. Instead, punishment is expressive and may be excessive from a group welfare perspective. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in similar designs may not reliably increase efficiency, and could even reduce it if punishment is costly and not strategically targeted. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff data, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the efficiency effect of punishment. Its main value is in highlighting that the behavioral mechanism of punishment may not align with efficiency improvements.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per round; 15 participants per session, randomly rematched into groups of 3 each period.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 periods per session: 4 periods without punishment, 20 periods with punishment (10 one-to-one, 10 sequential).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between subjects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: each subject chooses any amount 0 ≤ g_i ≤ 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (a) is 0.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 0.25 tokens per point of punishment assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each point of punishment reduces the target's earnings by 1 token; cost to punisher is 0.25 per point.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were informed of the three parts and the number of periods in each part as instructions were read at the start of each part.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants observed each other's team member earnings and contributions. Punishment received was also shown as aggregate points.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; subjects did not know the identity of punishers. Only aggregate punishment received was shown.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The experiment uses small groups (n=3) and a specific punishment structure, which may limit generalizability to larger groups or other institutional settings.
- The focus is on behavioral motivations for punishment, not on the aggregate consequences for group welfare.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
- Repeated random matching may allow for some learning or contagion effects, though the design attempts to minimize reputation effects.
