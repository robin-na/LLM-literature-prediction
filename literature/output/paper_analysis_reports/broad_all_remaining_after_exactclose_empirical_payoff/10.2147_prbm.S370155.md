# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Morality is Supreme: The Roles of Morality, Fairness and Group Identity in the Ultimatum Paradigm
Authors: Liu, W. X., Wang, H., Zhu, H. J., Zhu, X. Y., He, X. Y., & Zhang, W.
Journal: Psychology Research and Behavior Management
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rate of offers (proportion of offers accepted)
- effect of proposer morality on acceptance rate
- effect of group identity (moral priming) on acceptance rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the acceptance rate of offers in the ultimatum game, which is a behavioral outcome reflecting willingness to accept or reject offers based on fairness, proposer morality, and group identity. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in the ultimatum game, responders are more likely to accept offers (including unfair ones) from proposers with positive moral backgrounds than from those with negative moral backgrounds. This effect is robust across fair, moderately unfair, and extremely unfair offers. Group identity, manipulated via moral priming, has only a partial effect: positive or negative moral priming increases acceptance rates for positive moral proposers in unfair allocations, but does not significantly affect responses to negative moral proposers. The gender of the proposer does not significantly affect acceptance rates. The study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so its findings are not directly transferable to efficiency prediction tasks.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that in ultimatum game settings, the perceived morality of the proposer strongly influences acceptance rates, even for unfair offers. However, because the outcome measured is acceptance rate (not efficiency or group payoff), and because the ultimatum game is not a public goods game or a direct variant, the findings are only indirectly relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment mechanisms, costs, or efficiency, so it does not provide direct evidence for the prediction task. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of social/moral context in responder behavior, which may inform broader models of cooperation and norm enforcement.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game with 2 players: one proposer, one responder.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 150 trials per participant; Experiment 2: 80 trials per participant. Each trial is a one-shot ultimatum game with a new scenario.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are via computer-presented scenarios.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept the offer (both get proposed split) or reject (both get zero).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials in the experiment; each trial is independent.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to public goods games; findings may not generalize to PGG-like environments.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanism is present; rejection of offers is not modeled as costly punishment in the PGG sense.
- The main outcome is acceptance rate (a behavioral measure), not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- No analysis of how design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, or MPCR affect efficiency or payoff.
- Group identity is manipulated via moral priming, which may not fully capture real-world group effects.
- The study does not address repeated interaction, learning, or dynamic effects relevant to multi-round PGGs.
