# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Habits of Virtue: Creating Norms of Cooperation and Defection in the Laboratory
Authors: Peysakhovich, A., & Rand, D. G.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prosociality (normalized transfer decisions in PGG, TG, DG, UG)
- third-party punishment (amount spent to punish in 3PDG, 3PPD, PGP)
- antisocial punishment
- self-reported generalized trust
- cognitive reflection test (CRT) moderation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: transfer/prosociality rates and punishment behavior. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported. The PGG is used, but efficiency is not calculated or discussed as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Exposure to environments that support cooperation (via repeated PDs with high continuation probability and favorable payoffs) leads to higher prosociality in subsequent one-shot games, including the public goods game, trust game, dictator game, and ultimatum game. In a second experiment, exposure to cooperative environments increases third-party punishment of selfishness and reduces antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment. These effects are strongest among subjects who rely on intuitive (heuristic) decision-making. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that institutional environments can shape prosocial and punishment behaviors in public-goods-game-like settings, and that these behaviors spill over into new contexts. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency. The design details (e.g., group size, punishment cost/tech, MPCR) are reported and can inform mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG and PGP use groups of 4; 3PDG uses groups of 3; 3PPD uses groups of 4. Player count is fixed and reported for each game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage A: C-Culture: 53 rounds (10 games), D-Culture: 51 rounds (45 games) in Experiment 1; ~40 rounds in Experiment 2. Stage B: all games are one-shot (strategy method, no feedback).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players in any stage.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG and DG allow continuous contribution/transfer (0-100 MU); TG trustor is binary; TG trustee is continuous; UG proposer is continuous. Punishment decisions are continuous (0-20 MU).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG and PGP: Each MU contributed is multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 MU spent reduces target's payoff by 5 MU (cost:impact = 1:5) in all punishment games.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 MU spent reduces target's payoff by 5 MU.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available only in the 3PPD game (subjects can spend to increase a PD player's payoff by 5 MU per 1 MU spent). Not available in PGG or other games.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost: 1 MU spent increases target's payoff by 5 MU in 3PPD.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 MU spent increases target's payoff by 5 MU in 3PPD.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage A: Each repeated PD game has a random number of rounds (stochastic, not known in advance). Stage B: all games are one-shot and subjects know this.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round in Stage A, subjects are informed of their partner's decision and both players' earnings. In Stage B, no feedback is given between decisions; in PGG/PGP, group contributions are not summarized before punishment decisions (strategy method).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; punished subjects do not know who punished them. No identification of punishers is provided.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus as outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games cannot be determined.
- All main outcomes are behavioral (prosociality, punishment), not payoff-based.
- The public goods game is one-shot in Stage B, not repeated, and efficiency is not calculated.
- The experimental manipulation is prior exposure to cooperative or noncooperative environments, not direct manipulation of punishment availability or cost within the PGG.
- Generalizability to real-world or longer-term institutional effects is not tested.
- No direct comparison of efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled PGGs is provided.
