# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Markets and socially responsible behavior: do punishment and religion matter?
Authors: Nigus, H. Y., Nillesen, E., Mohnen, P., & Di Falco, S.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings (welfare) per participant
- amount of money destroyed (losses) in joy-of-destruction game
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of fair product purchases (socially responsible behavior)
- punishment frequency
- retaliation (burning) behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average earnings (welfare) and money destroyed (losses), with extensive analysis of socially responsible behavior (fair product choice) and punishment/retaliation. Efficiency is operationalized as average group earnings relative to possible maximum (see Table 11, Table 12).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study uses a lab-in-the-field market experiment in Ethiopia to test the effects of punishment and religious priming on socially responsible behavior and welfare. In the market baseline (no punishment), only about 21% of purchases are fair (no negative externality), and average earnings are about 56 Birr. When punishment is introduced (observers can punish at a cost of 1 Birr to reduce others' earnings by 5 Birr), the proportion of fair purchases rises to 95%, and average earnings increase to 66 Birr. Punishment also reduces anti-social behavior in a subsequent joy-of-destruction game (money burning). Religious priming increases fair purchases to 32% but does not significantly improve welfare compared to the baseline. The results show that punishment mechanisms are highly effective at increasing efficiency (group welfare) in this market context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a market game with negative externalities, adding a punishment mechanism (with a cost of 1 to reduce another's payoff by 5) substantially increases efficiency (average group earnings) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is large and robust. The design is not a standard PGG but is adjacent: it is a market/trade game with externalities and third parties, and the efficiency measure is directly comparable (average earnings relative to maximum possible). The punishment mechanism is costly and effective. Religious priming has a much weaker effect. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar market or externality games, enabling punishment with a moderate cost-to-impact ratio will lead to a large increase in efficiency over the control. The effect is much larger than that of non-monetary priming.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Market game: 16 or 18 participants per session (4 sellers, 6 buyers, 6 third parties, 2 observers in punishment treatment). Each group: 4 sellers, 6 buyers, 6 third parties (plus 2 observers in punishment).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds in all market and non-market treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Buyers choose between two discrete options: fair or unfair product (or no trade). Sellers choose which product to offer. Contribution is not continuous.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but the marginal per-capita return can be inferred: fair product has a net social benefit of 20 Birr, unfair product has a net social cost of -10 Birr (due to negative externality).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs 1 Birr to the punisher to reduce the target's earnings by 5 Birr. Maximum punishment per assignee is 20 Birr (costs 4 Birr to punisher).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 Birr cost reduces other's payoff by 5 Birr (5:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 rounds; number of rounds is known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Observers in punishment treatment are informed about the decisions of their assigned group. No evidence that buyers/sellers/third parties see others' summary info, but observers do.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; matched participants never learn each other's identity.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a standard public goods game but a market/trade game with negative externalities and third parties; results may not generalize to all PGG-like settings.
- The punishment mechanism is implemented by external observers, not by direct peers; this may affect generalizability to peer punishment settings.
- The study is conducted in rural Ethiopia; cultural and institutional context may limit external validity.
- The religious priming treatment is only administered at the start of the experiment and may not be comparable in salience to repeated punishment.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is tested.
- The number of rounds is limited to 10, which may affect learning and long-term dynamics.
