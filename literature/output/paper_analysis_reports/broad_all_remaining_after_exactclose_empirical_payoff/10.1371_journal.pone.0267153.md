# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Motivations to reciprocate cooperation and punish defection are calibrated by estimates of how easily others can switch partners
Authors: Arai, S., Tooby, J., & Cosmides, L.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- payoff to truster and responder (Table 1)
- amount paid to punish
- amount reciprocated
Non-payoff outcomes:
- reciprocation rate
- punishment frequency and amount
- decision to switch partners
- perceptions of relational mobility
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are reciprocation (amount returned), punishment (amount paid to punish), and partner switching. Payoff tables are provided, but efficiency or group welfare is not directly analyzed as an outcome. Analyses focus on behavioral responses (reciprocation, punishment, switching) rather than aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that participants who perceive higher relational mobility (i.e., that others can easily switch partners) reciprocate more and punish less in a dyadic trust game with punishment. The opportunity to switch partners (experimental manipulation) did not affect punishment, but did interact with relational mobility to affect reciprocation in American participants. Punishment did not increase subsequent cooperation and made punished partners more likely to leave. The main outcomes are behavioral (reciprocation, punishment, switching), not efficiency or group payoff. Payoff tables are provided, but efficiency is not a dependent variable.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting efficiency in punishment-enabled games: higher perceived partner choice (relational mobility) leads to more reciprocation and less punishment, which could plausibly increase efficiency, but efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured or analyzed. The game is a dyadic trust game with punishment, not a standard public goods game, so findings are adjacent but not directly transferable. The paper is most informative about how punishment and partner choice cues affect individual behavior, not aggregate efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is dyadic (2 players: truster and responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played two rounds with the same partner (once as truster, once as responder); a third round was planned but not played.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (amount sent/returned) is continuous in 10-point increments, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Points sent by truster are tripled (multiplier = 3), so marginal per-capita return for responder is 3, for truster depends on responder's return rate.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 10 points to subtract 30 points from partner; up to 50 points to subtract up to 150 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (10 points spent removes 30 points from partner).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism in the game; only punishment is available. Reward is discussed in the introduction as a contrast.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told in advance how many rounds they would play and whether they could switch partners after two rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants saw the amount sent/returned and punishment decisions of their partner after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was always attributed to the partner; in the dyadic game, the identity of the punisher is always known.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a dyadic trust game with punishment, not a standard public goods game; findings may not generalize directly to multi-player PGGs.
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed as a dependent variable; main outcomes are behavioral (reciprocation, punishment, switching).
- The experimental manipulation of partner switching had limited effect on punishment and only interacted with relational mobility for reciprocation in American participants.
- Participants interacted with simulated partners, not real others, which may affect ecological validity.
- The study is limited to US and Japanese participants recruited online; results may not generalize to other cultures or real-world settings.
