Outcome notes:
The paper is a conceptual/theoretical review and framework, not an empirical study. It synthesizes findings from prior empirical and theoretical work, focusing on the reputation consequences of punishment in social dilemmas, including public goods games and related environments. The main outcomes discussed are reputational, emotional, and partner choice effects, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the reputation consequences of punishment are context-dependent. In forced play games where punishment is possible, a punitive reputation can induce fear and stabilize cooperation, potentially supporting group efficiency. However, when partner choice is available, individuals with a nonpunishing cooperative reputation are typically preferred over punishers, unless punishment is the only available signal of cooperative intent. The efficiency of punishment (fee:fine ratio) also moderates reputational outcomes: 'efficient' punishment (fee:fine > 1) may be viewed as self-serving, while 'inefficient' punishment (fee:fine < 1) is more likely to be seen as genuinely norm-enforcing. The paper synthesizes prior empirical and theoretical work but does not provide new data or direct estimates of efficiency effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is most useful for identifying moderators and mechanisms that may affect the impact of punishment on cooperation and, by extension, efficiency in public goods games and similar environments. It highlights the importance of partner choice, the efficiency of punishment, and the availability of alternative signals of cooperation. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct evidence on efficiency outcomes, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is primarily in identifying which dimensions are likely to be important moderators (e.g., partner choice, punishment efficiency, information structure).

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a conceptual/theoretical review and does not provide new empirical data or direct estimates of efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Most claims are based on synthesis of prior work, which is itself sometimes limited or inconclusive regarding efficiency effects.
