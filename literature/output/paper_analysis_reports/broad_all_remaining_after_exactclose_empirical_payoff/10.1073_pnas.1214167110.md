## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical and simulation results show that, in finite populations with stochastic evolutionary dynamics, fairness (both higher offers and nonzero rejection thresholds) can be favored by natural selection in the Ultimatum Game, even without reputation or repeated interaction. The favored strategies depend on mutation rate and selection strength. The behavioral experiment supports the prediction that greater uncertainty about others' success and less behavioral consistency are associated with higher offers and/or demands. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total welfare, or surplus, and the Ultimatum Game structure is only adjacent to public goods games with punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the evolution of costly punishment and fairness in strategic interactions, but its findings are not directly transferable to predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. The Ultimatum Game is a two-player bargaining game with a binary accept/reject punishment mechanism, not a multi-player public goods game with explicit punishment options. The main outcomes are behavioral (offer and demand), not group efficiency. Thus, while the paper supports the idea that costly punishment (rejection) can be favored and lead to fairer outcomes, it does not provide quantitative evidence for the efficiency impact of punishment in PGG-like environments. Use this paper for mechanism insight, not for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game is two-player; simulations use N=100 agents, but each game is always between two players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each interaction is a single round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; game is anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Offer and demand are continuous between 0 and 1; responder's choice is binary (accept/reject).
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Rejection is a costly punishment: responder pays the cost of forgoing the offer, proposer loses the proposed amount. Cost is endogenous to the offer/demand structure, not parameterized.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is binary (rejection = both get zero); effectiveness is maximal but not parameterized.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is explicitly one-shot; players know there is only one round.

## Important Limitations
- The Ultimatum Game is structurally different from public goods games; results may not generalize to multi-player PGGs with explicit punishment options.
- No group efficiency, total welfare, or surplus outcomes are reported; main outcomes are behavioral (offer and demand).
- Punishment is endogenous (rejection) and binary, not parameterized as in PGG punishment treatments.
- Findings are primarily theoretical and simulation-based, with only a small behavioral experiment for support.
- No direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods game-like environments.
