## Source
Title: Evolution of altruistic punishment in heterogeneous populations
Authors: de Weerd, H., & Verbrugge, R.
Journal: Journal of Theoretical Biology
Year: 2011

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of altruistic individuals (cooperators and punishers)
- proportion of altruistic punishers
- distribution of strategies (altruistic punisher, altruistic non-punisher, selfish non-punisher, loner)
- specialization of punishment roles across heterogeneous classes
- stability of altruistic behavior
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the evolution and stability of altruistic punishment and cooperation in agent-based models of the public goods game, with and without population heterogeneity. Outcomes are primarily the prevalence of different strategies (altruistic punisher, altruistic non-punisher, selfish non-punisher, loner) and the conditions under which altruistic behavior stabilizes. Payoff is used as a mechanism for strategy updating, but aggregate efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported as a main outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in both infinite well-mixed and spatially structured populations, heterogeneity in the cost of punishing (and, when combined, in the effectiveness of punishment) can make it easier for altruistic punishment and cooperation to stabilize. Specialization occurs, with the most efficient punishers (those with low cost and/or high impact) carrying out most of the punishment. In the spatial model, this effect is robust and can lead to higher prevalence of altruistic strategies. However, the paper does not directly analyze or report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff; instead, it focuses on the prevalence and stability of cooperative and punishing strategies as the main outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that heterogeneity in punishment cost and effectiveness can facilitate the evolution and maintenance of altruistic punishment and cooperation in public goods games, especially when efficient punishers are present. However, because the main outcomes are the prevalence of altruistic strategies rather than direct measures of group efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, under certain heterogeneity conditions, punishment is more likely to support stable cooperation, which is typically associated with higher efficiency, but this link is not directly quantified in the paper. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides contextual and mechanistic support but not direct quantitative evidence.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N=5 is used in both infinite and spatial models; spatial model uses 5-person neighborhoods on a 50x50 lattice.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 500 rounds (spatial model) or until equilibrium (infinite model); mutation analysis uses 20,000 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a binary choice: individuals either invest in the public good or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is r/N; r=3.0, N=5 (infinite model), r=1.9, N=5 (spatial model).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (gamma) is a key manipulated variable; heterogeneity in cost facilitates altruistic punishment and cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (beta) is manipulated; heterogeneity alone has little effect, but when combined with cost heterogeneity, can amplify or hinder altruistic punishment depending on the configuration.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are the prevalence and stability of altruistic strategies, not direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The models are agent-based simulations, not empirical experiments with human subjects.
- Punishment is the only sanctioning mechanism considered; no reward or positive incentives are modeled.
- Contribution is modeled as a binary (all-or-nothing) choice, not a continuous contribution.
- Heterogeneity is modeled as discrete classes, not as a continuous distribution of abilities.
- No explicit analysis of the quantitative relationship between punishment and group efficiency or surplus.
- No mutation in main models (except in Appendix B); mutation effects are only briefly explored.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms beyond local imitation.
