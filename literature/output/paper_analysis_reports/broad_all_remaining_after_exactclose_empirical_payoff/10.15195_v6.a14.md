# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sharing Compromising Information as a Cooperative Strategy
Authors: Gambetta, D., & Przepiorka, W.
Journal: Sociological Science
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- defection rate
- trust decisions
- label revelation decisions
- punishment decisions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: rates of cooperation, defection, trust, and information sharing (label revelation), as well as punishment assignment. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes, though payoffs are described in the game structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when a punishment mechanism is available (i.e., when norm-enforcement is possible), cooperation among less cooperative types ('hawks') increases, especially if both parties can mutually reveal their compromising information and thus make themselves vulnerable to punishment. In conditions without punishment, hawks rarely cooperate with each other. The effect is strongest when both the revelation of compromising information and the ability to punish are present and mutually chosen. The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, defection, information sharing), not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that punishment opportunities can enable cooperation among otherwise uncooperative individuals, but the paper does not quantify the effect on group efficiency or total earnings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism in a trust-game-like environment can increase cooperation among less cooperative individuals, especially when mutual vulnerability is possible. However, because the main outcomes are behavioral (not efficiency or group payoff), the findings should be used as indirect support for the prediction task. The game is a one-shot trust game variant, not a standard repeated public goods game, and efficiency is not directly measured. The punishment mechanism is costly and only available in certain conditions. The results suggest that punishment can increase cooperation, but the magnitude of efficiency gains (if any) is not reported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each part consists of multiple rounds: Part 1 (8 rounds), Parts 2 and 3 (9 rounds each).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all interactions are via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/trust decisions are discrete: stay out, trust and cooperate, or trust and defect.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment costs €0.50 to the punisher and inflicts a €4 penalty on the punished. Only available in T3 and T4 conditions.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is €4 penalty per €0.50 cost to punisher (8:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told the number of rounds is between 7 and 10, but not the exact number; thus, the end is uncertain.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game variant (venture game), not a standard public goods game, so results may not generalize to PGG settings.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, defection, information sharing), not efficiency or group payoff.
- Efficiency, welfare, or total earnings are not directly reported or analyzed as dependent variables.
- The game is played in pairs (2 players), not groups, limiting generalizability to larger group settings.
- Punishment is only available in certain conditions and is not peer punishment in the standard PGG sense.
- The number of rounds is uncertain to participants, which may affect strategic behavior.
- External validity is limited due to the artificial lab setting and use of student subjects.
