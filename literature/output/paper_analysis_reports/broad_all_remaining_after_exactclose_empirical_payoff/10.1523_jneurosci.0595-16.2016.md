# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Intention Modulates the Effect of Punishment Threat in Norm Enforcement via the Lateral Orbitofrontal Cortex
Authors: Zhang, Y., Yu, H. B., Yin, Y., & Zhou, X. L.
Journal: Journal of Neuroscience
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount allocated to partner (norm compliance)
- self-reported emotions (positive/negative affect, perceived trust)
- brain activation (fMRI)
- effect of brain stimulation (HD-tDCS) on allocation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount allocated to the partner in a modified dictator game under different punishment threat and intention conditions. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. Emotional and neural outcomes are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that the intention behind punishment threat critically shapes norm compliance: when a partner intentionally retains the right to punish, participants allocate less to the partner (reduced norm compliance), but when the partner intentionally waives the right, participants allocate more (increased norm compliance and positive affect). When the punishment threat is unintentional (computer-decided), these effects are weaker or absent. The rLOFC is shown to mediate these effects, as brain stimulation modulates the behavioral response to intention. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, and the game is a modified dictator game rather than a public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task: it shows that the social context and perceived intention behind punishment can strongly affect norm compliance (proxied by allocation), but does not report efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that in environments where punishment is perceived as self-serving or intentional, punishment may reduce cooperation or norm compliance, while unintentional or third-party punishment may be less detrimental or even neutral. For prediction, this implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may depend on whether punishment is perceived as legitimate or self-interested, but the lack of efficiency data and the use of a dictator game limit direct applicability to public goods game efficiency predictions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round is a two-player (allocator and responder) modified dictator game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 64 rounds (2 blocks of 32 trials), with each of the four conditions (Partner/Computer x Retain/Waive) having 16 trials.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; all interaction is via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocation is continuous in steps of 2 yuan from 0 to 20 yuan; not all-or-nothing.
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. Initial allocation screen is balanced: initial amount on participant's side is either 0 or 20 yuan, balanced within each condition.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a deduction of 4 yuan from the allocator if allocation is below responder's (hidden) minimum and punishment threat is retained. No explicit cost to punisher (partner or computer); punishment is executed automatically.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction of 4 yuan from allocator's payoff if triggered. No variable effectiveness.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment threat is manipulated.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the experiment consists of two blocks of 32 trials each; total number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. No trial-by-trial feedback about payoffs is provided to prevent learning specific strategies. No summary information about others' contributions or payoffs is shown.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told whether the decider is the partner (intentional) or computer (unintentional) in each trial; the identity of the punisher is always known.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a modified dictator game, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player public goods settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only allocation (norm compliance) is measured.
- Punishment is not costly to the punisher; it is executed automatically by the partner or computer.
- The study focuses on intention behind punishment threat, not on the structural parameters (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR) relevant to public goods games.
- The sample consists of university students in a lab setting; external validity may be limited.
