# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Culture and cooperation
Authors: Gächter, S., Herrmann, B., & Thöni, C.
Journal: Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned (free-rider and antisocial)
- variance in contributions (within and between groups)
- change in contributions between punishment and no-punishment conditions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings, but does discuss how contributions relate to potential group welfare. Punishment expenditures and their targets are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a standard public goods game, the opportunity to punish increases average contributions in some cultures but not in others. Cultural background explains a much larger share of the variance in contributions when punishment is available than when it is not. Punishment patterns themselves (including antisocial punishment) differ strongly across cultures and are linked to differences in cooperation. The presence of punishment reduces within-group variance in contributions but has mixed effects on between-group variance. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but higher contributions in the punishment condition in some cultures suggest likely higher efficiency in those cases, while in other cultures the effect is negligible.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by implication, efficiency) is highly culture-dependent, even in otherwise identical public goods game designs. For prediction tasks, this means that game design dimensions alone (such as player count, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are not sufficient to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency without accounting for cultural context. The paper's results suggest that, in some environments, punishment can substantially increase cooperation (and likely efficiency), while in others it has little or no effect. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its quantitative value for efficiency prediction is limited to inferring that higher contributions generally imply higher efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each condition (with and without punishment) lasted 10 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: players could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 ECU.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (public good multiplier 1.6 divided by 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point cost the punisher 1 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the punished player's income by 3 ECU.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the game lasted 10 periods in each condition.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the contributions of all other group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; all main results are based on contribution rates.
- Cultural context is a major moderator, but the paper does not provide a model or quantitative prediction for efficiency as a function of game design dimensions.
- The sample consists only of university students in developed countries, limiting generalizability.
- Punishment is the only sanctioning mechanism; no reward or positive sanctioning is studied.
- The study does not analyze the cost of punishment relative to gains in cooperation, so net welfare effects are not directly assessed.
