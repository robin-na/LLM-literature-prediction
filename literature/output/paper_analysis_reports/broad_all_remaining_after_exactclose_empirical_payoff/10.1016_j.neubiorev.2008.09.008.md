Outcome notes:
The paper is a narrative review of the literature on game-theoretical paradigms (including Public Goods Games, Ultimatum Game, Dictator Game, Trust Game, Prisoner's Dilemma) and focuses on behavioral and neural mechanisms, individual differences, and psychopathology. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes, but discusses how punishment and cooperation are studied in these paradigms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews evidence that punishment of non-cooperators in Public Goods Games and related paradigms increases cooperation and reduces free-riding, with punishment typically administered by cooperators and targeted at defectors. It discusses the neural correlates of punishment and cooperation, the evolutionary rationale for sanctioning, and the influence of individual differences (e.g., personality, genetics, psychopathology) and contextual factors (e.g., culture, gender, resource availability) on social exchange behavior. However, it does not provide quantitative or direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff effects of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment mechanisms in Public Goods Games increase cooperation and reduce free-riding, which are typically associated with higher group payoffs and efficiency in the literature. However, it does not report or synthesize quantitative efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to directly predict the magnitude of efficiency changes due to punishment. It is most useful for understanding mechanisms, moderators, and the general expectation that punishment can sustain cooperation, but not for parameterizing efficiency effects for specific game designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Public Goods Games are described as involving an 'optional number of players', with specific mention of a four-player game in Fehr & Gächter (2002). No systematic analysis of player count effects.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Mentions both one-shot and repeated games; notes that contributions decline over repeated rounds unless punishment is available.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes contribution as a continuous choice (how much to invest), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Describes the public goods multiplier as 'larger than one and smaller than the number of players', but does not specify values or analyze effects.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher, but no specific cost structure or variation is discussed.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as reducing the payoff of defectors; effectiveness is implied but not quantified or systematically varied.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players are informed about the investments of other players before punishment decisions in the referenced Public Goods Game.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Mentions that punishment can be anonymous or not, and that antisocial punishment is more likely under anonymity.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a narrative review and does not report new experimental data or quantitative efficiency/payoff outcomes.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are not directly analyzed; the focus is on behavioral and neural mechanisms, and individual/contextual moderators.
- Game design dimensions are discussed only at a high level, without systematic variation or analysis of their effects on efficiency.
- Findings are synthesized from multiple studies with potentially differing methodologies, making it difficult to extract parameter-specific predictions.
