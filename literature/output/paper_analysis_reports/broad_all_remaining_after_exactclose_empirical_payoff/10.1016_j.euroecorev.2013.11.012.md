## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated trust games with two groups, the ability to sustain efficient, cooperative outcomes depends on the punishment strategy adopted by individuals. Solidarity-based punishment (where all individuals punish any opportunism) supports high efficiency and deters cheating. However, if individuals only punish opportunism against their own group (discriminatory trigger), the minority group is more likely to be cheated, leading to lower efficiency for the minority and lower overall surplus. The model also shows that selective legal enforcement (protecting only the majority) can reduce the effectiveness of reputational punishment, making the minority worse off. Only anti-discrimination enforcement (penalizing group-based opportunism) robustly increases efficiency and reduces discrimination. The results highlight that the structure of punishment and enforcement mechanisms critically shapes efficiency and the distribution of payoffs across groups.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on whether punishment is coordinated across groups (solidarity) or limited to within-group (discriminatory). In environments where punishment is only triggered by harm to one's own group, efficiency gains from punishment are limited, especially for minorities. The model also warns that adding legal enforcement in a way that only protects the majority can reduce overall efficiency for minorities by undermining reputational incentives. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs enabling broad, group-independent punishment (solidarity) will yield higher efficiency gains from punishment, while designs that fragment punishment or allow selective enforcement may see limited or even negative effects on efficiency, especially for minority subgroups.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The game is a repeated trust game, not a standard public goods game; only one individual acts per round, and the firm is the only possible cheater.
- Punishment is reputational (via trigger strategies), not a costly, explicit punishment action as in many PGG experiments.
- No direct modeling of communication, chat, or reward mechanisms.
- The model assumes infinite repetition and common knowledge of game structure, which may not hold in real-world or experimental settings.
- Extensions to imperfect information are stylized and may not capture all real-world complexities.
