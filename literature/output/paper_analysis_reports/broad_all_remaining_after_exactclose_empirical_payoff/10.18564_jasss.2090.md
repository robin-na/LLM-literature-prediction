Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are donation rates (frequency of altruistic acts) and mean tolerance values. These are behavioral measures, not direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Fitness is used as an agent-level variable for evolutionary selection, but group-level efficiency or total payoff is not reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the sustainability of tag-mediated altruism is highly sensitive to how cheaters are defined. When cheaters are defined as agents who can never donate (strong cheaters), altruism collapses completely and rapidly. With medium cheaters (who can revert to altruism via mutation), donation rates are very low. Only with ultraweak or weak cheaters do substantial donation rates persist. The paper also finds that parameters such as neighborhood size, number of pairings, and mutation rate affect donation rates, but the presence of strong cheaters overwhelms these effects. The authors suggest that additional mechanisms, such as punishment or multi-level selection, may be necessary to sustain cooperation in the presence of strong cheaters, but these are not modeled directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or close variants. It does not implement punishment or sanctions, nor does it report group efficiency or payoff outcomes. However, it is informative in showing that, in tag-based donation models, the presence of strong cheaters (analogous to unpunished defectors) eliminates cooperation, and that additional mechanisms (such as punishment) may be required to sustain altruism. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that in environments where strong, unpunished defection is possible, efficiency will be low unless additional mechanisms (like punishment) are present. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct manipulation of punishment, so its value for prediction is mainly conceptual and indirect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Population size (N) is varied (e.g., 40, 100, 900) and shown to affect donation rates, especially in interaction with neighborhood radius and pairings.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 30,000 generations; data are collected over the final 1,000 generations. Number of generations is fixed, not varied as a treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Donation is a binary (all-or-nothing) act: an agent either donates (incurring cost, giving benefit) or does not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to donor (c) and benefit to recipient (b) are specified (e.g., c=0.1, b=1), but there is no group fund; the game is a donation game, not a standard PGG. The effective 'mpcr' is b/c=10, but this is not a group return.

## Important Limitations
- The models are not public goods games or direct variants; they are tag-based donation games with pairwise interactions.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is implemented; punishment is only discussed as a possible future extension.
- Outcomes are behavioral (donation rates), not group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
- The models are evolutionary simulations, not experiments with human subjects.
- Findings about the effect of punishment or sanctions are speculative and not empirically tested in the models.
