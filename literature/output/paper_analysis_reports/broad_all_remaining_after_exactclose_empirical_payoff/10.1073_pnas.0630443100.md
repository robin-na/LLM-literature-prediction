The main outcomes are frequencies of behavioral types (cooperators, punishers, defectors) and the long-run average frequency of cooperation. The model does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare directly, but discusses payoff differences and evolutionary stability.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that adding punishment to a public goods game model with group selection allows for the maintenance of high levels of cooperation in much larger groups than group selection alone. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the cost structure of punishment (punishment must be more costly to the punished than to the punisher, and costs to punishers must decline as defectors become rare). The model is robust to various parameter changes, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff; instead, it focuses on the frequency of cooperation and punishment as the main outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that punishment can increase and sustain cooperation in public goods game-like environments, especially in larger groups, under certain cost structures. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is indirect. It is most useful for understanding the mechanisms and conditions under which punishment is likely to have a positive effect on cooperation, which is often correlated with higher efficiency, but this link is not explicitly modeled or measured in terms of payoffs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size (player count) is a key parameter varied in the simulations. The presence of punishment allows cooperation to be sustained in much larger groups than without punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run for 2,000 time periods, but the model is evolutionary and not a repeated game in the standard experimental sense. The number of rounds is fixed for simulation purposes.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main model uses discrete types (cooperator, defector, punisher), but a variant with continuous cooperation and punishment is also analyzed. The main results are for all-or-nothing choices.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The benefit of cooperation is parameterized as b, with per capita benefit b/n in some model variants. The marginal per-capita return is thus present and varied in sensitivity analyses.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to the punisher (k) is a key parameter. Lower costs to punishers (relative to cost to punished) make punishment more effective at sustaining cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (cost to punished per unit cost to punisher, p/k) is varied. Higher effectiveness (higher p relative to k) increases cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- Outcomes are frequencies of cooperation and punishment, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff; implications for efficiency are inferred but not quantified.
- No reward mechanisms or communication are modeled.
- The model assumes group selection and imitation dynamics that may not map directly to laboratory or field public goods games.
