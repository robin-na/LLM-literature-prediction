# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Institutions and Cooperation: A Meta-Analysis of Structural Features in Social Dilemmas
Authors: Jin, S. X., Spadaro, G., & Balliet, D.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- proportion of cooperative choices
- mean contribution
Outcome notes:
The primary outcome is cooperation rate (proportion of cooperative choices or mean contribution), not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The paper does not directly analyze efficiency or group welfare, but focuses on behavioral cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that enabling punishment (and/or reward) in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games robustly increases cooperation rates. The meta-analytic effect size for punishment is d = 0.60 (Study 2), indicating a substantial increase in cooperation when punishment is available. The effect is stronger in repeated interactions (partner matching) than in one-shot (stranger matching) games. Communication is also highly effective. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or net welfare, and notes that prior research (e.g., Bochet et al., 2006) found that punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency due to its cost. Thus, while the evidence for increased cooperation is strong, the effect on efficiency is not directly addressed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games increases cooperation rates, especially in repeated interactions. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment is likely to increase average contributions. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform whether punishment increases, decreases, or leaves unchanged the efficiency of the group (i.e., total payoff relative to full cooperation). The costliness of punishment is not analyzed in relation to efficiency. Therefore, the paper is highly informative for predicting behavioral cooperation, but only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (player count) is coded and analyzed as a moderator. Most studies use small groups (median 3, range 2-324). Within-study meta-analysis finds that larger groups have lower cooperation, but meta-regression across studies finds no effect. No direct analysis of interaction with punishment effects.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Repeated (partner-matching) vs. one-shot (stranger-matching) is coded and analyzed. Punishment is more effective in repeated interactions for increasing cooperation. Number of rounds per se is not always specified, but repeated vs. one-shot is a key moderator.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) is coded and analyzed. Allowing communication robustly increases cooperation rates. No direct analysis of interaction with punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Both dichotomous (all-or-nothing) and continuous contribution games are included and coded. The effect of choice type on cooperation is analyzed, but not in relation to punishment or efficiency.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. MPCR is discussed as a key structural variable (transformed into the K index for analysis). Lower MPCR (higher conflict) reduces cooperation. No direct analysis of interaction with punishment.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Presence of reward is coded and analyzed. Reward increases cooperation rates, similar to punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly analyzed.
- Punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_tech) are not analyzed as moderators; only the presence/absence of punishment is coded.
- The meta-analysis does not distinguish between different implementations of punishment (e.g., costliness, severity, centralization).
- Most studies use small group sizes; generalizability to large groups is limited.
- No direct analysis of whether participants know the number of rounds, see others' contributions, or can identify punishers.
- The paper notes that prior research found punishment may not increase efficiency due to its cost, but does not analyze this directly.
- Heterogeneity in study designs and reporting may limit the precision of moderator analyses.
