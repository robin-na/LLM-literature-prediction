# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: COMPLEX COOPERATIVE STRATEGIES IN GROUP-TERRITORIAL AFRICAN LIONS
Authors: HEINSOHN, R., & PACKER, C.
Journal: Science
Year: 1995

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual latency to approach simulated intruder
- order of approach within group
- number of backward glances to lagging companions
- classification of individuals as leaders or laggards
- variation in lagging behavior as a function of group size and odds
- response to presence of laggards by leaders
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral strategies in group territorial defense among female lions, such as leading or lagging in response to simulated intruders. No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Outcomes are primarily behavioral (latency, order, glances, strategy classification).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that female lions show consistent individual differences in their willingness to participate in group territorial defense, with some acting as leaders and others as laggards. Laggards are recognized by leaders, who become more cautious in their presence but do not punish them or withdraw cooperation. Some laggards adjust their participation based on the odds of group success, while others do not. The diversity of strategies suggests that cooperation in large groups is more complex than predicted by simple reciprocity or punishment models. No evidence of punishment or sanctioning of laggards is found.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the maintenance of cooperation in group settings without formal punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. It suggests that in naturalistic, high-stakes group conflicts, cooperation can persist with a mix of unconditional and conditional cooperators and laggards, and that punishment is not always used to enforce cooperation. However, because the study does not use a public goods game or measure efficiency/payoff outcomes, it offers only adjacent, qualitative insight for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments. It highlights the possibility that cooperation can be stable without punishment, but does not provide quantitative or design-specific evidence for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Prides studied contained 2-7 adult females; group size is discussed as a contextual factor in behavioral strategies.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and naturalistic, not an experimental manipulation of game design dimensions.
- No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; outcomes are behavioral (latency, order, strategy).
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or tested, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be assessed.
- The context (lion territorial defense) is only adjacent to public goods games and may not generalize to laboratory or human PGG settings.
- No quantitative data on group-level outcomes or payoffs; only individual behavioral strategies are analyzed.
