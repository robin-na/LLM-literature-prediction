## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a spatial public goods game, punishment is generally the most effective strategy for promoting cooperation and group efficiency, outperforming both reward and the combined punishment+reward strategy except in very narrow parameter regions. The combined strategy (B) can survive and even outperform elementary strategies only when the cost of punishment/reward is extremely low relative to their impact, and/or when the synergy factor is low (i.e., cooperation is otherwise hard to sustain). However, these conditions are considered unrealistic for most practical or experimental settings. The results suggest that adding reward to punishment does not generally increase group efficiency beyond what punishment alone achieves, and that the evolutionary advantage of combining positive and negative reciprocity is limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, enabling punishment (with or without reward) generally increases group efficiency compared to no punishment, especially when the cost of punishment is not prohibitively high. However, adding reward to punishment does not further increase efficiency except in very narrow, low-cost parameter regions. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with reasonable cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the marginal benefit of also enabling reward is likely negligible except under harsh conditions (low MPCR, low cost). The results are most directly applicable to spatial (networked) PGGs with group size 5, continuous contributions, and symmetric cost/impact for punishment and reward.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; results may not generalize to human behavior in laboratory or field settings.
- The model assumes fixed group size (G=5) and spatial structure (square lattice), which may not match all experimental designs.
- Contribution is not a choice variable; all cooperative strategies contribute a fixed amount.
- No communication, reputation, or identification mechanisms are modeled.
- Parameter regions where the combined strategy outperforms punishment are extremely narrow and may be unrealistic in practice.
- Payoff outcomes are inferred from evolutionary success (strategy prevalence), not directly measured as group earnings or efficiency in the experimental sense.
