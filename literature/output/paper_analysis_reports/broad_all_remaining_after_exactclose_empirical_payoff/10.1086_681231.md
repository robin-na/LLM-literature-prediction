# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do Reputation Systems Undermine Trust? Divergent Effects of Enforcement Type on Generalized Trust and Trustworthiness
Authors: Kuwabara, K.
Journal: American Journal of Sociology
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- generalized trust (measured as amount entrusted in investment game)
- generalized trustworthiness (measured as amount returned in investment game)
- self-reported generalized trust (Likert scale)
- attitude toward opportunism (anti-Machiavellianism)
- reputational concern
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral measures of trust and trustworthiness in investment games, and self-reported attitudes. No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported. The design is based on the investment game, not a public goods game.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Reputation systems, as a form of diffuse, non-material enforcement, reinforce generalized trust and trustworthiness more than contractual (material punishment) or relational (repeated partner) enforcement. After exposure to reputational enforcement, participants are more trusting and trustworthy in subsequent unenforced exchanges than after exposure to contracts or repeated partners. Contractual enforcement, in contrast, can undermine intrinsic motivation to trust or be trustworthy once enforcement is removed. These effects are robust across multiple studies and are supported by both behavioral and self-report measures. However, the study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the experimental paradigm is the investment game, not a public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the effects of punishment-like enforcement (contracts) and reputation systems on subsequent cooperation, but not on efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that material punishment (contracts) can undermine intrinsic motivation for cooperation, while reputation systems can reinforce it. However, because the outcomes are trust/trustworthiness (not efficiency) and the game is not a public goods game, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. The evidence is more relevant for understanding how enforcement mechanisms affect willingness to cooperate after enforcement is removed, rather than direct effects on group efficiency during enforcement.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players: truster and trustee) in the investment game paradigm.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds varies by study: Study 1A (11 rounds), Study 1B (23 rounds), Study 2A (15 rounds), Study 2B (23 rounds), with pretest-posttest design (enforcement removed after initial rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were via web interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/trust decisions are continuous (0-10 points/hours), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Investment game: truster's entrusted amount is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is not a PGG; returns are not shared among a group.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the contract condition, punishment is a material penalty (e.g., 10 points with 90% probability for low-quality service; 4 points per hour in Study 1B). In the reputation condition, punishment is non-material (negative feedback). Material punishment undermines trust/trustworthiness after removal.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Contractual punishment is highly effective (large, certain penalty); reputational punishment is diffuse and indirect. High punishment effectiveness (contract) leads to lower post-enforcement trust/trustworthiness.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the experiment would last up to a certain number of rounds (e.g., 15 in Study 1A), but the actual number of rounds in each phase was not always revealed in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the reputation condition, participants saw reputation scores (positive/negative feedback) for agents. In other conditions, no summary information about others' behavior was provided.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental paradigm is the investment (trust) game, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to PGG environments.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; all outcomes are behavioral (trust/trustworthiness) or self-reported attitudes.
- All partners in the main experiments are simulated and programmed to fully cooperate, which may not reflect real-world variation in partner behavior.
- The reputation system is simple (binary feedback, aggregated), not multidimensional or qualitative as in some real-world systems.
- The effects of enforcement are measured after removal (spillover), not during active enforcement.
- No analysis of group-level outcomes or dynamics; all games are dyadic.
- No explicit manipulation or measurement of communication, group size, or other PGG-relevant dimensions.
