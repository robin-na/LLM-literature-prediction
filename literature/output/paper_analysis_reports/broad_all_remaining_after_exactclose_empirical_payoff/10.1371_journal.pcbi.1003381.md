Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the ranking and abundance of strategies (e.g., cooperators, defectors, punishers) in evolutionary games, including public goods games with punishment. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. Outcomes are about which strategy is most common under different selection intensities, not about group welfare or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in evolutionary games with finite populations, the ranking of strategies by abundance (e.g., cooperation, defection, punishment) can change as the intensity of selection increases. This is shown for public goods games with punishment as well as for general multiplayer and multi-strategy games. The main implication is that predictions based on weak selection (where payoffs have little effect on fitness) may not hold for moderate or strong selection. The paper does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, but rather focuses on which strategies are most common under different evolutionary conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. Instead, it shows that the abundance of strategies (including punishers) can change non-monotonically with selection intensity, and that results from weak selection may not extrapolate to stronger selection. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests caution in using weak selection results to predict efficiency effects of punishment, especially in games with more than two strategies or players. However, since efficiency or payoff outcomes are not analyzed, the paper's direct value for predicting efficiency is limited.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes.
- Outcomes are limited to the abundance and ranking of strategies, not to payoff-based measures.
- The results are based on evolutionary dynamics with rare mutations and may not directly translate to repeated public goods games with human subjects.
- The models assume well-mixed populations and do not address structured populations or network effects.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented.
