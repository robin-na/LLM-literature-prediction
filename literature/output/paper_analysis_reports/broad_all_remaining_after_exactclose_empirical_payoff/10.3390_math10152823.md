## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The negative feedback punishment approach, in which punishment intensity decreases as group cooperation increases, leads to more stable and higher cooperation rates, requires less time to reach high cooperation, and results in lower cumulative punishment intensity (interpreted as lower cost) compared to constant or positive feedback punishment methods. Theoretical analysis shows that this approach allows for stable intermediate levels of cooperation, while constant or positive feedback methods tend to produce unstable or all-or-nothing outcomes. The findings are based on evolutionary simulations of a public goods game with pool punishment, but do not report efficiency or group payoff directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment rule (specifically, negative feedback between cooperation and punishment intensity) can substantially affect the stability and cost of achieving high cooperation in PGG-like environments. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The results suggest that negative feedback punishment rules may reduce the cost (in terms of punishment applied) and time to reach high cooperation, but do not quantify the effect on group efficiency. The findings are most useful for understanding how punishment rule design (punishment_tech) and dynamic adjustment can moderate the behavioral and cost outcomes of punishment institutions.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use n = 100 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each simulation runs for 300 rounds per punishment intention.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: C (cooperate, invest 4) or D (defect, invest 2).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy coefficient mu = 3; each player receives equal share of multiplied pool. With n = 100, MPCR = 3/100 = 0.03.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a deduction from defectors' resources; cost to punisher is not explicitly modeled, as punishment is administered by an institution (pool punishment).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The main manipulation is the mapping from group cooperation to punishment intensity (negative feedback, constant, positive feedback). The negative feedback approach is found to be more stable and less costly in terms of punishment applied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Simulations are for a fixed number of rounds (300), but no explicit mention of whether players know the total number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Players update strategies based on observing others' resources, implying some summary information is available for learning.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings; all main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rate, stability, punishment applied).
- Results are based on evolutionary simulations and theoretical analysis, not empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is implemented as pool punishment by an institution, not peer punishment.
- No analysis of heterogeneity in player count, MPCR, or other game parameters beyond the main simulation setup.
- No reward mechanisms are considered.
- No direct mapping to real-world efficiency or welfare outcomes.
