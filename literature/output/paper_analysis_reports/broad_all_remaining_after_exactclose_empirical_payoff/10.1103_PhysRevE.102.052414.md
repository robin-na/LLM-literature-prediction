The main dependent variables are payoff-based: expected payoffs for cooperators and defectors, and the stationary abundance of cooperation, which is determined by these payoffs in an evolutionary process. The paper also discusses the impact of preference parameters and consensus rules on these outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size N and population size M are explicit model parameters. The effect of group size on cooperation and efficiency is analyzed, with larger groups generally requiring higher willingness to punish for cooperation to dominate.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and does not have discrete rounds in the experimental sense, but rather repeated random matching and updating. The concept of rounds is not directly manipulated.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: individuals choose to cooperate (contribute c) or defect (contribute nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is r/N, where r is the enforcement factor and N is group size. r is varied in the model.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost parameters (G for pool punishment, gamma for peer punishment) are explicit and varied. Lower punishment cost increases the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per unit cost) is parameterized (B for pool, beta for peer). Higher punishment effectiveness increases the impact of punishment on defectors and promotes cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not account for network or spatial structure.
- Punishment and conformity are parameterized as continuous preferences, which may not map directly to discrete experimental treatments.
- No reward mechanisms or positive incentives are considered.
- No communication, reputation, or information feedback is modeled.
- The evolutionary process does not correspond to a fixed, finite number of rounds as in laboratory experiments.
- The model assumes binary contribution choices (all-or-nothing), which may differ from continuous contribution settings.
