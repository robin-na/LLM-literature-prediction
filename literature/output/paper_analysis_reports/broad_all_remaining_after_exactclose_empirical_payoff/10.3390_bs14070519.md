# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Exemplary Self-Discipline, Leniency towards Others: Competitive Contexts Amplify the Black Sheep Effect in Restoring Ingroup Trust
Authors: Cao, N. M., Miao, R. R., Sun, B. H., Ren, Z. R., & Yue, G. A.
Journal: Behavioral Sciences
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment amount in trust game (proxy for trust restoration)
- trust propensity
- trait forgiveness
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount invested by participants in a repeated trust game, interpreted as a measure of trust restoration after a trust violation and apology. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings is reported, though participants' compensation was proportional to performance.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that after a trust violation and apology, participants are more willing to restore trust (measured by increased investment) with out-group members than with in-group members, but this effect is only present in competitive contexts. In cooperative contexts, trust is restored equally for both in-group and out-group members. The results are interpreted as evidence for the 'black sheep effect' in trust repair, moderated by the nature of the interaction (competition vs. cooperation). The outcome is investment behavior, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It uses a repeated trust game (not a PGG), and the main outcome is trust restoration (investment behavior) rather than efficiency or group payoff. There is no explicit punishment mechanism; the 'punishment' is implicit in reduced trust/investment after a violation. The findings may be relevant for understanding how group identity and context affect willingness to restore cooperation after norm violations, but they do not provide quantitative or direct evidence about efficiency or payoff outcomes in PGG-like games with explicit punishment options.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trust game is played between a single participant (trustor) and a simulated trustee (computer), so the effective player count per game is 2.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trust game consists of 7 rounds: 4 trust-building, 2 trust-violation, 1 trust-repair.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between participants; all interactions are via the game interface and pre-scripted messages (e.g., apology).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment is continuous: participants choose any amount between 0 and 100 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Investment is tripled (multiplier = 3), but since this is a trust game (not a PGG), the concept of MPCR is not directly applicable. For each token invested, the trustee receives 3 tokens.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are aware of the round structure and sequence (7 rounds, with specific phases for trust-building, violation, and repair).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are shown the outcome of each round (how much was returned by the trustee). No summary statistics about others, as each participant interacts with a simulated partner.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a repeated trust game, not a public goods game or a direct PGG variant.
- There is no explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism; 'punishment' is inferred from reduced trust/investment after a violation.
- The main outcome is behavioral (investment/trust restoration), not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- All interactions are with a simulated trustee (computer), not real human partners.
- The minimal group paradigm may not capture real-world group dynamics or ecological validity.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or payoff-based outcomes relevant to the downstream prediction task.
