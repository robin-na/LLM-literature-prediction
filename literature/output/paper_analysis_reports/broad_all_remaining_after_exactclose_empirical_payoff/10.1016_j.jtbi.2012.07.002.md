## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the efficiency of cooperation in group-structured indirect reciprocity games depends critically on the social norm used for reputation assignment and the frequency of intragroup versus intergroup interactions. Full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency, with payoff b-c per round) is stable only when all observers use a 'serious' social norm (standing or judging) for all types of interactions. If observers use double standards (different norms for ingroup and outgroup interactions), then ingroup favoritism emerges, leading to lower efficiency (payoff reduced by the fraction of intergroup interactions). The key condition for any cooperation to be stable is b*r_in > c, where r_in is the probability of intragroup interaction. The model shows that changing the social norm (e.g., forcing consistency across interaction types) can shift the equilibrium from inefficient ingroup favoritism to efficient full cooperation. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward, but reputation loss/gain serves a similar function.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting efficiency in indirect reciprocity games with group structure. It shows that the efficiency outcome (full cooperation, partial, or ingroup favoritism) is determined by the social norm for reputation assignment and the structure of interactions (r_in). In the absence of explicit punishment or reward, but with reputation-based indirect reciprocity, efficiency is maximized only if the social norm is consistent and 'serious' (standing/judging) across all interaction types. If the game design allows or encourages double standards (different norms for ingroup/outgroup), expect lower efficiency due to ingroup favoritism. The model's efficiency results are directly parameterized by the benefit-to-cost ratio and the frequency of intragroup interaction, which can be mapped to MPCR and group structure in PGG-like games. However, the absence of explicit punishment/reward means the results are most applicable to games where reputation is the main enforcement mechanism.

## Important Limitations
- The model is purely theoretical and assumes infinite population and infinite rounds, which may not capture finite-group or finite-horizon effects.
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are modeled; only reputation-based indirect reciprocity is considered.
- The model does not address evolutionary dynamics or the likelihood of different equilibria emerging in practice.
- All players are assumed to use the same social norm, which may not reflect real-world heterogeneity.
- The model does not include communication, explicit group competition, or migration between groups.
- Results may not generalize to settings where punishment or reward is explicit rather than reputation-based.
