Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based: total payoffs, resource exhaustion, and resource levels. Behavioral outcomes such as voting, frequency of institution, and reactions to punishment are also analyzed, but the primary focus is on efficiency and group welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated CPR lab experiment with three-person groups, the introduction of collective (pool) punishment (10P) does not significantly improve efficiency or payoffs compared to the baseline without punishment. Only a minority of groups in the 10P treatment maintain higher resource levels and payoffs. When second-order punishment (20P) is added (punishing both over-harvesters and tax evaders), the sanctioning institution persists, but efficiency and payoffs decline: subjects extract more to compensate for tax costs, and resource exhaustion becomes more frequent. The effect of punishment is thus context-dependent: collective punishment can promote conservation in some groups, but second-order punishment undermines efficiency overall. The paper provides direct evidence on group payoffs, resource exhaustion, and the mechanisms by which punishment affects behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in CPR-like environments is not uniformly positive and depends on the institutional design. Pool punishment (collective, voluntary tax-funded) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to no punishment, and second-order punishment (punishing tax evaders) can reduce efficiency by increasing extraction and resource collapse. For prediction, the presence of second-order punishment is a strong negative moderator for efficiency, while voluntary collective punishment has a mixed or null effect. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to guarantee higher efficiency; the details of the punishment institution and group responses are critical. Control efficiency is a strong predictor, but the effect of punishment depends on whether it is voluntary, enforced, and how costs are distributed.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game lasts up to 30 rounds, but subjects are not told the exact number to avoid endgame effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvest) is continuous; players can extract fractions of tokens.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishers pay a tax (0.5, 0.25, or 0.15 tokens depending on number of payers) to establish the institution; punished players lose 1 token. In 20P, tax evaders are also punished.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is 1 token lost per punished player, regardless of harvest size. Effectiveness depends on the relative size of penalty to harvest.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are not told the total number of rounds; only that the experiment will not exceed 1.5 hours.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see the harvests of each co-player (in random order, identities not revealed).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is collective and anonymous; players do not know who voted for which punishment threshold or who paid the tax.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a CPR (common-pool resource) game, not a standard linear PGG, so results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- Punishment is implemented as a collective institution, not as individual peer punishment.
- The penalty is fixed (1 token), not proportional to harvest, which may affect perceptions of fairness and effectiveness.
- No communication is allowed, which may limit the external validity for real-world resource dilemmas.
- The sample is from a single university and country, which may limit generalizability.
- The number of rounds is unknown to participants, which may affect strategic behavior.
- The effect of punishment is highly heterogeneous across groups, making prediction less certain.
