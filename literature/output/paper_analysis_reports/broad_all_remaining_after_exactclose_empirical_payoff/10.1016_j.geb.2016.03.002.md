Outcome notes:
The paper is theoretical and focuses on equilibrium selection in 2x2 games, including a mini public goods game with punishment. It does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, but rather analyzes which equilibrium (cooperative or asocial) is selected under quantal response equilibrium (QRE) as a function of punishment strength and game parameters.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a mini public goods game with punishment, the QRE method selects a cooperative equilibrium if the punishment is sufficiently strong (i.e., if the fine-to-fee ratio is high enough). The selection of cooperation depends on the effectiveness and cost of punishment. For intermediate punishment, cooperation can dominate for some parameter values even if the limiting QRE is defection. The results are derived analytically and illustrated with theoretical examples, but no empirical or simulated efficiency or payoff data are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical conditions for when punishment will lead to the selection of a cooperative equilibrium in a 2x2 mini public goods game. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it implies that strong punishment can shift equilibrium selection from defection to cooperation, which would be expected to increase efficiency in a public goods context. The results are most directly relevant for games with two players, binary choices, and explicit punishment options, and can inform predictions about the qualitative effect of punishment strength and cost on equilibrium selection, but not quantitative efficiency outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is purely theoretical and does not report or simulate efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- The results are specific to 2x2 games (two players, binary choices) and may not generalize to larger groups or more complex public goods games.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes one-shot interactions; repeated game effects are not considered.
- Payoff-based outcomes such as efficiency are not directly analyzed, only equilibrium selection (cooperation vs. defection).
