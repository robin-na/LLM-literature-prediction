## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated dyadic cooperation games with involuntary defection, unconditional punishment (retaliation for any defection) undermines cooperation and reduces efficiency, as it leads to cycles of mutual defection. In contrast, an empathic retaliator strategy—where punishment is withheld if the opponent's defection follows one's own involuntary defection—can be evolutionarily stable and support high efficiency, especially when the expected number of repeated interactions is large. The stability and efficiency of cooperation depend on the cost of helping, the probability of being able to help, and the expected number of interaction cycles. The paper provides explicit conditions and payoff matrices for these outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the design of punishment mechanisms critically affects efficiency in repeated cooperation games. Specifically, unconditional punishment (retaliation) in the presence of involuntary defection leads to lower efficiency, while empathic (conditional) punishment can sustain high efficiency if repeated interactions are frequent. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on whether punishment is unconditional or conditional/empathic, the frequency of repeated interactions, and the possibility of involuntary defection. However, the model is not a public goods game, and punishment is not costly in the sense of standard PGG experiments; rather, it is refusal to help. Thus, direct quantitative predictions for PGGs should be made with caution, but the qualitative insight is that punishment can either harm or help efficiency depending on its design and the game context.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a public goods game but a repeated dyadic helping game; results may not transfer directly to multi-player PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as refusal to help (retaliatory defection), not as costly punishment in the standard PGG sense.
- No empirical or experimental data are provided; all results are theoretical.
- The model assumes perfect memory and strategy implementation, except for involuntary defection.
- No consideration of communication, group size greater than two, or explicit reward mechanisms.
