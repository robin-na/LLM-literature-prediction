## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that allowing or encouraging costly punishment in a donor-recipient game increases both the size of the basin of attraction for the cooperative evolutionary stable state (CESS) and the speed at which a society converges to this high-payoff state. Under parameter settings where punishment is not too costly, the presence of punishment options increases the probability that a society will escape the non-cooperative trap and reach high efficiency. The effect is stronger when the social norm actively encourages punishment. However, in the long run, after full cooperation is achieved, continued costly punishment can reduce efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs. The findings are based on mathematical modeling and numerical simulations, not empirical data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated social dilemma environments with reputation and norm dynamics, enabling costly punishment increases the likelihood and speed of reaching high-efficiency cooperative states, especially when the social norm encourages punishment. The effect is most pronounced in 'developing' societies or initial conditions with many defectors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment (especially with supportive norms) will generally increase efficiency relative to control, unless punishment is extremely costly. However, in fully cooperative steady states, punishment may reduce efficiency due to its cost. The model is not a standard PGG but is structurally similar, so findings are likely transferable to PGG-like settings with reputation and norm evolution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: cooperate (give), defect (ignore), or punish. No continuous contribution levels.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The benefit-to-cost ratio (b/c) is a key parameter; cooperation costs c and gives benefit b to the recipient. Equivalent to MPCR in two-player context.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs \alpha to the punisher per act; \alpha is varied in analysis.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment inflicts cost \beta on the punished per act; \beta is varied in analysis.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reputation of each individual is public information; players can condition actions on others' reputations.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game but a donor-recipient game with reputation and norm evolution; results may not fully generalize to PGGs with group interactions.
- No empirical or experimental data; all findings are theoretical and based on mathematical modeling and simulation.
- No fixed group size or player count; the model assumes a large, well-mixed population.
- No communication, reward, or more complex strategies (e.g., antisocial punishment) are modeled.
- Long-run efficiency can be reduced by continued costly punishment after full cooperation is reached, which may not be captured in short-run or static analyses.
