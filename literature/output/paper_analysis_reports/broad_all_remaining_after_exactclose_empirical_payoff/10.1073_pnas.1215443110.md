# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Similarity increases altruistic punishment in humans
Authors: Mussweiler, T., & Ockenfels, A.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned (altruistic punishment)
- conditional punishment as a function of opponent's cooperation
- cooperation level in initial transfer phase
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of costly punishment assigned to the partner, conditional on their cooperation. The paper also reports cooperation levels in the initial phase, but does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as primary outcomes. Payoff is only discussed as a mechanism or in the context of the punishment cost structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that perceived similarity (induced by a cognitive priming task) increases the willingness to engage in costly punishment of uncooperative behavior in a two-person cooperation game with a punishment phase. In contrast, group identity (in-group vs. out-group) reduces altruistic punishment toward in-group members. The effect of similarity is not explained by group identity, and the two have opposite effects on punishment. The study does not directly measure or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the cooperation phase is single-shot, so there is no opportunity for punishment to feed back into increased cooperation or efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that psychological framing (similarity focus vs. group identity) can significantly alter the frequency and intensity of costly punishment in a cooperation game with a punishment phase. However, it does not provide direct evidence on how punishment affects efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate or report most of the structural game design dimensions relevant for predicting efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding psychological moderators of punishment behavior, rather than for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design parameters. Thus, its direct value for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) cooperation games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Single-shot (one-shot) game: one cooperation phase and one punishment phase per participant.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and punishment choices are continuous (any portion of endowment can be transferred or used for punishment). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each euro sent is doubled (multiplier = 2), so for two players, MPCR = 1.0.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: For each €1 reduction in the other’s payoff, the punisher pays €0.50.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: Each €0.50 spent reduces the other’s payoff by €1 (punishment multiplier = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is single-shot (one cooperation phase, one punishment phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed of the other’s cooperation level before assigning punishment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not know the identity of their partner.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not directly analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; the main outcome is punishment behavior.
- The game is single-shot, so there is no opportunity for punishment to feed back into increased cooperation or efficiency.
- Most game design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant and not varied experimentally.
- The findings are specific to psychological manipulations of similarity and group identity, and may not generalize to structural game design changes.
- No direct evidence is provided on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff.
