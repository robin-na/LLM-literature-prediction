# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Resource scarcity, and democratic elections in commons dilemmas: An experiment on forest use in Ethiopia
Authors: Gatiso, T. T., Vollan, B., & Nuppenau, E. A.
Journal: Ecological Economics
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- harvest ratio (HR): ratio of individual harvest to maximum allowed
- proportion of participants harvesting maximum allowed
- cooperation level (inferred from HR)
- conditional cooperation/reciprocity
- effect of sanctions (imposed/elected) on HR
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the harvest ratio (HR), which is a behavioral measure of cooperation. The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes, though it does provide theoretical efficiency levels in Table 3. All main analyses and regressions focus on HR, not on payoff or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that resource scarcity (lower initial stock) leads to less cooperative behavior (higher HR), and that the way sanctions are implemented matters: democratically elected sanctions increase cooperation (lower HR) both under abundance and scarcity, while imposed sanctions can reduce cooperation (higher HR) under abundance (crowding-out effect), but not under scarcity. The main outcome is behavioral (HR), not efficiency or group payoff. Theoretical efficiency levels are provided, but not empirically analyzed as outcomes. The findings suggest that participatory rule-making is more effective than top-down sanctions for sustaining cooperation, especially when resources are not extremely scarce.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how the method of sanction implementation (election vs. imposition) moderates the effect of punishment on cooperation in a common-pool resource game with real-world framing. However, because the main outcome is behavioral (harvest ratio) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the results must be mapped with caution to efficiency prediction tasks. Theoretical efficiency levels are given, but empirical results focus on HR. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, no explicit MPCR, probabilistic punishment, no rewards, summary info shown, anonymous punishment) are well described, supporting mapping to prediction models. The main implication is that democratically elected sanctions are more likely to increase cooperation (and thus efficiency) than imposed sanctions, especially under resource abundance. Under scarcity, the difference is smaller, but elections still outperform imposition.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions used groups of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game lasted up to 10 rounds, or until resource collapse.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No interpersonal communication was allowed in the game or between games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (harvest) was continuous: each participant could choose any amount up to the maximum allowed per round.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a deduction of trees (twice the amount overharvested) if caught; cost is in the form of lost trees, not explicit monetary cost to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic: 1/5 chance of being monitored per round; if caught, twice the excess harvest is deducted from the individual's account.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. No explicit reward mechanism; however, at the end of the game, the remaining forest stock is shared equally among participants as a conservation bonus.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told the game would last 10 rounds or until the resource collapsed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the total group harvest at the end of every round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: it was not communicated to the group if someone was caught overharvesting.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is behavioral (harvest ratio), not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is only theoretically discussed.
- The experiment uses a dynamic CPR extraction game, not a standard linear PGG, so mapping to PGG efficiency may require caution.
- Punishment is probabilistic and implemented by the experimenter, not by peer assignment, which may differ from standard PGG punishment designs.
- No explicit MPCR; the resource dynamics are nonlinear and context-specific.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency or total earnings as a primary outcome.
- The sample is from a specific Ethiopian forest community, which may limit generalizability.
