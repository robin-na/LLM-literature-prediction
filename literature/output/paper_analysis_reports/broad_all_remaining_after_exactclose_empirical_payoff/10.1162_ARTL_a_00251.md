## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes institutionally coordinated punishment and reward, not individual-level punishment as in many lab experiments.
- The mapping between model parameters and real-world public goods game designs is indirect; some dimensions (e.g., chat, default contribution, knowledge of rounds) are not modeled.
- The model assumes infinite or very long time horizons (millions of generations), which may not correspond to typical experimental settings.
- Payoff and efficiency outcomes are determined by model structure and parameterization, not by observed human behavior.
- The model does not address the effects of communication, reputation, or other social mechanisms outside institutional rules.
