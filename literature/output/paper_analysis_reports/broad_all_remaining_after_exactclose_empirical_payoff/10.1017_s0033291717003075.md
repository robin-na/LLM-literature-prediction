## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that paranoia is associated with reduced generosity in both the Dictator and Ultimatum Games, and with a greater tendency to punish in the Dictator Game. The effect of paranoia on punishment is mediated by harmful intent attributions. There is no evidence that paranoia increases Ultimatum Game offers (contrary to a distrust-based account), and paranoia does not reliably predict rejection of Ultimatum Game offers. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, and the games are not public goods games but are adjacent in that they involve costly punishment and cooperation decisions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. The games studied are two-player allocation games (Dictator and Ultimatum), not multi-player public goods games. The main outcomes are behavioral (offers, donations, punishment/rejection), not efficiency or group payoff. However, the study does provide evidence that the availability of punishment increases punitive behavior, especially among individuals with higher paranoia, and that punishment is more likely when the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable. For prediction tasks involving efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, suggesting that punishment may be more frequent when individuals attribute harmful intent, but it does not address efficiency or welfare outcomes.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dictator/receiver or proposer/responder).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played each game once per wave, with two waves; games are one-shot, not repeated.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/offer choices are continuous in increments ($0.05) from $0.00 up to the full endowment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the Dictator Game, punishment cost is $0.05 to the punisher to reduce the dictator's bonus by $0.15 (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3). In the Ultimatum Game, rejection is costlier and variable.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dictator Game: $0.05 cost yields $0.15 fine (1:3). Ultimatum Game: rejection cost varies from 1:9 to 1:1 depending on offer size.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; participants know the structure and that each game is played once per wave.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Receivers are informed of both players' starting bonuses and the dictator's decision. No mention of further summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The games studied are not public goods games or their close variants; they are two-player allocation games (Dictator and Ultimatum Games).
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed; all outcomes are behavioral (offers, donations, punishment/rejection decisions).
- Punishment is only available in the Dictator Game, and rejection in the Ultimatum Game is interpreted as costly punishment, but the structure differs from standard PGG punishment.
- The study focuses on individual differences in paranoia, not on the effect of punishment as a treatment or intervention.
- The online sample may not be fully representative of the general population, though the authors argue it is more representative than typical student samples.
- Stake sizes are small, and the games are one-shot rather than repeated.
