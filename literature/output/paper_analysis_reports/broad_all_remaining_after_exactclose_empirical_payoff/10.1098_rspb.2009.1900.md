# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Eyes are on us, but nobody cares: are eye cues relevant for strong reciprocity?
Authors: Fehr, E., & Schneider, F.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average back-transfer
- relative back-transfer (share of points returned over points received)
- average investment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- trustee choice (nothing, compensate, equalize)
- trustor investment choice
- Machiavellianism score
- fairness standard
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the trustee's back-transfer (absolute and relative), which is a behavioral measure of reciprocity/altruism, not group efficiency or total payoff. Average investment by trustors is also reported. Group efficiency (total group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly calculated, but can be inferred from the payoff matrix and average behaviors.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that subtle, implicit reputation cues (eyespots) do not increase trustees' altruistic behavior in a trust game, nor do they affect trustors' willingness to invest. In contrast, explicit pecuniary reputation incentives—where trustees' past back-transfers are revealed to future trustors—lead to a large and significant increase in both trustees' back-transfers and trustors' investments. This effect is particularly pronounced among individuals with high Machiavellianism scores. The results suggest that explicit, material reputation incentives are powerful drivers of prosocial behavior, while implicit cues without real consequences are ineffective in this context. The findings cast doubt on the hypothesis that most prosocial behavior in anonymous experiments is due to uncontrolled implicit reputation cues.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that explicit, material reputation incentives (a form of positive sanctioning or reward) can substantially increase prosocial behavior and, by implication, group efficiency in trust-based economic games. However, it also shows that implicit cues (such as eyespots) have no such effect. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of explicit reputation or reward mechanisms is a key moderator of efficiency, while subtle cues without real payoff consequences are not. The results are adjacent to public goods games: the trust game is a two-player, sequential, one-shot game repeated with random rematching, not a standard PGG, and there is no punishment mechanism. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation or reward is at stake, and less so for punishment-based designs. The paper does not provide direct evidence on punishment, but supports the importance of explicit incentive structures for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trust game is played between two players (trustor and trustee). Sessions involved 36 participants, but the game is strictly dyadic.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 10 periods (rounds) of the trust game, with random rematching each period.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; subjects were seated in isolated compartments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices (trustor investment) are discrete: 1, 4, 7, or 10 points. Trustee can choose among three discrete options: nothing, compensate, or equalize.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The trustor's investment is quadrupled (multiplied by 4) and given to the trustee. For the trustor, the marginal per-capita return is 0 (since only the trustee receives the multiplied amount), but for the trustee, the return is 4 per unit invested by the trustor.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the explicit reputation treatment, trustees can build a reputation for rewarding trustors, which leads to higher investments and payoffs. This is a form of positive incentive (reward via future trustor behavior).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the trustee for rewarding (back-transferring) is the amount given up (i.e., the trustee gives up own payoff to increase the trustor's payoff).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the amount the trustee chooses to back-transfer; the impact is direct and specified in the payoff matrix.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were 10 periods (rounds) in the experiment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the explicit reputation treatment, trustors are informed of the trustee's decisions in previous periods (i.e., summary of past back-transfers). In baseline and implicit cue treatments, no such information is provided.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game, not a public goods game or a direct variant; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; only positive reciprocity (reward) is studied.
- Efficiency (total group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly reported; only back-transfers and investments are measured.
- The study focuses on the effect of reputation cues and incentives, not on punishment or negative sanctions.
- The game is dyadic (two players), not multi-player as in standard PGGs.
- Behavioral outcomes are used as proxies for efficiency, but actual efficiency calculations are not provided.
