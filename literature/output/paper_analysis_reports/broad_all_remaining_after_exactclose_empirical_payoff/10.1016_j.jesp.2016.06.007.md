# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Third parties promote cooperative norms in repeated interactions
Authors: Nakashima, N. A., Halali, E., & Halevy, N.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total earnings (reported as average earnings per role)
- joint profits (self-reported motivation)
- individual profits (self-reported motivation)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (adversaries)
- intervention rate (third party)
- conditional cooperation
- conditional intervention
- self-reported motivations
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are cooperation rates (adversaries) and intervention rates (third party). Payoff-related outcomes are reported as average total earnings per role, but efficiency or group payoff is not the primary focus. Some analyses use self-reported motivation to maximize joint or individual profits, but the main analyses are on behavioral outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Third party intervention in repeated adversarial interactions (modeled as a Peacemaker Game) increases cooperation rates among adversaries. Early intervention is more effective at establishing persistent cooperative norms than late intervention. Third parties are more likely to intervene when adversaries have a history of cooperation, and adversaries are more likely to cooperate following third party intervention. The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation and intervention rates), not efficiency or group payoff. The study provides indirect evidence that mechanisms which combine reward and punishment can transform competitive repeated interactions into more cooperative ones, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the idea that enabling third-party interventions (which combine reward and punishment) can increase cooperation in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-like settings. However, because the main outcomes are cooperation and intervention rates, not efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used as suggestive rather than directly predictive for efficiency outcomes. The game structure is adjacent to public goods games, and the intervention mechanism is not a standard peer punishment or reward system. The results suggest that early, consistent intervention is more effective at establishing cooperation than late intervention, and that the presence of a third party who can both reward and punish can transform group behavior. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that enabling such mechanisms is likely to increase cooperation, but the quantitative effect on efficiency is not directly reported.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: 2 adversaries and 1 third party.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 60 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: cooperate or compete (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG; in the Prisoner's Dilemma phase, mutual cooperation yields 0.30 each, mutual defection 0.20 each, unilateral cooperation 0.10/0.40. In the Maximizing Difference Game (intervention), mutual cooperation yields 0.40 each, mutual defection 0.10 each, unilateral 0.20/0.30. No explicit MPCR, but the payoff structure is fully specified.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third party intervention is costly if both adversaries compete (third party gets 0.10 vs 0.25 for not intervening), but beneficial if both cooperate (third party gets 0.40). The cost is not a per-unit punishment but a bundled intervention.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Intervention changes the payoff structure for adversaries: it simultaneously rewards cooperation (+0.10) and punishes competition (-0.10) per adversary. The 'technology' is a bundled transformation, not a per-unit punishment.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Intervention both rewards cooperation and punishes competition; reward and punishment are bundled.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding cooperation is part of the bundled intervention; third party's payoff increases if both adversaries cooperate. No explicit per-unit cost for reward, but the third party's payoff is contingent on adversaries' choices.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is delivered by increasing adversaries' payoffs for cooperation by 0.10 each when intervention occurs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the experiment involved multiple rounds but did not know the exact number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players received feedback about the choices and profits of all players in their group, including their own.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the roles (red, blue, green) and could see which role intervened, but all interactions were anonymous and roles were fixed. No ambiguity about who intervened.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation and intervention rates), not efficiency or group payoff.
- The game is not a standard public goods game; it is a repeated three-player Prisoner's Dilemma with a third-party intervention mechanism.
- Punishment and reward are bundled in the intervention; there is no separate, voluntary, per-unit punishment or reward as in standard PGG punishment treatments.
- No direct analysis of efficiency, welfare, or group payoff as a primary outcome.
- Players did not know the total number of rounds in advance.
- No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- The intervention mechanism is not peer punishment or reward, but a third-party transformation of the payoff structure.
