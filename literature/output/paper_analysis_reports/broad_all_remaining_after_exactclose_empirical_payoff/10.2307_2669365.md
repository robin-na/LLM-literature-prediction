# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation, reciprocity, and the collective-action heuristic
Authors: Lubell, M., & Scholz, J. T.
Journal: American Journal of Political Science
Year: 2001

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- percentage of cooperative choices
- cooperation rate
- effect of niceness and reciprocity on cooperation
- effect of penalties on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the percentage of cooperative choices (i.e., contribution/investment decisions) in repeated public goods games. The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, but does discuss the implications of cooperation rates for payoffs and social capital.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiments show that both reciprocity and niceness in the environment increase cooperation rates, but their effects depend on whether subjects are initially predisposed to cooperate or defect. Past experience with reciprocity increases cooperation even when current reciprocity is absent, while past nonreciprocity does not reduce cooperation when reciprocity is present. The introduction of punishment (penalties) increases cooperation among defectors and in nonreciprocal environments, but can reduce cooperation among initial cooperators in reciprocal environments. The effect of punishment is thus asymmetric: it is most beneficial in environments lacking reciprocity, but may undermine cooperation where social capital is already high. The main outcome is cooperation rate, not direct efficiency or group payoff, but the authors discuss the implications for the evolution of cooperation and social capital.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by implication, efficiency) depends on the underlying level of reciprocity and the initial disposition of players. Punishment increases cooperation in environments where reciprocity is low (nonreciprocal), but can reduce cooperation among those already inclined to cooperate in high-reciprocity environments. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the marginal effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive in low-cooperation, low-reciprocity settings, but may be neutral or even negative in high-cooperation, high-reciprocity settings. However, since the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, these implications are inferred from cooperation rates. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are well specified, but the main outcome is behavioral (cooperation), not payoff-based.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use eight-person public goods games (one human subject, seven simulated partners).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are repeated for a minimum of 16 and a maximum of 25 rounds, with a 0.25 probability of ending each round after round 16. Subjects are not told in advance when the game will end.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No subject discussion or communication is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is dichotomous: invest (cooperate) $4 or not invest (defect) each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each $4 contribution is doubled and split among 8 players: MPCR = 2/8 = 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a penalty (fine) imposed on defectors, with no explicit cost to the punisher (the institution/experimenter imposes the penalty).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is varied by changing the penalty amount ($0.10 to $3.00) and detection probability (0 to 0.5).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are not told in advance when the game will end; the number of rounds is uncertain.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects can see their own decision history, payoffs, and cumulative payoff. It is not explicitly stated whether they see others' contributions, but the design implies that the number of cooperators in each round is observable (since the environment is manipulated to be nice/nasty/reciprocal).

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is cooperation rate, not direct efficiency, group payoff, or welfare. Efficiency effects are inferred, not measured.
- Punishment is implemented as an institutional penalty, not as peer-to-peer punishment; there is no cost to the punisher.
- Payoffs are hypothetical (not real money), which may affect external validity.
- All other players are simulated by computer, not real human subjects.
- The design does not include a reward or positive sanction mechanism.
- The effect of punishment on actual group efficiency or surplus is not directly reported.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability.
