# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Paying to punish on a free ride: member and nonmember punishments in voluntary coalitions
Authors: McEvoy, D. M.
Journal: Applied Economics Letters
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- public good provision (contribution level)
- coalition participation
- punishment frequency (sanctioning noncompliance and nonparticipation)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution to the public good, coalition participation, and punishment behavior. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare directly, but public good provision is closely related.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a public good game with voluntary coalition formation, allowing voluntary punishment (where any player can punish any other) does not increase public good provision relative to no punishment. However, when coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance (quasi-voluntary punishment), public good provision increases significantly. Both coalition members and nonmembers are equally likely to punish noncompliance, and the extent of nonmember punishment does not change when members can credibly enforce compliance. The main outcomes are contribution levels and punishment frequencies; efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper suggests that simply enabling voluntary punishment (without credible commitment) is not sufficient to increase efficiency or public good provision in coalition-based public good games. Only when punishment is structured so that coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance does cooperation (and likely efficiency) increase. For prediction, the presence of a credible, committed punishment mechanism is a key moderator; voluntary, non-committed punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per session; fixed throughout.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 12 periods (rounds) per session.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is a discrete choice: contribute full endowment or not.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player’s endowment is $7; public benefit of contributing is $3 per player, so MPCR = 3/7 ≈ 0.43.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as refusal to trade: both players forgo $4 if either refuses. Thus, cost to punisher is $4 per punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact is $4 loss to both punisher and target per refusal to trade.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know there are 12 periods (rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are provided with complete information regarding other players' decisions from coalition and compliance stages before punishment/trading decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented via bilateral trading decisions; identity of the punisher is known to the punished.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported; only public good provision (contribution) is measured.
- The punishment mechanism is implemented via refusal to trade, which may differ from standard monetary deduction punishments.
- The game structure involves endogenous coalition formation, which is not standard in all PGGs.
- Results may not generalize to settings with different group sizes, MPCRs, or punishment/reward structures.
