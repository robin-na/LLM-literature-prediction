# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Do professions curb free-riding? An experiment
Authors: Krawczyk, M., & Szczygielski, K.
Journal: European Journal of Law and Economics
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/investment in public account
- punishment assigned
- change in investment following punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on behavioral outcomes: investment/contribution levels, punishment behavior, and changes in investment. It does not report group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or surplus. Payoff outcomes are only discussed in the context of game mechanics and illustrative examples, not as dependent variables.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that allowing peer punishment in a standard linear PGG leads to typical patterns: punishment is directed at low contributors and can increase subsequent contributions. However, the main treatment—group homogeneity—does not significantly affect investment or punishment. Homogeneous groups (same profession) do not cooperate more; if anything, they may cooperate less. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that, in this design, punishment works similarly regardless of group composition, and that group homogeneity does not enhance cooperation when punishment is available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides a clear example of a standard PGG with punishment, but does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, it confirms that punishment is implemented in a typical way (costly to punisher, more costly to target, anonymous, with summary feedback), and that the presence of punishment supports cooperation (as in prior literature). The lack of efficiency data limits its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes, but the behavioral results are consistent with the literature showing that punishment can sustain cooperation. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, etc.) are well specified and can inform parameter mapping for prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of four players per session, fixed matching.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ten periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players can allocate any amount (0-20 points) to the public account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.375 (public account multiplied by 1.5, split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point deducts 3 points from the punished player (punishment effectiveness 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told there are 10 periods in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see the contributions of all other group members after each allocation phase.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are told how many punishment points they received, but not the identity of the punisher(s).

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, total earnings, or welfare as dependent variables; only behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) are analyzed.
- The sample consists of students and recent graduates, not actual professionals, which may limit generalizability.
- The main treatment (group homogeneity) is not a standard game design dimension for prediction tasks.
- No control condition without punishment is included, so the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The experiment is limited to a single MPCR, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness setting.
