# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social influence on third-party punishment: An experiment
Authors: Fabbri, M., & Carbonara, E.
Journal: Journal of Economic Psychology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- third-party punishment assigned (tokens used to punish)
- conformity to peer punishment levels
- change in punishment after social information
- probability of revising punishment toward peer average
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: punishment decisions by third parties, and the extent to which these are revised toward peer averages after social information. No direct analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs is conducted. Payoff formulas are described, but outcomes are not reported or analyzed in efficiency terms.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that social information about peer punishment decisions leads third-party punishers to revise their own punishment levels toward the peer average. This effect is robust and stronger when conformity requires increasing punishment, even though this reduces the punisher's own payoff. The addition of a normative social influence channel (emoticon feedback) does not significantly change punishment behavior compared to informational social influence alone. The study does not report or analyze efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral conformity in punishment decisions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that social information about peer punishment can substantially alter third-party punishment behavior, increasing conformity to peer norms. However, it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-like environments. The design is a modified dictator game with third-party punishment, not a public goods game, and the main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, conformity), not efficiency. The findings may be relevant for understanding how social information moderates punishment behavior, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: receiver (A), dictator (B), and third-party punisher (C).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes punishment decisions in two periods (before and after social information). The payment period is randomly selected from these two.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication among participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (dictator take) and punishment choices are both continuous in discrete steps (take: 0-30 in steps of 5; punishment: 0-20 tokens). Not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For each token used by the punisher, the dictator loses 4 tokens; the punisher pays 1 token per unit of punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4:1 (each token spent by punisher reduces dictator's payoff by 4 tokens).
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In the info+normative treatment, peers can send a smiley (reward) or frowny (sanction) emoticon, but this has no effect on earnings. No material reward mechanism for the main game.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Emoticon reward/sanction is costless and has no effect on payoffs.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Emoticon reward/sanction is symbolic only; no material impact.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure: two periods, with one randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the informational and info+normative treatments, participants are shown the average punishment assigned by peers for each dictator action.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the info+normative treatment, individual punishment choices are shown to 5 randomly selected peers (anonymized by ID), who then vote on emoticon feedback.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a modified dictator game with third-party punishment, not a public goods game or a direct PGG variant.
- No efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed; all main outcomes are behavioral (punishment assigned, conformity).
- The effect of punishment on group efficiency or welfare cannot be inferred from the reported results.
- The social influence manipulation is specific to punishment behavior and may not generalize to efficiency outcomes in public-goods-like environments.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants; external validity to real-world or field settings is untested.
- Potential experimenter demand effects due to the design (eliciting punishment decisions immediately after providing peer information).
