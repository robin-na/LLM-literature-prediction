# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Endogenous group formation
Authors: Ahn, T. K., Isaac, R. M., & Salmon, T. C.
Journal: Journal of Public Economic Theory
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual earnings
- group earnings
- group size
- efficiency (implied by earnings relative to group optimum)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- group formation/membership
- success/failure of entry/exit applications
- voting behavior on entry/exit
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are individual and group earnings (payoff), group size, and contribution levels. Efficiency is discussed in terms of earnings relative to the group optimum. Behavioral outcomes such as contribution and group formation are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that group formation rules significantly affect both contribution behavior and efficiency in a public goods environment. Restricted entry (a form of social punishment) leads to higher contributions in mid-sized groups but smaller group sizes, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to free entry/exit or restricted exit. Restricted exit leads to declining contributions over time and does not improve efficiency. The ability to punish by denying entry is more effective at increasing contributions than the ability to punish by reducing one's own contribution. However, the efficiency gains from higher contributions in restricted entry are offset by the smaller group sizes, so overall earnings are lower. The study highlights that the specific form of the punishment or sanction mechanism is crucial for its effect on cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the design of group formation and sanction mechanisms (such as voting to deny entry) can have substantial effects on both contribution behavior and efficiency in public goods games. However, the effect on efficiency is not always positive: mechanisms that increase contributions may also reduce group size, lowering overall group earnings. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and type of punishment/sanction mechanism, as well as group formation rules, should be considered alongside standard game parameters. The findings caution that not all punishment-like mechanisms increase efficiency, and their effects may depend on how they interact with group size and formation dynamics.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 12 players; group size was endogenous and could range from 1 to 12.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Preliminary phase: 3 rounds; main phase: 20 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: players could allocate 0-15 tokens between private and group accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 1.5 ECUs per token contributed to the group account, for each group member (including self).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is indirect: denying entry (cost is foregone earnings from not adding a member) or reducing own contribution (cost is lower own earnings). No explicit cost per punishment unit.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is via group voting to deny entry or by reducing own contribution. Denying entry is effective at increasing contributions; reducing own contribution is not.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the number of rounds in advance (finite repeated game).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were shown average contribution levels and group sizes for all groups over the previous 5 periods.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. When voting on entry/exit, group members saw the applicant's contribution history (but not explicit identity beyond group label).

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanisms are indirect (voting to deny entry/exit, or reducing own contribution) and do not map directly to standard costly punishment mechanisms in the literature.
- The environment is designed so that adding group members is always beneficial (no congestion), which may limit generalizability to standard PGGs where free-riding can reduce others' payoffs.
- No explicit monetary cost per punishment unit; punishment is implemented via group voting or self-sacrifice.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The study focuses on group formation mechanisms rather than direct manipulation of punishment parameters.
