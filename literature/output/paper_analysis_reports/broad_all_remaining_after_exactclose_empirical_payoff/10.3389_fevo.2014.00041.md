# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions
Authors: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C.
Journal: Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- final account (total earnings)
- mean final payoffs
- correlation between image/punishment score and account
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation frequency
- punishment frequency (of defectors and cooperators)
- image score
- punishment reputation score
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables include both payoff-based outcomes (final account, mean payoffs) and behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment rates, reputation scores). However, efficiency is directly addressed via final account/earnings and their relationship to cooperation and punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In reputation-based helping games (without punishment), cognitive disturbance (superfluous words) reduces cooperation and lowers group payoffs, indicating that efficiency is sensitive to cognitive constraints. In reputation-based punishment games, disturbance increases antisocial punishment and reduces punishment of defectors, but does not significantly change overall cooperation or mean final payoffs. The link between reputation and payoff is weakened under disturbance. The study highlights that experimental outcomes in reputation and punishment games are highly sensitive to environmental and cognitive factors, and that the expected efficiency gains from punishment or reputation mechanisms may not materialize under cognitive load.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effects of punishment and reputation mechanisms in indirect reciprocity games are sensitive to cognitive context. For prediction tasks, it suggests that efficiency gains from punishment (or reputation) may be undermined or nullified if cognitive load is high, even when game design parameters are otherwise favorable. The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 6-10 players: Study 1 used 8 groups of 9 or 10; Study 2 used 14 groups of 6 to 9.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 24 rounds per group; Study 2: 16 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. No chat or communication between players; the only manipulation was the experimenter saying superfluous words (not player chat).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: Donor can give (green light) or not (red light).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: Donor gives, Receiver gains (exact amounts not fully clear due to image, but similar to previous studies). Study 2: Donor's donation costs 1 CHF, Receiver gains 2 CHF (mpcr = 2).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 2: Punishment costs 1 CHF to punisher, reduces punished account by 2 CHF.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 CHF cost to punisher, 2 CHF loss to target (ratio 2:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were not told the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw graphical displays of image score (generosity) or punishment reputation (punishment) for themselves and/or others before each interaction.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment reputation was displayed, but only the Receiver's ID and punishment reputation were shown in Study 2. The identity of the punisher was known to the Donor in the context of the reputation score.

## Important Limitations
- The games are not standard public goods games but indirect reciprocity (helping) games with pairwise interactions; thus, generalizability to standard PGGs is limited.
- The main manipulation is cognitive disturbance, not a game design parameter per se, which may limit direct applicability to design-based prediction tasks.
- Sample sizes for disturbed groups are small (3 groups per condition), which may affect statistical power.
- The study highlights high sensitivity of outcomes to minor environmental/contextual factors, suggesting that results may not be robust across settings.
- Payoff structures and group sizes differ between the two studies, which may confound direct comparison.
