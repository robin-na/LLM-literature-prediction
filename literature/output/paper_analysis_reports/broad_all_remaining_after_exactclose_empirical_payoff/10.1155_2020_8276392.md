## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- evolutionary stability of cooperation strategies among local government, social organizations, and poverty groups
- frequency of cooperative vs. noncooperative strategies
- impact of parameter changes (support, punishment, benefits) on cooperation rates
Outcome notes:
The paper models and simulates the evolution of cooperation among three agents (local government, social organizations, poverty groups) in a poverty alleviation context. Outcomes are the equilibrium frequencies of cooperative strategies, not group payoffs or efficiency. Payoff functions are specified, but the main results are about behavioral strategy selection, not aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that: (1) Increasing support from local governments and social organizations to poverty groups can promote the willingness of poverty groups to cooperate, but excessive support from higher-level government does not necessarily lead to more cooperation or better outcomes. (2) Moderate increases in punishment (negative returns) for noncooperation by social organizations and poverty groups can encourage cooperative behavior. (3) Increasing the basic economic benefits for social organizations and poverty groups significantly increases their willingness to cooperate. The model suggests that a combination of incentives and moderate sanctions is more effective than top-down subsidies alone. However, the main outcomes are the equilibrium rates of cooperation, not efficiency or group payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. It does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, but it does analyze how punishment (modeled as negative returns for noncooperation) and other design parameters affect the evolution of cooperation in a multi-agent setting. The findings suggest that moderate punishment can increase cooperation rates, which in standard public goods games is often associated with higher efficiency, but this is not directly demonstrated here. The model is more complex than standard PGGs, involving three types of agents and context-specific payoffs. Thus, the paper is best used for qualitative, indirect support: it reinforces that punishment and incentives can promote cooperation, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency effects for PGG-like environments.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Three agents: local government, social organizations, poverty groups. Not a standard PGG group size, but a fixed three-party model.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Model is evolutionary and continuous-time; not structured in discrete rounds as in standard PGGs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each agent chooses between two discrete strategies: cooperate or not cooperate (binary choice).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as negative returns (penalties) for noncooperation; the magnitude of these penalties is varied in simulation.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is represented by the size of negative returns for noncooperation; higher penalties increase cooperation rates.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Rewards are modeled as increased basic benefits for cooperation; increasing these benefits increases cooperation rates.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of rewards is represented by the size of benefit parameters; higher benefits increase cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it involves three heterogeneous agents with context-specific payoffs.
- Outcomes are behavioral (cooperation rates), not efficiency or group payoff.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff.
- Punishment and reward are modeled as parameter changes in negative or positive returns, not as explicit costly punishment or reward actions.
- Findings may not generalize quantitatively to standard PGGs due to structural differences.
- No experimental or empirical test of the model's predictions; results are based on simulation and theoretical analysis.
