# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Self-Serving Punishment of a Common Enemy Creates a Public Good in Reef Fishes
Authors: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R.
Journal: Current Biology
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- probability that blenny switches to another species after being punished
- frequency of punishment (chasing) by victim fish
- blenny preference for punishing vs nonpunishing plates
- identification of free riders by blennies
Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are behavioral: whether punishment (chasing) by a victim fish reduces the likelihood of being attacked again, whether this creates a public good for conspecifics, and whether blennies can discriminate between punishers and nonpunishers. No direct measurement of group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that (1) punishment by victim fish (chasing the blenny) reduces the likelihood of being attacked again, (2) in shoaling species, this punishment increases the probability that the blenny switches to another species, creating a public good for the group, and (3) blennies can learn to discriminate between punishers and nonpunishers, which selects against free riding. The findings are based on both field observations and controlled laboratory experiments using artificial plates to simulate fish. The outcomes are behavioral and do not include direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff, but the results are relevant to understanding how punishment can create public goods in repeated natural interactions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that in repeated, naturalistic interactions, self-serving punishment can create public good benefits for a group, and that the ability of the 'cheater' (blenny) to identify and target nonpunishers can sustain punishment even in the absence of kin structure. However, the study does not use a formal public goods game, does not manipulate standard game design dimensions (e.g., MPCR, player count, explicit payoff structure), and does not report efficiency or group payoff. Thus, while the behavioral mechanisms are informative for understanding the conditions under which punishment can sustain cooperation and public goods, the findings cannot be directly mapped to predictions about efficiency in laboratory public goods games with or without punishment. The main value is in highlighting the importance of repeated interactions, individual identification, and self-serving motives in the emergence of public goods via punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Shoaling species are observed, but the exact group size is not systematically manipulated or reported as a variable. The study discusses group size in the context of natural aggregations.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Repeated interactions are essential to the system, but the number of rounds is not fixed or manipulated as in laboratory games.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment (chasing) is described as energetically costly for the punisher, but the cost is not quantified or manipulated.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is discussed in terms of its impact on blenny behavior (reducing future attacks), but not quantified as a per-unit cost/impact ratio.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Blennies can learn to discriminate between individual punishers and nonpunishers, implying that punishment identity is observable to the cheater.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a formal public goods game or laboratory economic game structure.
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported; outcomes are behavioral.
- Game design dimensions such as MPCR, player count, and explicit payoff structure are not manipulated or systematically varied.
- Findings are based on a natural system with repeated interactions and individual identification, which may not generalize to anonymous, one-shot, or simultaneous-move laboratory games.
- The public good is inferred from behavioral changes (blenny switching species) rather than measured payoffs.
