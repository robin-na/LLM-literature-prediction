## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the efficiency of outcomes in a two-player common-pool resource game depends critically on the social value orientations (SVOs) of the players. When both players are competitive, the Nash equilibrium is often Pareto-inefficient (i.e., group efficiency is low), and this inefficiency is robust to the introduction of fair punishment. In fact, punishment can increase the parameter space in which inefficiency occurs for competitive players. When at least one player is individualistic or prosocial, the Nash equilibrium is almost always Pareto efficient (i.e., high group efficiency). The model demonstrates that punishment does not always improve efficiency and can backfire in certain social contexts.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games is highly sensitive to the social preferences of the players. In environments where players are competitive (i.e., care about outperforming others), introducing fair punishment can worsen efficiency, increasing the likelihood of Pareto-inefficient outcomes. In contrast, with individualistic or prosocial players, efficiency is generally high even without punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment does not guarantee improved efficiency and may even reduce it in competitive settings, especially in small, binary-choice, one-shot games. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, single-round, all-or-nothing, common-pool resource dilemmas, but the underlying logic may transfer to similar public goods environments.

## Important Limitations
- The model is limited to two players and a single round; results may not generalize to larger groups or repeated games.
- Only three SVO types (individualistic, competitive, prosocial) are analyzed in detail; other SVOs (e.g., altruistic, sadistic) are only discussed in supplementary material.
- Punishment is modeled as an exogenous, symmetric penalty, not as voluntary or targeted peer punishment.
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- No communication, reputation, or network effects are included.
