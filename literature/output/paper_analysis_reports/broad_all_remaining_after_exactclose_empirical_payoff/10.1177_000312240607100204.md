Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable in the computational experiments is the stable proportion of actors choosing to work (participation), which is directly tied to group productivity and collective welfare. The model also tracks the enforcement of norms (prosocial and antisocial), but these are secondary to the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that collective action and group efficiency in public-goods-like environments are shaped by a three-way interaction between the value of selective incentives (rewards), the rivalness of those incentives, and the strength of peer influence (cohesiveness). When incentives are nonrival, increasing their value or increasing peer influence both increase participation and group welfare. When incentives are rival, however, strong peer influence can lead to the enforcement of antisocial norms that discourage contribution, reducing group efficiency. The model demonstrates that under high rivalness and high incentive value, increasing peer influence or incentive value can actually decrease collective action and group welfare. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and are most applicable to small groups.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, rival selective incentives and norm enforcement) on group efficiency is highly contingent on the design dimensions of the incentive system and the strength of peer influence. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling strong peer enforcement or increasing the value of selective incentives will not always increase efficiency—if the incentives are rival, these interventions can backfire and reduce group welfare. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with reward-based incentives, especially when those rewards are rival. While the paper does not model punishment per se, the logic and results are highly informative for any system where sanctions or rewards are rival and peer enforcement is possible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size is fixed at N=10 in all computational experiments. Theoretical results are stated for any finite N, but simulations use N=10.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations iterate until a stable distribution of behaviors is reached, typically within a few rounds. The number of rounds is not fixed in advance but determined by convergence.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are binary: each actor chooses to 'work' (contribute) or 'shirk' (not contribute).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The production function is linear: each contribution yields benefit g=1 to each group member, cost c=5 to the contributor. For N=10, total group benefit per contribution is 10, so MPCR = 1/5 = 0.2 for the contributor.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. A centralized selective incentive (reward) is always present, with its value (mu) and rivalness (lambda) manipulated.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the central authority is not modeled; the focus is on the value of the reward to recipients. Enforcement of norms (promotion/opposition) has a cost (e=2) to the enforcer.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is manipulated via the value parameter (mu) and the rivalness parameter (lambda), which determines how much the reward is diluted as more people receive it.

## Important Limitations
- The model does not include punishment; only reward-based selective incentives and norm enforcement (promotion/opposition) are modeled.
- All simulations use a fixed group size of N=10; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Contribution choices are binary (work or shirk); continuous contribution is not modeled.
- The model assumes homogeneous actors (except in Experiment 2, where susceptibility to influence is heterogeneous).
- The model assumes no communication or network structure among actors.
- The model does not include learning, history, or repeated-game effects; actors are myopic and do not update based on past outcomes.
- The cost to the central authority for providing rewards is not modeled.
- The model is theoretical and computational; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The main results are most applicable to small groups and may not hold in large groups or with more complex social structures.
- The model does not address the effect of punishment, only rewards and peer enforcement.
