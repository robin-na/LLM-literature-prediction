Outcome notes:
The main reported outcomes are group achievement (b7_G) and institution prevalence (b7_I), which are not direct measures of efficiency as defined by total payoff relative to full cooperation, but are closely related. The paper also discusses average fitness (overall welfare), but does not provide explicit efficiency ratios. Most results are presented in terms of the prevalence of cooperation and the success rate of public good provision.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Graduated institutional punishment, where the severity and cost of punishment increase with the number of defectors, is more effective than strict (fixed) punishment in promoting cooperation and public good achievement in collective-risk dilemmas. This effect is especially pronounced at low risk levels and when both fines and costs are graduated. The model shows that graduated punishment reduces the time populations spend in all-defector states and increases the prevalence of cooperation and sustainable institutions. The approach also helps solve the second-order free-rider problem associated with institutional punishment. The findings are robust across a range of parameters and extend to linear public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in threshold public goods games with risk and institutional punishment, making punishment and its costs graduated (i.e., increasing with the number of defectors) leads to higher group achievement and overall welfare than strict, fixed punishment. While the main outcomes are not reported as efficiency ratios, the close relationship between group achievement, average fitness, and efficiency means the results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar structure (threshold, risk, institutional punishment), enabling graduated punishment will likely increase efficiency relative to strict punishment, especially at low risk and when punishment costs are also graduated. The results are robust to changes in group size, threshold, and other parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N is a key model parameter (e.g., N=4 in main figures), and results are shown to be robust to changes in N.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (c0_t) is a key parameter, and the effect of making it graduated (vs. fixed) is directly analyzed. Graduated costs increase cooperation and institution prevalence.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact (c0_f) is varied, and the effect of making it graduated (vs. fixed) is directly analyzed. Graduated punishment increases cooperation and group achievement.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency is not reported as a direct ratio of group payoff to full cooperation, but as group achievement and average fitness.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not include network structure except in discussion of related work.
- No communication, reward, or peer punishment mechanisms are modeled.
- The main results are for threshold public goods games (collective-risk dilemmas), though some results extend to linear PGGs in the Supplementary Data.
