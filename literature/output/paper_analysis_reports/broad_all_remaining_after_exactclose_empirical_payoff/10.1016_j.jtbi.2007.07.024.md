Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variables are population-level frequencies of altruism and punishment, group productivity (total payoff relative to maximum possible), and fitness. The model also tracks the correlation between altruism and punishment, but the efficiency of the group (i.e., total payoff relative to full cooperation) is a central outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size (N) is a key parameter; increasing N reduces equilibrium levels of altruism, punishment, and thus group efficiency, but does not eliminate them except at very large N.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds (R) is directly manipulated; more rounds increase the effectiveness of punishment and the maintenance of cooperation and efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each individual chooses a proportion of their endowment to contribute (A in [0,1]). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (the group fund is doubled and split equally among N players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (C) is a key parameter; higher C reduces equilibrium cooperation and efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (D, probability of removal upon detection) is manipulated; higher D increases the effect of punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds (R) is fixed and known in the simulation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the total contribution of other group members, but not individual contributions.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.
- Punishment is modeled as exclusion (removal from the group), not as a fine or deduction from earnings.
- Players only know the total contribution of others, not individual contributions, which may differ from some experimental designs.
- No communication, reward, or identification of punishers is modeled.
- The model assumes infinite population and asexual reproduction, which may not match real-world or experimental settings.
- Parameter sweeps are limited to certain ranges; effects outside these ranges are not explored.
