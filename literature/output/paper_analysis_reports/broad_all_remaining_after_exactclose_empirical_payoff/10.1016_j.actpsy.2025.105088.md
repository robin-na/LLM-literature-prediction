# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The influence of third-party's power on multi-player fairness decision-making
Authors: Lu, X. Q., Li, T., Li, B. M., & Yang, Z.
Journal: Acta Psychologica
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rates (offer acceptance/rejection)
- fairness ratings
- reaction times
- responsibility ratings
- computational model parameters (inequity aversion, social comparison)
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral responses (accept/reject) in a three-person ultimatum game, fairness ratings, and psychological mechanisms (inequity aversion, responsibility), not on group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. No explicit analysis of efficiency or group welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that participants care about fairness towards third parties in three-person ultimatum games, but their concern is modulated by whether the third party has decision power. When the third party is powerless, participants enforce fairness norms and show both disadvantageous and advantageous inequity aversion. When the third party has power, participants incorporate both fairness and social comparison norms, showing greater disadvantageous inequity aversion and even advantageous inequity preference towards the third party. The presence of a powerful third party leads to diffusion of responsibility, reducing the participant's willingness to punish unfairness. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so its findings are only indirectly relevant to efficiency prediction in public-goods-like games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how punishment and norm enforcement mechanisms may operate in multi-player bargaining contexts, especially regarding the psychological mechanisms (inequity aversion, responsibility, social comparison) that underlie acceptance/rejection of unfair offers. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main value is in highlighting that the structure of power and responsibility among players can modulate willingness to punish norm violations, which may, in turn, affect efficiency in related but not identical game structures.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are three-player ultimatum games (proposer, responder, third party).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each task consisted of 88 trials (divided into four blocks), with 22 offer types repeated 4 times.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; all offers are pre-determined and presented via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the entire offer.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is implicit: rejection of an offer results in zero payoff for all (or for some, depending on third party power). The cost is the loss of own potential earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the rules: rejection can deny payoffs to proposer, responder, and/or third party depending on condition.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of trials (blocks and repetitions are specified).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants see the full offer (proposer, responder, third party allocations) and the outcome after their decision.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare; all outcomes are behavioral (acceptance rates, fairness ratings, model parameters).
- The game is a three-person ultimatum game, not a public goods game or direct variant; thus, findings are only adjacent to PGG-like environments.
- Punishment is operationalized as rejection of offers, not as a separate costly punishment action.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the effects of positive incentives are not addressed.
- All participants are college students from a single university, limiting generalizability.
- The third party's decisions are partially pre-programmed, not fully interactive or based on real third-party behavior.
