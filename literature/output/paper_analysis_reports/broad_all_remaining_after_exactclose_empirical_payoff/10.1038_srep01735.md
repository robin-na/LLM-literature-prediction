## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing peer pressure mechanisms—where peers are incentivized to exert costly pressure on each other to reduce negative externalities—can lead to higher social surplus (efficiency) than traditional Pigouvian mechanisms, especially in large, sparse networks. Theoretical results show that peer pressure reduces equilibrium action levels (i.e., reduces over-consumption or under-contribution), but not all the way to the social optimum unless subsidized. Social mechanisms that reward peers for their neighbors' good behavior are more cost-effective than direct rewards to individuals. The model predicts that the efficiency gain from peer pressure increases as the cost of exerting pressure decreases and as social ties strengthen. An illustrative experiment on physical activity supports these predictions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling peer-based sanctions or rewards (peer pressure) can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, especially when the cost of peer pressure is moderate and the network is sparse. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, introducing peer-based punishment or reward mechanisms should increase efficiency relative to control, and that the effect is stronger when the cost of peer pressure is lower and when social ties are stronger. The model is not an exact PGG, but is closely related and the efficiency results are directly relevant. The paper does not provide direct empirical estimates for standard PGG parameters, but the qualitative direction is clear: peer pressure mechanisms are more efficient than no intervention, and often more efficient than centralized punishment/reward, especially at low budgets.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not an exact public goods game; it focuses on externality-generating actions in networks.
- Peer pressure is modeled as a costly, continuous action, not as discrete punishment choices as in standard PGGs.
- Empirical evidence is limited to an illustrative experiment on physical activity, not a direct test in a PGG environment.
- The model assumes perfect monitoring of peers' actions, which may not hold in real-world or experimental PGGs.
- Negative peer pressure may lead to retaliation, which is not fully modeled.
- The model does not address repeated or multi-round dynamics.
