## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a punishment mechanism into the classical public goods game prevents the collapse of cooperation and increases average payoffs for both cooperators and defectors, compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is present for both perfectly rational and boundedly rational agents. The degree of rationality (K) also modulates the equilibrium, but punishment always leads to higher system payoffs and more cooperation than the control. The results are based on theoretical analysis and numerical simulations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that, in a standard PGG with fixed group size (N=6), a punishment mechanism (with specified cost and fine) increases average payoffs (i.e., efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to changes in the rationality parameter K. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to defection. The specific parameterization (N=6, r=3.6, c=1.25, punishment fine and cost) is informative for quantitative prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main simulations use N=6 players per group.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose to either cooperate (contribute c) or defect (contribute nothing); binary choice.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. mpcr = r/N; in main simulations, r=3.6, N=6, so mpcr=0.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher is mu=0.1; fine to defector is nu=0.35. The presence of punishment increases efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is the fine per unit cost (nu/mu = 0.35/0.1 = 3.5). The model uses fixed values for simulations.

## Important Limitations
- The study is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- The model assumes fixed group size (N=6) and specific parameter values; generalizability to other settings is not directly tested.
- No human subjects or real-world data are used; results depend on the assumptions of the evolutionary dynamics and rationality parameter.
- No analysis of finite rounds or end-game effects; the model focuses on equilibrium outcomes.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or identification of punishers.
