Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the behavioral dynamics of cooperation, defection, and hypocrisy in a networked public goods game with punishment/social-pressure. Outcomes are reported as steady-state proportions of behavioral types, not as group payoff, efficiency, or welfare. The model assumes negligible MPCR, so group payoff is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study shows that in large networked public goods games with negligible MPCR, cooperation can emerge from a population of mostly defectors if mild but appropriately calibrated punishment (social-pressure) is applied, especially when an intermediate behavioral type (hypocrisy) is present. The emergence of cooperation is highly sensitive to the level of punishment against hypocritical players: too little or too much punishment leads to persistent defection or hypocrisy, while an intermediate level enables a transition to widespread cooperation. The model is theoretical and supported by numerical simulations, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical insight into the conditions under which punishment can promote cooperation in large public goods games with negligible MPCR. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation depends critically on the calibration of punishment levels, especially toward intermediate types like hypocrites. However, because the model does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not provide direct quantitative evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in experimental or empirical settings. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of punishment calibration and the role of intermediate behavioral types in the dynamics of cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations use large networks (e.g., 1000 players), but the model is general for n players. The effect of player count is not directly analyzed for efficiency or payoff.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations run for a fixed number of rounds (e.g., T=10 or T=20), but the model focuses on steady-state behavior. Number of rounds is not varied as a treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: players choose to be a cooperator, defector, or hypocrite (not continuous contribution levels).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is set to zero (benefits from others' cooperation are negligible). This is a key modeling assumption.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the two-order model, the cost of punishing (second-order cooperation) is explicitly parameterized as alpha_2. The main model assumes social-pressure is beneficial or costless, but the generalized model includes explicit punishment cost.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is parameterized as beta_1 and beta_2 in the two-order model, and as rho_d and rho_h in the main model. The effect of varying these is central to the analysis.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The number of rounds is fixed in simulations, but there is no discussion of whether players know the total number of rounds (since this is a theoretical/simulation model, not a human experiment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players are assumed to know the behaviors of their neighbors in the network (to compute costs), but there is no explicit summary information shown as in experimental designs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical or experimental; no real participant data.
- Outcomes are reported as behavioral proportions (cooperation, defection, hypocrisy), not as efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Assumes MPCR is zero, which may not generalize to standard public goods games with positive MPCR.
- No analysis of payoff-based outcomes, so direct prediction of efficiency effects is not possible.
- No reward mechanism is modeled.
- Network structure is assumed fixed and regular in main results; real-world networks may differ.
