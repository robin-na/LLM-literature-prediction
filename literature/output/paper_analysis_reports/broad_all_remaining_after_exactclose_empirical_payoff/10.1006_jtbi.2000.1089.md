The paper's main dependent variable is the average payoff (efficiency) of strategies in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma under evolutionary dynamics. It does not report on contribution rates or explicit punishment frequency, but rather on the evolutionary stability and average payoffs of memory-one strategies.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma with memory-one strategies and evolutionary dynamics, the population tends to evolve toward a small set of stable strategies. The Pavlov (Win-Stay Lose-Switch) strategy is particularly robust, achieving high average payoffs and being resistant to invasion by other strategies, especially in the presence of noise. The analysis partitions the space of strategies into basins of attraction: cooperative (high payoff), defecting (low payoff), alternating (zero payoff), and a small equilibrium plane. The size of these basins depends on the initial conditions and the payoff matrix. The paper also shows that strategies that combine trust with readiness to retaliate (but not excessive forgiveness) are most successful. While explicit punishment mechanisms are not modeled, the evolutionary success of strategies that retaliate after being exploited is highlighted.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not study public goods games or explicit punishment institutions, it analyzes the efficiency (average payoff) of strategies in a closely related repeated social dilemma (the IPD) under evolutionary dynamics. The findings suggest that strategies incorporating contingent retaliation (analogous to punishment) can sustain high efficiency, but only if they avoid excessive forgiveness or spite. The mapping of strategy space to efficiency outcomes can inform predictions about the likely efficiency of repeated games with different behavioral rules, especially regarding the role of contingent sanctions. However, the lack of explicit group structure, multi-player interactions, and institutional punishment limits direct transfer to public goods games.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is always a two-player (dyadic) iterated Prisoner's Dilemma.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main analysis assumes an infinite number of rounds, but simulations show that results are similar for games with at least 8 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choices are binary: cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Payoff matrix is specified; for the standard matrix, mutual cooperation yields 1 per player, mutual defection -1, temptation 2, sucker -2. MPCR is not directly defined but can be inferred from the matrix.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds is assumed known (infinite or fixed, e.g., 8), and the effect of round number is analyzed.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is limited to two-player iterated Prisoner's Dilemma games, not multi-player public goods games.
- There is no explicit institutional punishment or sanctioning mechanism; punishment is only implicit in strategy responses.
- The model does not include communication, group structure, or reputation effects.
- Findings may not generalize directly to public goods games or environments with explicit punishment/reward institutions.
- All results are theoretical or simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
