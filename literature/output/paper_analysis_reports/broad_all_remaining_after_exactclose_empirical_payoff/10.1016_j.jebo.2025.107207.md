# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Social context, framing, and compliance with the law: experimental evidence
Authors: Chavez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., & Stranlund, J. K.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- compliance rate (effort allocated to compliant activity)
- probability of perfect compliance
- probability of zero compliance
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is compliance (effort allocated to the compliant/public good activity), not group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. While payoffs are described and the design is payoff-equivalent across treatments, the analysis and results focus on compliance behavior, not on efficiency or group welfare.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that higher monitoring probabilities increase compliance in all treatments. Law framing increases compliance only in the strategic (public good) environment, and actually decreases compliance in the non-strategic environment where the law does not benefit others. Compliance is not higher when noncompliance imposes an externality on third parties. Compliance is higher in public good (strategic) environments than in non-strategic ones, especially when monitoring is weak. The main outcome is compliance, not efficiency or group payoff, so the results are most directly informative about behavioral responses to punishment rather than efficiency effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about how punishment (monitoring and sanctions) affects compliance rates in public goods game-like environments, especially under different social framings. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and results are highly relevant for predicting compliance behavior, and the strategic (public good) treatments are close analogs to standard PGGs with punishment. The findings suggest that punishment is more effective at increasing compliance (and thus likely efficiency) when the public good benefits group members, especially under weak enforcement. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 in all main treatments (excluding third parties in externality treatments).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 12 rounds per session, with 3 blocks of 4 rounds each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was not permitted between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (effort allocation) is continuous between 0 and 40 hours.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In strategic (public good) treatments, each unit of effort to the public good yields 10 pesos to each of the 3 other group members; with 4 players, MPCR = 0.75 (10/40) if total group return is considered, or 0.25 if only own return is considered.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a sanction equal to the per-unit gain from the prohibited activity (20 pesos per unit); cost is not paid by other players but is a loss to the punished.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is perfectly effective: detected noncompliance results in full confiscation of illegal earnings (per-unit sanction equals per-unit gain).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds (12) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In strategic treatments, only the total number of hours allocated to the cooperative activity was announced; individual choices were not publicly revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is compliance (behavioral), not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency effects must be inferred indirectly.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings by treatment.
- Punishment is implemented as centralized monitoring and sanctioning, not peer punishment.
- The subject pool is artisanal fishers in Chile, which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- No reward mechanism is present, so no evidence on reward effects.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation effects.
- The experiment uses a continuous contribution (effort allocation) rather than a binary all-or-nothing choice.
