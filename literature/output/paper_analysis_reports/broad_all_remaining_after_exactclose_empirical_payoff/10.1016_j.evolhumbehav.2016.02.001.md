# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Local competition increases people's willingness to harm others
Authors: Barker, J. L., & Barclay, P.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings from endowments
- total payoff
- proportion of participants with zero earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount spent on burning others' money
- amount spent burning ingroup vs outgroup members
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount spent to harm others (burning), and the resulting earnings/payoff. The paper explicitly analyzes group and individual earnings as a function of the experimental conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that when competition is local (i.e., participants compete for prizes within small groups), people are more willing to pay a cost to harm others, resulting in lower group and individual payoffs (efficiency). In contrast, under global competition (competing against all participants), less money is spent on harming, and payoffs are higher. This effect holds whether endowments are earned or given. The findings are robust across three studies, including one where participants could harm both ingroup and outgroup members: under local competition, harming is targeted at ingroup members (the direct competitors), further reducing within-group efficiency. The results are interpreted as a 'tragedy of the commons' driven by local competition, where wasteful harming reduces overall welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in economic games where players can pay to reduce others' payoffs (money-burning), the structure of competition (local vs global) is a critical moderator of efficiency. When competition is local, efficiency is much lower due to increased costly harming, even when the cost-effectiveness of harming is high (burning $1 to reduce $4). The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of local competition (small group contests) should be expected to strongly reduce efficiency when punishment/harming is possible, compared to global competition. The results are robust to whether endowments are earned or given, and to whether harming is targeted at ingroup or outgroup members. However, the game is not a standard public goods game or punishment game: the main action is costly harming, not punishment for norm violation or contribution decisions. Still, the efficiency implications are directly relevant for settings where costly sanctions are possible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Each session had 9 players, divided into groups of 3 for local competition; in global competition, all 9 competed for prizes. The number of competitors is a key manipulated variable (local: 3, global: 9).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies used a single round of the money-burning game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed; decisions were made anonymously and in isolation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (burning) decisions were continuous: participants could choose any amount to spend on burning each partner.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return for burning is negative: every $1 spent reduces another's payoff by $4 (burning ratio 1:4). This is not a public goods game, but the cost-effectiveness of harming is specified.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (burning) cost is $1 per $4 reduction in the target's payoff.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 4:1 (each $1 spent reduces target's payoff by $4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was a single round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (burning) was anonymous; players were identified only by code numbers, and identities were not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental game is not a standard public goods or punishment game; it is a money-burning (costly harming) game, so findings may not generalize to cooperation or norm-enforcement contexts.
- All studies used a single round, so repeated interaction effects are not addressed.
- No positive sanction (reward) mechanism was available, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- The effect of local competition is tested only in the context of costly harming, not in the context of cooperation or contribution decisions.
- The sample sizes are moderate and drawn from university populations, which may limit generalizability.
- The psychological mechanisms underlying the effect are not directly measured; only behavioral outcomes are observed.
