# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Public disclosure for carbon abatement: African decision-makers in a PROPER public good experiment
Authors: Akpalu, W., Abidoye, B., Muchapondwa, E., & Simbanegavi, W.
Journal: Climate and Development
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of red cards retained (pollution level)
- probability of retaining more red cards in last 8 rounds than first 8 rounds
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the number of red cards retained (i.e., pollution), which is a measure of non-cooperation in the public good game. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare is reported, though the payoff structure is described and the social optimum is referenced.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that public disclosure of individual pollution levels (via visible color-coded cards) reduces pollution (i.e., increases cooperation) in a linear public good game. The effect persists even after disclosure is withdrawn. The study does not use monetary punishment or reward, but rather a reputational/shame mechanism. The main outcome is the number of red cards retained (pollution), not group efficiency or total payoff. The results suggest that public disclosure can be an effective non-monetary sanction to promote cooperation in public goods settings, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that public disclosure (a reputational/shame-based sanction) can reduce free-riding in a linear public good game, even among experienced policy-makers. However, it does not test monetary punishment or reward, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on the effect of monetary punishment on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that non-monetary sanctions can increase cooperation, but does not quantify efficiency gains or compare to monetary punishment. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, known MPCR) are well described, but the absence of monetary punishment means the results are not directly transferable to settings with explicit punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group had 8 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was not permitted within or across groups.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many of 4 red cards to surrender (0-4).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each surrendered red card gives $1 to each player (total $8 per card), so MPCR = 1/8 = 0.125.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (24) was predetermined and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, the total number of red cards collected was announced to the group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the public disclosure treatment, each subject's pollution level was made visible to all via color-coded cards.

## Important Limitations
- No monetary punishment or reward mechanism; only reputational/shame-based public disclosure.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as outcomes.
- Small sample size (three groups of eight participants each), all drawn from African climate decision-makers.
- Results may not generalize to broader populations or to settings with explicit monetary sanctions.
- The effect of public disclosure is not directly comparable to monetary punishment treatments in standard PGG experiments.
