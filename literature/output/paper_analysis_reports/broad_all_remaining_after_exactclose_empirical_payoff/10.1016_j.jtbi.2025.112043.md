# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Costly punishment sustains indirect reciprocity under low defection detectability
Authors: Murase, Y.
Journal: Journal of Theoretical Biology
Year: 2025

## Study Type
theory; empirical subtype=N/A; experimental subtype=N/A

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff at equilibrium
- cooperation level (fraction of good players)
- evolutionary stability of norms (ESS) based on payoff
- explicit formulas for group payoff under different norms
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- reputation dynamics
- frequency of punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the average payoff (efficiency) of the population under different social norms (with and without costly punishment), as a function of model parameters. Cooperation rate and reputation are also analyzed, but always in relation to payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the efficiency of costly punishment in sustaining cooperation depends critically on the detectability of defection. When defection is difficult to detect (i.e., high probability that defection is misperceived as cooperation), social norms that include costly punishment (CP norms) can be the most efficient ESS, achieving high cooperation and high average payoff. When defection is easy to detect, punishment is rarely efficient and often reduces payoffs. The model provides explicit boundaries for when punishment is efficient, and shows that punishment is most effective when it is rarely executed but strongly deters defection. Execution errors and high punishment costs shrink the parameter space where punishment is efficient. The results challenge previous theoretical work that found only a narrow efficiency margin for punishment, showing that under asymmetric detection errors, punishment can be broadly efficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, parameterized theoretical predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency in indirect reciprocity games with pairwise donation structure. While not a standard public goods game, the model is adjacent and the efficiency results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment in environments where defection is hard to detect. The explicit formulas and phase diagrams allow mapping from game design dimensions (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, error rates) and baseline efficiency to the expected efficiency with punishment enabled. The main moderator is the detectability of defection: when it is low, punishment can substantially increase efficiency; when high, punishment is unnecessary or even harmful. The results are most transferable to settings with pairwise interactions, reputation, and indirect reciprocity, but provide strong mechanistic insight for broader PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes an infinitely large population with random pairwise interactions (donor and recipient). There is no fixed group size as in standard PGGs, but the effective 'player count' per interaction is 2.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes repeated, indefinitely ongoing interactions (infinite rounds) in a population; not a finite repeated game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are discrete: cooperate (C), defect (D), or punish (P). No continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b/c for cooperation (b>c), but in a pairwise donation game, not a group PGG. The model analyzes how b, c, and their ratio affect efficiency and ESS.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost (alpha) is a key parameter. High punishment cost reduces the parameter space where punishment is efficient; low cost makes punishment more likely to be efficient.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (beta, loss to recipient per unit cost) is explicitly modeled. High punishment effectiveness increases the efficiency of punishment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players' reputations are publicly shared and updated based on observed actions; this is a core feature of the model.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game structure is pairwise donation with reputation (indirect reciprocity), not a standard multi-player public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGGs with group interactions.
- Assumes infinite population and infinite rounds; does not address finite group or round effects.
- No communication, reward, or more complex social structures are modeled.
- Assumes binary reputations and deterministic norms; does not consider stochastic or higher-order norms except in discussion.
- The model focuses on the stability and efficiency of established norms, not on the evolutionary process by which punishment norms emerge.
