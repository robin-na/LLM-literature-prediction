## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total voluntary cooperation (number of voluntary cooperative interactions)
- total cooperation (voluntary + coerced)
- proportion of strategy types (Honorables, Trusters, Exploitive Egoists, Opportunists)
- group composition under different punishment/appeasement regimes
Non-payoff outcomes:
- prevalence of strong reciprocity (Honorables)
- prevalence of opportunists
- correlation between positive and negative reciprocity
- strategy choice frequencies
- effect of punishment severity and standards on voluntary cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of voluntary cooperation (interpreted as a proxy for social capital), not direct group payoff or efficiency. The model and experiment track the prevalence of different behavioral strategies and the amount of voluntary (vs. coerced) cooperation, but do not report group efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible payoffs. Some payoff-related outcomes (e.g., total cooperation) are reported, but these are not mapped directly to efficiency as defined in public goods games.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that (1) more severe punishment for noncooperation reduces the amount of voluntary cooperation in a population, both in agent-based simulations and in a human experiment; (2) raising the standard for what counts as sufficient cooperation to avoid punishment also reduces voluntary cooperation; (3) the presence of opportunists is necessary for strong reciprocators to thrive, but when punishment is too severe, opportunists proliferate and strong reciprocators decline; (4) these dynamics help explain why tight or collectivist cultures with strong norms and sanctions may have lower social capital (voluntary cooperation) than expected. The main outcome is the amount of voluntary cooperation, not direct group efficiency or total payoff, but the results imply that harsher punishment can reduce overall welfare by crowding out voluntary, uncoerced cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments structurally similar to public goods games with punishment, increasing the severity of punishment or raising the bar for what counts as sufficient cooperation can reduce the amount of voluntary cooperation, and thus likely reduce group efficiency or welfare. The presence of punishment is necessary for sustaining cooperation, but making punishment too severe or standards too high backfires. For prediction tasks, this suggests a non-monotonic or negative effect of increasing punishment severity on efficiency, especially when punishment is harsh or standards are high. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible payoffs, so the mapping to efficiency is indirect. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of punishment severity and standards on voluntary cooperation and, by extension, on group efficiency in similar multi-agent, multi-round games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use 4,800 agents; experiment uses 35 players per game (1 real, 34 bots).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 30,000 turns; experiment uses 50 rounds per participant.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is parameterized as 'Punish Amount' (severity of punishment), but the cost to the punisher per unit is not directly specified. The effect of increasing punishment severity is analyzed.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is varied via 'Punish Amount' and its impact on outcomes is analyzed. Higher punishment effectiveness (severity) reduces voluntary cooperation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is fixed and known in both simulation and experiment (30,000 turns in simulation, 50 rounds in experiment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the experiment, some participants are shown the distribution of strategies in the population; this manipulation had little effect on main findings.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is voluntary cooperation, not group efficiency as defined by the ratio of actual to maximum possible payoffs in public goods games.
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is a multi-strategy, multi-agent game with different behavioral types and interaction rules.
- Punishment is parameterized as severity ('Punish Amount'), but the cost to the punisher per unit of punishment is not directly specified.
- The mapping from voluntary cooperation to group efficiency or welfare is indirect; actual payoffs or efficiency ratios are not reported.
- The experiment uses robot confederates rather than real multiplayer interaction, which may affect ecological validity.
- Findings may not generalize to all PGG-like environments, especially those with different payoff structures or group sizes.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
