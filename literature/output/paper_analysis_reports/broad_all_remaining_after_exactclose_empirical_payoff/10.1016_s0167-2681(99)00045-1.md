# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Collective action as a social exchange
Authors: Gächter, S., & Fehr, E.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 1999

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- participation in collective action
- free-riding
- social approval/disapproval ratings
- correlation between approval motive and contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the contribution rate to the public good. The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings as a primary outcome, though it does provide some information about average earnings per treatment. The focus is on behavioral responses (contributions) to social approval opportunities and group familiarity.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that social approval opportunities alone (i.e., the ability to express approval/disapproval after the game) do not significantly increase cooperation in a standard public goods game among strangers. However, when minimal group familiarity is introduced (via a brief pre-game interaction), the combination of social approval and familiarity leads to a large and significant increase in contributions. The effect is not observed for social approval or group familiarity alone. The main outcome is the contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that social approval can be a powerful motivator for cooperation, but only when social distance is reduced. The study does not test or report on material punishment or pecuniary sanctions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of material punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is relevant for understanding the role of non-pecuniary social incentives (approval/disapproval) and their interaction with group familiarity, but not for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that social mechanisms can increase cooperation, but only under certain social conditions. For the downstream prediction task (punishment-enabled efficiency), this paper is only indirectly informative, as it does not manipulate or report on punishment, nor does it report efficiency as a primary outcome.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players per group in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public goods game was repeated for 10 periods in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication was allowed during the game in any treatment. In the S and GS treatments, a group discussion was allowed only after the final period, not during the game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: subjects could allocate any number of tokens (0-20) to the public good each period.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) was 0.4 (each token contributed to the public good yielded 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Social (non-pecuniary) reward in the form of approval was possible in S and GS treatments, but no material reward mechanism.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It was common knowledge that the game would last exactly 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects were informed about the total group contribution, but not individual contributions.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not implement or analyze material punishment or pecuniary sanctions; only social approval/disapproval is studied.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are not reported as primary outcomes; the main outcome is contribution rate.
- Findings on social approval may not generalize to material punishment effects.
- The increase in cooperation is only observed when social approval is combined with minimal group familiarity; social approval alone is not effective among strangers.
- The study uses a fixed group size (n=4), fixed mpcr (0.4), and a specific lab context, which may limit generalizability.
