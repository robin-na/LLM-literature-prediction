# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Managing expectations: How selective information affects cooperation and punishment in social dilemma games
Authors: Engel, C., Kube, S., & Kurschilgen, M.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2021

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- cooperation rate
- punishment assigned (pro-social and anti-social)
- counter-punishment assigned
- beliefs about others' contributions
- heterogeneity of contributions
- reaction to punishment (change in contribution)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average contribution levels, patterns of punishment and counter-punishment, and beliefs about others' cooperativeness. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare directly, though these could be inferred from contribution data and the game structure.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Selective pre-play information about others' cooperativeness can strongly affect initial beliefs, contribution behavior, and the effectiveness of punishment in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment. Unfavorable information (showing low cooperation in other groups) leads to lower initial and sustained contributions, and reduces the ability of punishment to increase cooperation. Favorable information has little effect in already cooperative environments (ceiling effect), but in less cooperative environments it can prevent further decline in cooperation and enhance the positive effect of pro-social punishment. The effect of information does not depend on whether it is public or private, but is strongest when the information is surprising to participants. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the strong effects on contribution rates imply likely effects on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing cooperation (and thus likely efficiency) is moderated by participants' expectations, which can be manipulated by selective pre-play information. In environments where baseline cooperation is high, punishment is effective unless unfavorable information is provided, which undermines both cooperation and the effectiveness of punishment. In less cooperative environments, favorable information can help maintain cooperation and enhance the effect of pro-social punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the baseline expectations and informational context (not just structural game parameters) are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, predictions must be inferred from contribution data.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main experiments use 10 repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (each token contributed increases each player's payoff by 0.4 tokens).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is nonlinear: 1 point costs 1 token, 2 points cost 2 tokens, 3 points cost 4 tokens, etc. (see Table 1 and instructions).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's income by 10% of their stage 1 income, up to 100%.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment and counter-punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of periods (10) is announced in advance; participants know when the game will end.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, participants are shown others' contributions, punishment received, and other relevant feedback.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. After punishment, players observe who punished them and by how much (identities within group, but IDs are randomized each round to prevent reputation formation).

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare directly; only contribution levels and punishment patterns are reported.
- Findings about the effect of punishment on efficiency must be inferred from contribution data and game structure, not directly measured.
- The main manipulations concern pre-play information, not structural game parameters such as MPCR or punishment cost.
- Results may be sensitive to the baseline cooperativeness of the participant pool (Bonn vs. London) and may not generalize to all environments.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment and counter-punishment are available.
