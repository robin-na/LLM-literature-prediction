## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior

- no-volunteering rate

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate of volunteering (i.e., the probability or frequency with which individuals choose to volunteer in the volunteer's dilemma game). No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus are reported. The paper discusses over-volunteering as inefficient in the discussion, but does not provide quantitative efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that intergroup competition significantly increases the rate of volunteering in the volunteer's dilemma, even when there is no additional monetary incentive or change in the payoff structure. Risk (a 50% chance of public good failure) reduces volunteering rates, but intergroup competition mitigates this negative effect. Gender and risk aversion moderate these effects: females volunteer more in no-risk settings, males increase volunteering more under risky intergroup competition. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but notes in the discussion that over-volunteering (stimulated by intergroup competition) is socially inefficient.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. Its findings are about volunteering rates in a volunteer's dilemma, which is structurally adjacent to but not the same as a standard public goods game. The results suggest that intergroup competition increases pro-social action rates, especially under risk, but also that this can lead to inefficiency through over-volunteering. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the main takeaway is that mechanisms increasing pro-social action (like competition) may not always improve efficiency, especially in anti-coordination settings like the volunteer's dilemma. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the paper's predictive value for the target task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 3 (N=3).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment consists of 10 consecutive decision periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses whether or not to volunteer (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard MPCR, but the payoff structure is: if at least one volunteers, all get V=12; if none, all get L=4; cost of volunteering is C=2. For N=3, the marginal effect of volunteering is (V-L-C)=6 for the volunteer, (V-L)=8 for non-volunteers.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of rounds (10 per treatment).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed only about the binary outcome of public good production (whether one or more people in their own group invested), not about individual choices or the number of volunteers.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is volunteering rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study does not address punishment effects.
- The game is a volunteer's dilemma (anti-coordination), not a standard public goods game (strategic complementarity).
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency or total group payoff; efficiency is only discussed qualitatively.
- Findings about over-volunteering and inefficiency are not quantified.
- Results may not generalize to standard public goods games or to settings with punishment.
