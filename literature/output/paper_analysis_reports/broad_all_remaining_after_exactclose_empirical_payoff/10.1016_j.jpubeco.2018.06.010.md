# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation and norm enforcement - The individual-level perspective
Authors: Albrecht, F., Kube, S., & Traxler, C.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contribution levels
- group average contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- classification of cooperation and punishment types
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution levels (individual and group), punishment behavior, and type classifications. The paper does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, welfare, or surplus. All analyses focus on contributions and punishment, not on payoff-based efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds substantial heterogeneity in both cooperation and punishment patterns. About half of subjects are pro-social punishers, and about 40% are non-punishers. There is only a moderate correlation between cooperation and punishment types: many conditional cooperators do not punish, and some free-riders do punish pro-socially. In repeated public goods games with punishment, groups with more pro-social punishers achieve higher and more stable contribution levels, even after controlling for the number of conditional cooperators. Regression analyses show that the number of pro-social punishers in a group is a stronger predictor of group contribution levels than the number of conditional cooperators. However, the paper does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public goods games with punishment, the presence of pro-social punishers in a group increases contribution levels. This suggests that enabling punishment can increase cooperation, but the paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoffs. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is informative about behavioral mechanisms (contributions and punishment) but not about the net payoff effects of punishment. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the experimental setup matches the canonical PGG with punishment, so the findings are highly relevant for understanding how group composition and punishment opportunities affect cooperation rates, but not directly efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 in both one-shot and repeated games (partner protocol); matching groups of 8 in stranger protocol.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot games (C-game and P-game); repeated game (R-game) has 10 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject chooses how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4 (each token contributed yields 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Assigning one punishment point costs 1 token to punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished subject's payoff by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism is not available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds in the repeated game (10 periods).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the R-game, results and payoffs are revealed after each period. In C- and P-games, results are revealed at the end.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; all main results are about contribution and punishment behavior.
- No direct evidence on whether punishment increases or decreases group efficiency, due to the lack of payoff-based outcome reporting.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability.
- Punishment is always possible in the relevant games; there is no direct comparison of efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings do not address interactions between punishment and reward.
