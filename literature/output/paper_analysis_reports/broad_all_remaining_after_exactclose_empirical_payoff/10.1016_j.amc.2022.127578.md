Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are the stationary fraction of cooperators and the evolution of fairness/unfairness strategies. Payoff is discussed in the model, but the main results and figures focus on cooperation rates and strategy dynamics, not group efficiency or total payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that allowing individuals to reject unfair resource allocations (interpreted as a form of punishment) leads to higher levels of cooperation, especially when moderate unfairness is present. Both extreme fairness and extreme unfairness are suboptimal for cooperation. The mechanism is explained by cyclic dominance among strategy types. In continuous strategy settings, cooperators tend to act fairer than defectors, and clusters of cooperators can resist exploitation. The model is theoretical and simulation-based, and does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but rather focuses on the fraction of cooperators and the evolution of fairness/unfairness strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the ability to reject unfair offers (a form of costly punishment) can promote cooperation in public-goods-like games, especially when resource allocation is neither perfectly fair nor extremely unfair. However, because the main outcomes are cooperation rates and not efficiency or group payoff, the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to punishment. The model is also theoretical and does not manipulate or report most of the game design dimensions used in the prediction task. Thus, while the paper supports the general idea that punishment for unfairness can promote cooperation, it does not provide direct quantitative guidance for predicting efficiency outcomes in empirical PGG experiments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations are on a 100x100 lattice (10,000 players), but the model is spatial and not a standard small-group PGG.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Simulations run for tens of thousands of steps, but not in discrete rounds as in lab PGGs.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution is binary (cooperate or defect), but resource allocation is continuous in some model variants.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Synergy factor r is varied (e.g., r=1.975, 1.985, etc.), which is analogous to MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Rejecting an unfair offer is interpreted as costly punishment (both get nothing), but the cost is not parameterized as in standard PGG punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection of unfair offers (both lose payoff), not as a separate parameterized technology.

## Important Limitations
- The study is entirely theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.
- Main outcomes are cooperation rates and strategy frequencies, not group efficiency or total payoff.
- The punishment mechanism is implemented as rejection of unfair offers, not as a standard PGG punishment stage.
- Most game design dimensions relevant to empirical PGGs are not manipulated or reported.
- Findings may not transfer directly to small-group, round-based PGG experiments.
