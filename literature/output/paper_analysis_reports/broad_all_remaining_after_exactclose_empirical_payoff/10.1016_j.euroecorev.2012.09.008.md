# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Norm enforcement in the city: A natural field experiment
Authors: Balafoutas, L., & Nikiforakis, N.
Journal: European Economic Review
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rate of norm enforcement (punishment) in response to norm violations
- determinants of norm enforcement (e.g., violator's height, gender, observer's gender)
- survey responses on willingness to punish and reasons for (not) punishing
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is whether an observer punishes a norm violator in a one-shot field setting. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are measured or reported. The study focuses on behavioral norm enforcement, not on economic payoffs or group efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that some individuals are willing to engage in costly punishment to enforce social norms in one-shot, anonymous field interactions, but this is rare (11.7% overall, 4% for non-littering, 19.3% for escalator norm). Men are more likely to punish than women; the violator's height and gender do not significantly affect enforcement rates. The main reason for not punishing is fear of retaliation. The better-known (universal) norm (non-littering) is enforced less often than the environment-specific norm (escalator), despite being more widely valued. The study does not measure or report group efficiency, payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence on the willingness to engage in costly punishment in real-world, one-shot norm violation settings, but does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its findings suggest that, in natural field settings, the rate of costly punishment is much lower than in laboratory public goods games, possibly due to higher perceived costs (e.g., risk of retaliation) and lack of anonymity. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the mere availability of punishment may not translate into high rates of enforcement or efficiency gains in field-like conditions. However, because no group payoff or efficiency data are reported, the paper cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes from design dimensions or control efficiency. Its main value is in highlighting the behavioral limits of punishment in naturalistic settings and the importance of context (e.g., fear of counter-punishment, norm universality) as moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each norm violation is observed by a single individual (the 'observer'), so the effective player count per event is 2 (violator and observer). The broader context is a large public setting, but only one observer can punish per event.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Each interaction is a one-shot event; there are no repeated rounds for any participant.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Observers can verbally confront violators, but there is no pre-play communication or chat among participants. Communication is limited to the act of punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is binary: the observer either enforces the norm (punishes) or does not. There is no gradation or continuous contribution/punishment.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is considered costly (risk of retaliation, social discomfort), but the cost is not quantified or manipulated experimentally.
- punishment technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment consists of verbal confrontation or insistence; the impact per unit cost is not measured or varied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Interactions are one-shot and not repeated; there is no uncertainty about the number of rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is face-to-face and not anonymous; the violator knows who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are measured or reported; only behavioral norm enforcement is observed.
- The study is not a public goods game or direct variant; it examines norm enforcement in naturalistic field settings.
- Punishment cost is not quantified or experimentally manipulated; only inferred from survey responses and context.
- Only one observer can punish per event, limiting generalizability to group settings with multiple potential punishers.
- No control condition without punishment is present; the study does not compare efficiency or cooperation rates with and without punishment.
- Findings may not generalize to laboratory or incentivized economic games due to differences in context, anonymity, and perceived costs.
