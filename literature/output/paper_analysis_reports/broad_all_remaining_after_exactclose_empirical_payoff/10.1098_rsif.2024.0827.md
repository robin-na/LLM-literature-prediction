No direct analysis of punishment or explicit sanctioning mechanisms; the focus is on indirect reciprocity and reputation-based cooperation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods games with collective reputation and indirect reciprocity, all of the leading-eight social norm strategies can sustain cooperation in social dilemmas when the group assessment criterion (social strictness) is set at a moderate level. Too relaxed or too strict assessment criteria undermine cooperation. The evolutionary success of different strategies and assessment criteria depends on the payoff environment: stricter criteria are favored in low-payoff (high-dilemma) environments, while relaxed criteria are favored as payoffs increase. The model shows that collective reputation can mitigate social dilemmas and that the interplay between game structure and reputation structure is crucial for the evolution of cooperation. However, the model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and efficiency is not directly measured.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task by showing that, in public goods games with indirect reciprocity and collective reputation (but without explicit punishment), cooperation and expected payoffs are maximized at moderate levels of social strictness. The findings suggest that the structure of reputation assessment (a design dimension not in the standard list) can play a similar role to punishment in sustaining cooperation, but the absence of explicit punishment means the results cannot be directly transferred to predict the effect of enabling punishment. The paper is most useful for understanding how non-punitive, reputation-based mechanisms affect cooperation and payoffs, and for contextualizing the importance of social norm design. It does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Population size N=60 in main simulations; group size k=10. The model allows for varying group size k, and some results discuss the effect of group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 2x10^5 steps per experiment; evolutionary dynamics are analyzed in the limit of rare mutations (long timescales).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses to cooperate (contribute c=1) or defect (contribute 0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Synergy factor R = alpha * k; MPCR = R / k = alpha. Alpha is varied as the main payoff parameter.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe each other's actions within the group; outside observers see actions with probability q=0.9. Reputation updates are based on observed actions.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanisms are included; all effects are due to indirect reciprocity and reputation dynamics.
- Efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly measured or reported.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment, as the mechanisms differ.
- Model is theoretical and agent-based; no empirical or experimental data.
- Assumes well-mixed populations and homogeneous groups; does not address heterogeneity or spatial structure in detail.
- Assessment criterion (social strictness) is a key design dimension in this model, but is not a standard dimension in most public goods game experiments.
