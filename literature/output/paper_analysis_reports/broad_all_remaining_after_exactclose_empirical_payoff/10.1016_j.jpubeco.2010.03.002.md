# Analysis Report

## Source

Authors: Gächter, S., Nosenzo, D., Renner, E., & Sefton, M.
Journal: Journal of Public Economics
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- aggregate earnings
- individual earnings
- aggregate contributions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual contributions
- punishment and reward behavior (via contribution deviations)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are aggregate and individual earnings (payoff), and aggregate and individual contributions. Punishment and reward are not implemented as explicit mechanisms, but are inferred from contribution patterns (e.g., second movers contributing less or more than best response).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that, in two-player public goods games with asymmetric returns, sequential move structures can lead to lower aggregate contributions and earnings compared to simultaneous moves, but only when the high-return player moves first (FMA treatments). When the low-return player moves first, or when asymmetry is very high (NOFMA treatments), aggregate earnings and contributions are similar across move structures. The predicted first-mover advantage is not observed; instead, fairness concerns and the threat of 'punishment' by second movers (via low contributions) compress the distribution of earnings and contributions. Explicit punishment and reward mechanisms are not present, but behavioral analogues are observed. Efficiency (aggregate earnings relative to the social optimum) is highest in simultaneous and low-first-mover sequential games, and lowest in high-first-mover sequential games, but the differences are moderate and depend on parameterization.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in two-player public goods games with asymmetric returns, the move structure (sequential vs. simultaneous) can affect efficiency, but only under certain conditions. Specifically, efficiency is lower in sequential games when the high-return player moves first, but not otherwise. There is no explicit punishment mechanism, so the findings are most relevant for predicting efficiency in games without formal punishment but with the possibility of endogenous 'punishment' via contribution choices. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that, in the absence of explicit punishment, efficiency is sensitive to move structure and player asymmetry, but not to the presence of formal punishment mechanisms. The results are less directly informative for games with explicit punishment options.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (HIGH and LOW roles).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds per session, with random rematching each round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how many tokens (0-1717) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return varies by player and by number of tokens in the Shared Account (see Table 1). For HIGH: starts at 90, declines; for LOW: 60/55, declines. Not a constant MPCR.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know there are 15 rounds, but only one randomly selected round counts for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of their own and their partner's contributions and payoffs after each round.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment or reward mechanism; punishment and reward are only inferred from contribution behavior.
- Only two-player games are studied, limiting generalizability to larger groups.
- MPCR is not constant and varies by player and aggregate contribution, making direct comparison to standard PGGs more complex.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with explicit punishment or reward options.
- Random rematching and only one round paid may reduce repeated game effects but also limit ecological validity.
