# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Paying for sanctions in social dilemmas: The effects of endowment asymmetry and accountability
Authors: De Cremer, D., & van Dijk, E.
Journal: Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contributions to public good
- contributions to sanctioning system
- concern for social evaluation
- accountability perception
- evaluation expectancy
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount contributed to the public good (first-order dilemma) and the amount contributed to the sanctioning system (second-order dilemma). No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported. Payment structure is described, but efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a step-level public good dilemma, high-endowment members contribute proportionally more to the public good than low-endowment members, regardless of accountability. However, when it comes to paying for a sanctioning system (which is framed as a second-order, continuous public good dilemma without a threshold), high-endowment members do not contribute more than low-endowment members unless they are made accountable and expect to be morally evaluated by the group. Accountability and evaluation expectancy increase contributions to the sanctioning system among high-endowment members, but these effects are not observed for contributions to the public good itself. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as outcomes, so the effect of these manipulations on efficiency is not directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about the psychological and social moderators of willingness to pay for punishment systems in public good dilemmas, especially under endowment asymmetry. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that accountability and evaluation expectancy can increase willingness to pay for sanctions among high-endowment members, but the translation of this effect to efficiency or group payoff is not established. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly about the behavioral mechanisms that might affect the implementation of punishment systems.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Participants make a single contribution decision for each dilemma; no repeated rounds after sanctioning system is implemented.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players; participants are seated in separate cubicles and interact only via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (any amount from 0 to endowment), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the public good dilemma, total group contributions are doubled and divided equally among all group members. For a group of 4, MPCR = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Sanctioning system is funded by group contributions; the least cooperative member is punished by paying 30% of their total income. The cost to the punisher is not individualized; the system cost is shared.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Effectiveness of the sanctioning system is described as increasing with more resources, but no quantitative mapping of cost to punishment impact is given.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment via the sanctioning system.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Participants are told about the structure of the experiment and the number of sessions, but only make one decision per dilemma.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Accountability manipulation varies whether participants expect to have to justify their decisions to the group (identifiability), but the identity of the punished is determined by lowest relative contribution.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes; only contribution behavior is measured.
- The sanctioning system is implemented as a second-order public good without a threshold, which may not match all real-world or experimental punishment systems.
- No repeated rounds after the sanctioning system is implemented; only one-shot contribution decisions are observed.
- No direct evidence on how the presence of a funded sanctioning system affects subsequent cooperation or efficiency.
- Findings are limited to step-level public good dilemmas with endowment asymmetry and may not generalize to other PGG variants.
