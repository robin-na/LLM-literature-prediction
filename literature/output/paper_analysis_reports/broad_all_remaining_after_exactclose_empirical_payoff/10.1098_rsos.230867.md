# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Individualistic attitudes in Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma undermine evolutionary fitness and may drive cooperative human players to extinction
Authors: Pulcu, E.
Journal: Royal Society Open Science
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- evolutionary fitness (expected payoff at Markov stationary state)
- average reward acquired by human and computerized agents
- group and individual payoff matrices
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- transition probabilities (memory-1 strategies)
- mutual cooperation rates
- extinction probability
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based: evolutionary fitness, average rewards, and extinction rates. Cooperation rates and transition probabilities are also analyzed, but the focus is on payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that human players, when interacting with individualistic (extortionate) computerized agents in an iterated UG/IPD setting, are unable to avoid being exploited, leading to significantly lower evolutionary fitness compared to their opponents. In contrast, interactions with prosocial agents result in higher mutual payoffs and more stable cooperation. Over time, cooperation erodes, and human players are at risk of 'extinction' (i.e., being outperformed in payoff terms) in 70% of cases against individualistic agents. The paper demonstrates that the structure of the opponent's strategy (prosocial vs. individualistic) is a key moderator of efficiency outcomes, but does not directly manipulate or analyze punishment mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in iterated dyadic games structurally adjacent to public goods games, the presence of extortionate (individualistic) strategies leads to lower efficiency and higher risk of poor payoff outcomes for cooperative human players. While explicit punishment is not present, the findings are relevant for predicting efficiency in environments where exploitation or extortion is possible. The results suggest that, absent effective countermeasures (such as punishment or reputation), efficiency will be lower when players face individualistic, non-cooperative strategies. For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency will be low when extortionate strategies are possible, and that mechanisms to counteract exploitation (such as punishment) may be necessary to sustain high efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players: human vs. computer agent).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 120 binary decisions per opponent (2 opponents: prosocial and individualistic).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions are via offers and responses.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/offer choices are binary (choose between two ultimatums per trial).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (120 per opponent) is fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observe the accept/reject responses of the computerized opponent after each offer.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; extortion is analyzed as a property of individualistic strategies, not as a formal punishment treatment.
- The game is a dyadic iterated UG/IPD, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant, so generalizability to multi-player PGGs may be limited.
- All computerized opponents are pre-programmed; no human-human interactions are analyzed.
- The number of rounds (120 per opponent) is moderate; longer games may yield different dynamics.
- The mapping from UG to IPD is innovative but may not capture all features of standard PGG or punishment environments.
