## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper develops a formal model in which third-party punishment (TPP) acts as a costly signal of trustworthiness in repeated games. The model shows that when individuals differ in their probability of future interaction (continuation probability), only those with high continuation probability (trustworthy types) find it worthwhile to engage in TPP, as they are more likely to receive future reciprocal benefits. The signaling value of TPP can make punishment equilibrium behavior in parameter regions where, absent signaling, punishment would not occur. The model provides explicit payoff formulas and equilibrium conditions, and demonstrates via evolutionary simulations that the punishment signaling equilibrium is dynamically stable. The presence of TPP as a signal increases the set of parameter values for which cooperation and higher group payoffs are sustained, thus improving efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline.

## Prediction Guidance
For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the availability of punishment as a costly signal can increase group efficiency in repeated games, especially when individuals differ in continuation probability and when signaling is possible. The model gives explicit conditions (in terms of cost/benefit ratios, continuation probabilities, and punishment costs) under which punishment increases efficiency. While the model is not a direct PGG or lab experiment, its structure is closely related to repeated social dilemma games and provides mechanistic insight into when and why punishment will improve efficiency. The findings suggest that, in environments where signaling is possible and individuals can benefit from future interactions, enabling punishment can lead to higher efficiency than disabling it, provided the cost of punishment is not too high.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is based on pairwise interactions (Signaler, Chooser, and Victim roles), with evolutionary simulations run in a population of N=60 agents. The core game is dyadic, but the population context is larger.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The game is an asynchronous repeated PD with continuation probability (infinite horizon, geometric discounting). Higher continuation probability (i.e., more expected rounds) is critical for punishment to be equilibrium behavior and for efficiency gains.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/cooperation choices are binary: cooperate (pay cost c, give benefit b) or defect (do nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is b/c. The equilibrium conditions for cooperation and punishment depend on the ratio c/b and the continuation probability. Higher b/c (i.e., higher mpcr) makes cooperation and punishment more likely to be equilibrium behavior, increasing efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is parameterized as k (additional cost of punishing). The model shows that punishment increases efficiency only when k is not too high; if k is too high, punishment does not occur and efficiency is lower.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is modeled as the benefit j to the victim (set to 0 in main examples, but can be varied). Higher effectiveness (higher j) would make punishment more likely to be equilibrium behavior and increase efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is modeled as an infinite-horizon repeated game with known continuation probability; players know the structure but not the exact number of rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is observable and serves as a signal; Choosers can condition their acceptance on whether the Signaler punished.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- The game structure is based on repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG settings.
- Strategy space is restricted to TFT, ALLD, and Reject; more complex or realistic strategies are not considered.
- No explicit modeling of group size effects beyond dyadic interactions.
- No communication, reward, or other institutional mechanisms are included.
- Assumes perfect observability of punishment as a signal.
- Parameter values are illustrative; real-world applicability depends on actual costs, benefits, and continuation probabilities.
