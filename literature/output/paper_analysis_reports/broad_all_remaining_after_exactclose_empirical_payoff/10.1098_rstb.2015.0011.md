# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Interactions between personality and institutions in cooperative behaviour in humans
Authors: Schroeder, K. B., Nettle, D., & McElreath, R.
Journal: Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to the public good
- punishment assigned and received
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variable is the number of tokens contributed to the public good. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings as an outcome, though the structure allows for such calculation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that the opportunity for decentralized punishment in a repeated public goods game increases contributions to the public good compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect is especially pronounced for individuals high in extraversion, who tend to free-ride in the absence of punishment but increase their contributions when punishment is possible. Agreeableness is associated with higher initial contributions regardless of institution, while neuroticism is associated with lower contributions in both conditions. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in contributions with punishment suggests a likely increase in efficiency, subject to the cost of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game increases contributions, particularly among certain personality types (notably extraverts). However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be quantified from this paper alone. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the punishment mechanism is standard (costly, decentralized, 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio). The findings support the general prediction that punishment increases cooperation, but do not allow direct prediction of efficiency outcomes for the downstream task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups were formed from session sizes of 8 to 28, but the exact group size for the PGG is not explicitly stated. The PGG structure follows Herrmann et al. (2008), which typically uses groups of 4, but this is not confirmed here.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game (no punishment and punishment) was played for 10 rounds, for a total of 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: each player could contribute any number of tokens (0-20) per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is 0.4 (public good multiplied by 1.6, divided by 4 players assumed, but group size not explicitly stated).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each negative token assigned costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each negative token assigned reduces the recipient's payoff by 3 tokens (1:3 cost-to-impact ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told they would play 10 rounds, then a different version for another 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, each player was shown the contribution and income of all players in her group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player identity could not be tracked from round to round; punishment was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as an outcome, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
- The exact group size for the PGG is not explicitly stated, though the structure suggests a standard format.
- The order of games was not counterbalanced (no-punishment always before punishment), which could confound order effects, though the authors argue this is unlikely to explain the results.
- The study focuses on individual contribution behavior and personality interactions, not on aggregate welfare or efficiency.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with different punishment structures, group sizes, or sociocultural backgrounds.
