# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Merely Opting Out of a Public Good Is Moralized: An Error Management Approach to Cooperation
Authors: Delton, A. W., Nemirow, J., Robertson, T. E., Cimino, A., & Cosmides, L.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- explicit moralization ratings
- implicit association test (IAT) scores
- anger ratings
- costly punishment assigned
- perceived likelihood of free riding
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are moralization (explicit and implicit), anger, and costly punishment. Only one study (Study 4) uses a real economic game with monetary consequences, but the outcome measured is tokens spent on punishment, not group efficiency or total payoff. No study reports group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that people who opt out of contributing to public goods are moralized and sometimes punished, even if they have not yet free ridden. This preventive moralization is stronger in public goods than in club goods, and is mediated by the perceived likelihood that the non-contributor will free ride in the future. The effect is robust across implicit, explicit, and behavioral measures, and is observed in both student and nationally representative adult samples. However, the studies do not report on group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings, and thus do not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment and moralization are triggered by non-contribution in public goods contexts, even before actual free riding occurs. It demonstrates that costly punishment is more likely in public goods than in club goods, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the presence of punishment mechanisms can increase the likelihood of punishment being used in public goods games, especially when free riding is possible. However, it does not provide direct evidence about how punishment affects group efficiency or welfare, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to informing about the behavioral context in which punishment is likely to be applied.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 4 uses four-person groups in the economic game. Other studies use hypothetical or survey vignettes with group contexts described but not always with a fixed number.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 4 uses a single contribution round and a single punishment round (one-shot game). Other studies are vignette-based and do not specify repeated rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants in the lab experiment (Study 4).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in Study 4 are all-or-nothing: participants choose to contribute all 10 tokens or none.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Study 4, each contribution of 10 tokens is multiplied to 28 tokens and divided among 4 players (public good), so MPCR = 28/4/10 = 0.7.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Study 4, each token spent on punishment costs the punisher 1 token and reduces the target's payoff by 2 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2:1 (each token spent removes 2 tokens from the target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the game has a single round (one-shot).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants learn about others' contribution intentions in vignettes; in Study 4, they do not learn others' actual choices before punishment (strategy method), but are told to assume the target did not contribute.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous in Study 4; participants do not know the identity of the punisher or the punished.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcomes are moralization, anger, and punishment behavior, not group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- Only one study (Study 4) uses a real economic game with monetary consequences, and it does not report group efficiency or total group earnings.
- The experimental games are one-shot and do not model repeated interaction or long-term effects.
- No direct evidence is provided about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or welfare.
- Most studies use hypothetical vignettes or survey methods rather than incentivized economic games.
