# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: From good institutions to generous citizens: Top-down incentives to cooperate promote subsequent prosociality but not norm enforcement
Authors: Stagnaro, M. N., Arechar, A. A., & Rand, D. G.
Journal: Cognition
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate in PGG
- giving in Dictator Game (DG)
- punishment in Third-Party Punishment Game (TPPG)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contributions in a repeated PGG, giving in a DG, and punishment in a TPPG. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is reported. The closest is the analysis of contribution rates, but efficiency is not explicitly calculated or discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The presence of a centralized punishment institution in a repeated PGG (with 3 players, 10 rounds, and varying inspection probabilities) leads to a substantial increase in contribution rates compared to a no-punishment control. This institutional exposure also increases subsequent prosociality in a Dictator Game, but does not directly increase punishment behavior in a Third-Party Punishment Game. The effect on punishment is only indirect, mediated by increased prosociality. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a centralized punishment institution in a repeated PGG increases contribution rates, which would likely increase efficiency, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly. The design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified, and the manipulation of punishment is clear. However, for the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with and without punishment), this paper only provides indirect support via contribution rates. It does not allow direct estimation of efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that even minimal punishment is sufficient to substantially increase cooperation, and that further increases in punishment probability do not yield additional gains in contribution. The absence of direct efficiency data is a limitation for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG groups consisted of 3 players in all conditions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The repeated PGG lasted 10 rounds, and this was public knowledge.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication between players; standard online PGG with no communication implied.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could contribute any amount from 0 to 140 points in each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR was either 0.4 (M=1.2) or 0.5 (M=1.5), randomly assigned across sessions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Centralized punishment: if inspected, participants were fined 1.5 points for each point below the maximum contribution. No cost to the punisher (punishment is institutional, not peer).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each point not contributed (below 140) resulted in a 1.5 point fine if inspected. Inspection probability varied (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism was present; only punishment was used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of rounds (10) was made public knowledge to all participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Standard PGG design implies that players saw the total group contribution and their own payoff, but the paper does not specify details about summary information shown.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Punishment was centralized and anonymous; no identification of who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral outcomes (contribution, giving, punishment) are analyzed.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the reported results.
- The sample is limited to US-based MTurk participants, which may limit generalizability.
- No pre-treatment measure of prosociality or efficiency is included, so the direction of change (increase vs. decrease) is ambiguous.
- The punishment mechanism is centralized and probabilistic, which may differ from peer punishment or other institutional forms.
