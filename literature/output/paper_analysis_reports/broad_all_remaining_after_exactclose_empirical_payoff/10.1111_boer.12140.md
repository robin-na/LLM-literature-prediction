## Source
Title: POLITICAL INEQUALITY, CENTRALIZED SANCTIONING INSTITUTIONS, AND THE MAINTENANCE OF PUBLIC GOODS
Authors: Chang, H. I., Dawes, C. T., & Johnson, T.
Journal: Bulletin of Economic Research
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per round/condition
- final earnings
- earnings of monitors
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public goods
- minimum contribution level set by monitor
- expectations about minimum contribution
- perceptions of legitimacy
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good. Average earnings are reported by condition, but efficiency or group welfare is not directly analyzed as a primary outcome. Payoff data are used as descriptive statistics and as controls, not as the main focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that introducing a centralized sanctioning authority (who can punish low contributors at a cost) increases contributions to the public good compared to no authority. However, electing the authority (democratically) does not further increase contributions compared to random selection. When voting power is distributed unequally, contributions only decrease if the inequality is based on prior economic performance (earnings in the PGG), not if it is randomly assigned. The negative effect of wealth-based political inequality appears to operate through reduced perceived legitimacy of the institution. Average earnings are reported by condition, but efficiency is not directly analyzed as a main outcome.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized punishment institution increases contributions (and likely efficiency) relative to no punishment, but does not support the claim that democratic selection of the punisher further increases efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is likely positive in this design, but the paper does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as the main outcome. The findings suggest that the way punishment institutions are selected (random vs. democratic) may not matter for efficiency, unless political inequality is tied to economic wealth, which can reduce contributions. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment should be coded as increasing efficiency, but the selection mechanism (random vs. democratic) should not be expected to matter unless wealth-based voting is used.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Groups were randomly assigned to 4 or 5 players. Larger group size (5 vs. 4) is associated with lower contributions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 12 rounds total: 6 rounds of standard PGG, then 6 rounds of treatment (with or without sanctioning authority).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is described or allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose any amount from 0 to 1000 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Tokens contributed to the public account are doubled and split equally. For n=4, MPCR=0.5; for n=5, MPCR=0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Monitor pays 100 tokens to deduct 300 tokens from a low contributor.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (100 tokens spent by monitor deducts 300 tokens from target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the number of rounds (12 total, 6 per part).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see the distribution of all group members' contributions and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. The monitor is known as the group monitor, but individual punishments are not described as being attributed to a specific person; the monitor is not anonymous, but the punished may not know who punished them in other designs. Here, the monitor is a known role.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency is not directly analyzed as a primary outcome.
- The experiment is conducted with US undergraduates, which may limit generalizability.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is inferred from contribution and average earnings, not directly measured.
- The study does not include decentralized punishment or peer punishment conditions.
- The negative effect of wealth-based political inequality may be specific to the PGG context, where wealth is associated with selfishness.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may affect external validity.
