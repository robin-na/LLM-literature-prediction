## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
MDD patients exhibit a negative emotional bias and are more likely to reject moderately unfair offers in the ultimatum game compared to healthy controls. This over-sanctioning is not explained by differences in fairness perception or cognitive deficits. The tendency persists after 6 weeks of treatment and is associated with depression severity, impulsivity, and anxiety. The study does not measure or report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral pattern suggests increased personal costs due to more frequent punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that negative emotional states (major depression) can increase the frequency of costly punishment in a bargaining game (ultimatum game). However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions relevant to public goods games. The findings suggest that individual differences in emotional state can moderate punishment behavior, but do not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled in public goods or similar games. The study is most useful for understanding psychological moderators of punishment, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency from game design parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game is a two-player game (proposer and responder); all participants played as responders.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 54 one-shot (single-round) ultimatum game trials per session, each with a different (simulated) proposer.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions were one-shot and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection of an offer, which is costly to the responder (they forgo the proposed amount) and to the proposer (they receive nothing). The exact cost per unit is not specified, but the structure is standard for the ultimatum game.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is standard for the ultimatum game: rejection results in zero payoff for both players.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told there would be no repeated interactions and each trial was a one-shot game; the number of trials was known.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, not a public goods game or direct variant, so findings may not generalize to multi-player cooperation settings.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior (rejection rates) is measured.
- All participants played as responders; proposer behavior was only measured in a single trial.
- The sample consists of clinical MDD patients, all of whom were medicated, which may confound disease and medication effects.
- The study does not manipulate or systematically vary game design dimensions relevant to public goods games.
- The findings are limited to the effect of emotional state (depression) on punishment behavior, not on the structural effects of punishment mechanisms.
