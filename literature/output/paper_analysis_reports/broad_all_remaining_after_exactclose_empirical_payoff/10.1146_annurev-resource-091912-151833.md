## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

## Important Limitations
- This is a review article, not a primary empirical study; it synthesizes results from many experiments but does not report new data.
- The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes or meta-analytic estimates for the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Many findings are context-dependent and may not generalize across all public goods game designs or real-world settings.
- Some dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, mpcr, group size) are discussed only in passing or not systematically analyzed.
- The review highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not robust and can be negative in some contexts (e.g., with counter-punishment or in certain cultures).
