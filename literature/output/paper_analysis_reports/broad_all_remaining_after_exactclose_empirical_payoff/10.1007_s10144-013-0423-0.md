## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper's third model demonstrates that in social dilemma situations (akin to public goods or common-pool resource games), introducing punishment for selfish behavior can increase group welfare, especially when punishment is graduated (i.e., increases with the harm caused). The model shows that with evaluation errors and heterogeneity in player sensitivity to utility, graduated punishment is optimal for maximizing efficiency (group payoff). Severe, non-graduated punishment is only optimal under unrealistic assumptions. The findings support the use of graduated sanctions to improve efficiency in resource management games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public-goods-like environments with social dilemmas, enabling punishment (especially graduated punishment) increases group efficiency/welfare compared to no-punishment baselines, provided that punishment is not excessively severe and is sensitive to the harm caused. The model is abstract but maps closely to PGG-like games with two actions (cooperate/defect), and the efficiency outcome is directly analyzed. The findings are most applicable to settings where punishment can be graduated and where player heterogeneity and evaluation errors exist. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it gives qualitative guidance that enabling (graduated) punishment should increase efficiency relative to control.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Models assume a finite group of N players; results are not parameterized by specific player counts, but group size is a parameter in the welfare equations.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Players choose between two discrete actions: prosocial (cooperate) or selfish (defect).
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model includes parameters for benefit to self (B) and harm to group (H), which together determine the incentive structure analogous to MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. The model assumes pool punishment with cost distributed evenly; cost to punishers is not a central focus, but punishment reduces group welfare if misapplied.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (P) is a key parameter; the model analyzes how varying punishment level affects group welfare and finds that graduated punishment (increasing with harm) is optimal.

## Important Limitations
- All results are theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- Models are highly stylized and may not capture all complexities of real-world public goods or punishment games.
- The model assumes pool punishment with cost distributed evenly, which may differ from peer punishment in lab experiments.
- No explicit parameterization for player count, MPCR, or punishment cost as in standard PGG experiments.
- No analysis of repeated rounds, communication, or other behavioral interventions.
