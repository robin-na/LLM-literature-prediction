Outcome notes:
The chapter is a review and synthesis of experimental and theoretical work, summarizing both payoff-based outcomes (e.g., group earnings, net benefit, total payoff) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation, punishment, trust). It does not report new empirical results but synthesizes findings from many studies.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The chapter synthesizes evidence that punishment opportunities in public goods games reliably increase cooperation and contributions, but the net effect on group efficiency or total payoff is not always positive. The net benefit of sanctions depends on game parameters such as MPCR and the punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When the MPCR is high and/or the punishment is efficient (high fine-to-fee ratio), the net benefit of punishment is more likely to be positive. However, in many experiments, the total costs of punishment (including both the cost to punishers and the fines paid by punished) exceed the extra benefits from increased cooperation, resulting in a net loss in group welfare. The chapter also reviews evidence that sanctions can have negative psychological effects, such as reducing intrinsic motivation and trust, and that the indirect effect of sanctions (changing expectations about others' behavior) can be as important as the direct deterrent effect.

## Prediction Guidance
This chapter provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on structural game parameters, especially MPCR and punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When predicting the efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, it is crucial to consider whether the increased cooperation from punishment will be sufficient to offset the costs of administering punishment. If the control game (without punishment) already has high efficiency, or if the punishment is costly and/or the MPCR is low, enabling punishment may reduce overall efficiency. Conversely, with high MPCR and efficient punishment, enabling punishment may increase efficiency. The chapter also highlights the importance of considering psychological and indirect effects, such as changes in trust and expectations, which may moderate the long-term impact of punishment on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The chapter is a review and synthesis, not a primary empirical study; it does not report new experimental data.
- Reported effects are often context-dependent and may not generalize across all public goods game designs.
- Net benefit of punishment is highly sensitive to structural parameters (MPCR, fine-to-fee ratio, group size), making generalization difficult.
- Negative psychological effects of punishment (e.g., reduced trust, intrinsic motivation) may undermine long-term efficiency, but these effects are not always quantified.
- Some reviewed studies use artificial or stylized laboratory settings, which may limit external validity.
