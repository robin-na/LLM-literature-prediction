## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.
