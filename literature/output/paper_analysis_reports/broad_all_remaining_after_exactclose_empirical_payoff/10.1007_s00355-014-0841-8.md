# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishment, reward, and cooperation in a framed field experiment
Authors: Noussair, C. N., van Soest, D., & Stoop, J.
Journal: Social Choice and Welfare
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of fish caught (as a proxy for cooperation)
- fishing effort (casts per minute)
- punishment and reward assignments
- change in catch/effort after punishment/reward
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: number of fish caught and effort (casts per minute), both interpreted as proxies for cooperation. No direct group payoff, efficiency, or welfare measures are reported. The design induces a social dilemma where group payoff is maximized by full cooperation, but the analysis focuses on catch and effort, not on explicit efficiency ratios.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that introducing punishment or reward opportunities does not increase cooperation compared to baseline: average catch and effort are statistically indistinguishable across treatments. Punishment is directed at non-cooperators, but does not induce greater cooperation in subsequent periods. Reward is used more frequently than punishment, but is not targeted based on recipient cooperation. The results suggest that, in this field setting, peer punishment and reward are ineffective at increasing cooperation, in contrast to many laboratory PGG studies. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported, but the behavioral proxies (catch and effort) do not improve with punishment or reward.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a field setting with a common-pool resource framed as a social dilemma, enabling peer punishment (and reward) does not increase cooperation or its behavioral proxies, compared to a baseline with no sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency observed in many lab PGGs may not generalize to field environments with similar incentive structures but different context, framing, or real-world constraints. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited; it is most informative as a caution about generalizability and as evidence that punishment may not always improve group outcomes, even when the game structure is similar to a PGG.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 participants; group membership fixed for the session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Part 1: 3 periods of 30 minutes each (3 rounds); Part 2: 1 period (free fishing). Punishment/reward only in part 1.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was not allowed and was monitored.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (catch) is discrete and capped: at most 2 fish per period in part 1 (all-or-nothing within period, but not binary).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each fish caught in part 1 reduces each other group member's part 2 fishing time by 10 minutes; the marginal per-capita return is negative for others, positive for self (social dilemma).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher gives up 5 minutes of own part 2 fishing time to reduce recipient's time by 15 minutes (cost: 5 min per 15 min impact).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 5 min block spent on punishment reduces recipient's time by 15 min (punishment effectiveness = 3x cost).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward treatment exists: participants can spend own time to increase others' part 2 fishing time.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder gives up 5 minutes of own part 2 fishing time to increase recipient's time by 15 minutes (cost: 5 min per 15 min impact).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 5 min block spent on reward increases recipient's time by 15 min (reward effectiveness = 3x cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed about the number and structure of periods (3 periods in part 1, 1 period in part 2).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants are informed about the number of fish caught by each other group member (anonymously, random order).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward assignments are anonymous; recipients know how much they received from each group member, but not the identity of the assigner.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measures of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare are reported; only behavioral proxies (catch and effort) are analyzed.
- The field setting (recreational fishing) may differ in important ways from standard laboratory PGGs, including context, framing, and real-world constraints.
- The social dilemma is implemented via time reductions in a real activity, which may affect the salience and interpretation of cooperation and punishment.
- The number of rounds is small (3 periods with punishment/reward), which may limit dynamic effects.
- Punishment and reward are implemented as time transfers, not monetary, which may affect motivation and comparability to lab studies.
