# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Lessons from collaborative governance and sociobiology theories for reinforcing sustained cooperation: a government food security case study
Authors: Montoya, L. A., Montoya, I., & González, O. D. S.
Journal: Public Health
Year: 2015

empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- emergence and sustainability of interagency cooperation
- collaboration between agencies
- commitment and participation in coordination systems
- presence or absence of collaboration under different institutional conditions
Outcome notes:
The paper focuses on the emergence and maintenance of cooperation and collaboration in interagency government arrangements, using a case study of food security policy in Bogotá. Outcomes are qualitative and relate to the presence or absence of collaboration, not to efficiency, group payoff, or any quantitative payoff-based outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that sustained interagency cooperation in public policy (here, food security in Bogotá) depends on (1) strong sponsorship (e.g., from central government or consensus), (2) stability of staff in the coordination system, and (3) mechanisms to reward or punish collaboration levels. The absence of these factors leads to decreased collaboration. The paper draws analogies from sociobiology (e.g., reciprocal altruism, punishment, reward) and collaborative governance theory, suggesting that similar mechanisms are relevant in human institutional settings. However, the findings are qualitative and do not include quantitative measures of efficiency or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It is adjacent to the prediction task, as it discusses the importance of reward and punishment mechanisms for sustaining cooperation in real-world, multi-agent institutional settings, and draws on sociobiological theory. However, it does not manipulate or measure game design dimensions, nor does it report efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its main value is in supporting the general idea that mechanisms for reward and punishment can help sustain cooperation, especially when other supports (like sponsorship or staff stability) are present. It does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence relevant to the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- reward availability: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper discusses the importance of reward mechanisms (e.g., public rankings, recognition) for sustaining cooperation, but does not implement or measure them.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The paper suggests that publicizing rankings or collaboration levels (i.e., making performance visible) could help sustain cooperation.

## Important Limitations
- The study is a single qualitative case study and does not provide generalizable or quantitative evidence.
- No experimental manipulation or measurement of game design dimensions relevant to public goods games.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or other quantitative payoff-based outcomes are reported.
- Punishment and reward are discussed only as theoretical or institutional mechanisms, not as experimentally varied treatments.
- Findings are context-specific to Bogotá's food security policy and may not transfer to laboratory or game-theoretic settings.
