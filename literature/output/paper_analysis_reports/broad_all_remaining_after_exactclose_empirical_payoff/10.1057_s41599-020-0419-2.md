# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Segregating socioeconomic classes leads to an unequal redistribution of wealth
Authors: Pansini, R., Campenni, M., & Shi, L.
Journal: Palgrave Communications
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- final earnings
- redistribution of wealth
- inequality in earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- frequency of punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is final earnings (payoff), with secondary analysis of behavioral choices (cooperation, defection, punishment). Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to maximum possible, but total/group earnings and their distribution are central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated dyadic Prisoner's Dilemma with a costly punishment option, the ability to punish was restricted to one class (rich or poor) in segregated treatments, and randomly assigned in the integrated treatment. In segregated conditions, the class with punishment power earned nearly double the other, increasing inequality, but total group earnings were similar across all treatments. In the integrated condition, earnings were more equal and the marginal increase in total earnings was small. Thus, punishment's effect on efficiency (total earnings) is neutral, but it strongly affects the distribution of payoffs depending on who can punish.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (total group earnings) in a repeated dyadic social dilemma is minimal: enabling punishment does not substantially increase or decrease total earnings compared to control. However, the distribution of earnings is highly sensitive to who can punish: if only one class can punish, that class captures a much larger share of the total payoff. For prediction, this suggests that in similar dyadic games, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency, but can increase inequality if punishment power is asymmetric. The design dimensions most relevant are the symmetry of punishment access and the matching structure (segregated vs integrated).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are played in dyads (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Average of 82.5 iterated rounds per session; number of rounds per dyad determined by a 75% probability stopping rule (randomly ended, unknown to players).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No mention of chat or communication; standard protocol suggests no chat allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choices are discrete: cooperate, defect, or punish (when available). Not continuous contributions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: e.g., if Player 1 punishes, payoff is 1 for punisher, -3 for punished (vs 1,1 for mutual cooperation).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost is defined by the payoff matrix: e.g., punisher gets 1, punished gets -3 (cooperation vs punishment), or -1 and -2 (defection vs punishment).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the number of rounds in advance (random stopping rule, unknown to players, to avoid end-game effects).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, each participant was shown their partner's choice and total payoff scores.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dyadic game: each player knows their partner's action, including punishment, after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a dyadic iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to larger group settings.
- Efficiency is measured as total earnings, not as a ratio to maximum possible cooperation.
- Punishment is only available to one class at a time in segregated treatments, which is not typical of standard PGG designs.
- Socioeconomic status is assigned based on a self-reported index, which may not perfectly capture real-world class effects.
- The sample is limited to Chinese university students, which may limit generalizability.
- The number of rounds is random and unknown to players, which may affect strategy compared to fixed-round games.
