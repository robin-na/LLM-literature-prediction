The paper is theoretical and analyzes equilibrium social welfare (efficiency) in repeated public goods and related games with commitment types. It does not report experimental or empirical outcomes, but all main results are about efficiency, welfare, or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in large repeated public goods or similar games, the possibility of sustaining cooperation and high efficiency depends critically on the ability to punish defectors specifically. If the only available incentives are rewards or withdrawal of provision (i.e., voluntary mechanisms), then cooperation collapses and efficiency is low when there are even a few commitment types (bad apples) who always defect. Involuntary punishment (such as fines) that can be targeted at defectors restores the possibility of cooperation and high efficiency. The main technical result is that if a 'population dominant' action exists (i.e., defecting yields a higher payoff than the population average, regardless of others' actions), then equilibrium efficiency converges to the inefficient outcome as the population grows. If no such action exists, and punishment can be targeted, then high efficiency is possible. The results are general and apply to a wide class of symmetric repeated games, including public goods games with or without transfers.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods and related games. It implies that enabling targeted, involuntary punishment (e.g., fines for non-contributors) is necessary for sustaining high efficiency in large groups with possible commitment types. If only rewards or voluntary mechanisms are available, efficiency will be low in the presence of even a small fraction of defectors. The paper's results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is symmetric, the population is large, and there is a possibility of commitment types. For the prediction task, the presence or absence of targeted punishment (punishment_tech), the nature of the incentive (reward_exists, punishment_cost), and the ability to distinguish defectors are critical moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) will be low in large groups with commitment types unless involuntary punishment is available; enabling such punishment can dramatically increase efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental evidence.
- Results are asymptotic in large populations; for small groups, the conclusions may not hold.
- The model assumes the possibility of commitment types (bad apples) with independent probability, which may not match all real-world settings.
- The analysis focuses on symmetric games; results may not generalize to highly asymmetric environments.
- The model does not consider communication, chat, or other social mechanisms that may affect cooperation.
- The main results assume either perfect or well-specified monitoring structures; the impact of private or noisy monitoring is only discussed briefly.
