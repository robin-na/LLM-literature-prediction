# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The role of emotions in public goods games with and without punishment opportunities
Authors: Noussair, C. N., Tucker, S., Xu, Y. L., & Breaban, A.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution level
- punishment assigned
- emotional state (happiness, anger, valence, etc.)
- change in contribution from period to period
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates, punishment assigned, and emotional states. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare directly, though it discusses how punishment affects cooperation and references prior work on payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that both monetary and non-monetary punishment increase cooperation relative to a baseline with no punishment, with monetary punishment sustaining higher contributions over time. Emotional states, especially happiness and negative valence, are correlated with contribution and punishment behavior, but not directly with payoffs. The effect of punishment on future contributions is stronger for monetary punishment, and the emotional state of the recipient moderates the effect of punishment. The study does not report efficiency or group earnings, but the behavioral patterns are consistent with prior literature showing that punishment increases group welfare by increasing cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players and 10 rounds, both monetary and non-monetary punishment increase cooperation compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to directly predict the change in efficiency from enabling punishment. The findings support the general expectation that punishment increases efficiency via increased cooperation, but for quantitative prediction, one must rely on prior studies that report payoff or efficiency outcomes. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and match canonical PGG lab experiments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of four players per session, fixed for all rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how much of 20 ECU to contribute (0-20).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In monetary punishment (MP), each punishment point costs the punisher 1 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. In MP, each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 3 ECU.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment (monetary or non-monetary) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, players see their own contribution, sum of group contributions, and their earnings. In punishment treatments, they also see individual contributions of each group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: the order of contributions is randomized each period, so players cannot identify who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings outcomes; only contribution and punishment behavior are analyzed.
- All findings are correlational with respect to emotions; the study does not establish causal effects of emotional states on cooperation or punishment.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is included, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The emotional measurement relies on facial recognition software, which, while validated, may not capture all relevant aspects of emotional experience.
