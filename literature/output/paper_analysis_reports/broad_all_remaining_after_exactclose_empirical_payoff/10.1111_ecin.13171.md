# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Dishonesty as a collective-risk social dilemma
Authors: Jiang, S. G., & Villeval, M. C.
Journal: Economic Inquiry
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average net payoff per period
- frequency of collective sanctions (group payoff lost)
- comparison to reservation payoff from honest reporting
- frequency of group reaching sanction threshold
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average claim per die roll (lying behavior)
- frequency of high claims (4,5,6)
- variance of payoffs
- effect of mindset priming
- effect of group size
- risk preferences
- moral identity
- willingness to help
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average net payoff per period (tokens earned), frequency of collective sanctions (group payoff lost), and average claim per die roll (lying). The paper explicitly compares group payoffs to the expected payoff from full honesty, and analyzes how often groups fall below this efficiency benchmark. Non-payoff outcomes include lying behavior, claim distributions, and psychological measures.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in a repeated group lying game with a probabilistic collective sanction (if group claims exceed a threshold, all group members lose their period payoff with 60% probability), most groups fail to coordinate on honest reporting. As a result, average payoffs are significantly below the efficiency benchmark (full honesty). Lying is common, and collective sanctions are frequent. Preferences for truth-telling (moral costs of lying) reduce the frequency of collective sanctions by over 40% compared to a no-lying variant, but are not strong enough to sustain efficiency. Smaller groups have lower average claims and fewer collective sanctions, leading to higher efficiency than larger groups. Mindset priming (collectivist vs. individualist) has only a modest, short-lived effect on claims and no robust effect on efficiency. Risk aversion is associated with lower claims and higher efficiency. Experience with sanctions does not lead to learning or improved efficiency; in fact, some participants increase lying after sanctions to recover losses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that in a collective-risk social dilemma with group-level, probabilistic sanctions (not targeted punishment), the introduction of such a sanction mechanism does not sustain high efficiency: most groups earn less than the full-cooperation benchmark. Efficiency is higher in smaller groups and when moral costs of lying are present, but the effect is partial. The findings suggest that, in public-goods-like environments with only collective (not targeted) punishment, efficiency is likely to be low unless additional mechanisms (e.g., peer punishment, communication) are present. The paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in games with group-level, probabilistic punishment, especially regarding the negative effect of group size and the limited effect of moral costs or mindset priming. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with targeted punishment, but the efficiency loss from non-targeted, collective punishment is clear.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Group size was manipulated between subjects: small groups (3) and large groups (6). Larger groups had higher average claims, more frequent collective sanctions, and lower efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game was repeated for 20 periods in all treatments. No variation in number of rounds, but time trends and block analyses are reported.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players in any treatment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (claim) choices were continuous (1-6 per die roll), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a collective sanction: if group claims exceed threshold, all group members lose their period payoff with 60% probability. There is no cost to any individual for imposing punishment; the sanction is automatic and probabilistic.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: if triggered, all group members lose their period payoff (100% loss for that period). The probability of sanction is 60% if the group exceeds the threshold.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game would last 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants received feedback on their own total claim, whether a collective sanction occurred, and their own payoff. No feedback was provided on other group members' claims.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (collective sanction) was blind: no information about which group member(s) caused the sanction; all group members were punished equally.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is collective, probabilistic, and blind—not targeted peer punishment as in standard PGGs. Results may not generalize to settings with targeted or voluntary punishment.
- The game is a threshold public bad, not a standard linear public goods game; there is no marginal per-capita return.
- No communication or feedback about others' behavior was allowed, which may limit coordination and learning.
- Priming of collectivist/individualist mindset had only weak effects; the manipulation may not have been strong enough.
- The no-lying control was only implemented for small groups with collectivist priming, limiting direct comparison across all conditions.
- The study was conducted in China with student samples; cultural and demographic generalizability may be limited.
- Prosociality was measured with a non-incentivized questionnaire, which may not capture true social preferences.
