## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group payoff (mean DM per group in public goods rounds)
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rate
- probability of receiving help in indirect reciprocity game
- reputation/image score
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group payoff and cooperation rate. Payoff is explicitly reported and compared between treatments. Non-payoff outcomes (contribution, reputation) are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when public goods games are alternated with indirect reciprocity games (where players' reputations are visible and affect future help), cooperation in the public goods game remains high and group payoffs are significantly higher than in treatments where public goods games are played in blocks without reputation incentives. When the possibility of future indirect reciprocity is removed (by announcing only public goods rounds remain), cooperation and efficiency quickly decline. Thus, reputation-based incentives can substitute for direct punishment in maintaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms which link reputation to future rewards (indirect reciprocity) can sustain high efficiency in public goods games, even in the absence of explicit punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that the presence of reputation/reward mechanisms (even without punishment) can lead to high efficiency, especially when players expect future interactions where their reputation matters. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation is salient and can affect future payoffs, and less directly informative for games with explicit costly punishment but no reputation/reward linkage.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session; structure varied by treatment (alternating or block).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; decisions made in silence, separated by partitions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution in public goods game was binary: contribute DM 2.50 or 0.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contribution of DM 2.50 is doubled (DM 5) and split among 6 players: MPCR = 5/6 ≈ 0.83.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Indirect reciprocity game functions as a reward mechanism: donors can give at a cost to themselves, increasing recipient's payoff.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In indirect reciprocity game, donor loses DM 2.50 to give DM 4 to recipient.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: each DM 2.50 spent by donor gives DM 4 to recipient (1.6x impact).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In some treatments, players were told when only public goods rounds remained; this announcement led to a decline in cooperation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players saw all others' decisions, contributions, and payoffs after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were assigned pseudonames for the whole session; all actions were linked to pseudonames, so reputation could be tracked, but real identities were anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit punishment mechanism is present; results pertain to reputation/reward-based incentives, not direct punishment.
- Laboratory setting with student subjects may limit generalizability to real-world public goods dilemmas.
- The effect of reputation is tested in a specific alternating-game structure; results may not generalize to all forms of repeated public goods games.
- Player anonymity is maintained via pseudonames, which may differ from real-world social contexts.
