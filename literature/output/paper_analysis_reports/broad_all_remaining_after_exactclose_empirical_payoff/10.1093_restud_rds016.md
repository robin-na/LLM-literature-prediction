## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. For pure public goods, efficiency is typically increasing in group size; for divisible public goods, it is typically decreasing. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (n) is a key parameter; comparative statics are provided for how efficiency changes with n. For pure public goods, efficiency increases with n; for divisible public goods, it decreases.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model is infinite horizon (repeated game with discount factor δ), but the logic applies to long but finite games as well. Longer games (higher δ) allow higher efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (x_i in R+), not all-or-nothing. The model explicitly assumes continuous action spaces.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is embedded in the benefit function f and the α parameters. Higher marginal benefit (mpcr) increases sustainable efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as grim trigger (loss of future cooperation), not as a costly action per se. The cost is the loss of future benefits, not a direct cost. Lower cost of punishment (i.e., more effective punishment) increases efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the monitoring network's ability to spread news of deviations (effective contagiousness). Higher contagiousness (more effective punishment) increases efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model allows for both punishment and reward in principle, but the main result is that only punishment (grim trigger) is needed to maximize cooperation; rewards are not required.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Infinite horizon (or known finite horizon) is assumed; players know the structure of the game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players observe the actions of their neighbors according to the monitoring network; the structure of information is central to the model.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players can identify who deviated if they observe the deviation; identification is determined by the monitoring network.

## Important Limitations
- The model is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental validation is provided.
- Assumes infinite horizon (or very long games) and common knowledge of the monitoring structure.
- Assumes continuous contribution choices; does not address all-or-nothing or discrete action settings.
- Assumes punishment is implemented as loss of future cooperation (grim trigger), not as a costly action.
- Does not model noisy public monitoring or hybrid monitoring structures (only network monitoring).
- Does not address stochastic costs of cooperation or robustness to mistakes/noise in actions.
- Does not address fairness or distributional concerns; maximum cooperation may be asymmetric.
