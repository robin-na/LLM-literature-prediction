# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Spite and development
Authors: Fehr, E., Hoff, K., & Kshetramade, M.
Journal: American Economic Review
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total group payoff in trust game with third-party punishment
- coordination on Pareto-superior equilibrium in stag hunt
- dictator game allocations (total surplus, equality, and payoff differences)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of punishment of defectors and cooperators
- prevalence of spiteful punishment
- caste differences in punishment and cooperation
- verbal reports of motives for punishment
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (punishment, cooperation, coordination) and payoff-related outcomes (total group payoff, surplus, equilibrium selection). Efficiency is not always directly calculated, but group payoff and surplus are central to the analysis.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper documents widespread spiteful punishment in a one-shot trust game with third-party punishment in rural India: 61–73% of third parties punished cooperators, not just defectors. This antisocial punishment reduces the expected returns to cooperation and undermines the efficiency benefits of punishment. In a repeated stag hunt game, high-caste pairs (who are more spiteful) were much less likely to coordinate on the efficient, high-payoff equilibrium than low-caste pairs. Dictator games show that high-caste subjects are more likely to engage in payoff-reducing, spiteful choices. The findings suggest that in environments where spiteful preferences are prevalent, punishment opportunities may fail to increase, or may even decrease, group efficiency and welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent: when antisocial or spiteful punishment is common, punishment can reduce or fail to improve efficiency, even in games structurally similar to public goods or trust games. The presence of spiteful preferences, as measured by the frequency of punishment of cooperators and by dictator game choices, is a key moderator. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in settings with high antisocial punishment or spite, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and may even decrease it, especially if punishment is not tightly targeted to defectors. Game design dimensions that allow for antisocial punishment (e.g., anonymity, lack of accountability, or cultural context) should be considered as risk factors for negative efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust game with third-party punishment uses 3 players (A, B, C). Stag hunt and dictator games use 2 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust game is one-shot. Stag hunt is repeated for 10 rounds (5 with one partner, 5 with another). Dictator games are one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust game: binary choice for A (send or keep endowment); B (share equally or keep all). Stag hunt: binary cooperate/defect. Dictator: binary allocation choices.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the trust game, for every 2 rupees spent by C, B loses 10 rupees. Cost to punisher is 2 per 10 punished.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 2 rupees spent by C reduces B's payoff by 10 rupees (5:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is known in repeated stag hunt (10 rounds, with partner switch after 5). Trust and dictator games are one-shot and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the trust game, C is informed of A and B's choices before punishing. In the stag hunt, players receive feedback about their partner's choice after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The main experimental game is a trust game with third-party punishment, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- The context is rural India with strong caste divisions; prevalence of spiteful punishment may differ in other cultures or settings.
- Efficiency is not always directly measured as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but is inferred from group payoff, surplus, or equilibrium selection.
- Punishment is only available to a third party, not to direct participants, which may affect generalizability to peer punishment settings.
- The study does not manipulate or systematically vary all game design dimensions relevant to public goods games.
