# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Intergroup cooperation in the lab: asymmetric power relations and redistributive policies
Authors: Mitterbacher, K., Palan, S., & Fleiss, J.
Journal: Empirica
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- migrant contribution rate (binary, all-or-nothing)
- citizen voting for discriminatory redistribution policy
- reciprocity between groups (conditional cooperation and discrimination)
- time trends in contribution and voting behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the binary contribution decision of migrants and the binary voting decision of citizens for or against discriminatory redistribution. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. The paper discusses the social optimum and Nash equilibrium in terms of contribution and distribution, but does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that in an asymmetric intergroup setting, the threat or implementation of discriminatory redistribution (a form of institutionalized sanction) reduces cooperation by the disadvantaged group (migrants). When citizens choose equal sharing, migrants are more likely to contribute, and when migrants contribute, citizens are less likely to vote for discrimination. This reciprocity can lead to mutually beneficial cooperation, but discrimination or lack of cooperation can trigger downward spirals. The mere possibility of discrimination reduces cooperation compared to a baseline where only equal sharing is possible. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution and voting rates), not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, discriminatory redistribution) on efficiency in public-goods-like games with asymmetric power. The findings suggest that institutionalized sanctions (discriminatory redistribution) can reduce cooperation and thus would likely reduce efficiency, especially when the disadvantaged group perceives the system as unfair. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. The design and behavioral results are informative for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment or sanctions may backfire in asymmetric settings, but the lack of payoff data limits its direct utility for the prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 6 players: 3 migrants and 3 citizens. All sessions use this fixed group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per session; first 5 are initialization with only equal sharing, periods 6-20 allow for discriminatory policy voting.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Migrants' contribution decision is binary (all-or-nothing): contribute full endowment or nothing. Citizens always contribute full endowment automatically.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total contributions are multiplied by 2.5; with 6 players, MPCR = 2.5/6 ≈ 0.417.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are aware of the total number of periods (20).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, citizens and migrants observe each other's contribution and voting behavior.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (contribution and voting rates) are analyzed.
- The 'punishment' mechanism is institutional (redistribution policy) rather than individual costly punishment; thus, results may not generalize to standard punishment treatments.
- All participants are university students, not actual migrants or citizens, limiting external validity.
- Lab setting may not capture all relevant social norms or real-world complexities.
- Fixed group size and roles; only one form of asymmetry (redistribution power) is studied.
