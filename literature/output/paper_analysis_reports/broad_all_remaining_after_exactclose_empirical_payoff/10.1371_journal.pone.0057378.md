## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, punishment can be self-interested and increase group efficiency when the cost to punishers is outweighed by the benefits of increased cooperation. The key condition is that the cost to the punisher (p) is less than the product of the punishment impact (q), the benefit per cooperative act (b), divided by the net cost of cooperation (c): p < q*b/c. Simulations confirm that with typical lab parameters (e.g., group size 3, k=2, p=1, q=3), punishment leads to high stable cooperation and high group payoffs. The effect is robust to group size and number of rounds, provided the parameter condition holds. The paper also explains why punishment sometimes fails to increase payoffs (e.g., when p is too high or q is too low), and why punishment is more effective in repeated than one-shot games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for predicting that, in repeated public goods games with punishment enabled and with favorable punishment parameters (low p, high q), efficiency will be substantially higher than in control games without punishment. The key moderators are the punishment cost (p), punishment effectiveness (q), group size (g), and the number of rounds (m). The paper gives explicit formulas for when punishment will increase efficiency, and shows that the effect is robust across a range of typical lab parameters. If the control game has low efficiency and the punishment parameters satisfy p < q*b/c, enabling punishment should lead to a large increase in efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and simulation-based; it does not report new empirical or experimental data.
- Simulations assume binary (all-or-nothing) cooperation and punishment decisions, which may not capture the full range of behaviors in real experiments.
- The model assumes defectors are responsive to punishment and can switch to cooperation, which may not always hold in practice.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or reward mechanisms, which can also affect cooperation and punishment dynamics.
- The analysis focuses on repeated games; results may not generalize to one-shot games or games with uncertain continuation.
- No direct modeling of information conditions (e.g., whether players know the number of rounds, or can identify punishers).
