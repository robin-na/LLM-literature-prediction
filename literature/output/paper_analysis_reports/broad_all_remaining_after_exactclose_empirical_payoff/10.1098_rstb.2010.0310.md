## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper synthesizes theoretical and empirical literature to argue that human cooperation and the willingness to punish defectors in public goods games are products of gene-culture coevolution. It reviews evidence that, in public goods games, cooperation typically decays over time unless costly punishment is available, in which case cooperation is sustained or even increases. The paper references Fehr & Gächter's experiments showing that allowing punishment prevents the decay of cooperation, but does not report efficiency or group payoff data. The main findings are about the prevalence and rationality of prosocial and punitive behaviors, not about their impact on group efficiency or welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, and references classic experiments that show this effect. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence or direct analysis of efficiency or group payoff outcomes, which limits its direct utility for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions. The referenced experiments (e.g., Fehr & Gächter) are more directly relevant for such predictions, but this paper itself does not supply the necessary outcome data.
