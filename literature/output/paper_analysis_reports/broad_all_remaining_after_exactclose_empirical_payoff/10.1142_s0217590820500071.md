## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

Punishment effectiveness is determined by the selectivity parameter s_P; higher s_P (punishing only the lowest contributor) is most effective for sustaining cooperation and maximizing welfare.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is entirely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes exogenous, institutionally provided incentives (reward and punishment), not peer-to-peer or endogenous punishment.
- The model is static or evolutionary but does not include repeated rounds with learning or history effects.
- No consideration of communication, social preferences, or psychological factors beyond payoff maximization.
- Assumes continuous contribution choices and does not address all-or-nothing or discrete action settings.
- Does not model the cost of maintaining the institution or second-order free-riding in detail, though these are discussed.
