## Source
Title: Money Talks: Rebate Mechanisms in Reputation System Design
Authors: Li, L. F., & Xiao, E.
Journal: Management Science
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- proportion of efficient trades (buyer buys and seller ships)
- average earnings of buyers
- average earnings of sellers
Non-payoff outcomes:
- buyer reporting rate
- buyer purchasing rate
- seller shipping rate
- seller rebate offer rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (proportion of efficient trades) and participant earnings. Behavioral outcomes such as reporting, purchasing, and shipping rates are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that when feedback reporting is costly, market efficiency (proportion of efficient trades and participant earnings) declines. Introducing a rebate mechanism, where sellers can offer a partial rebate to buyers for leaving feedback, increases efficiency—especially when sellers frequently offer rebates. The rebate acts as a signal of seller cooperativeness, increasing buyers' willingness to purchase and sellers' willingness to ship. The effect is robust even when the rebate does not fully cover the reporting cost. The more often sellers offer rebates, the higher the efficiency and earnings for both buyers and sellers. If sellers do not offer rebates, efficiency can be even lower than in the no-rebate, high-cost condition. Over time, sellers learn to offer rebates more frequently, further improving efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a mechanism for sellers to precommit to (partially) covering buyers' feedback costs increases efficiency in a repeated trust game with feedback, especially when sellers frequently use the mechanism. The effect is strongest when the rebate is offered often, and the mechanism works even if the rebate is less than the full reporting cost. For prediction, if a game design allows for a precommitment rebate mechanism and sellers are likely to use it, efficiency should be higher than in a comparable game with high feedback costs and no rebate. The control efficiency (with high feedback cost and no rebate) is a lower bound; the treatment efficiency (with rebate) will be higher, approaching the efficiency of the low-cost or automatic feedback conditions as rebate frequency increases. The findings are most directly relevant to trust/reputation games with feedback as a public good, and less directly to standard PGGs or punishment games, but the mechanism is analogous to reward/precommitment interventions in PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 24-36 pairs (48-72 participants), with buyers and sellers randomly paired each round. The game is two-player (buyer-seller) per match.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment consists of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed; all interactions are via the experimental interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/action choices are all-or-nothing: buyers choose to buy or not, sellers choose to ship or not.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The rebate mechanism is a reward/precommitment mechanism: sellers can offer a monetary rebate to buyers for leaving feedback.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the C10r5 treatment, the seller can offer a rebate of 5E$ (half the reporting cost) per feedback. The cost is only paid if the buyer leaves feedback.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The reward (rebate) is a direct monetary transfer from seller to buyer, paid only if feedback is left. The impact is partial (does not fully cover reporting cost).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game has 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Buyers see the paired seller's feedback from all previous rounds before deciding whether to purchase.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is a repeated trust game with feedback, not a standard public goods game or punishment game; results may not generalize directly to PGGs.
- The intervention is a reward/precommitment mechanism (rebate), not punishment; findings are more analogous to reward than to punishment effects.
- The rebate mechanism's effectiveness depends on sellers' willingness to offer rebates; if sellers do not use the mechanism, efficiency gains are limited.
- The experiment uses a fixed, partial rebate (5E$) and does not test full coverage or variable rebate sizes.
- The experiment is limited to 10 rounds; long-term dynamics in real markets may differ.
- Participants are rematched each round, which may affect learning and reputation dynamics compared to fixed groups.
