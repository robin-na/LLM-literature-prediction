## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that gossip, understood as the spread of reputation (meta-evaluations with hidden sources), enables more efficient social control by reducing the costs and risks (especially retaliation) associated with punishing or excluding cheaters. Ethnographic evidence shows that gossip is structured to avoid direct responsibility and retaliation, while agent-based simulations demonstrate that reputation-based information exchange can maintain higher product quality and more robust partner selection even with many cheaters. However, these findings are in the context of market-like artificial societies and do not directly address efficiency or payoff in public goods games with or without punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that decentralized, reputation-based information exchange (gossip) can improve the effectiveness and reduce the costs of social control mechanisms, such as punishment or exclusion, in environments where cheating is possible. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. The agent-based simulation results suggest that reputation mechanisms can sustain higher system performance (e.g., product quality) under high cheating rates, but these are not directly translatable to PGG efficiency outcomes. Thus, the paper may inform mechanistic reasoning about the role of information and retaliation risk in punishment systems, but does not provide parameter-level evidence for the prediction task.

- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. A central argument is that reputation transmission (gossip) hides the identity of the source, reducing retaliation risk and thus the cost of social control. This is contrasted with image transmission, where the source is known and can be punished. The paper argues that hiding the punishment/reputation source increases the efficiency of social control.

## Important Limitations

- Punishment is discussed as a theoretical mechanism, but not manipulated or measured in controlled experiments.
- Simulation outcomes (e.g., product quality, partner selection) are not directly translatable to PGG efficiency or payoff.
- No quantitative or parameter-level evidence is provided for the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments.
