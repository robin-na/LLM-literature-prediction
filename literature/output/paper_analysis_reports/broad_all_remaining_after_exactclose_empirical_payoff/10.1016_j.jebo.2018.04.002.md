# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Group size effect and over-punishment in the case of third party enforcement of social norms
Authors: Kamei, K.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- expected payoff of PD players (cooperators and defectors)
- group efficiency (implied by total payoffs relative to social optimum)
- aggregate punishment costs
- payoff matrices after punishment
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/cooperation rate (percentage of PD players sending 10 points)
- punishment frequency and intensity
- beliefs about others' punishment
- voting outcomes for punishment rules
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are expected payoffs for cooperators and defectors, and the group-level efficiency loss due to over-punishment. The paper also reports behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rates and punishment behavior, but these are linked to payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Third party punishment in a prisoner's dilemma is effective at deterring defection as group size increases, because aggregate punishment increases even though individual punishment per punisher decreases only mildly. However, with large groups (e.g., 10 third party punishers), over-punishment leads to severe efficiency losses: defectors' payoffs become negative, and cooperators' payoffs are also reduced due to perverse punishment. When third parties are allowed to coordinate punishment levels democratically, over-punishment is mitigated, and efficiency is restored. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive at moderate group sizes but negative at large group sizes without coordination.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on group size and the presence of coordination mechanisms. For small to moderate group sizes, enabling third party punishment increases efficiency by deterring defection. For large group sizes, punishment without coordination leads to over-punishment and large efficiency losses, reducing group payoffs below the social optimum. If coordination mechanisms (e.g., voting) are present, over-punishment is mitigated and efficiency is restored. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency from game design, group size and the presence/absence of coordination mechanisms are key moderators. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper provides expected payoffs for both control and punishment conditions, allowing for estimation of efficiency changes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Group size (number of players per group) is systematically varied: 3, 4, 6, and 12 (corresponding to 1, 2, 4, and 10 third party punishers). The effect of group size on efficiency is non-monotonic: efficiency increases at moderate group sizes due to deterrence, but decreases at large group sizes due to over-punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game (strategy method); each subject makes decisions for all group sizes, but each decision is for a single round per condition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players or third parties.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: send 10 points (cooperate) or not send (defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.3 (10 points sent are tripled, so recipient gets 30; sender loses 10). This is a prisoner's dilemma, not a standard linear PGG, but the MPCR is analogous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost per unit: 1 point deducted from punisher for each punishment point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each punishment point assigned deducts 3 points from the target (punishment tech = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism does not exist; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot (or that only one round is paid); number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed of the actions of the PD players (cooperate/defect) for the purpose of punishment assignment. No feedback between rounds.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; third party punishers assign punishment independently and privately. PD players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a prisoner's dilemma with third party punishment, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Only one-shot interactions are studied; repeated game effects are not addressed.
- No direct measurement of group efficiency as a ratio to the social optimum is reported, but expected payoffs and payoff matrices allow inference.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- Coordination mechanisms are only tested in the largest group size (N=12) and only via median voting; other forms of coordination are not explored.
- The maximum group size is 12; effects for much larger groups are not tested.
- Punishment technology is fixed (cost 1, effect 3); other punishment technologies are not tested.
- Subjects are university students in a lab setting; external validity may be limited.
