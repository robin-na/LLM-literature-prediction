# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Effect of Power Asymmetries on Cooperation and Punishment in a Prisoner's Dilemma Game
Authors: Bone, J. E., Wallace, B., Bshary, R., & Raihani, N. J.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- punishment frequency
- retaliation frequency
- opting out frequency
- switching from defection to cooperation after punishment
- switching from defection to cooperation after partner cooperation
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment, retaliation, opting out) rather than efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The authors explicitly state their focus is on behavioral consequences of punishment, not payoffs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a two-player iterated prisoner's dilemma with punishment, punishment did not increase cooperation rates. Instead, cooperation from a partner was more effective at eliciting cooperation than punishment. Punishment, especially from strong players, sometimes provoked retaliation and further defection. Weak players never retaliated against punishment, but both weak and strong players retaliated against strong punishers. Players were more likely to opt out after being punished or when facing a strong partner. The study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, and the authors explicitly state their focus is on behavioral rather than payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that, in two-player iterated prisoner's dilemma games with punishment, enabling punishment does not increase cooperation and may even have detrimental effects on cooperative behavior, especially when power asymmetries exist. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings suggest that, at least in two-player, all-or-nothing, binary-choice games with possible opting out, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen behavioral outcomes. Caution should be used in generalizing to multi-player or continuous-contribution public goods games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) iterated prisoner's dilemmas.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game lasted 50 rounds, but players were told it would last between 20 and 100 rounds to avoid end effects.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices (cooperate/defect) are binary, all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG, but payoffs are specified: cooperation costs 1, gives partner 3; defection takes 2 from partner, gives 3 to self. Not a linear PGG structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 unit to punisher for both weak and strong players.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: weak players inflict 1 unit loss (1:1), strong players inflict 4 unit loss (1:4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told the game would last between 20 and 100 rounds (actual: 50) to avoid end effects; exact number not known in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw their own and their partner's choices and payoffs for that round, as well as cumulative payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was always attributable; players knew who punished whom, enabling retaliation.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a two-player iterated prisoner's dilemma, not a public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player or standard PGG settings.
- Outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, punishment, retaliation, opting out), not efficiency or group payoff; no direct efficiency data is reported.
- Contribution choices are binary (all-or-nothing), not continuous as in many PGGs.
- Players could opt out of rounds, which is not standard in most PGGs.
- The study focuses on power asymmetries and retaliation, which may interact with punishment effects differently than in symmetric or anonymous settings.
- The time horizon was uncertain to participants, which may affect strategies compared to fixed-length games.
