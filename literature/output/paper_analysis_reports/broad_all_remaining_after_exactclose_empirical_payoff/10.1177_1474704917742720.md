# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Evolutionary Psychology of War: Offense and Defense in the Adapted Mind
Authors: Lopez, A. C.
Journal: Evolutionary Psychology
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- willingness to participate in coalitional aggression
- punitive sentiment toward nonparticipants
- reward sentiment toward participants
- expected personal benefit
- expected group benefit
- sex differences in participation and sentiment
- effect of labor sufficiency/insufficiency on participation and punitive sentiment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are willingness to participate, punitive sentiment, and reward sentiment, all measured via self-report in hypothetical scenarios. Expected personal and group benefit are also measured, but these are expectations rather than realized payoffs. No actual efficiency, group payoff, or realized earnings are measured or calculated.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that (1) willingness to participate in collective aggression is predicted by expected group benefit in defense (public good) and by expected personal benefit in offense (private/club good); (2) punitive sentiment toward nonparticipants is more strongly triggered by willingness to participate in defense than in offense, supporting the idea that punishment is used to enforce participation in public goods contexts; (3) reward sentiment is also modulated by conflict type, with willingness to participate predicting reward sentiment in offense, and expected benefit predicting it in defense; (4) sex differences exist, with males generally more willing to participate, but labor sufficiency cues can increase female participation in defense; (5) labor sufficiency/insufficiency manipulations affect both willingness to participate and punitive sentiment, especially in defense. However, all outcomes are self-reported attitudes or intentions in hypothetical scenarios, not actual behavior or payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect, conceptual support for the idea that punishment is more likely to be used and to have a motivational effect in public goods (defense) contexts than in private/club goods (offense) contexts, and that the effectiveness of punishment as a recruitment/enforcement tool depends on the structure of the collective action problem and cues about labor sufficiency. However, it does not provide any direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency, group payoff, or realized cooperation in public goods games or similar environments. The findings may inform predictions about when punishment will be more or less effective as a cooperation-enforcing mechanism, especially in relation to the publicness of the good and the perceived need for labor, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency outcomes. The study is best used for mechanism and moderator hypotheses, not for direct efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Reward sentiment toward participants is measured as an attitude, and its relationship to participation and expected benefit is analyzed, but no actual reward mechanism or cost is implemented.

## Important Limitations
- All outcomes are self-reported attitudes or intentions in hypothetical scenarios, not actual behavior or realized payoffs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or actual contribution data are collected; the study cannot directly inform quantitative predictions about efficiency.
- No explicit group size, payoff structure, or marginal per-capita return is specified, limiting comparability to standard public goods games.
- Punishment and reward are measured as sentiments, not as costly or incentivized actions with specified impact.
- The subject pool consists of undergraduate students, limiting external validity.
- The within-subjects design for offense/defense may reduce internal validity and power to detect effects.
- The scenarios are stylized and set in a nomadic/tribal context, which may not generalize to modern institutional settings.
