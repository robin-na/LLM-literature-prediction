Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is total revenue (sum of all agents' payoffs), which is directly analogous to group efficiency. The paper also reports the evolution of strategy proportions (trustworthy/untrustworthy) and reputation scores, but these are secondary to the payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that implementing a dynamic, reputation-based reward and punishment mechanism in a trust game on a square lattice network increases the proportion of trustworthy participants and can increase total group revenue (efficiency) compared to a baseline without such mechanisms. The effect depends on the reputation threshold: moderate thresholds maximize both trust and total revenue, while very high thresholds reduce revenue due to excessive punishment. The model demonstrates that, for a wide range of initial conditions, appropriate punishment/reward settings can drive the system to full trust and higher efficiency. The results are robust to population size. The model is not a standard PGG but is adjacent, and the efficiency outcome is directly comparable.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in networked trust games with dynamic reputation-based punishment and reward, enabling punishment (and reward) can increase group efficiency compared to a baseline with no sanctions, provided the punishment/reward parameters are set appropriately. The effect is non-monotonic: too much punishment (high threshold) reduces efficiency, while moderate levels maximize it. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and calibration of punishment/reward mechanisms are critical moderators of efficiency gains from sanctions. The model is adjacent to PGGs but the efficiency outcome is directly relevant.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main simulations use 10,000 players (L=100), with robustness checks at 250,000 (L=500).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10,000 time steps, with results averaged over the last 1,000 steps.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players choose discrete strategies: trustworthy or untrustworthy, as provider or consumer. No continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a reduction in payoff for players below the reputation threshold; the cost is endogenous to the payoff function and depends on the difference between reputation score and threshold.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the magnitude of the reputation threshold and the payoff adjustment formula. Higher thresholds increase punishment severity.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players above the reputation threshold receive a reward (payoff bonus).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward is implemented as a payoff bonus for high-reputation players; cost is endogenous to the payoff function.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the magnitude of the reputation threshold and the payoff adjustment formula.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations have a fixed, known number of time steps (10,000).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players observe neighbors' strategies and payoffs for imitation, but there is no explicit summary information shown as in some PGGs.

## Important Limitations
- The model is not a standard public goods game; it is an adjacent trust game with discrete roles (provider/consumer, trustworthy/untrustworthy).
- All results are from agent-based simulations, not empirical experiments.
- Punishment and reward are implemented via platform-level reputation mechanisms, not peer-to-peer assignment.
- Parameterization of punishment/reward is endogenous to the reputation threshold and may not map directly to real-world costs.
- No communication or chat is modeled.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) as in standard PGGs.
- No analysis of stochasticity or noise in real-world behavior.
