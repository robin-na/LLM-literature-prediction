# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Impact of Team Identity and Gender on Free-Riding Responses to Fear and Cooperation Sustainability
Authors: Gomez-Ruiz, L., & Sánchez-Expósito, M. J.
Journal: Sustainability
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- cooperation sustainability (difference in cooperation rate between last and first five periods)
- cooperation (proportion of high contributions over 30 periods)
- total earnings (average total earnings reported in euros)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- free-riding response to fear (probability of free-riding after both teammates free-rode)
- early/middle/late fear and cooperation rates
- post-experiment questionnaire on intentions to follow free-riding
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: free-riding response to fear and cooperation sustainability, both measured as contribution rates. Payoff outcomes (total earnings) are reported only as descriptive statistics, not as main dependent variables. Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that strong team identity significantly reduces free-riding responses to fear and increases cooperation rates among men, but not among women. The effect of team identity on cooperation sustainability (maintenance of cooperation over time) is marginally significant for men and not significant for women. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. No formal punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on informal control (team identity) and gender differences. The findings suggest that team identity can be an effective informal mechanism to sustain cooperation among men in repeated social dilemmas with binary contribution choices and feedback, but not among women.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It studies a repeated binary-choice social dilemma (close to a public goods game) without any punishment or reward mechanism. The main manipulations are team identity and gender. The outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. Therefore, this paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it does provide contextual evidence about the role of informal mechanisms (team identity) and gender in sustaining cooperation. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation rates in binary-choice, feedback-rich, no-punishment settings, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups are three-person teams.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Binary contribution choice: high (cost 15) or low (cost 0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return: Each high contribution costs 15, increases group income by 20 (from 15 to 75 if all cooperate; 45 if two cooperate). Each player's share is 1/3 of project income.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of periods (30).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants received information about each teammate's contribution and earnings.

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on informal control (team identity) and gender.
- Main outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff.
- Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly analyzed.
- Generalizability to real-world settings is limited due to laboratory context and lack of communication.
- Cannot distinguish between different motives for defection (inequity aversion, risk, competition).
