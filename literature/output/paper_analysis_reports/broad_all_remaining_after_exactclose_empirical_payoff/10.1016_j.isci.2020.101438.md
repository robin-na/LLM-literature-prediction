# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Network Engineering Using Autonomous Agents Increases Cooperation in Human Groups
Authors: Shirado, H., & Christakis, N. A.
Journal: Iscience
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- individual earnings (cooperators vs defectors)
- total contributions (Figure S1C)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- fraction of cooperative players per round
- network structure (clustering, degree, tie rewiring)
- formation of cooperative clusters
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the cooperation rate (fraction of cooperative players per round). Some analysis of individual earnings (cooperators vs defectors) is provided in the supplement, but group-level efficiency or total group payoff is not the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing bots that facilitate the breaking of ties with defectors ('disengaged' intervention) in dynamic network public goods games leads to a substantial increase in cooperation rates among human participants, compared to controls (no bots, bots that always cooperate, or tit-for-tat bots). The effect is robust even when only a single bot is present and when humans are aware of the bots' presence. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperative players per round. There is some supplementary evidence that, under the disengaged intervention, cooperators earn more than defectors, but group-level efficiency or total group payoff is not the main focus. The study does not involve punishment or sanctions in the standard sense, but the network exclusion of defectors is interpreted as a form of decentralized ostracism.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include a punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Instead, it provides evidence about the effect of network engineering (ostracism via tie-breaking) on cooperation rates. The findings suggest that enabling players to break ties with defectors (or having bots facilitate this) can increase cooperation and, to some extent, the earnings of cooperators. However, because efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) is not directly measured or reported, and because punishment is not present, the paper's direct value for predicting efficiency under punishment is limited. It may be useful as adjacent evidence for the effects of exclusion or ostracism mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 16 human subjects per session in both experiments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 30 rounds per session; subjects did not know when the game would end.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; interactions were anonymous and limited to game actions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing per round: cooperate (pay cost per neighbor to benefit all neighbors) or defect (no cost, no benefit).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each cooperator pays $0.05 per neighbor to give $0.10 per neighbor; MPCR per neighbor = 0.10/0.05 = 2, but since each neighbor is a recipient, the group MPCR is not standard; the game is a networked PGG.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects did not know when the game would end (unknown number of rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects were informed of the choices made by their neighbors (cooperate or defect).

## Important Limitations
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; exclusion is via network tie-breaking, not costly punishment.
- Primary outcome is cooperation rate, not group efficiency or total payoff.
- Laboratory setting with stylized online interactions; real-world generalizability is limited.
- Does not explore effects of group size, network topology, or payoff structure variations.
- Does not allow for partner-specific cooperation choices (only global cooperate/defect per round).
