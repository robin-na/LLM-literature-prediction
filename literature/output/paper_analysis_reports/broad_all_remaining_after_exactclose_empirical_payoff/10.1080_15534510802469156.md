# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When sanctions that can be evaded still work: The role of trust in leaders
Authors: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., & De Cremer, D.
Journal: Social Influence
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of chips allocated to the yellow pool (cooperation)
- number of chips kept to self (sanctioned defection)
- number of chips allocated to blue pool (alternative defection/evading punishment)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: allocations to cooperation, sanctioned defection, and alternative defection. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation depends on both the trustworthiness of the leader who installs the sanction and the general trust participants have in authorities. When an alternative, more harmful defection option is available (i.e., sanctions can be evaded), participants with low general trust in authorities are less likely to cooperate if the leader is untrustworthy, and instead shift to the evasion option. High-trust participants are less sensitive to leader trustworthiness. The main outcomes are behavioral allocations; the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of an evadable punishment option (i.e., a trilemma with an alternative defection route) can undermine the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation, especially among participants with low trust in authorities and when the leader is perceived as untrustworthy. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in designs where punishment can be evaded, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and thus efficiency) may be reduced or even reversed for some subgroups. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of evasion options and trust, but not about the quantitative effect on efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of 4 players (plus a non-group leader E).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game was one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: participants could allocate any number of 10 chips among options.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each chip allocated to the yellow pool (public good) was worth 0.20 EUR to each of 4 players, so MPCR = 0.20.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanction was a fine of 0.25 EUR per chip allocated to self (defection). The cost to the punisher (leader) is not specified; the fine is imposed by the leader but whether it is costly to the leader is not described.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each chip allocated to self is fined 0.25 EUR (reducing its value from 0.50 to 0.25 EUR).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was a single round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was imposed by a known leader (person E), and participants were told who installed the sanction.

## Important Limitations
- No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only behavioral allocations are analyzed.
- The experiment is one-shot (single round), so dynamic or repeated-game effects are not addressed.
- The punishment is imposed by an external leader, not by peers, which may limit generalizability to standard PGG punishment settings.
- The cost of punishment to the punisher (leader) is not specified; only the fine to the punished is described.
- The sample is relatively small (n=78) and consists of university students.
- The study focuses on the moderating effects of trust and leader trustworthiness, not on the main effect of punishment on efficiency.
