## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The introduction of implicated punishment (where all group members are fined if a defector is detected) can transform the public goods game into a coordination game, making both full cooperation and full defection stable equilibria. Increasing the monitoring probability, the implicated punishment fine, or the within-group enforcement probability all enlarge the basin of attraction for full cooperation, thus promoting higher cooperation rates. Group size has a nonlinear effect: intermediate group sizes are least conducive to cooperation, while both small and large group sizes favor cooperation. The addition of within-group enforcement (peer punishment) further relaxes the conditions for cooperation to thrive. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but higher cooperation rates in standard PGGs are typically associated with higher efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that implicated punishment and within-group enforcement can substantially increase cooperation rates in public goods games, especially when monitoring probability and punishment fines are sufficiently high. While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the close relationship between cooperation rate and efficiency in standard PGGs suggests that these mechanisms would also increase efficiency. The nonlinear effect of group size is important: intermediate group sizes may be less effective for promoting cooperation than either small or large groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of implicated punishment and within-group enforcement, as well as the values of monitoring probability, punishment fine, and group size, should be considered as key moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are not directly analyzed; only cooperation rates and related evolutionary dynamics are reported.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not consider network or spatial structure.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or explicit round structure.
- Findings may not generalize to empirical settings with more complex social dynamics or additional institutional features.
