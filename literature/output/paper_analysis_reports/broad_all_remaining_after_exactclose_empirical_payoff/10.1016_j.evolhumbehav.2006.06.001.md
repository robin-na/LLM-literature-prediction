# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Audience effects on moralistic punishment
Authors: Kurzban, R., DeScioli, P., & O'Brien, E.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2007

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of third-party punishment
- amount spent on punishment
- emotional ratings (anger, disgust, contempt, selfishness)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and amount of costly third-party punishment under different audience conditions. No group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The presence of an audience (even just the experimenter) increases the frequency and amount of third-party punishment of norm violators in both Trust Game and sequential Prisoner's Dilemma Game settings. Under anonymous conditions, punishment is infrequent and small; when observed, punishment is more common and larger. The effect is strongest when the audience includes other participants. The study does not report on group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs, and does not use a public goods game, but it demonstrates that reputational concerns strongly increase costly punishment behavior.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. However, it offers informative evidence that the salience of observation (audience effects) can substantially increase the willingness to engage in costly punishment. If a public goods game design includes features that make punishment decisions observable to others, one might expect higher rates of punishment, which could (in other contexts) affect cooperation and possibly efficiency. The lack of efficiency or payoff data limits direct applicability to the prediction task, but the findings are relevant for understanding moderators of punishment behavior.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trust Game and sequential Prisoner's Dilemma involve two players per game in Stage 1; third-party punishment is decided by a separate group of participants in Stage 2. The number of punishers per session is reported.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Games are one-shot; each participant makes a single decision per role.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment decisions are continuous (participants can spend any amount in $1 increments up to their endowment). Contribution/defection choices in the games are binary.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is $1 per $3 reduction in target's payoff (i.e., 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed at $3 reduction per $1 spent.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment (deduction) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot; the structure is fully explained.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the 'Participants' condition, punishment decisions are announced to all; in other conditions, only the experimenter or no one knows. Audience presence increases punishment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the 'Participants' condition, punishment decisions are publicly announced by each participant, making identities and decisions known to the group.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or any group contribution game; it uses Trust Game and sequential Prisoner's Dilemma Game settings.
- No efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes are reported or analyzed; only individual punishment behavior is measured.
- The findings may not generalize to public goods games or to efficiency outcomes.
- The audience manipulation is specific to punishment decisions, not to contribution or cooperation choices.
- Sample sizes are moderate and drawn from a single university population.
