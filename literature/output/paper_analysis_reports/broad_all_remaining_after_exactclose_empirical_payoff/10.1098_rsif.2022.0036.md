Outcome notes:
The main outcomes are population-level cooperation rates and the evolutionary stability of strategies, both of which are directly tied to group payoff and efficiency in the evolutionary game-theoretic sense. The model explicitly computes payoffs for all strategies and analyzes how institutional incentives (punishment/reward) affect the prevalence of cooperative, compliant, and non-compliant strategies.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that institutional incentives (punishment or reward) can promote the evolution of cooperation and commitment compliance in a social dilemma with a prior commitment stage. Reward is generally more effective than punishment at promoting cooperation, especially when the cost of commitment is high or the benefit-to-cost ratio is large. Allocating part of the incentive budget to encourage participation in the commitment (not just compliance) further increases cooperation, particularly when participation is costly. The presence of noise in participation decisions can also stabilize cooperation. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and are based on explicit payoff calculations and evolutionary stability analysis.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in social dilemma games with a prior commitment stage, enabling punishment (or reward) increases group efficiency (cooperation frequency) compared to no incentives, provided the incentive budget is sufficient. Reward is more effective than punishment, especially as the cost of participation increases. Allocating part of the incentive budget to encourage participation (not just compliance) further increases efficiency, especially when participation is costly. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in games with a commitment or opt-in stage, and the model's explicit payoff calculations can be mapped to efficiency outcomes. However, the model is based on a two-player, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with a commitment stage, not a standard repeated public goods game, so predictions should be transferred with caution to multi-player, repeated settings.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested.
- The game is a two-player, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with a prior commitment stage, not a standard multi-player, repeated public goods game.
- Findings may not generalize directly to multi-player or repeated settings.
- Assumes institutional incentives are perfectly cost-efficient (cost to institution equals impact on recipient), which may not hold in real-world settings.
- No communication or reputation mechanisms are modeled.
- No explicit analysis of second-order free-riding or who funds the institutional budget.
