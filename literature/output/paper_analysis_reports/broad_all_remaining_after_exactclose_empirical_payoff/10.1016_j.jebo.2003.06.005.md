The model's main dependent variable is the average payoff (efficiency) of network members, as a function of network structure, parochialism, and information. Behavioral outcomes (cooperation, defection, inspection) are also analyzed, but payoff is central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that parochial networks can achieve higher efficiency (average payoff) than anonymous markets in social dilemma environments, due to better information and informal enforcement (via monitoring and exclusion). However, the relationship between parochialism and efficiency is non-linear: too little parochialism leads to poor information and low trust, while too much parochialism reduces trading opportunities and group size, also lowering efficiency. The model includes a monitoring/inspection strategy (analogous to punishment), which, when information is sufficiently accurate, supports a stable trust equilibrium with higher payoffs. The optimal efficiency is achieved at an intermediate level of parochialism, balancing enforcement and trading opportunities.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (monitoring, exclusion) on efficiency in social dilemma games depends on the information structure and group composition. Efficiency is maximized at an intermediate level of parochialism, where information is good enough to support trust and informal enforcement, but group size and trading opportunities are not too restricted. The model suggests that enabling punishment/monitoring can increase efficiency relative to a control (anonymous market) when information is sufficiently accurate and group size is within a viable range. However, excessive exclusion (high parochialism) can reduce efficiency by limiting opportunities. The findings are most relevant for predicting efficiency in environments where punishment is implemented via monitoring and exclusion, rather than costly direct punishment as in standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Player count (network size) is a key variable: efficiency is maximized at intermediate group sizes; too small or too large groups cannot sustain trust equilibria.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model assumes one-shot interactions (not repeated games), to highlight the role of information and network structure rather than repeated-game incentives.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Payoff structure is a prisoner's dilemma: parameters a, b, c, d determine returns to cooperation/defection. Efficiency depends on these parameters, analogous to MPCR in PGGs.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment/monitoring is implemented via the 'Inspect' strategy, which enables exclusion of defectors based on information accuracy (signal quality p). Higher p increases efficiency.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Information about others' behavior (signal quality p) is central; higher information quality increases efficiency.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Network members can identify defectors via information sharing; identification is necessary for exclusion/monitoring to work.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- Punishment is modeled as costless monitoring/exclusion, not as costly punishment as in standard PGG experiments.
- The model assumes one-shot interactions, not repeated games.
- The setting is not a standard public goods game, but a networked prisoner's dilemma with exclusion and information sharing.
- Findings may not transfer directly to environments with explicit costly punishment or different group structures.
