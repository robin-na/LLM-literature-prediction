# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The psychology of deterrence explains why group membership matters for third-party punishment
Authors: Delton, A. W., & Krasnow, M. M.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned by third parties
- inferences about how much dictators value punishers and recipients
- dictator transfer decisions (amount given to recipient)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of punishment assigned by third parties in a third-party punishment game. The study also measures inferences about how much dictators value punishers and recipients, and the dictator's transfer decisions. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that third-party punishment is strongly influenced by group membership: punishers assign the most punishment when an outgroup dictator treats an ingroup recipient poorly, and the least when an ingroup dictator treats an outgroup recipient poorly. This pattern is best explained by deterrence psychology, where punishers infer that outgroup dictators who mistreat ingroup members are likely to mistreat the punisher as well. The group norm maintenance theory (fairness or parochialism norms) does not fit the observed punishment patterns. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on punishment behavior and inferences about personal valuation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality evidence about the psychological mechanisms underlying third-party punishment and how group membership moderates punishment behavior. However, it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most useful for understanding when and why punishment is likely to be assigned, especially in relation to group membership, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment interventions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves three players: dictator, recipient, and punisher.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; game is anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dictator's transfer is discrete: $0, $0.25, or $0.50 out of $1 (0%, 25%, or 50%). Not fully continuous, but not strictly all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers can spend up to $0.50; each $0.10 spent reduces dictator's earnings by 20%.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each $0.10 spent by punisher reduces dictator's earnings by 20% of their stake.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is not available in this experiment, but the absence of reward is discussed as a possible limitation.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are shown the dictator's transfer to the recipient before making punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is anonymous; dictators do not know the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; only punishment behavior and psychological inferences are measured.
- The game is a third-party punishment variant of the dictator game, not a public goods game or direct PGG variant.
- The experiment is one-shot and anonymous, which may limit generalizability to repeated or real-world settings.
- No reward mechanism is available, so the effects of positive incentives are not tested.
- The sample is drawn from US-based MTurk workers, which may limit generalizability.
