# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: When Rules Really Make a Difference: The Effect of Cooperation Rules and Self-Sacrificing Leadership on Moral Norms in Social Dilemmas
Authors: Mulder, L. B., & Nelissen, R. M. A.
Journal: Journal of Business Ethics
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation intentions (planned fish catch, intended break duration, number of lottery tickets taken)
- personal moral norms regarding cooperation
Outcome notes:
All three studies focus on behavioral intentions or actual choices in social dilemmas (resource extraction, break-taking, lottery ticket taking), but do not report group payoff, efficiency, or total welfare. The main dependent variables are individual-level cooperation (less extraction, shorter breaks, fewer tickets taken) and moral norm endorsement.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that installing a cooperation rule increases personal moral norms and cooperative behavior (less resource extraction, shorter breaks, fewer lottery tickets taken) only when the rule is installed by a self-sacrificing leader. If the leader is self-benefiting, rules only increase cooperation when behavior is monitored (i.e., when there is supervision). The effect of rules on cooperation is mediated by the instigation of moral norms, but only for self-sacrificing leaders. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the findings are limited to behavioral and attitudinal outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of rules (and by extension, possibly punishment) in increasing cooperation depends on the perceived legitimacy and self-sacrifice of the authority figure, and on the presence of monitoring. However, because the studies do not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings cannot be directly used to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The results suggest that rules (and possibly punishment) may be more effective in increasing cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) when installed by a self-sacrificing leader or when monitoring is present, but this is not tested in terms of payoff or efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of rules, monitoring (supervision), and leader characteristics, but not punishment per se.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 3: resource game groups of 5; public good game groups of 4. Study 1 and 2 are scenario-based and do not specify group size for the focal decision, but Study 1's scenario implies a village context (many players), Study 2 a workplace (multiple employees).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies use one-shot (single decision) social dilemmas; no repeated rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (e.g., kilos of fish, minutes of break, number of lottery tickets taken), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 3: public good game has MPCR = 0.5 (chips doubled and split among 4). Resource game: remaining tickets are doubled and split among 5. MPCR for resource game is 0.4 (each ticket left is worth 2/5 = 0.4 per person).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies are one-shot; participants know there is a single decision.

## Important Limitations
- The studies do not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; only individual-level cooperation and moral norm measures are reported.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the focus is on rules, leader characteristics, and monitoring (supervision).
- Most studies are scenario-based (Studies 1 and 2), which may limit ecological validity; only Study 3 uses real behavioral choices.
- The findings may not generalize to repeated games, as all studies use one-shot decisions.
- The effect of rules and leader self-sacrifice on actual efficiency or group payoff is not tested.
