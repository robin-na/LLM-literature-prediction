# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Communicating emotions, but not expressing them privately, reduces moral punishment in a Prisoner's Dilemma game
Authors: Philippsen, A., Mieth, L., Buchner, A., & Bell, R.
Journal: Scientific Reports
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- moral punishment rate
- hypocritical punishment rate
- antisocial punishment rate
- punishment bias
- cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are model-based estimates of different types of punishment (moral, hypocritical, antisocial, bias) and cooperation rates. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment is reported. Some descriptive information on participant earnings is provided, but not as a function of the punishment manipulation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two experiments, the authors find that giving participants the opportunity to communicate their emotions to their partner after each round of a Prisoner's Dilemma game with costly punishment reduces the rate of moral punishment compared to a control condition. However, this reduction is small, and moral punishment remains common. Privately expressing emotions (without communication) does not reduce moral punishment. Both emotion communication and private expression can reduce cooperation and increase other forms of punishment (hypocritical punishment, punishment bias). The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality evidence on how emotion communication and private expression affect punishment behavior in a repeated one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with punishment. However, it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment, so it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment rates (especially moral punishment) can be reduced by allowing emotion communication, but the effect is small. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it uses a two-player Prisoner's Dilemma with a punishment stage. The results are most useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) Prisoner's Dilemma games.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 26 rounds (first 6 as training, 20 for analysis).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Emotion communication is a form of restricted chat (sending emotion ratings or emojis to the partner). This reduced moral punishment rates.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: cooperate (invest fixed amount) or defect (invest nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In both experiments, if both cooperate: each invests 30 cents/points, bonus of 20 cents/points is added, total 80 split equally (40 each). MPCR = (total return to group from one person's contribution) / (cost to contributor). For each 30 invested, group gets 30 (other's share) + 10 (half the bonus) = 40, so MPCR = 40/30 ≈ 1.33.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: 1-9 cents to deduct 10-90 cents (1:10 ratio). Experiment 2: 1-3 points to deduct 10-30 points (1:10 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 10x: each unit spent deducts 10 units from partner.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of rounds (26, with 6 training).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see their own and their partner's choices and payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a function of punishment or the punishment manipulation.
- The game is a two-player Prisoner's Dilemma with punishment, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- The main outcomes are model-based estimates of punishment and cooperation rates, not payoff-based efficiency.
- Emotion communication is limited to rating scales or emojis, not free-form chat.
- Partners are simulated (preprogrammed), not real interactive partners.
- No analysis of how punishment affects group welfare or surplus.
