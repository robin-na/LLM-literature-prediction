## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the introduction of institutions—specifically those that include punishment for non-cooperation—transforms the payoff matrix of social dilemmas, such as the tragedy of the commons or property theft, resulting in a shift from inefficient Nash equilibria (low group payoff) to efficient cooperative equilibria (high group payoff). The models show that when punishment is available and used to sanction defectors or freeriders, the group achieves higher total payoffs and resource preservation. The argument is made through theoretical modeling, not empirical data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments will increase group efficiency and total payoff, relative to control conditions without punishment. The models show that punishment changes the incentives so that cooperation becomes the dominant strategy, leading to higher group welfare. However, the paper does not provide empirical estimates or test specific parameter values, so its guidance is qualitative: punishment mechanisms, when credible and enforced, are predicted to improve efficiency in social dilemmas.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Game-theoretic models are presented for two-player games (Prisoner's Dilemma, Hawk-Dove, etc.), but the argument is generalized to groups and populations.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is modeled as changing the payoff matrix to make defection/freeriding less attractive, but specific costs to the punisher are not quantified.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is assumed to be effective enough to deter non-cooperation; effectiveness is not parameterized.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Institutions are said to incentivize through punishment and/or reward, but the models focus on punishment.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical data or parameterized models.
- Game-theoretic models are stylized and do not specify real-world implementation details (e.g., punishment cost, effectiveness, group size, repeated play).
- No quantitative predictions or effect sizes are provided.
- The models assume that punishment is credible and effective, but do not address practical limitations or failures of punishment mechanisms.
- Findings are qualitative and may not account for complexities in real-world or experimental public goods games.
