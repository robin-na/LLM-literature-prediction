Outcome notes:
The model's main dependent variable is the expected payoff (fitness) to each strategy in each guild, which is a direct analog to efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report empirical behavioral outcomes, but rather theoretical predictions about payoffs and strategy evolution.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the ability to appropriate surplus (analogous to control over punishment/reward) and the ability to choose partners (partner choice) interact to determine the evolution of mutualism and the distribution of payoffs in mutualistic associations. When the guild with partner choice also receives the surplus, mutualistic strategies are favored and average payoffs are high. However, the presence of empty slots or high frequencies of exploiters can lead to cyclical dynamics where exploitative strategies temporarily increase, reducing average payoffs. The model predicts that partner choice is most effective at selecting against moderately exploitative partners, but highly exploitative partners can persist under some conditions. The overall effect on efficiency is mixed: partner choice and surplus control can increase efficiency, but not always, especially if exploiters are present or if association opportunities are limited.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical evidence that mechanisms analogous to punishment (partner choice, surplus appropriation) can increase efficiency (group payoff) in mutualism-like bargaining games, especially when combined. However, the effect is not universally positive: the presence of exploiters and the structure of association opportunities (e.g., empty slots) can lead to reduced efficiency or cyclical dynamics. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling partner choice and surplus control generally increases efficiency, but the effect may be moderated or reversed if exploiters can persist or if association opportunities are limited. The model is adjacent to public goods games, so its predictions should be transferred with caution, but it provides informative indirect evidence about the conditions under which punishment-like mechanisms improve or fail to improve efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. The model is two-guild, pairwise matching, but allows for multiple potential partners (e.g., z=2 slots per chooser). The effect of increasing the number of slots/partners is discussed as reducing the success of exploiters.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is evolutionary and assumes repeated generations, not discrete rounds as in PGGs. No direct mapping to finite rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution (demand) is continuous, not all-or-nothing; players can demand any value in a range.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested; predictions are based on mathematical analysis and simulations.
- The model is adjacent to, but not an exact variant of, public goods games; it focuses on pairwise mutualisms and bargaining rather than group contributions.
- No explicit punishment or costly sanctioning mechanism is modeled; partner choice and surplus appropriation are the main control mechanisms.
- No explicit marginal per-capita return (MPCR) or group size effects as in standard PGGs.
- No communication, repeated interaction, or negotiation is modeled; associations are one-shot and determined by fixed strategies.
- The model assumes infinite populations and evolutionary dynamics, which may not map directly to finite, repeated PGG experiments.
- Payoff structure is based on bargaining and surplus division, not on linear or threshold public goods production.
