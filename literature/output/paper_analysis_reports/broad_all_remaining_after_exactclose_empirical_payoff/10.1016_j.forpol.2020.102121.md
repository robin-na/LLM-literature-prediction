# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Collective punishment promotes resource conservation if it is not enforced
Authors: Safarzynska, K.
Journal: Forest Policy and Economics
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- social efficiency (average total earnings per period)
- group profit
- individual earnings
- Efficiency Index (EI)
- Utopia Index (UI)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contributions to sanctioning institution
- beliefs about others' contributions
- amount transferred (trust)
- trustworthiness (split/keep decision)
- player type (conditional cooperator, free-rider, etc.)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable for the prediction task is efficiency (group total earnings relative to maximum possible). The paper also reports on behavioral outcomes such as contributions, trust, and trustworthiness, but the efficiency analysis is central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when participants can voluntarily contribute to a centralized sanctioning institution (which probabilistically punishes untrustworthy behavior in a dyadic trust game), they frequently do so, but the resulting institutions are typically inefficient. The cost of forming the institution is not offset by the gains in trust and trustworthiness, so overall group earnings (efficiency) are lower than in the control condition where no sanctioning institution can be formed. This result holds for both partner and stranger matching protocols. The paper also finds that higher sanction probabilities increase trust and trustworthiness, but the aggregate welfare is still lower due to the cost of institution formation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a trust-game environment with voluntary centralized sanctioning, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline, even though trust and trustworthiness increase. For prediction, this suggests that the presence of costly, voluntarily funded centralized punishment may decrease efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly cost-effective. The findings are most relevant for trust-game-like settings with endogenous institution formation, and less directly for standard PGGs, but the negative efficiency effect of costly punishment is a cautionary signal for similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per session; both partner and stranger matching used.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per session; one randomly selected for payment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution to the institution is continuous (any integer amount up to endowment); in the trust game, the trustee's choice is binary (split/keep).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the public goods classification task, MPCR is 0.4 (private) and 1.6 (social). In the main game, the transfer is multiplied by 5 in the trust game stage.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Cost to form the institution is the sum of contributions; cost is paid regardless of whether sanction is used. This cost is central to the negative efficiency effect.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is probabilistic, with probability equal to total contributions divided by total endowments; if triggered, the sanction equals the surplus created. Returns to scale are constant.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know there are 10 periods; one is randomly selected for payment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributions, all members learn the total contribution and sanction probability. Beliefs about others' contributions are elicited and incentivized.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust-game-like environment with endogenous institution formation, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize to all PGGs.
- Punishment is centralized, probabilistic, and funded in advance; costs are incurred regardless of whether sanctions are used, which differs from many peer punishment PGGs.
- Returns to scale for punishment are constant, not increasing; this may underestimate the efficiency of coordinated punishment.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- The experiment is conducted in the lab with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The cost structure (contributions not refunded if not used) may overstate the inefficiency of institution formation compared to real-world or alternative lab designs.
