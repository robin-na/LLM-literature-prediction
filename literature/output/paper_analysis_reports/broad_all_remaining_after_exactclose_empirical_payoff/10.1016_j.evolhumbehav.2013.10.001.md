# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The cost of cowardice: punitive sentiments towards free riders in Turkana raids
Authors: Mathew, S., & Boyd, R.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punitive sentiments toward free riders (cowards/deserters)
- motivation to impose direct sanctions (punishment, criticism)
- motivation to impose indirect sanctions (withholding social support, lending, marriage, trust)
- judgment of norm violation
- willingness to cooperate with or support the violator
Outcome notes:
The study measures attitudes and motivations to punish or sanction free riders in a real-world collective action context (Turkana warfare), but does not measure actual payoffs, group earnings, or efficiency. Outcomes are behavioral and attitudinal, not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that Turkana participants are significantly more likely to express punitive sentiments (both direct and indirect) toward hypothetical warriors who free ride (display cowardice) in combat, compared to those who fail to contribute due to inability (lack of skill). Direct sanctions include criticism and corporal punishment, while indirect sanctions include withholding social support, lending, and marriage opportunities. The findings support the idea that both direct punishment and indirect reciprocity are important mechanisms for sustaining large-scale cooperation in acephalous societies. However, the study does not measure actual payoffs, group efficiency, or the effect of punishment on group outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that punitive motivations toward free riders are present in a real-world, high-stakes collective action context, and that both direct and indirect sanctions are socially endorsed. However, because the study does not measure efficiency, group payoff, or actual behavioral outcomes in a controlled game, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. The findings are most useful for understanding the psychological and social underpinnings of punishment in collective action, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Raiding parties are described as large, involving up to a few hundred warriors, but the vignette study is not a formal game with a set player count. The context is large-scale collective action.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Direct punishment (e.g., beating, fines) is described as costly to the punisher (e.g., effort, risk), but no quantitative cost is specified.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is described as effective in changing behavior and as a deterrent, but no quantitative impact per unit cost is given.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Direct rewards are mentioned as potentially important for rare acts like bravery, but not studied in this paper.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is described as being administered by age-mates, often discreetly, and the identity of punishers is known within the social group.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not measure actual efficiency, group payoff, or surplus; all outcomes are attitudinal or motivational.
- The context is adjacent to public goods games but is not a formal economic game with controlled parameters.
- No quantitative manipulation of game design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) is performed.
- Findings are based on responses to hypothetical vignettes, not observed behavior in real or experimental games.
- No direct evidence is provided on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff.
