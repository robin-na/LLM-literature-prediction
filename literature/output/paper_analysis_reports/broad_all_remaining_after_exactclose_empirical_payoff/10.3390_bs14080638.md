# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Impact of Cognitive Load on Cooperation and Antisocial Punishment: Insights from a Public Goods Game Experiment
Authors: Zhao, Y. R., Li, Z. R., Jin, S., & Zhang, X. M.
Journal: Behavioral Sciences
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public goods pool
- rate of cooperation breakdown
- frequency and amount of punishment assigned
- frequency and amount of antisocial punishment
- frequency and amount of punishment for free riders
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contribution levels, punishment frequency, and antisocial punishment. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings. All analyses focus on contribution and punishment behavior, not on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that (1) the presence of a punishment option increases average contributions to the public goods pool, (2) high cognitive load reduces contributions and accelerates the collapse of cooperation, and (3) high cognitive load increases the frequency and amount of antisocial punishment, but does not significantly affect punishment of free riders. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from this paper.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment and cognitive load affect contribution behavior in a two-player linear public goods game. It confirms that punishment increases contributions, and that high cognitive load undermines cooperation and increases antisocial punishment. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The behavioral results (contribution rates) may be informative for predicting efficiency in similar designs, but the lack of payoff data is a key limitation for the downstream prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in two-person groups (dyads).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 10 tokens (integer values). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.8 (multiplier 1.6 divided by 2 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1, 2, or 3 tokens per punishment; each token costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see their own and their partner's contributions and payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants are randomly re-matched each round and cannot identify who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, total earnings, or welfare outcomes; all results are based on contribution and punishment behavior.
- The experiment uses only two-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- Punishment is always anonymous and only available as a costly option; no reward mechanism is present.
- The study focuses on cognitive load as a moderator, which may not generalize to other sources of variation in cooperation or punishment.
- Findings on contribution and punishment behavior may not directly translate to efficiency or welfare effects.
