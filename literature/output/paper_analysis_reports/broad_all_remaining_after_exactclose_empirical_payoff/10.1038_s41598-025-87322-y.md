Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is η_G, defined as the population average group achievement, which is a direct measure of group-level success in the collective risk dilemma. This is a payoff-based, efficiency-like outcome. The paper also discusses the stationary distribution of strategies and the prevalence of different institutional types, but these are secondary to the payoff/efficiency focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a collective risk dilemma (CRD) modeled as a threshold public goods game, the introduction of local, bottom-up institutions—created when a minimum number of group members contribute to their formation, and whose nature (reward or punishment) is decided by majority vote—substantially increases group efficiency (η_G) compared to no institution, reward-only, or punishment-only institutions. Reward-only institutions are generally more efficient than punishment-only, but the electoral (mixed) institution is superior to both, especially at lower risk levels. The model shows that the critical risk threshold for achieving a given level of group efficiency is lower under the electoral institution, and the transition from defection to cooperation is steeper. The model is theoretical and does not use empirical or experimental data, but provides direct predictions for how institutional design affects efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in threshold public goods games with risk of group failure, the presence and design of institutional punishment and/or reward mechanisms have large effects on group efficiency. Specifically, enabling local, flexible institutions that can implement either punishment or reward (as decided by group vote) leads to higher efficiency than fixed punishment-only or reward-only institutions. The model quantifies these effects using the η_G metric, which is directly analogous to efficiency. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that the presence of a flexible, locally decided institution (with both punishment and reward possible) should be expected to yield higher efficiency than either punishment-only or reward-only, and much higher than no institution. The model also provides parameterizations for group size, number of rounds, risk, and institutional costs, which can inform predictions when these dimensions are known.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is based on evolutionary dynamics with repeated group formation and play, but does not specify a fixed number of rounds; it analyzes stationary distributions (long-run outcomes).

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers and rewarders pay a tax (π_t) to fund the institution; the cost per unit of punishment is determined by π_t and the institution's rules.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. The impact per unit cost is determined by the multiplication factor δ (delta) and the way institutional funds are distributed as punishment or reward.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is explicitly modeled as one possible institutional outcome, decided by group vote.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarders pay the same tax (π_t) as punishers; cost per unit of reward is determined by π_t and δ.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. The effectiveness of reward per unit cost is determined by δ and the institutional rules for distributing rewards.

## Important Limitations
- The model is entirely theoretical and does not use empirical or experimental data.
- The CRD is a threshold public goods game with risk, which is close but not identical to the standard linear PGG.
- The model assumes well-mixed populations and does not address spatial or network effects.
- No explicit analysis of finite, fixed number of rounds; results are for stationary (long-run) distributions.
- No consideration of communication, reputation, or individual-level punishment identification.
- Parameter values (e.g., group size, risk, costs) are chosen for illustration and may not map directly to all empirical settings.
