# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Conflict, sticks and carrots: war increases prosocial punishments and rewards
Authors: Gneezy, A., & Fessler, D. M. T.
Journal: Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of costly punishment (Ultimatum Game rejections)
- frequency and magnitude of costly reward (Trust Game returns)
- conditionality of punishment/reward on first-mover behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the rates at which Player Bs punish (reject unfair offers in the Ultimatum Game) and reward (return money in the Trust Game) as a function of the context (wartime vs peacetime). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that during periods of violent intergroup conflict, individuals are more willing to incur costs to punish non-cooperative group members (as measured by higher rejection rates of unfair offers in the Ultimatum Game) and to reward cooperative group members (as measured by higher returns in the Trust Game when the first mover is generous). These effects are observed by comparing behavior before, during, and after the 2006 Israel-Hezbollah war among Israeli senior citizens. The findings support the hypothesis that intergroup conflict elevates sanctioning behaviors that promote within-group cooperation. However, the study does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, focusing instead on the behavioral propensity to punish and reward.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that real-world intergroup conflict increases individuals' willingness to punish non-cooperators and reward cooperators in economic games structurally related to public goods dilemmas. However, because the games used (Ultimatum and Trust Games) are not public goods games and the outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward rates) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The results suggest that contextual factors (such as intergroup conflict) can increase sanctioning behaviors, which, in public goods games, are often associated with higher cooperation and sometimes higher efficiency, but this link is not directly tested here. The study does not manipulate or report on most game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task (e.g., MPCR, player count, punishment cost/tech, etc.), so its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played in dyads (2 players per game: Player A and Player B).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays each game (Ultimatum and Trust) once, with a different partner for each game. No repeated rounds within a game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; interactions are anonymous and via pen and paper.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (proposals in NIS 10 increments in Ultimatum Game, NIS 5 increments in Trust Game), not all-or-nothing.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment in the Ultimatum Game is costly: rejection means both players get nothing, so the punisher forfeits their own share.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is fixed by the Ultimatum Game structure: rejection removes all payoffs for both players.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is possible in the Trust Game: Player B can return money to Player A at a cost to themselves.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding is costly: Player B returns money to Player A, reducing their own payoff.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the Trust Game structure: each unit returned to Player A is a unit less for Player B.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed in advance of the complete set of game parameters, including the number of games (one-shot per game).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Interactions are anonymous; players do not know the identity of their partner.

## Important Limitations
- The games used (Ultimatum and Trust) are not public goods games; findings may not generalize directly to PGG environments.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare; only behavioral outcomes (punishment/reward rates) are analyzed.
- Sample is limited to senior citizens in a single housing facility in Tel Aviv, which may limit generalizability.
- No manipulation or variation of most game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task (e.g., MPCR, group size, punishment cost/tech, etc.).
- The study examines only the effect of real-world conflict context, not structural game features.
