The main outcomes are the prevalence/fixation of honest officers and citizens, and the phase space regions where honest strategies dominate. These are not direct measures of group efficiency or total welfare, but are closely related to the reduction of corruption and the spread of honest behavior. Payoff matrices are analyzed, but the main dependent variables are behavioral (strategy frequencies, cluster growth/shrinkage).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in structured populations, the spread of honest officers (and thus reduction in bribery) is favored over a larger region of parameter space compared to mixed populations. However, the effect of network connectivity is non-monotonic: increasing the degree of the citizen network initially helps honest strategies, but beyond an optimal point, further increases hinder the spread of honesty. Similarly, the degree of the interaction network (citizen-officer links) has an optimal value for maximizing the prevalence of honest officers. Asymmetry in population sizes (more citizens per officer) makes it harder to eradicate bribery. The cost of complaining and the bribe amount are key parameters: lower cost of complaining and lower bribe amounts favor honest strategies. The main dependent variables are the fixation probability of honest officers and the phase space area where honest strategies dominate, not direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, it analyzes how network structure, punishment asymmetry, and behavioral parameters affect the prevalence of honest (non-corrupt) strategies. In public-goods-like environments with asymmetric punishment, structured populations can reduce corruption, but only up to an optimal level of network connectivity. The findings suggest that, for similar games, enabling punishment (asymmetric, targeting only one side) in a structured population can increase the prevalence of socially beneficial behavior, but the effect depends on network connectivity and behavioral costs. For prediction, the paper supports the idea that punishment can improve outcomes (in terms of honest behavior) in structured settings, but warns that too much connectivity can undermine this effect. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not directly measured, the findings should be used as indirect evidence.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player counts for citizens and officers are explicitly set (e.g., N_C=100, N_O=100; also N_C=1000, N_O=100 for asymmetric cases).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for up to 10^10 generations or until fixation; evolutionary dynamics, not fixed rounds.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is modeled as a penalty (p_o) paid by corrupt officers when prosecuted; cost to the punisher (citizen) is the cost of complaining (t), which is varied.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the probability of prosecution (k) and the penalty amount (p_o); both are parameters in the model.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Refund of bribe (r) is modeled as a reward to the citizen if the officer is prosecuted; scenarios with and without refund are analyzed.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is implicit: refund (r) is the bribe amount returned to the citizen; no explicit cost to a rewarder, but the model varies r=0 or r=b.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is the amount refunded (r), which is either 0 or equal to the bribe (b).

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data are reported.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, fixation probabilities), not direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff.
- The bribery game is an asymmetric, two-population game and not a standard public goods game; results may not transfer directly to PGG-like environments.
- Punishment is asymmetric (only officers are punished), which differs from most PGG punishment designs.
- Network structure is regular and stylized; real-world social networks may differ in important ways.
- No direct analysis of how changes in punishment parameters affect group efficiency or total welfare.
