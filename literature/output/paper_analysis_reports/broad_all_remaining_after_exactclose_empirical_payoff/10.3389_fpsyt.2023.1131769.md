Outcome notes:
The paper is primarily a theoretical modeling study, using evolutionary game theory to explain and fit behavioral outcomes from prior experiments. It reports model-predicted and empirical means for offers, cooperation rates, and investments, but does not directly report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff as a primary outcome. Most outcomes are behavioral (e.g., cooperation, punishment, trust), with payoff-based outcomes used as intermediate variables in the models.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that costly punishment, when amplified by network heterogeneity and increased inequality aversion (e.g., via oxytocin), can spread through a network and promote higher levels of fairness and cooperation. This effect is observed in both the ultimatum game and the two-stage prisoner's dilemma with punishment, but not in the trust game, where the absence of punishment means that increased trust does not diffuse. The models fit experimental data well for behavioral outcomes (offers, cooperation rates, punishment rates), but do not directly analyze or report group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms are more effective than reward/trust mechanisms for promoting prosocial behavior in networks.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling costly punishment in networked social dilemmas (such as public goods or prisoner's dilemma variants) increases cooperation and fairness, especially when network heterogeneity allows central nodes to amplify the effect. However, because the paper is theoretical and focuses on behavioral outcomes (not efficiency or group payoff), it does not provide direct quantitative evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The models suggest that punishment is more effective than reward in promoting prosocial outcomes, but efficiency implications must be inferred from increased cooperation and fairness, not directly measured. The paper is most useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators (e.g., network structure, inequality aversion), but less so for direct efficiency prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game and trust game: 9 proposers/investors and 9 responders/trustees; tPDG: 3 central and 9 peripheral nodes (12 total).
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. UG: 60 rounds; tPDG: last 20 rounds used for equilibrium estimation; TG: repeated rounds, but exact number not always specified.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. UG: proposers choose a single offer for all neighbors (continuous); tPDG: cooperate/defect is binary; TG: investment is a proportion (continuous).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. tPDG: 'cost and punishment are normalized by the neighborhood size'; cost and fine parameters (C, F) are in the model. Costly punishment is central to the mechanism.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per cost) is modeled as F-C; higher effectiveness increases willingness to punish and promotes cooperation/fairness.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Trust game (TG) is a reward-based game (investor's trust is a reward to trustee); reward is found to be less effective than punishment for spreading prosociality.
- reward cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In TG, investment is a cost to the investor (rewarder); the model includes investment as a cost, but does not analyze reward cost per se.
- reward technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is modeled as the return ratio r and multiplier g in TG; found to be insufficient to promote global prosociality.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players in the referenced experiments were informed of choices and payoffs of other types of subjects, but details are not the focus of this paper.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and does not directly report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as primary outcomes.
- Behavioral outcomes (cooperation, punishment, trust) are modeled and fit to data, but efficiency effects must be inferred indirectly.
- The models are based on prior experimental data (Li et al. 38), not new experiments.
- The games modeled are not classic public goods games, but adjacent games (ultimatum, prisoner's dilemma with punishment, trust game).
- Network structure is fixed and specific (heterogeneous networks with central and peripheral nodes); generalizability to other network types is not tested.
- Assumes evolutionary dynamics and mean-field approximations, which may not capture all features of real human decision-making.
