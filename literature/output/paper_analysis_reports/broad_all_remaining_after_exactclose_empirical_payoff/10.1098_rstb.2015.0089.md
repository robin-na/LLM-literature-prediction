## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Negotiation and appeasement mechanisms, modeled as responsive strategies where dominants can threaten aggression (punishment) and subordinates can appease by helping, lead to higher group productivity than kin-selected (unconditional) helping. The model shows that when negotiation is possible, even unrelated individuals can achieve high levels of help and group output, with low realized aggression. High relatedness, however, reduces the effectiveness of punishment/enforcement, leading to lower help and lower group productivity. Thus, punishment-like negotiation mechanisms are more effective at driving efficient cooperation than kin selection alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of responsive punishment or enforcement mechanisms (negotiation) increases group efficiency in public-goods-like environments, compared to settings where only kin selection operates. The model is not a literal PGG, but the structure is closely analogous: individuals can help (contribute) or not, dominants can punish (aggression), and group output (productivity) is tracked. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment/negotiation will increase efficiency, especially when relatedness is low. The effect is robust across a range of parameter values. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment-enabled conditions on efficiency, and highlights that high relatedness may reduce the marginal benefit of punishment.

- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Aggression (punishment) is costly to the punisher (breeder), with explicit cost parameters (k_a). The model shows that the threat of costly punishment can sustain high help and efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Effectiveness of aggression (punishment) is determined by the reaction norm slopes and cost/benefit parameters; higher effectiveness supports higher help and efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is dyadic (one dominant, one subordinate) and does not generalize directly to multi-player public goods games.
- Punishment is modeled as aggression in a biological negotiation context, not as explicit costly punishment in a standard PGG.
- No explicit reward mechanism is modeled.
- The model assumes continuous help and aggression, not discrete or all-or-nothing choices.
- Group size is fixed at two; effects in larger groups are discussed but not modeled.
- Empirical validation is limited; the model is inspired by, but not directly tested against, real animal data.
- No communication or information-sharing mechanisms are included.
