## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Theoretical analysis and numerical simulations show that: (1) Taxation, when used to fund both labor subsidies for executors and the costs of reward/punishment, can promote and stabilize cooperation in public goods games. (2) Moderate labor subsidies for executors (who implement reward/punishment) are optimal for forming stable alliances of cooperators and executors, excluding defectors. (3) Excessive reward (relative to punishment) or excessive subsidies can destabilize cooperation and allow defectors to invade. (4) The presence of a corruption (bribery) mechanism has little effect on cooperation levels when subsidies are high, but can slow the increase of cooperation as taxation increases. (5) The main outcomes are the steady-state fractions of each strategy; average payoffs are derived but not the focus. (6) The model is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical, mechanistic evidence about how the structure of punishment, reward, taxation, and executor subsidies affect the evolutionary stability of cooperation in public goods games. It is directly relevant for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms (and their funding) interact to affect cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum. The results suggest that moderate, well-funded punishment (and reward) mechanisms, especially when executors are compensated, can stabilize cooperation, but excessive reward or subsidy can backfire. The presence of corruption can reduce the effectiveness of punishment, but this effect is mitigated by higher subsidies. For prediction tasks, this paper informs the expected direction and possible moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The model is entirely theoretical and based on infinite well-mixed populations; no empirical or experimental data are provided.
- Outcomes are reported as steady-state fractions of strategies, not as efficiency or group payoff relative to the social optimum.
- No direct analysis of efficiency or welfare; average payoff is derived but not the main focus.
- The model assumes fixed group size and infinite population, which may not generalize to finite or structured populations.
- No consideration of communication, information feedback, or identification of punishers/rewarders.
