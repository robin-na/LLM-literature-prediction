Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the stationary total wealth of the society, which is directly analogous to group efficiency in a public goods context. The model also tracks the stationary cheater population, but the focus is on the tradeoff between minimizing cheaters and maximizing total wealth.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that in the absence of anti-cheating expenditure, cheaters dominate and total wealth collapses. Introducing and increasing expenditure to fight cheating (via social or punitive means) reduces the cheater population and increases total wealth, but only up to an optimal point. Beyond this, further expenditure reduces wealth due to the cost of enforcement. The optimal strategy involves a balance between social promotion and deterrence, with the best compromise (for the parameter values used) being to spend about 5% of wealth on anti-cheating measures, allocating about 90% of that to social promotion. The model also shows that the effectiveness of punishment (police efficiency) is a critical moderator: if punishment is highly effective, more resources should be allocated to it; if not, social measures are preferable. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and are supported by sensitivity analyses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that introducing punishment or anti-cheating measures in a public-goods-like environment increases efficiency (total wealth) up to an optimal point, after which further increases in punishment expenditure can reduce efficiency. The model highlights the importance of both the amount spent and the effectiveness of punishment. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that enabling punishment (or anti-cheating expenditure) will increase efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude depends on the cost, effectiveness, and allocation of resources. The model also warns that excessive punishment spending can be counterproductive. While the model is not an experimental PGG, its structure and outcomes are closely analogous and informative for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model assumes a large, fixed population, but does not specify a discrete player count as in experimental PGGs. Population is treated as continuous or normalized.
- number of rounds: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model is continuous-time and analyzes stationary (long-run equilibrium) outcomes, not discrete rounds.
- mpcr: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. The model has a fixed wealth production function, with rich and poor classes contributing at different rates. The marginal return per capita is not explicitly parameterized as in PGGs, but the concept is analogous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. The cost of punishment (expenditure on policing) is explicitly modeled as a fraction of total wealth (phi), and its effect on efficiency is analyzed. Increasing punishment cost increases efficiency up to a point, then reduces it if too high.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (parameter k) is directly varied in sensitivity analysis. Higher punishment effectiveness increases the efficiency gains from punishment and can shift the optimal allocation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not use empirical or experimental data.
- It assumes a large, homogeneous population and continuous-time dynamics, which may not capture individual-level or small-group effects present in experimental PGGs.
- Punishment is modeled as a centralized, population-level expenditure (e.g., policing), not as peer-to-peer punishment as in most PGG experiments.
- No reward mechanism is considered; only punishment and social promotion.
- The model does not include stochasticity or spatial structure, though these are discussed as future work.
- Parameter values are chosen for illustration and may not map directly to real-world or experimental settings.
