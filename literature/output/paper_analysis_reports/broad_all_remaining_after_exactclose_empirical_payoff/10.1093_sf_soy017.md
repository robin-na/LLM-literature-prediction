# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Competition for Leadership Promotes Contributions to Collective Action
Authors: Harrell, A.
Journal: Social Forces
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- leader selection/voting
- punishment assigned
- leader/follower enjoyment of role
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is contribution to the public good, both before and after the introduction of a leader with punishment power. No explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported; all results are in terms of contribution rates and related behaviors.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the opportunity to compete for an elected leadership position (with punishment power) increases contributions to the public good before the leader is chosen, but only when voters can observe and use prior contribution behavior in their decision. After the leader is installed, groups with elected leaders maintain higher contributions than those with appointed leaders. The leader's use of punishment does not differ significantly between elected and appointed conditions. The main outcome is contribution rate; no explicit group payoff or efficiency measures are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the process of leader selection (election vs. appointment) and the visibility of contribution history can significantly affect contribution rates in public goods games with punishment. However, because the paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. Its findings are most useful for predicting how leader selection mechanisms and information about contributions affect cooperation, which may indirectly inform efficiency predictions if contribution rates are assumed to map closely to efficiency in similar designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1 uses 4-person groups; Study 2 uses 3-person groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 9 rounds pre-leader, 9 rounds post-leader (18 total, not known in advance). Study 2: one-shot games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; participants were in private rooms and could not see or communicate with others.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: participants choose any whole number from 0 to 20 (Study 1) or 0 to 50 (Study 2).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: Each point contributed is doubled and split among 4 players (MPCR = 0.5). Study 2: Each point contributed is doubled and split among 3 players (MPCR = 0.67).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: It costs the leader 1 point to deduct 3 points from another group member. Study 2: No punishment phase; leader allocates contributions.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 1 point spent by leader deducts 3 points from a target (punishment effectiveness = 3). Study 2: No punishment phase.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment by leader is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told the number of rounds in advance in Study 1. Study 2 is one-shot.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants saw their own and each group member's contributions and earnings, identified by letter. In the election condition, a summary table of total contributions was shown before voting.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could see how many deductions they and others received, but not who assigned them. Participant identifiers changed between phases to prevent linking behavior to identity.

## Important Limitations
- No explicit group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported; all results are in terms of contribution rates.
- The experimental design uses artificial leader selection and punishment mechanisms that may not generalize to real-world collective action settings.
- Visibility of contribution history is high and may not reflect real-world information environments.
- Study 2 does not include a punishment phase; leader power is implemented as allocation of contributions rather than punishment.
- Sample is limited to university students and mTurk participants, which may limit generalizability.
