## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that mechanisms such as punishment and reward, studied extensively in human public goods games, are also relevant in non-human animal cooperation. It reviews how laboratory studies (often using public goods or n-player social dilemma games) have inspired comparative research, and how features like punishment, partner choice, and communication can affect cooperation rates. The authors emphasize the need for more ecologically valid experiments and for understanding how laboratory findings translate to real-world helping and cooperation. The review notes that while punishment and reward can increase cooperation in public goods games, most evidence is about behavioral outcomes, not efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and comparative context for how punishment and reward mechanisms can promote cooperation in public-goods-like games, but does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency effects. It is useful for understanding the mechanisms and moderators that may influence the effect of punishment on cooperation, but cannot be used to predict efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions or control efficiency. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of ecological validity, the role of communication, and the need to consider real-world payoff structures.

## Important Limitations
