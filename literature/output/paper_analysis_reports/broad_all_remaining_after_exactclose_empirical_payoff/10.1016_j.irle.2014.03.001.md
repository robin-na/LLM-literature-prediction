# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: How laws affect behavior: Obligations, incentives and cooperative behavior
Authors: Galbiati, R., & Vertova, P.
Journal: International Review of Law and Economics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- unconditional contribution
- conditional contribution
- beliefs about others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average unconditional contributions, conditional contributions (via the strategy method), and beliefs about others' contributions. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings as primary outcomes, though it notes that higher contributions increase efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Obligations (minimum required contributions) significantly increase average contributions to the public good, even in the absence of incentives. Non-binding incentives (probabilistic, low-stakes rewards/punishments) alone do not affect contributions, but when combined with obligations, they substantially increase contributions beyond the effect of obligations alone. The effect of obligations and incentives is complementary. The mechanisms are traced to changes in beliefs about others' contributions and in individuals' willingness to cooperate. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the design implies that higher contributions increase group welfare.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a one-shot linear public good game with 6 players and no communication, introducing a minimum contribution requirement (obligation) increases average contributions, and adding even weak, non-binding incentives (probabilistic punishment/reward) further increases contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the structure of the game means that higher contributions correspond to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that the presence of obligations and (even weak) punishment/reward mechanisms can be expected to increase efficiency relative to a baseline with neither, especially when both are present. However, the absence of direct efficiency data means that quantitative predictions must be inferred from the reported contribution rates.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main public good game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; interactions are anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous between 0 and the endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.3.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (and reward) is probabilistic and administered by the experimenter, not by peers. The penalty/reward rate is g=1.2, with probability p (either 1/12 or 1/100). The cost is not paid by other players but is a reduction in the subject's own payoff if monitored and found below (or above) the minimum.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the product of monitoring probability (p) and penalty rate (g). Values are p=1/12 or 1/100, g=1.2.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewards are present: if a player is monitored and contributes above the minimum, they receive a reward.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is administered by the experimenter, not by peers. The cost is not paid by other players but is a bonus to the subject if monitored and found above the minimum.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the product of monitoring probability (p) and reward rate (g).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot; players know the structure in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are not provided with information about other individuals' contributions in the same group. At the end, subjects only learn their own payoff.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is administered by the experimenter; there is no identification of punishers or rewarders.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcomes are contribution rates and beliefs, not efficiency or group payoff; efficiency must be inferred.
- Punishment and reward are administered by the experimenter, not by peers, so results may not generalize to peer-sanctioning environments.
- The incentives are non-binding and very weak; findings may not extend to settings with stronger or binding incentives.
- The game is one-shot; repeated interaction effects are not studied.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings.
- All participants are undergraduate students from one country, which may limit generalizability.
