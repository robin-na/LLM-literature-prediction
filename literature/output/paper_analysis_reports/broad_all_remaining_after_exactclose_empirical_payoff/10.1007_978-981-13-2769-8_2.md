## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player count is varied in simulations (e.g., N=10^4 in spatial games, N=221 in diffusion PGG, G variable in PGG definition). The effect of group size is discussed in the context of network degree and group selection.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations are run for many time steps (e.g., up to 10^4 or 200,000 steps) until equilibrium is reached. The effect of repeated interaction (direct reciprocity) is discussed theoretically.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Most models use binary (all-or-nothing) strategies (C or D), but some simulations also consider mixed strategies (probabilistic cooperation).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is discussed as the amplification factor r in PGGs, and as b/c in Nowak's condition for cooperation. The effect of mpcr is central to the dilemma strength.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is explicitly parameterized (e.g., c_s in jealous sanctions, delta in costly punishment). The effect of cost is analyzed in relation to efficiency and cooperation.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (penalty per unit cost, beta = p/c_s) is varied and its impact on cooperation and efficiency is analyzed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players (agents) have access to some information about neighbors' payoffs or reputations in some models (e.g., indirect reciprocity, jealous sanctions), but not in the sense of explicit summary statistics.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is primarily theoretical and simulation-based; it does not report empirical (lab or field) experiments with human subjects.
- Efficiency outcomes are often inferred from simulation or replicator dynamics, not measured in real-world settings.
- The models assume large (often infinite) populations and may not capture all aspects of finite, small-group human behavior.
- Punishment is modeled in various forms (e.g., jealous sanctions, costly punishment), which may not map directly onto standard experimental punishment protocols.
- Some results are sensitive to network structure, update rule, and parameter choices, limiting generalizability.
- Reward mechanisms are not analyzed, so the findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- No explicit analysis of communication, framing effects, or other behavioral interventions.
