Outcome notes:
The main reported outcomes are the sum of contributions (total donations) and the fractions of different strategy types (cooperators, defectors, heterogeneous defectors, altruistic cooperators). The paper does not report efficiency as the ratio of realized group payoff to the maximum possible group payoff, nor does it report total group payoff or welfare directly. Outcomes are primarily behavioral, with sum of contributions as a proxy for group performance.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing punishment mechanisms in PGGs with heterogeneous defectors can significantly increase the sum of contributions (i.e., promote cooperation), especially when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to punishers. The effect is strongest when the loss to defectors is double the cost to punishers. If the punishment is too costly for punishers relative to the loss inflicted, the mechanism fails to promote cooperation. The network structure also matters: small-world and scale-free networks allow cooperation to emerge more easily with lower punishment costs. The main outcome is the sum of contributions, not efficiency or total group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment can increase group contributions in PGG-like environments, especially when the punishment is effective (defector loss >= punisher cost) and when the network structure facilitates information flow. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or total group payoff, its findings should be used as indirect support for the prediction task. The results suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment with sufficient effectiveness (high punishment impact per cost) is likely to increase group contributions, and thus may increase efficiency, especially in networked settings. The effect is sensitive to the relative cost and impact of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group size N=5 in the main lattice model; population size for network models is 600 or 10,000 nodes, but local group size is 5.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 10,000 steps (rounds), with averages over the last 2,000 steps for equilibrium analysis.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: cooperators contribute 1, defectors contribute 0, heterogeneous defectors contribute between 0 and 1 based on their free-rider coefficient.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR (r) is varied (e.g., r=1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4). Higher r increases cooperation and contributions.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost to punisher (p) is varied (e.g., p=0.1 to 0.5). Effect depends on relation to punishment impact (s).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost (s/p) is a key moderator. Higher s relative to p increases cooperation.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players do not know others' strategies in each round, but can know their own previous strategies or donations. No explicit mention of summary information about others.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare directly; only sum of contributions and fractions of player types are reported.
- All results are from agent-based simulations, not laboratory or field experiments.
- The model assumes specific forms for punishment assignment and impact, which may not generalize to all PGG settings.
- No empirical (human subject) data is presented.
- No analysis of stochasticity or robustness to parameter changes beyond those reported.
