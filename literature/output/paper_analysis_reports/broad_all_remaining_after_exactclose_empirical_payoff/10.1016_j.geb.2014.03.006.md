# Analysis Report

## Source

Authors: Lacomba, J. A., Lagos, F., Reuben, E., & van Winden, F.
Journal: Games and Economic Behavior
Year: 2014

Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

- destruction rate (as it affects earnings)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- conflict expenditures (behavioral)
- take rate (behavioral)
- destruction rate (behavioral)
- frequency of peaceful periods
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based: mean earnings, conflict expenditures (as a cost), and the frequency of peaceful outcomes. Behavioral outcomes (conflict, take, destruction) are also reported, but efficiency and group earnings are central.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in one-shot or randomly matched (Strangers) conflict games, conflict expenditures are high and escalate over time, leading to low efficiency (mean earnings well below the endowment). When losers can destroy resources (akin to punishment), conflict expenditures decrease, and in the 'Resistance' game (where destruction can be conditioned on the winner's appropriation), mean earnings are significantly higher than in the baseline. However, destruction itself is wasteful and only increases efficiency if it deters appropriation and reduces conflict. In repeated interaction (Partners), a substantial minority of groups achieve long-lasting peace (zero conflict, high efficiency), but most do not. Peace is achieved through costly signaling (not fighting, low appropriation), not through destruction. Overall, the effect of punishment-like destruction on efficiency is mixed: it can increase efficiency by reducing conflict, but can also reduce it if destruction is frequent. The design dimensions (matching protocol, post-conflict options) strongly moderate these effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, the ability of losers to destroy resources) on efficiency in conflict games is highly context-dependent. In one-shot or random-matching settings, allowing punishment (destruction) can reduce conflict expenditures and, if destruction is conditional on appropriation, can increase efficiency. However, if destruction is frequent, efficiency may not improve. In repeated games, the possibility of peace (high efficiency) depends more on costly signaling and restraint than on punishment. For prediction, the presence of a punishment mechanism (destruction) is likely to reduce conflict and increase efficiency only if it credibly deters appropriation and is not overused. The matching protocol (repeated vs. random) and the structure of post-conflict options are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low in these games; enabling punishment can increase efficiency in some variants (notably Resistance/RE), but not always.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) contests.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 20 rounds: 10 as Strangers (random matching), 10 as Partners (fixed pair).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication allowed; standard lab anonymity and no identifiers.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution to conflict is continuous: players choose any amount from 0 to y.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard PGG; here, the 'return' is 1 for production, 0 for conflict, and appropriation is zero-sum. No explicit MPCR, but the opportunity cost of conflict is direct.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Destruction (punishment) is costly: losers destroy their own resources, reducing their own payoff. The cost is 1:1 (each unit destroyed is lost to both players).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment (destruction) is fully effective: each unit destroyed is lost to both players, not transferred. The effectiveness is 1:1.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds (10 per matching protocol).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the other’s choices (conflict, take, destruction) after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Partners, players know their counterpart; in Strangers, no identifiers. In all cases, the punished party knows who punished (since only two players).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a conflict/contest game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Punishment is implemented as destruction of own resources (scorched earth), not as targeted costly punishment of others.
- All games are dyadic (two-player); group effects may differ in larger groups.
- No communication or chat is allowed; effects of communication are not tested.
- The efficiency effects of punishment depend on the frequency and context of destruction, which may differ in other settings.
- Repeated interaction enables peace in some groups, but most groups do not achieve it; results may depend on subject pool or lab context.
