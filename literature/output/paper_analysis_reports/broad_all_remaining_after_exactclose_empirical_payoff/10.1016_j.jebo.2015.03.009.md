# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inequality, social sanctions and cooperation within South African fishing communities
Authors: Visser, M., & Burns, J.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate (fraction of endowment contributed)
- punishment assigned (punishment points allocated)
- punishment received
- antisocial punishment
- retaliation in punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates and punishment behavior. The paper does not report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare/surplus directly. All outcome analysis is in terms of contributions and punishment, not aggregate payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Punishment opportunities increase contribution rates in both equal and unequal endowment groups, with a larger effect in unequal groups. Low endowment players contribute a higher share of their endowment than high endowment players. Punishment is used less in unequal groups, but is more effective at raising contributions. Demand for punishment does not differ significantly between high and low endowment players, despite higher relative costs for low endowment players. Punishment is mainly targeted at free-riders, with antisocial punishment more common in equal groups. Socio-economic status and social attitudes (e.g., distrust in formal institutions) influence punishment behavior. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that peer punishment increases contribution rates in linear public goods games, especially in groups with unequal endowments. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified, and the findings about contribution rates and punishment behavior are informative for predicting behavioral responses to punishment. For efficiency prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is likely to increase efficiency in similar settings, especially in unequal groups, but the magnitude cannot be inferred. The absence of payoff/efficiency data is a key limitation for direct prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment (VCM and punishment) had 6 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0 to endowment), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR was 0.5 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point assigned cost the punisher 1 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the target's payoff by 5 ECUs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew the number of rounds (6) in each treatment.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players saw the endowments and contributions of all group members before punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players only saw the total punishment received, not who assigned it.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all results are in terms of contribution rates and punishment behavior.
- Findings on punishment effectiveness are based on contribution rates, not on actual group payoffs or surplus.
- External validity may be limited due to the artificial nature of the experimental setting and small group size.
- No reward mechanism was included, so results do not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- Real-world wealth and inequality were not observable to participants in the experiment, limiting the interpretation of those variables.
