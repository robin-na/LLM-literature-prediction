## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that punishment, including altruistic and third-party forms, is a key mechanism for maintaining cooperation in humans, especially in public goods game contexts. It reviews evidence from the literature that allowing punishment in public goods games increases cooperation, but it does not provide new quantitative results on efficiency or group payoff. The paper also discusses the psychological and evolutionary underpinnings of punishment and spite, suggesting that negative other-regarding preferences (spite) may be as important as positive ones (empathy) in shaping human cooperation. The review highlights that while punishment can deter free-riding and enforce norms, it can also be motivated by antisocial preferences, and the net effect on group welfare or efficiency is not always clear from the literature.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and conceptual support for the idea that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation in public goods games, referencing experimental findings from the literature. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the inclusion of punishment as a potentially positive moderator of cooperation, but does not allow for precise quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is useful for understanding mechanisms and for contextualizing empirical findings, but not for direct parameterization of efficiency effects.
