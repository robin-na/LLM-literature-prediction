# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperative Networks: Altruism, Group Solidarity, Reciprocity, and Sanctioning in Ugandan Producer Organizations
Authors: Baldassarri, D.
Journal: American Journal of Sociology
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution in public goods game
- cooperation (measured as selling in bulk via the group)
- attendance at group assembly
- dictator game giving (altruism, group solidarity)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: contributions in PGG, giving in DG, and real-life cooperation (selling in bulk, meeting attendance). No explicit group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes are reported. PGG contributions are used as a proxy for cooperation, but efficiency is not directly calculated or discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that both communication and the threat of sanctioning increase contributions in a repeated PGG compared to baseline. Communication (reciprocity) has a stronger effect than punishment. However, only cooperation in the communication condition predicts real-life cooperative behaviors in the farmer organizations. The threat of sanctioning increases contributions in the lab, but this does not translate to real-world cooperation among council members. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in small, cohesive groups with repeated interaction, communication (reciprocity) is a more effective and relevant mechanism for promoting cooperation than punishment. While punishment increases contributions in the lab PGG, it does not predict real-world cooperative behavior in this context. The absence of efficiency or payoff data means the paper cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it suggests that in similar settings, punishment may not translate into real-world cooperative gains. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that communication/reciprocity is a key moderator, and that punishment's effect may be context-dependent and less robust in small, high-trust groups.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. PGG sessions were run within 50 farmer organizations; group size is not explicitly stated for each session, but the DC council size is reported (N=1,447 across 50 groups, so average group size ~29, but only council members participated in games). Number of subjects per session is included as a control in models (see Table A2).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG consisted of 6 rounds, with 2 preliminary rounds and 4 main rounds after treatment (communication or punishment) was introduced.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (verbal discussion) was allowed in one treatment condition and shown to increase contributions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices in the PGG were continuous: participants could allocate any amount of their 10 MUs between private and public accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public account was doubled and redistributed equally among all group members. Exact MPCR depends on group size, which is not precisely specified for each session.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the punishment condition, the monitor could spend 1 MU to reduce another player's payoff by 3 MUs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 MU spent removes 3 MUs from target).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds (6) in advance.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral measures of cooperation are analyzed.
- The effect of punishment on real-life cooperation is not significant in this context, but this may be specific to small, cohesive groups with repeated interaction.
- Group size in the PGG sessions is not precisely specified, which limits the ability to calculate MPCR and efficiency.
- The punishment mechanism is centralized (monitor-based), not peer-to-peer, which may limit generalizability to other PGG punishment designs.
- Findings may not generalize to larger, less cohesive groups or to settings with different institutional structures.
