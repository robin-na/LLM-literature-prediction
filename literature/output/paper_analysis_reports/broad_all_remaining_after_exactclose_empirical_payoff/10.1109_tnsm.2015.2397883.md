## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
VPEF, an incentive mechanism based on voluntary participation, round-based entry fees, and voluntary costly punishment, leads to high efficiency in community-based collaborative networks. The system spends most of its time in the punisher state, where all participants contribute and punish defectors, minimizing efficiency loss. The efficiency loss is analytically shown to be 4/(8+M), which becomes negligible for large communities. Simulations confirm the theoretical predictions. The mechanism is robust to changes in group size, entry fee (as long as it is not too high), and multiplexing/diversity factor. The model assumes strong selection (users imitate higher-payoff strategies) and does not require tracking long-term behavior or permanent exclusion.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling voluntary costly punishment in a public-goods-like environment with voluntary participation and a small entry fee can dramatically increase efficiency, driving the system to near-full cooperation (punisher state) and minimizing efficiency loss. The efficiency loss formula (4/(8+M)) can be used to predict the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, given the number of users. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values, provided the multiplexing/diversity factor is above 1 and defectors can be reliably detected. The model is most applicable to dynamic, anonymous, and voluntary participation settings, and less so to settings with continuous contribution levels or where punishment/reward is not voluntary.

## Important Limitations
- The model assumes binary contribution choices (contribute or not); does not allow for continuous contribution levels.
- Assumes strong selection (users always imitate higher-payoff strategies), which may not hold in all real-world settings.
- Assumes high probability of detecting defectors; if detection is imperfect, efficiency drops.
- Does not model heterogeneity in user characteristics or preferences.
- Simulations and theory are based on idealized assumptions (e.g., type-neutral entry/exit, perfect mixing).
- No empirical (lab or field) data; results are theoretical and simulation-based.
