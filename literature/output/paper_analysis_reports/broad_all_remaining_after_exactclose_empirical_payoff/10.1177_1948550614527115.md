# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: For Members Only: Ingroup Punishment of Fairness Norm Violations in the Ultimatum Game
Authors: Mendoza, S. A., Lane, S. P., & Amodio, D. M.
Journal: Social Psychological and Personality Science
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance/rejection rates of offers (costly punishment)
- fairness perceptions
- emotional reactions (anger, happiness, irritation)
- proposer evaluations (likability, competence, team inclusion)
- expectations of offer size from in-group/out-group
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate of rejection of offers (interpreted as costly punishment), not group or total payoff. No direct analysis of efficiency, welfare, or group earnings is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that in the ultimatum game, participants are more likely to reject marginally unfair offers from in-group members than from out-group members, even though rejection is costly to both parties. This pattern is interpreted as stricter enforcement of fairness norms within groups. The effect is most pronounced for offers that are only slightly unfair (e.g., $8/$20), and is stronger among participants with higher in-group identification. The study does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, and the outcome is measured in terms of behavioral responses (accept/reject) rather than efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence about the psychological and social moderators of costly punishment in a bargaining context (ultimatum game), specifically the role of group membership and social identity. However, it does not provide data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate or report on most game design dimensions relevant to public goods games. The findings may inform predictions about when punishment is more likely to be used (e.g., in-group contexts, marginal norm violations), but do not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency or welfare in public-goods-game-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum game is played between two players: one proposer and one responder.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played multiple one-shot interactions (10 critical offers in Study 1, similar in Study 2), but each interaction was with a new partner and was one-shot.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection of the offer, which costs both players their potential earnings for that round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: rejection reduces both players' payoffs to zero for that round.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told they would interact with each player only once; the number of trials was known.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, which is structurally different from public goods games; findings may not generalize to multi-player or repeated public goods contexts.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral (accept/reject) responses are analyzed.
- Punishment is implemented as rejection of offers, which is a binary, all-or-nothing mechanism, not a graduated or targeted punishment as in many public goods games.
- All interactions are one-shot and anonymous, limiting ecological validity for repeated or reputation-based environments.
- No manipulation or analysis of most game design dimensions relevant to public goods games (e.g., MPCR, reward, communication, etc.).
