## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average energy of all agents (proxy for group payoff/efficiency)
- disparity (variation in energy among agents)
- coefficient of variation (disparity/average energy)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- number of sharing actions
- number of punishment actions
- number of antisocial punishments
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average energy of all agents, which is directly analogous to group payoff or efficiency. Disparity and coefficient of variation are also reported as secondary outcomes. Behavioral outcomes (sharing, punishment) are reported but not primary.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that punishment can facilitate higher group efficiency (average energy) in environments where resources are scarce (low MPCR), but only when the punishment mechanism is designed to avoid antisocial punishment and minimize costs (legitimate, zero loss, hired gun). Unrestricted punishment consistently reduces efficiency. When resources are abundant (high MPCR), punishment mechanisms do not improve and may reduce efficiency compared to no-punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size and punishment cost ratio. The study provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by resource availability (analogous to MPCR in PGGs) and by the design of the punishment mechanism.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong simulation-based evidence that the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments depends critically on both the marginal per-capita return (MPCR, here operationalized as resource availability) and the specific punishment mechanism. When MPCR is low (resources scarce), enabling well-designed punishment (legitimate, zero loss, hired gun) increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but unrestricted punishment reduces efficiency. When MPCR is high (resources abundant), punishment does not improve and may reduce efficiency. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the effect of punishment on efficiency should be modeled as conditional on both MPCR and the punishment mechanism. The results are robust to moderate changes in group size and punishment cost ratio, so these dimensions are less likely to moderate the direction of the effect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main simulations use 50 agents; sensitivity analysis in Appendix C tests 35, 50, and 65 agents. Changing player count does not alter the ranking of punishment mechanisms or the qualitative effect on efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for 30,000 iterations (rounds); some extended to 100,000 for stability checks. All results are based on the last 10,000 iterations.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Resource availability (50, 100, 200) is directly analogous to MPCR. When MPCR is low, punishment (except unrestricted) increases efficiency; when MPCR is high, punishment reduces efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main simulations use cost of 1 per punishment; sensitivity analysis in Appendix C tests cost/punishment ratios of 0.2 and 0.5. Changing cost does not alter the ranking of mechanisms.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment removes 5 energy from target per 1 energy cost to punisher (main), but mechanism varies: in ZLP, cost and loss are redistributed; in HGM, external agent punishes. Sensitivity analysis on cost/punishment ratio.

## Important Limitations
- The study is a simulation, not an empirical experiment with human subjects; results may not generalize to real-world or laboratory PGGs.
- Only resource availability (MPCR analog), punishment mechanism, group size, and punishment cost ratio are systematically varied; other potentially important dimensions (e.g., communication, heterogeneity, dynamic group size) are not explored.
- Agents are simple, lack cognitive sophistication, and learn only through evolutionary selection on fixed parameters.
- No reward mechanisms are tested, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The model assumes local neighborhoods and indirect reciprocity, which may not map directly to all PGG settings.
