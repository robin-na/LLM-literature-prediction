# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The Costs of Collective Action: An Experimental Study of Risk, Punishment, and Immunity
Authors: Cheung, G., & Siegel, D. A.
Journal: Journal of Politics
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per subject
- group success rate (probability group achieves collective action and receives benefit)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual participation (act/not act)
- group outcome (success/failure)
- participation rates by immune/nonimmune status
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are individual participation (binary) and group success (binary: whether the group achieves the collective action and receives the benefit). Average payoffs are reported descriptively, but the main analyses focus on participation and group success, not on efficiency or total group payoff relative to a full cooperation benchmark. Efficiency as defined in public goods games is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that higher punishment risk and cost reduce participation and group success among nonimmune individuals. Immune individuals are more likely to participate, but their participation is not affected by punishment risk or cost. As the number of immune individuals increases, nonimmune individuals participate less, consistent with free riding. Group size has no significant effect on nonimmune participation or group success, but immune individuals participate more in larger groups. The main outcomes are participation and group success, not efficiency or total group payoff. Average payoffs are reported, but not analyzed as a function of the experimental treatments. The findings support the theoretical model's predictions about the effects of punishment risk, cost, immunity, and group size on participation and collective action success.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about how external punishment risk and cost reduce participation and group success in a collective action setting, and how immunity and group size moderate these effects. However, the game is not a standard public goods game: the group benefit is all-or-nothing and depends on a random pivotal actor, and only participants can be punished by an external agent. There is no within-group punishment or contribution to a divisible public good. Efficiency as defined in public goods games (total group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed, though average payoffs and group success rates are reported. The results are most relevant for predicting how external punishment risk and cost affect participation and group-level success in threshold-like or all-or-nothing collective action settings, especially when some individuals are immune to punishment. The findings are less directly transferable to standard PGG efficiency predictions, but may inform models where external sanctions or risk of punishment for cooperation are present.

No significant effect on nonimmune participation or group success; immune individuals participate more as group size increases.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays 15 rounds: 5 training (no punishment), 5 punishment (no immunity), 5 immunity phase. Number of rounds is fixed and known.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: act or not act. The group benefit is all-or-nothing, determined by a random pivotal actor.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost (C) is manipulated in the pilot ($0.75, $1, $1.50, $2), fixed at $1.50 in the main study. Higher punishment cost reduces participation and group success among nonimmune individuals.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the probability of being selected for punishment (P/N). Higher probability reduces participation and group success among nonimmune individuals.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in advance (15 rounds, divided into 3 phases).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are told whether their group received the reward, whether they were punished, their own immunity status, their payoff for the round, and their cumulative payoff. No information about other individuals' choices.

## Important Limitations
- The game is not a standard public goods game: the group benefit is all-or-nothing and depends on a random pivotal actor, not on the sum of contributions.
- Punishment is external and only applies to those who participate, not to free riders; there is no within-group punishment.
- Efficiency as defined in public goods games (total group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly analyzed.
- The main outcomes are participation and group success, not efficiency or total group payoff.
- Findings may not generalize to standard PGGs with divisible public goods, continuous contributions, or endogenous within-group punishment.
