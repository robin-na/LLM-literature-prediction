Outcome notes:
The paper synthesizes and discusses findings from multiple empirical studies (lab and field) on cooperation, punishment, and leadership, with a focus on efficiency and group outcomes. It does not present new experimental data but summarizes and interprets results from prior work, including Kosfeld & Rustagi (2015), Falk & Kosfeld (2006), and others.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Drawing on a range of lab and field experiments, the paper finds that: (1) Groups with a higher share of conditional cooperators achieve better efficiency and group outcomes; (2) Punishment of non-cooperation (especially when administered by leaders with prosocial motives) increases group efficiency and cooperation, as shown in field data from Ethiopian forest user groups and lab public goods games; (3) Antisocial punishment by leaders is associated with significantly worse group outcomes; (4) Trust by leaders (as opposed to control) can increase efficiency in principal-agent settings; (5) Self-selection mechanisms can help attract motivated types, further improving group efficiency. The paper provides strong evidence that punishment, when properly targeted and motivated, is a key mechanism for sustaining high efficiency in public-goods-like environments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games will increase group efficiency, especially when punishment is prosocially motivated and not antisocial. The evidence is drawn from both lab and field settings, including real-world resource management and controlled experiments. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the motives of the punisher (leader or group member), the presence of motivated types, and the structure of the group. For prediction tasks, the paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, particularly when the punishment technology is effective and antisocial punishment is minimized. The paper also highlights the importance of leader behavior and group composition, which may interact with the game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Player count is specified in the discussed experiments (e.g., two-player public goods games, group sizes in field studies), but the paper does not systematically vary or analyze player count as a moderator of punishment effects.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Some experiments discussed are one-shot, others are repeated (e.g., 20 rounds in Bauer et al. 2017). The paper does not systematically analyze the effect of number of rounds on punishment or efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Contribution choices in the discussed public goods games are continuous (e.g., 0-6 Birr), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is specified in the public goods games (e.g., 0.75 in the Ethiopian field experiment). The effect of MPCR on punishment or efficiency is not systematically analyzed.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is specified (e.g., 1 Birr per deduction point in leader punishment game). The paper discusses how costly punishment, when prosocially motivated, increases efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is specified (e.g., each deduction point costs 1 Birr and reduces target's payoff by 3 Birr). Effective punishment by leaders is associated with higher group efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Rewards are discussed as an alternative to punishment, but not implemented in the main experiments.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In repeated games (e.g., Bauer et al. 2017), the number of rounds is known to participants. Not systematically analyzed as a moderator.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants receive feedback about others' contributions or average investments in some experiments (e.g., Bauer et al. 2017). Not systematically analyzed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the leader punishment game, group members do not learn the actual punishment decisions of their leader, so punishment is anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical and integrative review, not a primary empirical study; it synthesizes findings from multiple prior experiments rather than reporting new data.
- Game design dimensions are not systematically manipulated or analyzed as moderators of punishment effects; most evidence is indirect or contextual.
- Some outcomes (e.g., forest management) are field-based and may not map directly onto laboratory efficiency measures.
- The effect of antisocial punishment is highlighted, but the determinants of antisocial punishment are not fully understood.
- Reward mechanisms are discussed but not empirically tested in the main studies reviewed.
