# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Conditional Cooperation and Costly Monitoring Explain Success in Forest Commons Management
Authors: Rustagi, D., Engel, S., & Kosfeld, M.
Journal: Science
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- forest management outcome (potential crop trees per hectare)
- group-level time spent on monitoring (hours/month)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- share of conditional cooperators
- share of free riders
- individual and group-level monitoring behavior
- contribution rates in public goods game
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is a real-world group-level outcome (forest management success, measured as potential crop trees per hectare), which is interpreted as a proxy for group efficiency in commons management. The study also reports on monitoring (costly enforcement) and behavioral types, but does not directly measure monetary efficiency in a repeated public goods game with and without punishment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that (1) groups with a higher share of conditional cooperators have significantly better forest management outcomes, (2) costly monitoring (enforcement) is more prevalent in groups with more conditional cooperators, and (3) both conditional cooperation and costly monitoring are important for successful commons management. The main outcome is a real-world efficiency proxy (forest management success), not laboratory earnings. The public goods game is used to measure behavioral types, not as the main outcome. The study does not experimentally manipulate punishment or monitoring, but observes natural variation in monitoring (enforcement) and links it to outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect but informative evidence that costly monitoring (enforcement) is associated with higher group-level efficiency in a real-world commons context, and that the presence of conditional cooperators amplifies this effect. While the study does not manipulate punishment in a controlled PGG, it supports the idea that enabling or increasing costly enforcement (monitoring and punishment) can improve group efficiency, especially when conditional cooperators are present. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and intensity of enforcement mechanisms (even if costly) are likely to increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, particularly in groups with more conditional cooperators. However, the evidence is observational and context-specific (forest management), not from a lab PGG with toggled punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Group size is included as a control variable in regressions, but the effect of group size on efficiency or the effect of punishment as a function of group size is not directly analyzed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution in the public goods game is continuous (0-6 Birr), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.75 (contribution is multiplied by 1.5 and split between two players).
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Monitoring (enforcement) is costly in time/effort; groups with more costly monitoring have better outcomes. The cost is not experimentally manipulated, but is observed as time spent on patrols.
- punishment technology: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment is implemented via group-level sanctions after detection by monitoring; the effectiveness per unit cost is not quantified.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. In the real-world context, punishment is determined by an executive committee at the group level; the identity of punishers is not discussed in detail.

## Important Limitations
- The study is observational and does not experimentally manipulate punishment or monitoring; causal inference is based on correlational patterns and controls.
- The main outcome is a real-world proxy for efficiency (forest management success), not monetary efficiency in a controlled public goods game.
- Punishment is implemented as costly monitoring and group-level sanctions, not as direct peer-to-peer punishment in a lab PGG.
- The public goods game is one-shot and used to measure behavioral types, not to assess the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- Findings may not generalize to laboratory PGGs with toggled punishment or to other contexts with different enforcement mechanisms.
