# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Can monetary incentives overturn fairness-based decisions?
Authors: Weiss, M., Saulin, A., Iotzov, V., Hewig, J., & Hein, G.
Journal: Royal Society Open Science
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of fairness-congruent decisions (punishment or compensation)
- drift-diffusion model parameters (drift rate v, initial bias z)
- effect of monetary incentives on decision process efficiency
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral choices (fairness-congruent vs. incongruent decisions) and cognitive process parameters (drift rate, bias) from drift-diffusion modeling. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported as primary outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that offering monetary incentives that conflict with fairness-based punishment or compensation reduces the frequency of fairness-congruent decisions and slows the decision process (lower drift rate in DDM). This effect is similar for both punishment and compensation domains. The effect of conflicting incentives is stronger at higher incentive levels. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, but rather focuses on behavioral and cognitive process outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that monetary incentives can undermine fairness-based punishment and compensation behavior, reducing the likelihood and efficiency of such decisions. However, because the study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, using a modified dictator game with punishment/compensation framing, but lacks repeated interaction, group structure, or explicit efficiency measures. The main predictive value is in showing that the cost and alignment of incentives can strongly moderate the behavioral impact of punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each decision is a two-player allocation (participant and target: either allocator, receiver, or baseline partner). No group interaction; not a multi-person public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant made 120 decisions per partner (allocator/receiver and baseline), for a total of 240 trials. No repeated interaction with the same partner.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants; all decisions are anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each trial is a binary choice between two allocations (e.g., unfair vs. equal, or equal vs. hyperfair). Not a continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a choice to allocate fewer points to the norm violator (allocator), which increases the participant's own payoff. No explicit cost to the punisher; in fact, punishment is aligned with self-interest in some conditions.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a direct reduction in the target's payoff via allocation choice. The impact per unit is 1:1 (points not given to the target are kept by the participant).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of trials (240), as the task is fully described and practice trials are included.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants observe the allocation decisions of others in the observation phase, but do not see aggregate summaries of others' behavior during their own decision phase.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main results are based on behavioral choices and cognitive process parameters.
- The experimental design is a modified dictator game, not a public goods game or repeated group interaction; thus, findings may not generalize to PGG-like environments.
- Punishment is not costly in the standard sense; in some conditions, punishing the norm violator increases the participant's own payoff.
- No explicit group structure, repeated interaction, or feedback about others' behavior is present.
- No reward mechanism for positive reinforcement is included.
- The study is conducted online and anonymously, which may limit ecological validity for real-world or face-to-face interactions.
