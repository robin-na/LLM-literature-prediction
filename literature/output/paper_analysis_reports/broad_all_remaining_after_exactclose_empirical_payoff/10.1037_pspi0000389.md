# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cross-Cultural Variation in Cooperation: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Spadaro, G., Graf, C., Jin, S. X., Arai, S., Inoue, Y., Lieberman, E., Rinderu, M. I., Yuan, M. L., Van Lissa, C. J., & Balliet, D.
Journal: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate
- contribution rate
- proportion of endowment contributed
- frequency of cooperation
Outcome notes:
The primary outcome is the rate of cooperation (i.e., contribution or cooperation rate) in Prisoner's Dilemma and Public Goods Game paradigms. The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as primary outcomes. All analyses are based on behavioral measures of cooperation, not on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The meta-analysis finds that the presence of sanctions (punishment and/or reward) in social dilemma experiments is associated with significantly higher cooperation rates. Other situational features, such as communication and lower conflict of interest, also increase cooperation. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff. No significant cross-societal or cross-cultural differences in cooperation are found after controlling for study characteristics. The effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff is not directly analyzed or reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment or reward mechanisms increases cooperation rates in PGG and PD experiments, across a wide range of societies and experimental designs. However, because the outcome is cooperation rate rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The paper does not address whether increased cooperation from punishment leads to higher or lower efficiency, as it does not analyze payoff-based outcomes. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence about efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Group size (player count) is coded and included as a covariate in meta-regression. Range is 2 to 324, median 3.5. Group size was not a significant predictor of cooperation in the main models.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Repetitions (one-shot vs. repeated) are coded and included as a covariate. Not a significant predictor in the main global model, but significant in the US sample.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat) is coded and analyzed. Presence of communication is associated with higher cooperation rates.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of choice options (dichotomous/all-or-nothing vs. continuous) is coded. Continuous choice is associated with higher cooperation rates.
- mpcr: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Degree of conflict of interest (K index) is coded and analyzed. Higher K (less conflict, higher MPCR) is associated with higher cooperation rates.
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Sanctions variable includes both punishment and reward mechanisms. Presence of either is associated with higher cooperation rates.

## Important Limitations
- The primary outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not analyzed.
- Sanctions variable includes both punishment and reward; effects of punishment and reward are not separated.
- No coding or analysis of punishment cost, effectiveness, or identification of punishers.
- Most studies use student samples and are not nationally representative.
- Imbalances in the number of studies per society; most studies are from WEIRD societies.
- Potential publication bias, though effect is small.
- Heterogeneity in experimental paradigms across studies, though controlled for in analysis.
