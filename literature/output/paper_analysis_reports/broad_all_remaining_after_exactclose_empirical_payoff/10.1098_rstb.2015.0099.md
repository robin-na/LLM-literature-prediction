## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that contingent cooperation in large groups can invade and persist if relatedness is above a threshold that depends sensitively on the threshold fraction of cooperators required for cooperation to be sustained. The required relatedness increases rapidly as the threshold for cooperation increases. The number of repeated interactions (iterations) required for cooperation to invade also increases rapidly with the threshold and decreases with relatedness. The model shows that, under realistic levels of relatedness, only strategies that require cooperation from a small fraction of the group can invade; strategies that require near-universal cooperation are unlikely to evolve. The paper argues that contingent punishment (not formally modeled here) is a more plausible mechanism for the evolution of widespread cooperation, because even a small fraction of punishers can induce high levels of cooperation. The results are supported by mathematical derivations and numerical simulations of evolutionary dynamics in group-structured populations.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how group size, relatedness, and the threshold for contingent cooperation affect the evolutionary success (and thus efficiency) of cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It does not directly model or analyze punishment, but its results suggest that punishment-based mechanisms are more likely to support high efficiency in large groups than pure contingent cooperation. For prediction tasks, the paper's formulas can be used to estimate the conditions under which cooperation (and thus high efficiency) can be sustained, given the group structure and behavioral rules. However, because the paper does not model explicit punishment or compare efficiency with and without punishment, it provides only indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency.

Group size (n) is a key parameter in all models and simulations, varied from 20 to 320 in Table 1. Larger groups make cooperation harder to invade unless relatedness is high or threshold is low.

Number of rounds (T) is explicitly modeled as the number of repeated interactions. Higher T (more rounds) makes it easier for contingent cooperation to invade, all else equal.

The model allows for both continuous and threshold (all-or-nothing) strategies, but most results are for threshold-based contingent cooperation (cooperate if enough others cooperate).

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Punishment is not explicitly modeled; the main results are about contingent cooperation, not punishment.
- Efficiency is analyzed in terms of evolutionary fitness and invasion conditions, not as direct group earnings in a public goods game.
- The models assume large group sizes, weak selection, and specific forms of population structure (island model, budding viscosity), which may not generalize to all real-world settings.
- The results are most relevant for predicting the conditions under which cooperation can evolve, not for quantifying the effect of enabling punishment in a specific experimental design.
