# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Nonmonetary sanctions and rewards in an experimental coordination game
Authors: Dugar, S.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2010

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- average minimum action (proxy for group efficiency)
- average individual action
- final period minimum action
- regression on group minima
Non-payoff outcomes:
- use of sanctions/rewards (number of points assigned)
- patterns of sanctioning/rewarding behavior
- change in individual action in response to sanctions/rewards
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group efficiency (proxied by minimum action and group earnings) and individual action levels. The paper also analyzes behavioral responses to nonmonetary sanctions and rewards.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that enabling players to assign nonmonetary disapproval (sanction) points to others in a repeated minimum effort game leads to convergence on the efficient (payoff-dominant) equilibrium, with group earnings nearly doubling compared to the baseline. In contrast, enabling approval (reward) points does not improve efficiency and results in outcomes similar to or worse than the baseline. The effect of disapproval is not due to communication per se, but to the psychological impact of negative feedback. Sanctions are used more persistently and effectively than rewards, and their behavioral impact on raising effort is stronger. The findings are robust to initial group behavior and are supported by both aggregate and regression analyses.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling nonmonetary, costless, and payoff-neutral sanctions (disapproval) in a repeated coordination game can dramatically increase group efficiency, even when the sanctions have no direct monetary effect. The effect is not observed for nonmonetary rewards (approval). For prediction tasks, the presence of a nonmonetary sanction mechanism (even if costless and payoff-neutral) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such mechanism, while a nonmonetary reward mechanism may not. The game is a minimum effort game (not a standard PGG), so transfer to PGGs should be cautious but is likely informative for adjacent coordination settings.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session had 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No free-form chat; in the sanction/reward treatments, players could assign approval/disapproval points (structured, ex post, not open chat).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (action) is discrete, integer-valued from 1 to 7 (not continuous, not all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (disapproval) is costless to assign and has no direct payoff effect.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is nonmonetary, costless, and payoff-neutral; its 'effectiveness' is purely psychological/social.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward (approval) treatment exists as a separate condition; does not improve efficiency.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (approval) is costless to assign and has no direct payoff effect.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is nonmonetary, costless, and payoff-neutral; its 'effectiveness' is purely psychological/social.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the game had 10 periods (finite, known horizon).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, the entire distribution of actions and individual payoffs were revealed to subjects.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward points were assigned anonymously; only aggregate scores received were shown, not the identity of assigners.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a minimum effort coordination game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs.
- Sanctions and rewards are nonmonetary, costless, and payoff-neutral; effects may differ with costly or monetary sanctions/rewards.
- The psychological impact of nonmonetary sanctions may depend on subject pool or context.
- No free-form communication was allowed; only structured approval/disapproval points.
- The experiment was conducted with university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
