## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma with institutional (third-party) incentives, pure punishment is always sustainable and, when sufficiently strong, increases both the market size and the accumulated wealth of participants and the third party. Light punishment can lead to market shrinkage or collapse. Mixed incentives (reward plus punishment) can maximize the sum of accumulated wealth for both parties at moderate strength, but their sustainability depends on both incentive strength and agent rationality. Pure reward increases participant wealth but is unsustainable for the third party in this model. The results are robust across different levels of agent rationality, though higher rationality makes it easier to achieve high cooperation and affluence.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated pairwise social dilemmas with institutional punishment, increasing the strength of punishment (i.e., higher fines for defection) leads to higher efficiency (measured as accumulated wealth and market size), provided the punishment is not too light. Light punishment can reduce efficiency by shrinking the market. Mixed incentives can further optimize total efficiency at moderate strength. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar institutional settings, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to defection. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, so results should be transferred with some caution.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations use N=100 initial participants; market size can shrink due to elimination. The model is pairwise, not group-based.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Simulations run for T=30 time steps (rounds) after statistical tests for stability.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: cooperate (C) or defect (D) in each pairwise interaction.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to the third party is proportional to fine imposed, with unit cost alpha=0.3. Heavier punishment (higher fines) increases cost but also increases efficiency and sustainability up to a point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is varied by changing fine levels (F_CD, F_DD). Stronger punishment increases efficiency and market size, while light punishment can shrink the market.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is present in some treatments (pure reward, mixed incentives).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward cost is the amount paid by the third party to cooperators. Pure reward is unsustainable because the third party's wealth decreases over time.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is varied by changing reward levels (R_CC, R_CD). Higher rewards increase participant wealth but make the system unsustainable for the third party.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (T) is fixed and known in the simulation.

## Important Limitations
- The model is a repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma with institutional (third-party) incentives, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGG settings.
- All results are theoretical or simulation-based, not empirical or experimental.
- The elimination mechanism (bankruptcy and exit) may not be present in all real-world or experimental settings.
- No communication, group-level interaction, or peer-to-peer punishment is modeled.
- Parameter choices (e.g., cost coefficient alpha, initial wealth, commission fee) may affect quantitative results.
- The model assumes the third party is not composed of players, which may differ from some institutional designs.
