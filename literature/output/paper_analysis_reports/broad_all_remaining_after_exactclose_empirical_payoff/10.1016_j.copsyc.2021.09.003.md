## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The review finds that both reward and punishment can promote prosocial behavior in public goods games and related social dilemmas, but their effectiveness depends on context. Punishment can sometimes reduce group welfare, especially when it leads to retaliation or is applied under noisy conditions, where it may mistakenly target cooperators and harm group payoffs. Reward is often found to be at least as effective as punishment in promoting cooperation and may be more efficient, particularly under noise. The legitimacy and source of punishment (e.g., third-party or democratic punishment) can moderate its effectiveness. Theoretical models suggest that reward is important for establishing prosociality, while punishment helps maintain it once established, but this dynamic has not been directly tested in experiments. The review also notes that indirect strategies like gossip may be more efficient than punishment in some real-world settings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides useful qualitative guidance for predicting the effects of punishment and reward on prosocial behavior and, to a lesser extent, on efficiency in public goods games. It suggests that punishment does not always increase efficiency and can sometimes reduce it, especially under noise or when perceived as illegitimate. Reward may be more efficient in some contexts, particularly when monitoring is imperfect. The review also highlights the importance of the structure and legitimacy of punishment mechanisms. However, because the review does not report quantitative efficiency outcomes or systematically analyze game design dimensions, its value for direct prediction of treatment efficiency is limited to qualitative boundary conditions and theoretical expectations.

## Important Limitations
- This is a narrative review and does not report new empirical data or quantitative effect sizes.
- The review focuses on prosocial behavior and cooperation, not directly on efficiency or group payoff as primary outcomes.
- Game design dimensions are discussed only in general or illustrative terms, not systematically analyzed as moderators.
- Findings are often based on secondary synthesis of prior work, and the review highlights the need for more field research and integration of methodologies.
- Theoretical predictions about the optimal sequencing of reward and punishment have not been empirically tested.
