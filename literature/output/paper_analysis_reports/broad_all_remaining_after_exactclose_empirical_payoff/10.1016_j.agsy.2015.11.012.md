# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Pesticide use and cooperative management of natural enemy habitat in a framed field experiment
Authors: Bell, A., Zhang, W., & Nou, K.
Journal: Agricultural Systems
Year: 2016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- conditional cooperation slope
- probability of being a conditional cooperator
- punishment assigned
- classification of player types (conditional cooperator, free rider)
- punishment sensitivity to others' contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: the slope of conditional cooperation, the probability of being a conditional cooperator, and punishment behavior. The paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes. Payoff calculations are possible from the design, but efficiency is not directly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment uses a one-shot public goods game with three treatments: (1) Base (no punishment), (2) UCPun (conditional decision maker can be punished by an unconditional decision maker), and (3) CPun (conditional decision maker can punish an unconditional decision maker). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), but does lead to more sensitive punishment behavior. Responsibility effects (increased cooperation when given punishment power) are stronger than substitution effects. The overall effect of punishment on cooperation is positive, but the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment mechanisms affect conditional cooperation in a one-shot public goods game with four players, MPCR 0.4, and costless punishment. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings suggest that enabling punishment increases the responsiveness of cooperators to others' contributions and may increase overall cooperation, but also increases free riding. For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is likely to increase cooperation-related behaviors, but the net effect on efficiency is ambiguous due to the rise in free riders. The absence of direct efficiency analysis limits the ability to quantitatively predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency, but the behavioral mechanisms identified are informative for modeling.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token in the public account returns 0.4 to each player).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costless to the punisher (cost per unit = 0).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's payoff by 1 point; maximum punishment is 10 points.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot (single round).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After the decision, participants are told the overall contribution of their group and their own payoff.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; all main results are on behavioral variables (conditional cooperation, punishment behavior).
- Punishment is costless, which differs from most real-world and many experimental settings where punishment is costly.
- The experiment is one-shot (single round), so findings may not generalize to repeated public goods games.
- The sample is limited to non-economics students from a single German university, which may affect generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not address interactions between punishment and reward.
- The effect of punishment on actual group efficiency or surplus is not directly measured or discussed.
