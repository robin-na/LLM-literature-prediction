## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This review synthesizes theory and empirical evidence on the evolution of altruism, with a focus on public goods games and the role of punishment. It highlights that punishment mechanisms increase contributions to the public good, but typically reduce overall efficiency or group payoffs because the costs of punishing and being punished are not fully offset by increased cooperation. Exceptions exist, such as when punishment is cheap or the game is played for many rounds, but these are not the norm. The review also notes the existence of 'perverse' punishment (punishing high contributors) and the importance of contextual factors (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural variation).

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this review suggests that, in most standard lab PGGs, enabling punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to the direct costs of punishment. However, if punishment is relatively cheap or the game is played for many rounds, efficiency losses may be mitigated or reversed. The review provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the importance of punishment cost, number of rounds, and the structure of punishment opportunities as moderators of the efficiency effect. It also cautions that the presence of counter-punishment or 'perverse' punishment can further reduce efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- This is a review article, not a primary empirical study; it synthesizes findings from many sources but does not provide new experimental data.
- The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes or meta-analytic estimates for the efficiency impact of punishment.
- Many dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, summary information) are mentioned only in passing and not analyzed as moderators.
- Some claims about efficiency are based on referenced studies, not direct analysis in this paper.
- The review notes substantial heterogeneity in findings across studies and contexts, limiting the precision of predictions.
