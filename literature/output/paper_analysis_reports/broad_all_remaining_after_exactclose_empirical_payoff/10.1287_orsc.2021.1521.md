# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Cooperation with Strangers: Spillover of Community Norms
Authors: Molina, M., Nee, V., & Holm, H.
Journal: Organization Science
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate in one-shot prisoner's dilemma
- probability of cooperation with strangers
- association between belief in norm enforcement and cooperation
- effect of norm enforcement primes on cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is whether the participant cooperates in a one-shot PD with a stranger. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported or analyzed. All outcomes are behavioral (cooperation/defection), not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that among Chinese CEOs, stronger belief that norm violations are met with retaliatory sanctions in their business community is associated with a higher probability of cooperating with a stranger in a one-shot PD. This association is especially strong in some cities (Nanjing, Wenzhou). An online experiment with managers and nonmanagers shows that priming reciprocity norms increases cooperation with strangers in some subgroups and cities. However, the outcome is always the individual choice to cooperate, not group payoff or efficiency. There is no direct evidence on how punishment or norm enforcement affects efficiency or total group earnings.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. Its main contribution is showing that belief in norm enforcement (especially retaliatory sanctions) is associated with higher cooperation rates in a one-shot PD. While this suggests that punishment norms can promote cooperation, the lack of payoff or efficiency outcomes, and the use of a PD rather than a PGG, limits its direct applicability to the efficiency prediction task. The findings may be useful for understanding the role of punishment norms in promoting cooperation, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main game is a two-player one-shot prisoner's dilemma (PD).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD is played as a one-shot game (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; the PD is anonymous and one-shot.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PD is a binary choice: cooperate or defect (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The payoff structure is given: mutual cooperation yields 250 each, mutual defection 100 each, unilateral cooperation 50/400. This is a PD, not a PGG, so MPCR is not directly applicable, but the payoff structure is fully specified.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is a one-shot PD; the number of rounds is clear.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is cooperation rate in a one-shot PD, not efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings.
- No punishment or reward mechanism is implemented in the experimental game; only beliefs about norm enforcement are measured or primed.
- The game is a two-player one-shot PD, not a public goods game or a repeated game.
- No direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- Findings may not generalize to PGG-like environments or to settings with actual punishment/reward options.
- Statistical significance of some findings is weak or marginal, and confidence intervals often overlap.
- City-level heterogeneity suggests context-specific effects that may not generalize.
