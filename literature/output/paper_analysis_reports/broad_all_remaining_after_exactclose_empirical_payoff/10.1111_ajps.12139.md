# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Institutional Sources of Legitimate Authority: An Experimental Investigation
Authors: Dickson, E. S., Gordon, S. C., & Huber, G. A.
Journal: American Journal of Political Science
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings per period for citizens and authorities
- group-level contribution rates
Non-payoff outcomes:
- citizen intervention to help or hinder authority (behavioral legitimacy measure)
- authority targeting behavior (resoluteness, predation)
- deterrence index (punishment of noncontributors minus punishment of contributors)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is a behavioral measure of legitimacy: citizens' costly decisions to help or hinder the authority. Contribution rates and average earnings are reported, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) is not directly analyzed. Payoff outcomes are discussed but not the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that citizens are more willing to help salaried (fixed-compensation) authorities than those who personally benefit from collected penalties, but only when transparency is high. When transparency is low, support for salaried authorities collapses, while support for appropriator authorities is low regardless of transparency. Contribution rates and average earnings are reported, but do not differ significantly between treatments under full information; under limited information, contribution rates are lower for salaried authorities. The main outcome is a behavioral measure of legitimacy (citizen intervention), not efficiency or group payoff. The deterrence index (successful punishment of noncontributors minus punishment of contributors) is associated with higher contributions, but the causal effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly estimated.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence about how institutional features (transparency, authority incentives) affect citizen support for enforcement and, through deterrence, contribution rates. However, it does not directly estimate the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency or payoff. The design is a binary (all-or-nothing) public goods game with centralized, probabilistic punishment and a unique intervention stage. The findings suggest that the legitimacy of punishment (as measured by citizen support) is sensitive to transparency and authority incentives, which may in turn affect deterrence and contributions, but the direct impact on efficiency is not quantified. For prediction tasks, this paper is informative about mechanisms and moderators but does not provide direct treatment effect estimates for efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 5 players: 1 authority and 4 citizens.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players; all interaction is via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: contribute all 20 tokens or keep all.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 0.4 (each citizen and the authority receive 0.4 times the total contributed).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Authority pays 2 tokens to attempt enforcement; citizens pay 1 token to help or hinder.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. If enforcement is successful, the target pays a penalty of 30 tokens. Probability of success is 0.5 plus (number of helpers - number of hinderers)/6.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In full information treatments, citizens see all others' contribution decisions and who was targeted; in limited information, only whether targeting occurred and group total contributions at end.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In full information, citizens see which group member was targeted (by ID number); in limited information, only that someone was targeted.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is a behavioral measure of legitimacy (citizen intervention), not efficiency or group payoff.
- The effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency or welfare is not directly estimated.
- The punishment mechanism is centralized and probabilistic, with only one citizen targeted per round and other citizens able to help or hinder, which differs from standard decentralized PGG punishment.
- Contribution is binary (all-or-nothing), not continuous.
- The authority is external to the group of citizens, which may limit generalizability to standard PGG settings.
- No direct analysis of how punishment affects efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline.
