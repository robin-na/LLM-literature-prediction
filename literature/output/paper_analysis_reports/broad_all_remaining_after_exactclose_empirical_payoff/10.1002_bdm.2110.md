# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The effect of decision timing on the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish noncooperation: Sanctioning the past, the present, or the future
Authors: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral Decision Making
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- willingness to punish
- willingness to reward
- frequency of sanctioning
- size of sanction assigned
- attitude toward noncooperation
- ease of justifying punishment
- emotional reactions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the frequency and size of costly punishment and reward assigned by third-party observers in a public goods game, as well as attitudinal and justification measures in a scenario study. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The main finding is that people are less willing to assign costly rewards or punishments when they must decide before observing others' (non)cooperation, compared to after. This effect is robust across both dichotomous and continuous public goods game variants, and for both reward and punishment, though the effect is stronger for punishment. The effect is not explained by differences in emotional reactions. The results are interpreted as evidence for nonconsequential reasoning: uncertainty about future behavior reduces decisiveness and willingness to sanction. The study does not measure or report group efficiency or total payoffs, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be assessed.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to use costly punishment (and reward) in public goods games is lower when sanctions must be decided before observing others' behavior, compared to after. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding when punishment is likely to be used, not its payoff consequences.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public goods game involved 4 players (with a 5th as third-party observer).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The public goods game was one-shot (single round) in both experiments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: dichotomous (all-or-nothing) contribution; Experiment 2: continuous contribution.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each €10 contribution is multiplied by 1.5 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 1.5/4 = 0.375.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays €0.10 per point; each point reduces target's payoff by €0.30.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each €0.10 spent reduces target's payoff by €0.30 (3:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism was available in reward conditions.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarder pays €0.10 per point; each point increases target's payoff by €0.30.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: each €0.10 spent increases target's payoff by €0.30 (3:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the game was a one-shot (single round) public goods game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Third-party observer was informed of each group member's choice; no evidence of summary statistics shown to players themselves.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told a fifth person (observer) could assign rewards or punishments, but identities were anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes; only behavioral willingness to punish/reward is measured.
- All experiments use a third-party punishment/reward paradigm, not second-party (directly affected) punishment.
- The public goods game is one-shot (single round), so findings may not generalize to repeated games.
- The effect of actual punishment/reward on group cooperation or efficiency is not measured.
- Participants are not actual group members but impartial observers, which may limit generalizability to real-world group settings.
