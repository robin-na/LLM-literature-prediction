# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Shifting Punishment onto Minorities: Experimental Evidence of Scapegoating
Authors: Bauer, M., Cahlfková, J., Chytilová, J., Roland, G., & Zelinsky, T.
Journal: Economic Journal
Year: 2023

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- amount of punishment assigned to scapegoat (innocent bystander)
- amount of punishment assigned to wrongdoer
- prevalence of punishment (proportion of punishers choosing nonzero punishment)
- ethnic bias in punishment (difference in punishment by scapegoat's ethnicity)
- sensitivity of punishment to harm intensity
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are the amount of punishment (money deducted) assigned to the scapegoat or wrongdoer, and the prevalence of punishment. No group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings are reported. The design is not a public goods game or social dilemma, but a novel 'punishing the scapegoat' game focused on punishment behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when a member of the punisher's own group is harmed by a wrongdoer, majority-group punishers are much more likely to punish an innocent minority (Roma) scapegoat than an innocent majority scapegoat. The amount of punishment assigned to the scapegoat increases with the intensity of harm done by the wrongdoer, and this increase is twice as large when the scapegoat is from the minority group. No ethnic bias is observed when no harm is done. The effect is robust to controls and is not explained by collective responsibility, over-attribution of blame, or unconditional spite. The design does not involve a public goods game or measure efficiency, but provides direct evidence on punishment behavior and ethnic bias in scapegoating.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide evidence on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or social dilemma settings, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in such games. However, it provides high-quality evidence on how punishment behavior can be biased by group identity and context, especially in situations where punishment is displaced onto innocent parties. The punishment mechanism here is highly effective (low cost, high impact), and punishment is purely destructive. The findings may be relevant for understanding the social and psychological moderators of punishment behavior, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes in standard PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves 4 roles: punisher (decision-maker), wrongdoer, victim, and scapegoat. The punisher is the only active player; the others are passive and matched via photos.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant makes decisions for all five possible harm levels (strategy method), but each decision is a one-shot, anonymous interaction. No repeated rounds with feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between participants. All decisions are made privately and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is continuous: the punisher can choose any amount (in 1-euro increments) to reduce from the scapegoat's payment, from 0 to 8 euros.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly: reducing the scapegoat's (or wrongdoer's) payment by 1 euro costs the punisher 0.10 euros.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is highly effective: 0.10 euros spent reduces the target's payment by 1 euro (10:1 impact).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the structure: each task is a one-shot decision, and the number of decisions is clear (strategy method for 5 harm levels).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishers are shown the harm done by the wrongdoer to the victim, and the ethnic identity of all parties, but not any summary of others' choices or payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: the punished party does not know the identity of the punisher, and vice versa. No identification of who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or social dilemma; it is a punishment allocation task with no efficiency or group payoff outcome.
- No efficiency, welfare, or total earnings outcomes are reported; only punishment behavior is measured.
- The punishment mechanism is purely destructive and does not serve to increase cooperation or group welfare.
- The study focuses on ethnic bias in punishment, not on the effect of punishment on cooperation or efficiency.
- The findings may not generalize to standard PGG-like environments where punishment is used to enforce cooperation.
- Ethnic identity and socio-economic status are confounded to some extent; the design cannot fully disentangle these effects.
- All experimenters were from the majority group, which may have influenced responses, especially among minority participants.
