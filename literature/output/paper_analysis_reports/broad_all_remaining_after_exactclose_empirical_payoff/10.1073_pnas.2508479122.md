# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Profitable third- party punishment destabilizes cooperation
Authors: Alam, R., & Rai, T. S.
Journal: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- expected receiver earnings (Experiment 6, Fig. 6)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation rate (binary: share vs. keep)
- punishment frequency (prosocial and antisocial)
- attitudinal/judgment measures (trust, beliefs about motives, norm perceptions)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the rate at which dictators choose to share (cooperate) versus keep all money. Some analyses (Experiment 6) use expected receiver earnings, but most outcomes are behavioral (contribution/cooperation rates) or attitudinal. Group efficiency or total group payoff is not directly analyzed; payoff outcomes are only indirectly discussed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that profitable third-party punishment (i.e., paying punishers to punish) destabilizes cooperation: dictators are less likely to share when punishers are paid, both before and after any punishment is experienced. This effect is robust across multiple experimental designs, including repeated games and conditions optimized for reinforcement learning. Even when receivers (who benefit from cooperation) can choose the punishment regime, they systematically select profitable punishment, reducing their own expected earnings by about 20%. The negative effect of profitable punishment on cooperation is only eliminated when the game structure makes antisocial punishment impossible and this is clearly signaled. The main outcome is cooperation rate, but expected receiver earnings are also reported, showing a negative welfare effect. The study highlights the importance of the communicative function of punishment and the dangers of introducing profit motives into punishment systems.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing profit motives for punishment (i.e., making punishment profitable for the punisher) can reduce cooperation and lower welfare, even when punishment is otherwise available. While the experiments use dictator game variants rather than standard public goods games, the mechanisms are closely related: the presence and structure of punishment, and especially the motives signaled by punishment, are critical moderators of its effect on cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling punishment is not always beneficial for efficiency—if punishment is profitable for the punisher, it may reduce efficiency compared to both unpaid punishment and no punishment. The effect is robust to repeated play and persists even when learning is optimized. The results also show that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether antisocial punishment is possible) is a key moderator. However, because the main outcome is cooperation rate rather than group efficiency, and the game is a dictator game variant, direct quantitative predictions for public goods games should be made with caution, but the qualitative direction is clear: profitable punishment can backfire.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All economic game experiments use three players: dictator (Player 1), receiver (Player 2), and punisher (Player 3).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Both one-shot and repeated games are used: Experiment 1 and 4 are one-shot; Experiment 2 is 12 rounds; Experiment 3 is 8 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Dictator's choice is dichotomous: keep all or split evenly (binary, all-or-nothing).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishers can be paid (profitable punishment) or unpaid (costly punishment). In paid conditions, punishers receive a bonus (5 cents per punishment in repeated games); in unpaid, they pay a cost. The key manipulation is whether punishment is profitable for the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment severity is manipulated: punishers can remove $5 or $10 from the dictator. The impact per punishment is thus either $5 or $10.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed of the number of rounds in repeated games (e.g., 12 rounds in Experiment 2, 8 rounds in Experiment 3).

## Important Limitations
- The main experimental paradigm is a third-party punishment dictator game, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize fully to PGGs.
- The primary outcome is cooperation rate (binary share/keep), not group efficiency or total group payoff; efficiency is only indirectly addressed via expected receiver earnings.
- Most experiments are conducted online with US participants, limiting cultural generalizability.
- The effect of profitable punishment is shown in a specific context (third-party punishment with clear profit motive); other forms of punishment or real-world institutional settings may differ.
- The study does not manipulate or report on marginal per-capita return (MPCR), so direct mapping to PGG parameters is not possible.
- No communication or group deliberation is allowed, which may affect generalizability to real-world group settings.
