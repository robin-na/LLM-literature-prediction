# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Are They Bothered? How the Opportunity to Damage a Partner's Reputation Influences Giving Behavior in a Trust Game
Authors: Stiff, C.
Journal: Journal of Social Psychology
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- Amount of endowment (MUs) transferred to partner (trust/giving behavior)
- Perceived control over partner's reputation
- Perceived concern of partner for their reputation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of MUs transferred to the partner (i.e., trust/giving behavior). No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. Outcomes are behavioral, not payoff-based.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The opportunity to leave feedback (damage a partner's reputation) increases the amount participants are willing to give in a one-shot trust game, but only when the partner is perceived to care about their reputation. This effect is strongest when the partner's future interaction group consists of strangers rather than friends. The effect of feedback opportunity and group familiarity on giving is mediated by the participant's perceived control over the partner's reputation. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare, and the design is a dyadic trust game rather than a public goods game.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that reputation-based indirect sanctions (opportunity to leave feedback) can increase cooperative behavior (giving/trust) in dyadic trust games, especially when the target is reputation-sensitive. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of reputation-based sanctions depends on the perceived concern for reputation and the social context of future interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper is only adjacent: it supports the idea that indirect, reputation-based sanctions can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency effects or direct evidence for public goods games with explicit punishment mechanisms.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is a dyadic trust game (2 players: sender and receiver).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players; all interaction is via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (amount sent) is continuous from 0 to 15 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Amount sent is tripled before being received by the partner (multiplier = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the game is a one-shot interaction (single round).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Participants know whether they or their partner will communicate feedback to the new group; feedback is not anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a dyadic trust game, not a public goods game; findings may not generalize to multi-player PGGs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings are reported; only behavioral outcomes (amount given) are measured.
- The reputation-damage mechanism is indirect (feedback to third parties), not direct punishment or costly sanctioning.
- The currency used is a simulated monetary unit, not real money, which may limit ecological validity.
- The study does not examine repeated interactions or long-term effects.
