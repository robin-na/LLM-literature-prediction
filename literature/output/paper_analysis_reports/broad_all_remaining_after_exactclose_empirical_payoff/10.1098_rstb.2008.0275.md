# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Reciprocity, culture and human cooperation: previous insights and a new cross-cultural experiment
Authors: Gächter, S., & Herrmann, B.
Journal: Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- earnings (money units) per participant
- proportion of participants earning less than the selfish prediction
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- beliefs about others' contributions
- beliefs about punishment
- antisocial punishment frequency
- conditional cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution rates, punishment assigned, and beliefs. Earnings are reported as a secondary outcome, but efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly analyzed. The paper discusses how punishment affects cooperation and earnings, but does not provide explicit efficiency ratios or group-level payoff comparisons between punishment and no-punishment conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that in one-shot public goods games, the introduction of punishment opportunities increases contributions in Swiss participant pools but not in Russian pools. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is much more common in Russia, leading to lower earnings and less effective cooperation. The paper reports that 80% of Russian participants earned less than the selfish prediction, compared to 33% in Switzerland. However, the study does not directly report group efficiency (payoff relative to the cooperative maximum) or compare average payoffs between punishment and no-punishment conditions. The findings highlight that the effect of punishment on cooperation and earnings is strongly moderated by cultural context, with punishment sometimes failing to improve, or even reducing, group welfare due to antisocial punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation and earnings in public goods games is highly context-dependent, especially on cultural background. While the design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are well specified, the key moderator in this study is culture, which is not a standard game design variable. The paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency (payoff relative to maximum) from control efficiency and design dimensions, but it does show that punishment can increase, decrease, or have no effect on group earnings depending on the prevalence of antisocial punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of antisocial punishment (which may be proxied by cultural or social variables) is a critical moderator, and that design dimensions alone may be insufficient to predict efficiency effects of punishment without accounting for such context.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of three participants in the main cross-cultural experiment; prior and cited studies use groups of four.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each participant played both a one-shot no-punishment and a one-shot punishment game, with order randomized.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; anonymity and no prior acquaintance emphasized.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants decide how many tokens (out of 20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 (alpha = 0.5) in the main experiment.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point assigned costs the punisher one token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment impact per unit cost: each point assigned reduces the punished member's income by k ≥ 1 tokens; the exact k is not specified for this experiment, but prior studies often use k=3.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is one-shot; the number of rounds is explicit.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contribution decisions, group members are informed about how much the other group members have contributed.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; people are not informed about who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome variables are contribution rates and punishment assigned; efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly reported.
- Earnings are reported, but not in a way that allows direct calculation of efficiency or comparison of average group payoffs between punishment and no-punishment conditions.
- The study is limited to one-shot games with three players and MPCR=0.5; generalizability to other parameterizations is not tested.
- Cultural context (Russia vs Switzerland) is a major moderator, but this is not a standard game design variable and may limit the predictive value for other settings.
- Antisocial punishment is prevalent in some cultures, which can reverse the expected effect of punishment on cooperation and earnings.
- No reward mechanism is tested in the main experiment.
