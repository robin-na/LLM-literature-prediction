# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Pillars of cooperation: Honesty-Humility, social value orientations, and economic behavior
Authors: Hilbig, B. E., & Zettler, I.
Journal: Journal of Research in Personality
Year: 2009

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- points allocated to self (selfishness/fairness in allocation)
- difference in allocation between dictator and ultimatum game
- correlation of Honesty-Humility and allocation behavior
- social value orientation (prosocial vs pro-self choices)
Outcome notes:
The dependent variable is the number of points allocated to self in dictator and ultimatum games. No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported. The focus is on individual allocation behavior and its predictors.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that the possibility of punishment (ultimatum game vs dictator game) leads to more fair allocations, especially among individuals low in Honesty-Humility. High Honesty-Humility individuals are fair regardless of punishment possibility. Social value orientation partially mediates these effects. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, and all games are one-shot, hypothetical, and not incentivized with real payoffs.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides evidence that the introduction of punishment (recipient's power to reject) increases fairness in allocation, particularly among those predisposed to selfishness. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods or similar games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, using dictator and ultimatum games, and the outcome is individual allocation, not group welfare. The findings suggest that punishment can shift behavior toward fairness, but the lack of payoff-based outcomes and the hypothetical, one-shot nature of the games limit its direct predictive value for efficiency in repeated, incentivized PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played between two players: one allocator and one recipient (hypothetical).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays each game (dictator and ultimatum) once; both are one-shot games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; all decisions are made individually and anonymously.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Allocation is continuous: participants choose any amount from 0 to 100 points to allocate to self.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the ultimatum game, punishment is implemented as rejection, which costs both players all points (i.e., both get zero if the offer is rejected). The cost to the punisher (recipient) is the loss of their share.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: rejection leads to zero payoff for both players.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know that each game is played once (one-shot); the structure is fully transparent.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses hypothetical, one-shot games with no real monetary incentives; results may not generalize to incentivized or repeated settings.
- No group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported; only individual allocation behavior is measured.
- The design is adjacent to public goods games but not a direct variant; findings may not transfer directly to PGG-like environments.
- All recipients are hypothetical; no real interaction or feedback occurs.
- The sample is a convenience sample recruited online, which may limit generalizability.
