# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Applying a logic of appropriateness to understand behavioral differences between common resource dilemmas and public good dilemmas
Authors: Molenmaker, W. E., Lelieveld, G. J., De Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & Van Dijk, E.
Journal: Journal of Behavioral Decision Making
Year: 2022

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- cooperation decisions (degree of cooperation/contribution/taking/leaving/giving/keeping)
- punishment decisions (frequency of assigning decrement points)
- reward decisions (frequency of assigning increment points)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: cooperation (how much is contributed/taken/left/given/kept) and sanctioning (punishment or reward assigned to others). No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus is reported. Payoff functions are described, but outcomes are not analyzed in terms of efficiency or group payoff.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that, holding the underlying payoff structure constant, participants are more cooperative in CRDs than in PGDs. Punishment is used more frequently in CRDs, while reward is used more frequently in PGDs. These effects are robust to changes in the framing of the action (taking/leaving/giving/keeping). However, the study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides informative evidence about how the willingness to punish and cooperate differs between CRDs and PGDs, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with and without punishment), this paper does not provide direct evidence, but it does suggest that punishment is more likely to be used in CRDs and that cooperation is higher in CRDs. The game design dimensions are well described, so the paper is useful for understanding behavioral responses to punishment opportunities, but not for predicting efficiency effects.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant plays 15 CRDs and 15 PGDs (30 one-shot games per participant, not repeated with the same group). Each game is one-shot; there are no repeated rounds with feedback.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between participants is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/taking/leaving/giving/keeping choices are continuous in steps of 20% (six options from 0% to 100%). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5 in all games (group points left or contributed are doubled and divided equally among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs the punisher 1 point per group member punished; the punished loses 3 points per decrement point.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point cost to punisher results in 3 point loss to punished (ratio 3:1).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available as a between-subjects condition (participants are assigned to either punishment or reward condition).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Rewarding costs the rewarder 1 point per group member rewarded; the rewarded gains 3 points per increment point.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 point cost to rewarder results in 3 point gain to rewarded (ratio 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the number of games (15 CRDs and 15 PGDs) in advance; each game is one-shot.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are not informed about other group members' actual cooperation decisions before making their own decisions. In the sanctioning stage, they assign punishment/reward using the strategy method (for all possible choices), not in response to observed behavior.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined.
- All games are one-shot (no repeated interaction with feedback), which may limit generalizability to repeated public goods games.
- Participants know that a sanctioning stage will follow, which may influence their cooperation decisions.
- Sanctioning is implemented using the strategy method (pre-specified responses to all possible choices), not in response to actual observed behavior.
- No analysis of how punishment or reward affects actual payoffs or group efficiency.
- No direct comparison of efficiency with and without punishment or reward.
