# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Incentive contracts for teams: Experimental evidence
Authors: Landeo, C. M., & Spier, K. E.
Journal: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization
Year: 2015

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- principal's payoff
- agents' payoffs
- group payoff
- cost of achieving cooperation (principal's cost)
- frequency of high-payoff outcomes
Non-payoff outcomes:
- team cooperation rate (both agents work hard)
- frequency of sharing rule choices
- frequency of punishment (shirking as retaliation)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are principal's and agents' payoffs, and the cost to the principal of achieving cooperation. Team cooperation (both agents working hard) is a key behavioral outcome, but the analysis is explicitly tied to payoff and efficiency, especially for the principal.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that long-term team interaction (infinitely repeated games) increases both the likelihood of team cooperation and the principal's payoff, especially in the absence of communication. Communication between agents also increases cooperation and the principal's payoff in short-term (one-shot) settings, but is a less effective substitute for ongoing interaction. Long-term teams enable the principal to induce cooperation with lower sharing rules, reducing the cost of cooperation and increasing efficiency. However, if the principal chooses the lowest possible sharing rule, agents may engage in costly punishment (shirking) as negative reciprocity, which can reduce efficiency. The effects are robust across endogenous (human principal) and exogenous (computer) environments, but negative reciprocity is stronger when the principal is a human.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling ongoing interaction (repeated games) among team members increases efficiency and allows for cooperation at a lower cost to the principal, especially when communication is not available. Communication can substitute for repeated interaction to some extent in one-shot games, but does not further increase efficiency when repeated interaction is already present. The findings suggest that, in environments structurally similar to this experiment (team production with effort complementarity and binary effort choices), introducing repeated interaction or communication will increase efficiency, but the effect of communication is limited when repetition is already present. The results also highlight that punitive responses (shirking as punishment) can reduce efficiency if the incentive structure is perceived as unfair, especially with human principals. For prediction, the presence of repeated interaction (long-term teams) is a strong positive moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency, while communication is a weaker, context-dependent moderator.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 3 players: 1 principal and 2 agents.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Short-term teams: 1 round per match; Long-term teams: random continuation with delta=0.75, average 4 rounds per match, total 36 rounds over 9 matches.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (structured, intention-sharing) between agents is a treatment variable. Communication increases cooperation and principal's payoff in short-term settings.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Agents' effort choice is binary: work hard or shirk.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is not explicitly named, but can be calculated: e.g., for both agents working hard, total revenue is 344, each agent gets x*344-38, principal gets (1-2x)*344. The marginal return to each agent's effort is determined by the sharing rule x and the revenue structure.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is not an explicit mechanism; 'punishment' occurs as costly shirking by agents in response to low sharing rules. The cost is the foregone payoff from not cooperating.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is indirect: agents can reduce the principal's payoff by shirking, but at a cost to themselves. The 'impact per unit cost' is not directly measured.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. The sharing rule (x) is a reward mechanism: agents are rewarded with a share of the revenue if they work hard.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. The cost to the principal of rewarding agents is the share of revenue given up (x per agent).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the sharing rule x and the revenue structure; higher x increases agents' incentive to work hard.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In short-term teams, the number of rounds is known (1 per match). In long-term teams, subjects are told the probability of continuation (delta=0.75), but not the exact number of rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. At the end of each round, subjects receive information about their own group results and payoffs. In long-term teams, agents observe each other's actions after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental environment is not a standard public goods game, but a team production game with effort complementarity and endogenous sharing rules; results may not generalize to standard PGGs.
- Punishment is not implemented as a direct, explicit mechanism; instead, 'punishment' occurs as costly shirking in response to low sharing rules, which may differ from standard punishment treatments.
- The group size is fixed at 3 (1 principal, 2 agents), limiting generalizability to larger groups.
- Effort choices are binary (work hard or shirk), not continuous.
- The principal's role is not always present in standard PGGs, and the endogenous choice of sharing rule may not map directly to fixed-multiplier PGGs.
- Communication is limited to structured, intention-sharing messages between agents; richer forms of communication are not tested.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
