# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Sadism, the Intuitive System, and Antisocial Punishment in the Public Goods Game
Authors: Pfattheicher, S., Keller, J., & Knezevic, G.
Journal: Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin
Year: 2017

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- antisocial punishment frequency (punishing cooperators)
- punishment of uncooperative individuals
- contribution to the public good
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency of antisocial punishment (punishing those who contributed as much or more than the punisher). The paper also reports on punishment of uncooperative individuals and contribution levels. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a primary outcome; payoff is only described in the game instructions and as context.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The studies show that activating the intuitive system increases antisocial punishment among individuals with high sadism, while inhibiting intuition reduces it. This effect is specific to antisocial punishment and not to punishment of defectors or to contribution behavior. The presence of punishment in the game enables antisocial punishment, but the paper does not analyze the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff. The findings are relevant for understanding the psychological moderators of antisocial punishment but do not provide direct evidence about efficiency or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence about the psychological and cognitive moderators of antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is only indirectly informative: it confirms that antisocial punishment can occur in standard PGGs with punishment, and that its frequency is affected by individual differences and cognitive processing mode. However, it does not quantify the impact of punishment on efficiency or provide control vs. treatment efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper can help confirm the mapping of design features, but not the efficiency effect of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 4 players in the public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Six periods (rounds) of the public goods game were played.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all interactions were computer-mediated and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0-20 MUs), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU contributed was multiplied by 1.6 and divided among 4 players, so MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing cost 1 MU per unit, up to 10 MUs per target per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 1 MU spent on punishment reduced the target's payoff by 3 MUs (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew there were six periods (rounds) in the game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each contribution stage, players were shown the contributions of the other three players before making punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players did not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or total earnings as a primary outcome.
- The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment and contribution), not payoff-based.
- The punishment stage used pre-programmed contributions and punishment received, not real-time interactive group behavior, which may affect ecological validity.
- The findings are specific to antisocial punishment and its psychological moderators, not to the overall effect of punishment on cooperation or efficiency.
- No direct comparison of group outcomes with and without punishment; all conditions included punishment.
