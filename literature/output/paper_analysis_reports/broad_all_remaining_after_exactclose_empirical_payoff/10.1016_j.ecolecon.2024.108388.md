# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Does scarcity induce hostility? An experimental investigation of common-pool resources
Authors: Geschwind, S., & Lambsdorff, J. G.
Journal: Ecological Economics
Year: 2025

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group welfare loss (relative to social optimum)
- individual and group catch (resource extraction)
- remaining fish in pool
Non-payoff outcomes:
- destruction assigned to other player (joy-of-destruction)
- reciprocity/retaliation behavior
- inequality aversion
- nastiness (generic hostility)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the number of fish destroyed by a participant in the second stage (joy-of-destruction). While group welfare loss and overharvesting are reported, the focus is on hostility/destruction rather than efficiency or total payoff. Efficiency is discussed in terms of welfare loss from overharvesting, but not as a primary outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that when scarcity is perceived as human-induced (overexploitation), participants are more likely to destroy each other's resources in the subsequent stage, indicating increased hostility. When scarcity is exogenously imposed (low reproduction), destruction is lower, suggesting that environmental scarcity can foster restraint and cooperation. The destruction mechanism is not a standard punishment stage: destruction is costless, partially hidden (due to random 'bear' destruction), and not directly strategic. The study finds that reciprocity (retaliation for overexploitation) is a key driver of destruction, while inequality aversion plays a limited role. Overharvesting is substantial in the CPR game, leading to welfare losses, but the main focus is on behavioral responses (hostility) rather than efficiency or payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency outcomes for a standard PGG with or without punishment, it shows that the presence of a destruction (punishment-like) stage increases hostile behavior when scarcity is human-induced, and decreases it when scarcity is exogenous. The destruction mechanism differs from standard costly punishment: it is costless, partially hidden, and not designed to change future behavior. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency may depend on the perceived cause of scarcity and the design of the punishment stage. The paper is most informative for understanding how the framing and cause of scarcity moderate the impact of punishment-like options on group behavior, but does not provide direct data for predicting efficiency changes from enabling punishment in standard PGGs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 6 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication between participants; all communication with experimenters is via chat for technical issues only.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (catch) is continuous: participants choose any integer number of fish to catch between 0 and the available pool.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Destruction (punishment) is costless to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each unit of destruction removes one fish from the other player's payoff; up to 4 fish per round can be destroyed.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told in advance that the experiment consists of 6 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed about the other player's catch and the state of the pool before and after reproduction.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment (destruction) is anonymous and partially hidden: participants only see the total number of fish lost (sum of destruction and random 'bear' destruction), not who destroyed how many.

## Important Limitations
- The destruction (punishment) stage is not equivalent to standard costly punishment: it is costless, partially hidden, and not designed to change future behavior.
- The main outcome is hostility (destruction), not efficiency or group payoff.
- The game is a dynamic CPR, not a standard linear PGG; results may not generalize to PGGs.
- Only two players per group; effects may differ in larger groups.
- No communication between participants; results may differ with communication.
- No reward mechanism is present.
- Efficiency outcomes are reported only as welfare loss from overharvesting, not as a primary dependent variable.
