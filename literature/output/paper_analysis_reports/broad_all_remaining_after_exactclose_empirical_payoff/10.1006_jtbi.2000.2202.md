The paper analyzes under what conditions cooperation and punishment are stable and what average payoffs result.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in public goods dilemmas with costly punishment, the combination of payoff-biased and conformist social learning can stabilize both punishment and cooperation, even when punishment is costly and higher-order free-riding is possible. The model demonstrates that, for a finite number of punishment stages, an arbitrarily small amount of conformist transmission is sufficient to stabilize cooperation and punishment, leading to higher group payoffs. The expected payoff of individuals in cooperative groups is strictly higher than in non-cooperative groups, and these high-payoff equilibria can spread through cultural group selection. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation and punishment are stable and when prosocial genetic traits may invade.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling costly punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency, provided that some mechanism (such as conformist transmission) supports the stability of punishment. The model clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the costs of cooperation and punishment, the error rate, and the strength of conformist transmission. It also highlights that, in the absence of punishment, cooperation is not stable and efficiency is low. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of social learning mechanisms and the cost structure are critical. The model is most informative for settings where group size is large, punishment is costly but effective, and social learning is plausible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Model explicitly considers groups of size N, with analysis for large N. Larger groups make cooperation harder to sustain without punishment, but the model shows that with punishment and conformist transmission, cooperation can be stabilized even in large groups.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is a one-shot public goods game followed by a finite number of punishment stages (i+1 stage game). There is no repeated multi-period structure, but the number of punishment stages is a key parameter.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is dichotomous: individuals either cooperate (contribute) or defect (do not contribute).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is B/N, where B is the benefit of cooperation and N is group size. The model analyzes how the benefit-to-cost ratio affects stability of cooperation and efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost to the punisher is phi (φ). Higher punishment costs make it harder to stabilize punishment and cooperation, reducing efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is rho (ρ), the cost imposed on the punished. Higher ρ (relative to φ) makes punishment more effective and helps stabilize cooperation, increasing efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of punishment stages (i) is known and fixed in the model; the game is not repeated beyond these stages.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
- The model assumes specific forms of social learning (payoff-biased and conformist transmission) and does not address other possible learning mechanisms.
- The model is based on a one-shot public goods game with punishment stages, not repeated games or dynamic interactions over time.
- No consideration of communication, reward mechanisms, or identification of punishers.
- Assumes homogeneous groups and does not model heterogeneity in preferences or network structure.
- The stability of cooperation depends on the presence and strength of conformist transmission, which is assumed rather than empirically estimated.
