## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when punishment is administered by a second party (the direct victim of a transgression), it is perceived as more biased and is significantly more likely to provoke counter-punishment than when administered by a third party. Specifically, second-party punishment is 66.3% more likely to trigger counter-punishment, and the loss in earnings due to counter-punishment is 64.6% higher for second-party punishers. The experiment uses a one-shot 'taking game' with punishment and counter-punishment stages, but does not measure or report group efficiency or total welfare. The findings highlight the importance of the source of punishment in determining the social costs of norm enforcement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the identity of the punisher (second-party vs. third-party) is a critical moderator of the likelihood and severity of counter-punishment, which can increase the costs of norm enforcement. However, because the experiment is not a public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff, its direct value for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments is limited. The main implication is that punishment by affected parties may be less effective (or more costly) due to increased counter-punishment, but the paper does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes for use in prediction tasks.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of two or three players depending on condition: 2P (Player A and B), 3P (Player A, B, and C).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is played only once (one-shot design) to rule out strategic motives.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The 'take' decision is discrete (0, 5, or 10 EMU), and punishment is chosen in 10% increments (0-100%). Not strictly all-or-nothing, but not fully continuous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 5 EMU to the punisher, regardless of the percentage reduction chosen.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 5 EMU to reduce the target's income by a chosen percentage (in 10% increments, up to 100%).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game is played only once.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Players are informed of relevant actions (e.g., amount taken, punishment received) as required for their decisions, but no mention of broader summary statistics.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know who punished them (second-party or third-party), as this is the main experimental manipulation.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is not a public goods game or a direct variant; it uses a one-shot taking game.
- No direct measurement or analysis of group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff is reported.
- Findings on counter-punishment and perceived bias may not generalize to repeated or multi-player public goods games.
- Sample size for some conditions (e.g., specific punishment levels) is small, limiting statistical power for those comparisons.
- The study focuses on the source of punishment (second- vs. third-party) and does not vary other key game design dimensions relevant to efficiency prediction.
