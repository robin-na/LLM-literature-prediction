# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Altruistic punishment in modern intentional communities
Authors: Qirko, H.
Journal: Interaction Studies
Year: 2020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=observational; experimental subtype=N/A

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- presence of free-riding as a problem
- existence of rules/norms for punishment
- actual use of third-party punishment
- contextual factors influencing punishment
- attitudes toward enforcement of cooperative norms
Outcome notes:
The study focuses on whether free-riding is acknowledged, whether rules/norms for punishment exist, and under what conditions they are enforced. No direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes is reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that, in modern intentional communities, free-riding is often acknowledged as a problem and most communities have rules or norms to address it. However, only about half of the communities report actually enforcing these rules through third-party punishment, and even then, enforcement is context-dependent and often flexible. The data do not support the existence of a strong, inherited predisposition to engage in costly third-party punishment. Instead, punishment behavior appears to be shaped by contextual, self-maximizing considerations. No direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or payoff are reported.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It suggests that, in real-world cooperative groups with explicit rules and opportunities for punishment, actual use of costly third-party punishment is infrequent and context-dependent. This implies that the presence of punishment mechanisms does not guarantee their use or an increase in efficiency, especially outside of laboratory or game-like settings. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings are most useful for understanding the limits of external validity for lab-based punishment effects and for highlighting the importance of contextual moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Mean group size reported as 44.8 adults (median 45); group size did not significantly affect whether free-riding was seen as a problem or whether punishment was used.
- chat: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Communities often use group discussion and consensus decision-making; communication is integral but not systematically manipulated.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is described as sometimes involving costs (e.g., fines, social pressure), but often low or reputational; not systematically quantified.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is often low-cost, coordinated, and aimed at reducing conflict rather than maximizing cooperation; effectiveness is not quantified.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Members are generally aware of others' participation and contributions through group processes and discussions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. Punishment, when it occurs, is typically not anonymous (e.g., group discussions, direct requests to leave); identity of punishers is known.

## Important Limitations
- No direct measurement of efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes.
- Data are based on self-report from a single representative per community in most cases, not direct observation.
- Sample is small and not representative (predominantly WEIRD, possibly more so than general population).
- Intentional communities may not generalize to other real-world or experimental public goods settings.
- No experimental manipulation of game design dimensions; all evidence is observational and context-specific.
