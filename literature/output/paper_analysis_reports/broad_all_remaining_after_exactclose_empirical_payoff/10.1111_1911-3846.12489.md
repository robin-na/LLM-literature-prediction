# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Controls and Cooperation in Interactive and Non-Interactive Settings
Authors: Garrett, J. B., Livingston, J. A., Tayler, W. B., Cade, N. L., & McVay, S. E.
Journal: Contemporary Accounting Research
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- investment in trust game (proxy for future cooperative behavior)
- trusting belief (expected return in trust game)
- cooperation rate in prisoner's dilemma
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount invested in a trust game after a prior prisoner's dilemma with or without a control (audit/bonus). This is interpreted as future cooperative behavior, not as group efficiency or total payoff. No direct efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a control (audit with bonus) in a repeated prisoner's dilemma increases subsequent cooperative behavior (measured as investment in a trust game) for both participants who directly experienced the control and those who only observed it, but the effect is significantly larger for those who directly benefited from cooperation. The effect is mediated by observed cooperation in the initial game. The results suggest that controls can foster trust and reciprocity, and that these effects are stronger in interactive settings. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and the main outcome is behavioral (investment/cooperation), not payoff-based.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence that control mechanisms (akin to monitoring or incentive-based controls) can increase cooperative behavior in subsequent social dilemmas, especially in interactive settings. However, because the main outcome is investment in a trust game (a behavioral measure) and not efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not involve explicit punishment or sanctions, but rather a positive incentive (bonus for high contribution). The results suggest that mechanisms increasing observed cooperation can have positive spillovers on future cooperation, but do not directly inform the efficiency impact of punishment in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4: 2 managers (interactive) and 2 observers (non-interactive) per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage 1 (prisoner's dilemma) is played for 7 rounds; Stage 2 is a one-shot trust game.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. All interaction was via computer; no mention of chat or communication between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage 1: binary choice (low or high resource contribution); Stage 2: continuous investment (0-100 points).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Stage 1: joint project profit is split equally; Stage 2: trust game multiplier is 3x (each point invested yields 3 to receiver).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Control condition includes a bonus (reward) for high contribution, implemented with 80% probability per round.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Bonus is 15 points for high contribution if audited; no explicit cost to the rewarder (the 'firm').
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is 15 points per high contribution, with 80% chance per round (audit probability).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of rounds (7) in Stage 1.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, managers receive feedback about their partner's decision; observers see all decisions and payoffs.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not use a public goods game or a direct variant; it uses a repeated prisoner's dilemma followed by a trust game.
- No punishment or sanction mechanism is present; the control is a positive incentive (bonus for high contribution).
- The main outcome is future cooperative behavior (investment in trust game), not efficiency, group payoff, or welfare.
- Findings may not generalize to settings with punishment, sanctions, or explicit public goods dilemmas.
- Only one type of control (audit with bonus) is studied; results may not generalize to other control types.
- Participants are university students in a lab setting, which may limit external validity.
