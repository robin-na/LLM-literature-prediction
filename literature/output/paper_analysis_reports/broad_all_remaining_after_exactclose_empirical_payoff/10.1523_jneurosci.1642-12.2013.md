# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Computational Substrates of Norms and Their Violations during Social Exchange
Authors: Xiang, T., Lohrenz, T., & Montague, P. R.
Journal: Journal of Neuroscience
Year: 2013

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rejection rates of offers
- subjective feelings about offers (emoticon ratings)
- neural activation (fMRI) in response to norm and variance prediction errors
- model-based norm prediction errors
- model-based variance prediction errors
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are rejection rates (i.e., willingness to punish unfairness at a cost), subjective feelings, and neural responses. There is no direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. Payoff is only indirectly referenced via the structure of the ultimatum game (acceptance yields monetary reward, rejection yields zero for both).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that social norm expectations, manipulated via preconditioning to low, medium, or high offers in a repeated ultimatum game, strongly affect rejection rates for identical offers. Subjects preconditioned to low offers (unfair) are less likely to reject medium offers than those preconditioned to high offers (hyper-fair), even though the offers are objectively the same. Subjective feelings about offers and neural responses (in regions such as vmPFC, striatum, and anterior insula) track these norm prediction errors. The results support a model in which norm violations, rather than simple payoff inequality, drive punishment behavior (rejection), and that these processes are reflected in both behavior and brain activity. However, the study does not report on group efficiency or total payoff, and the outcome of interest is not efficiency but rather the psychological and neural mechanisms of norm-based punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction of punishment effects in public-goods-like environments, specifically regarding how expectations and norm adaptation modulate punishment (rejection) behavior. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment (here, costly rejection) on behavior is highly sensitive to context and prior expectations, which may inform models of punishment in other social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used as contextual or mechanistic evidence about the psychological drivers of punishment, not as a quantitative source for efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round is a two-player ultimatum game (one Responder, one Proposer). Subjects always play as Responder; Proposers are simulated or anonymous.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject plays 60 rounds (trials), with a new partner each round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing: accept or reject the offer.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as rejection: if the Responder rejects, both players get $0. The cost to the punisher (Responder) is the foregone offer amount.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is maximally effective: rejection reduces both players' payoffs to zero.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told there are 60 trials; each trial is with a new partner.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the ultimatum game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to the public goods game; punishment is implemented as rejection rather than as a separate costly punishment option.
- No direct measures or analyses of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported; only rejection rates, subjective feelings, and neural responses are analyzed.
- The Proposer role is not played by real participants; offers are generated from distributions, which may affect ecological validity.
- The findings are specific to two-player, one-shot (but repeated) ultimatum games and may not generalize to multi-player, repeated public goods games.
- No communication or reputation effects are present; each round is with a new, anonymous partner.
- The study focuses on the psychological and neural mechanisms of norm violation and punishment, not on the aggregate consequences for group efficiency.
