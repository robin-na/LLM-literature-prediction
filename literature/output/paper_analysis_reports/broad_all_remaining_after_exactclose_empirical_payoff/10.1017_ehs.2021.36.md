## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that commitment is a central mechanism for the evolution of cooperation, punishment, and other moral behaviors in humans. It reviews evidence and theory suggesting that commitment to punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and prisoners' dilemmas, even when punishment is costly. The authors contend that standard models based on population structure or repetition do not fully explain observed human behavior, and that commitment (and the ability to recognize commitment in others) is necessary for the evolution of costly punishment and conditional cooperation. The paper discusses the implications for efficiency and group welfare in a theoretical sense but does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical results on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for why punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and similar environments, emphasizing the role of commitment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the idea that the presence of punishment (and the ability to commit to punishment) can change the strategic environment and potentially increase cooperation, but it does not allow for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of commitment and recognizability of commitment for punishment to be effective.
