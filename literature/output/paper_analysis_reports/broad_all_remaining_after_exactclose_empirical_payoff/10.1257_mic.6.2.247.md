# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Communication, Renegotiation, and the Scope for Collusion
Authors: Cooper, D. J., & Kühn, K. U.
Journal: American Economic Journal-microeconomics
Year: 2014

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total earnings per round (ECUs)
- average payoffs for different strategies
- gains from cheating
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of collusion (play of High in Period 1)
- frequency of cheating on collusive agreements
- frequency and type of punishment (explicit/implicit threats, verbal admonishments)
- agreement rates on collusive strategies
- message content analysis (threats, promises, appeals, etc.)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: collusion rates, cheating rates, and message content. Payoff data is reported as supporting evidence (e.g., average payoffs, gains from cheating), but efficiency (as a share of maximum possible group payoff) is not directly calculated or reported. The focus is on how communication and punishment threats affect collusion and cheating, not on group efficiency per se.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment shows that communication, especially when it allows explicit threats of punishment, is critical for sustaining collusion in repeated games with incentives to cheat. Limited message spaces (even those allowing contingent punishment messages) do not sustain collusion, while open chat does. Renegotiation (chat between periods) further increases collusion, likely due to the role of verbal (non-monetary) punishment. Explicit threats of punishment significantly reduce cheating, and sending promises of trustworthy behavior also reduces cheating, though to a lesser extent. The effect of communication is not simply due to increased trust or appeals to mutual benefit, but specifically to the ability to coordinate on credible punishment strategies. The results are robust to various econometric specifications.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment depends critically on the communication environment. Open-ended chat that allows explicit threats of punishment leads to higher and more stable cooperation (collusion) than limited or structured message spaces. However, the game is not a standard public goods game, and efficiency is not directly measured as a share of maximum group payoff. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is possible, its effectiveness is greatly enhanced by the ability to communicate explicit threats, and that the structure of communication channels is a key moderator. The results also highlight that behavioral outcomes (collusion, cheating) can diverge from what would be predicted by monetary incentives alone, due to the role of verbal and social punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per session; each round is a two-period game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Open-ended chat is allowed in PChat and RChat treatments; these treatments show higher and more persistent collusion.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/choice is discrete: players choose among three options (Low, Medium, High) in each period.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is endogenous: after a deviation, the 'punishment' is the choice of a low payoff equilibrium in Period 2 (e.g., both play Low). There is no explicit cost to punishing, but both players receive lower payoffs. No monetary cost for sending threats or verbal punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the ability to coordinate on low-payoff equilibria after deviation. Explicit threats to punish with Low in Period 2 are effective at sustaining collusion.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told in advance that there are 20 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see summaries of outcomes (prices and payoffs) for all previous rounds, including the three most recent rounds automatically.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chat messages are tagged with a random 10-digit chat id, allowing players to distinguish their own and their opponent's messages within a round, but not across rounds. No persistent identification of punishers across rounds.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a two-player, two-period Bertrand-style collusion game, not a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to multi-player or continuous-contribution PGGs.
- Efficiency (as a share of maximum possible group payoff) is not directly reported; main outcomes are behavioral (collusion, cheating) and some average payoffs.
- Punishment is implemented as a switch to a low-payoff equilibrium, not as a costly, targeted punishment mechanism.
- Verbal (non-monetary) punishment is important in the renegotiation treatment, which may not generalize to settings where such punishment is not possible or not salient.
- All games are played in pairs; effects in larger groups are not tested.
- Communication is abstract and anonymous; real-world social and organizational contexts may yield different results.
- The study population is undergraduate students in a lab setting; external validity to firms or other populations is uncertain.
