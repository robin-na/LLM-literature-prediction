## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Ostrom reviews and synthesizes experimental and field evidence on collective action, focusing on public goods and common-pool resource dilemmas. She identifies that communication and the ability to punish free riders both substantially increase cooperation rates in laboratory public goods games. The paper argues that real-world resource regimes that allow users to design their own rules, monitor each other, and impose graduated sanctions tend to be more sustainable and effective. However, the paper does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report new experimental data. Instead, it provides a conceptual framework for understanding how punishment, communication, and institutional design affect cooperation and the sustainability of collective action.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong conceptual and qualitative support for the idea that enabling punishment and communication increases cooperation in public goods and collective action settings. It suggests that punishment mechanisms, especially when locally designed and enforced, can help sustain cooperation and reduce free riding. However, the paper does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it specify the effects of particular game design dimensions in a way that would allow direct prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design features. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the inclusion of punishment and communication as positive moderators of cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence for efficiency outcomes or for the effect sizes associated with specific design parameters.

- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `contextual` under this dimension. Group size is discussed as a contextual factor affecting cooperation and collective action, but not manipulated or analyzed quantitatively. Small groups are suggested to be more likely to sustain cooperation.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `contextual` under this dimension. Number of rounds is discussed in the context of repeated games; longer repetition is associated with slower decay of cooperation.
- chat: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Face-to-face communication is highlighted as strongly increasing cooperation; computer-mediated communication is less effective.
- mpcr: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Marginal per-capita return is described in the example of the linear public goods game, but not analyzed as a moderator.
- punishment cost: The paper provides contextual guidance on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is described as costly to the punisher, but the cost is not quantified or varied. The presence of costly punishment increases cooperation.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward is mentioned as a possible mechanism, but not analyzed or discussed in detail.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Some experiments are described as finitely repeated with the end period announced to subjects.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is a theoretical and integrative review, not a primary empirical or experimental study.
- No quantitative or direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported; outcomes are primarily behavioral (contribution, cooperation, punishment, norm compliance).
- Game design dimensions are discussed conceptually but not systematically manipulated or analyzed for their quantitative effects on efficiency.
- Punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed in general terms, without specification of costs, effectiveness, or implementation details.
- Findings are synthesized from multiple sources, so the paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of specific design parameters on efficiency.
