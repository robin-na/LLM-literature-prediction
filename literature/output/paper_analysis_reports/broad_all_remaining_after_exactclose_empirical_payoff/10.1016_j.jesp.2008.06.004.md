# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The difference between punishments and rewards in fostering moral concerns in social decision making
Authors: Mulder, L. B.
Journal: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution (number of coins put in the pool)
- moral concerns regarding cooperation
- motive of collective-interest
- moral judgment of others' behavior
- behavioral disapproval (willingness to disadvantage non-compliers)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral (contribution/cooperation), attitudinal (moral concerns, collective-interest), and judgmental (moral judgment, disapproval). No group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing the concept of punishment (without actual economic consequences) in a public-goods-like game increases cooperation and moral concerns compared to reward or control. Actual punishment (vs. reward) in a dictator-type allocation game leads to stronger moral disapproval of non-compliers and greater willingness to disadvantage them, even at a personal cost. The effect of punishment on cooperation is mediated by increased moral concerns and collective-interest motives. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment (as a concept or mechanism) increases cooperation and moral concerns more than reward in public-goods-like settings. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, which are the target for prediction. The findings suggest that, under similar game design dimensions, punishment is likely to increase cooperation, but the magnitude of efficiency gains or losses cannot be inferred from this paper. The results are most useful for predicting behavioral and attitudinal changes, not efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: 4 players per group in the social dilemma (public goods) game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Study 1: One-shot decision (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could choose how many coins (0-10) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each coin contributed is multiplied by 1.5 and divided among 4 players: MPCR = 1.5/4 = 0.375.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward condition is included as a treatment arm in both studies.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the decision was a one-shot game; number of rounds was clear.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral and attitudinal outcomes are measured.
- Punishment and reward are not implemented as actual participant-to-participant mechanisms in the public goods game; in Study 1, only the concept is primed.
- The public goods game is one-shot, limiting generalizability to repeated settings.
- No analysis of punishment cost, effectiveness, or impact on group surplus.
- Findings on cooperation and moral concerns may not translate directly to efficiency or payoff-based predictions.
