Contribution rates and implementation decisions are also analyzed, but the focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The model is for n-player public-goods games (n >= 2). The effect of player count is discussed: as n increases, the critical threshold for contribution changes, and public-good provision is nonincreasing in n.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is explicitly one-shot (single round). Repeated games are mentioned as a possible extension but not analyzed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous up to the player's endowment, but the best response is always either full contribution or zero (all-or-nothing in equilibrium).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is lambda/n, with lambda > 1 and lambda < n. Higher lambda (mpcr) increases the critical threshold for contribution and increases efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented by a sheriff (external enforcer) who must be paid a salary. The cost of incentivizing high effort (c) and the economic rents due to moral hazard are explicitly modeled. Higher punishment cost reduces efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the probability of detection (p_H) and the fine (f). Higher p_H and f increase the critical threshold for contribution and increase efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The game is one-shot; the number of rounds is known and fixed at one.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the size of the public fund but cannot observe who contributed how much.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is purely theoretical; no empirical or experimental data are presented.
- The model assumes a one-shot game; repeated interactions are not analyzed.
- Punishment is centralized and implemented by an external enforcer (sheriff), not by peers.
- The government is assumed to represent a single social class, which may not reflect real-world political processes.
- The model assumes that the expected cost of being punished is nonincreasing in wealth, which may not always hold empirically.
- No reward mechanisms or communication between players are considered.
- The model assumes perfect observability of the public fund but not of individual contributions.
