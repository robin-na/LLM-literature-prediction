# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Suffering Makes You Egoist: Acute Pain Increases Acceptance Rates and Reduces Fairness during a Bilateral Ultimatum Game
Authors: Mancini, A., Betti, V., Panasiti, M. S., Pavone, E. F., & Aglioti, S. M.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2011

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- acceptance rate of offers (responder behavior)
- offer size (proposer behavior)
- fairness ratings of offers
- reaction times
- subjective ratings of pain and attention
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are acceptance rates (responder), offer rates (proposer), and fairness ratings. No group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings are reported. The study focuses on behavioral responses to pain in the Ultimatum Game, not on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that acute pain leads to higher acceptance rates of unfair offers and lower fairness in offers made, suggesting that pain induces more self-interested behavior in both roles of the Ultimatum Game. The effect is specific to pain (not just heat) and is most pronounced for moderately unfair offers. The intensity of pain predicts acceptance rates for these offers. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or any direct payoff-based outcomes, and does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It uses the Ultimatum Game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to PGGs, and focuses on the effect of pain (a state manipulation) rather than institutional punishment. The outcomes are behavioral (acceptance, offer size) rather than payoff-based. Thus, its relevance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like games is limited.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Ultimatum Game is played between two players (proposer and responder) per round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 6 blocks (4 as responder, 2 as proposer), each block had 10 trials, for a total of 60 iterations.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; anonymity and no negotiation were enforced.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responder's choice is all-or-nothing (accept or reject the offer). Proposer chooses from discrete offer options.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (blocks and trials) is fixed and known to the experimenters; it is not explicitly stated if participants knew the total number, but the block structure suggests they were aware of the session's structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Responders received feedback about payoffs after each trial; proposers did not receive feedback to avoid outcome effects.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses the Ultimatum Game, not a public goods game or a direct variant.
- No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; rejection of offers is not equivalent to costly punishment in PGGs.
- No efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings outcomes are reported; only behavioral outcomes (acceptance, offer size, fairness ratings) are analyzed.
- The main manipulation is acute pain, not an institutional or design feature relevant to PGGs.
- Sample size is moderate (n=24 after exclusions), and the experiment is conducted in a lab setting with deception (computerized opponents).
