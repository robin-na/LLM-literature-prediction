## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model shows that introducing sanctions (punishment) can increase efficiency in CPR games by enabling the enforcement of rules that prevent over-extraction (static inefficiency) and/or promote conservation (dynamic inefficiency). The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the strength of the sanctioning mechanism relative to the incentives to deviate from cooperation. When sanctions are strong enough, the first-best (fully efficient) outcome can be achieved. With limited sanctions, only partial improvements are possible, and sometimes increasing the resource's value or growth rate can make enforcement harder, reducing efficiency. The model also shows that the number of players, extraction cost, and inequality among players all moderate the effect of sanctions on efficiency. In particular, more players or greater inequality can make conservation harder to enforce, while higher extraction costs can make it easier. The model provides explicit formulas and comparative statics for these effects.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (sanctions) on efficiency in CPR-like games. It shows that the effect of punishment depends on the interplay between the sanctioning technology (maximum enforceable sanction), the number of players, the resource's growth rate, extraction cost, and initial value. If sanctions are strong enough relative to the incentives to deviate, enabling rules can be enforced and efficiency increases. If not, efficiency may not improve, or may even decrease if resource value/growth increases incentives to defect. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the logic of how punishment affects efficiency is highly transferable. The paper is especially informative for settings where the punishment mechanism is exogenous and bounded, and where resource dynamics (growth, conservation) are important. For prediction, the model suggests that the effect of enabling punishment depends on whether the sanctioning parameters cross key thresholds relative to the game's other parameters.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. The model explicitly includes N players and analyzes comparative statics as N varies. Increasing player count makes conservation harder to enforce and reduces the effectiveness of sanctions for achieving dynamic efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. The main model is two-period (two rounds), but extensions to T-period and three-period games are analyzed. Longer horizons can make conservation easier if future growth rates are high, and sanctions can have greater effect over longer horizons.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (extraction effort) is continuous and bounded, not all-or-nothing. The model allows any effort between 0 and a maximum.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Sanctions are modeled as exogenous, with a maximum enforceable sanction (\bar{\sigma}). The cost to the punisher is not explicitly modeled, but the effectiveness of sanctions is a key parameter. Higher enforceable sanctions increase efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology is captured by the maximum enforceable sanction (\bar{\sigma}). Higher punishment effectiveness (higher \bar{\sigma}) increases the set of enforceable efficient outcomes.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of periods (rounds) is common knowledge in the model.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and not empirically tested in this paper.
- The setting is a common-pool resource extraction game, not a standard public goods game, though it is closely related.
- Punishment is modeled as an exogenous, community-level sanction with a maximum enforceable level, not as peer-to-peer punishment with explicit cost to the punisher.
- No behavioral or psychological factors (e.g., fairness, reciprocity, learning) are modeled.
- No communication, reward, or reputation mechanisms are included.
- The model assumes perfect information and rationality.
- Comparative statics are derived for model parameters, but real-world implementation may involve additional complexities.
