## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The operant conditioning model (ZL) can learn to cooperate in the IPD and achieves high average payoffs against a variety of strategies. In evolutionary simulations, ZL can dominate or survive in populations depending on the mix of strategies and the presence of noise (random moves). However, ZL is always slightly less efficient than non-learning strategies because it requires time to learn optimal responses. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; instead, it relies on reinforcement learning from payoffs. The findings are relevant for understanding how learning mechanisms can support cooperation and efficiency in repeated social dilemmas, but do not directly address the effect of enabling or disabling punishment in public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does, however, provide insight into how learning mechanisms (operant conditioning) can support cooperation and high payoffs in repeated dyadic dilemmas (IPD). The absence of explicit punishment or sanctioning means that the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task. The results suggest that learning-based strategies can achieve high efficiency, but may be less efficient than fixed strategies in the short run due to learning costs. For prediction tasks focused on punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled efficiency, this paper offers only indirect, contextual support.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (dyadic) iterated prisoner's dilemma.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair of players is matched for 1000 rounds in the main simulations.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round, players choose between two discrete actions: cooperate (C) or defect (D).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reinforcement (reward) is central to the model; payoffs are interpreted as appetitive stimuli (reward).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The number of rounds (1000) is fixed and known in the simulation setup.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model receives as input the opponent's previous move (C or D), which is sufficient summary information for the IPD.

## Important Limitations
- The paper does not study public goods games or group interactions beyond dyadic (two-player) IPD.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled; the focus is on learning from reinforcement (reward) only.
- Findings are not directly transferable to the effect of enabling or disabling punishment in public goods games.
- All results are from theoretical and computational models, not empirical experiments with human or animal subjects.
- Efficiency is measured as average score in the IPD, which is not directly equivalent to group efficiency in public goods games.
