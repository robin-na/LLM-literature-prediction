# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Detrimental effects of sanctions on human altruism
Authors: Fehr, E., & Rockenbach, B.
Journal: Nature
Year: 2003

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- investor's payoff
- trustee's payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- actual back-transfer
- actual back-transfer as a percentage of tripled investment
- actual back-transfer as a percentage of desired back-transfer
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount of money (back-transfer) sent by the trustee, which is a behavioral outcome. Payoff outcomes for both roles are reported in Table 1, but efficiency (as group payoff relative to maximum) is not directly analyzed. The focus is on how sanctions affect altruistic cooperation, measured by back-transfer, and on the payoffs to each role.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a one-shot sequential trust game, allowing the investor to impose a fine on the trustee for insufficient back-transfer leads to lower back-transfers from trustees and lower payoffs for investors, compared to both a baseline with no sanctions and to cases where the investor refrains from using the fine. The negative effect of sanctions is especially pronounced when the investor's desired back-transfer is high (i.e., when the sanction is used to enforce an unfair distribution). The paper interprets this as evidence that sanctions perceived as self-interested or unfair undermine altruistic cooperation, and thus can reduce overall efficiency or group payoff.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a sequential trust game (not a standard PGG), enabling punishment (sanctions) can reduce efficiency-related outcomes (investor payoff, group surplus) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is perceived as self-interested or unfair. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent: in trust games with one-shot interactions and potential for perceived unfairness, punishment may reduce efficiency. The findings are less directly transferable to standard PGGs, but highlight the importance of the perceived legitimacy and purpose of punishment as a moderator.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played by a pair: 2 players (investor and trustee).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each pair plays only once.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players; all decisions are anonymous and made in isolation.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (transfer) and back-transfer are continuous choices between 0 and 10 (or 0 and 30) MUs, not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The investor's transfer is tripled (multiplier = 3), but this is a trust game, not a standard PGG. The marginal per-capita return is not directly defined, but the tripling is analogous.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (fine) costs the trustee 4 MUs if imposed; it does not cost the punisher (investor) anything directly.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction of 4 MUs from the trustee's payoff per use; no direct cost to the punisher.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; no repeated interactions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Trustee knows whether the investor has chosen to impose a fine; the punisher is always the investor in the pair, so identity is known within the pair.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a sequential trust game, not a standard public goods game, so findings may not generalize directly to PGGs.
- Efficiency is not directly measured as group payoff relative to the fully cooperative outcome; only individual payoffs are reported.
- Punishment is costless to the punisher and fixed in size, which differs from many PGG punishment designs.
- All interactions are one-shot and anonymous, so repeated game effects and reputation are excluded.
- The negative effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on the perceived fairness or legitimacy of the sanction, which is context-specific.
