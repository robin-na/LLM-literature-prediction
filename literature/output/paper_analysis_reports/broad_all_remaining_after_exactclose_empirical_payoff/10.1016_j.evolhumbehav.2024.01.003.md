# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The role of costly commitment signals in assorting cooperators during intergroup conflict
Authors: Lang, M. R., Chvaja, R., & Purzycki, B. G.
Journal: Evolution and Human Behavior
Year: 2024

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=adjacent; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- total group pool generated
- probability of winning intergroup competition
- individual earnings
- variance in team earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- probability of contributing all or nothing
- probability of sacrificing to harm outgroup
- group selection (revealed/concealed)
- strategy classification (cooperative/selfish)

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings, group pool size, and probability of winning, all of which are efficiency/payoff outcomes. Behavioral outcomes (contribution, sacrifice, group choice) are also reported and analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper demonstrates that costly commitment signals can facilitate the assortment of cooperators in public-goods-game-like environments with intergroup competition, leading to higher group efficiency and greater probability of winning. However, the relationship between signal cost and efficiency is not monotonic: low-cost signals are generally more effective at producing efficient, cooperative groups, except when the cost is directly subtracted from the investable endowment during intergroup conflict, in which case high-cost signals can create highly efficient, winning groups. Forced signaling (where participants are assigned to signal or not) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff. The study does not include a punishment phase or peer-sanctioning mechanism, so its direct relevance to punishment is limited, but the costly signal serves as a commitment device that can be interpreted as a form of self-imposed cost or pre-commitment. The findings suggest that the design of commitment mechanisms (including their cost structure and voluntariness) is crucial for predicting their effect on group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of costly commitment mechanisms (but not peer punishment) on group efficiency in public-goods-game-like settings with intergroup competition. It shows that voluntary, low-cost signals are generally more effective at producing efficient groups, except when the cost is directly subtracted from the investable endowment, in which case high-cost signals can be effective. Forced (non-voluntary) signaling does not reliably increase efficiency. The findings suggest that the effect of commitment mechanisms on efficiency is sensitive to the cost structure, voluntariness, and whether the cost reduces investable resources. However, because the study does not include peer punishment or sanctions, its direct applicability to standard punishment treatments is limited. For prediction tasks involving commitment devices or entry costs, the results are directly relevant; for peer punishment, the transfer value is more indirect.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All studies use groups of 4 players for the PGG phase.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are one-shot (single round) PGGs.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: participants can allocate any amount from their endowment in 1 cent increments.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The sum in the public pool is doubled and then equally redistributed among the four players (MPCR = 0.5).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the game is one-shot; the number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are not shown others' contributions before making their own, but after the game, earnings are calculated and paid. No explicit summary feedback is described.

## Important Limitations
- The study does not include peer punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; the costly signal is a self-imposed entry cost, not a punishment assigned by others.
- The public goods game is one-shot, not repeated, which may limit generalizability to iterated settings.
- The effect of costly signals on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost structure and whether the cost is subtracted from investable resources.
- Forced (non-voluntary) signaling does not reliably increase efficiency, limiting the applicability of effort-justification or hazing analogies.
- The artificial nature of the intergroup conflict and relatively low stakes may not fully capture real-world dynamics.
- The US general population sample may not generalize to other cultural or institutional contexts.
- No chat or communication is allowed, which may affect the external validity for real-world group coordination.
