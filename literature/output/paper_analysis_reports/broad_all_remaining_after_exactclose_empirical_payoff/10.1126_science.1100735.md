# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: The neural basis of altruistic punishment
Authors: de Quervain, D. J. F., Fischbacher, U., Treyer, V., Schelthammer, M., Schnyder, U., Buck, A., & Fehr, E.
Journal: Science
Year: 2004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount of money spent on punishment
- payoff reduction imposed on defectors
Non-payoff outcomes:
- neural activation (dorsal striatum/caudate nucleus, thalamus, prefrontal cortex)
- desire to punish (self-report)
- perceived unfairness (self-report)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are neural activation patterns, self-reported desire to punish, and actual punishment behavior (money spent to punish). Group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare are not directly analyzed. Payoff reduction to defectors is measured, but not overall group efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that when participants are given the opportunity to punish defectors in a trust game, they frequently do so, even at a personal cost. Effective (costly) punishment, as opposed to symbolic (non-impactful) punishment, is associated with increased activation in the dorsal striatum (caudate nucleus), a brain region implicated in reward processing. The amount of money spent on punishment correlates with the level of caudate activation, suggesting that anticipated satisfaction motivates punishment. The costliness of punishment reduces the amount of punishment compared to costless punishment, but does not eliminate it. The study does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, but does show that punishment is used and is psychologically rewarding.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in trust-game-like environments, individuals are willing to pay to punish defectors, and that this behavior is motivated by anticipated satisfaction. However, the study does not report on group efficiency or total welfare, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it uses a trust game with a punishment stage. The findings support the idea that costly punishment will be used when available, but do not provide direct evidence about whether this increases or decreases group efficiency. The costliness of punishment reduces its frequency/intensity, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency effects in similar designs.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game involves two players: Player A (trustor/punisher) and Player B (trustee/defector).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject in the role of A played seven sequential rounds, each with a different B.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Players interacted anonymously; no mention of chat or communication between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decision by A is all-or-nothing (send all 10 MUs or keep all). B's return decision is also all-or-nothing (send back half or nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. If A sends 10 MUs, the amount is quadrupled to 40 MUs for B. The marginal per-capita return for A is 0 if B keeps all, or 1 if B returns half. The structure is not a standard linear PGG, but a trust game with a multiplier.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In the 'intentional and costly' (IC) and 'nonintentional and costly' (NC) conditions, each punishment point costs A 1 MU and reduces B's payoff by 2 MUs. In 'intentional and free' (IF), punishment is costless for A. Costliness reduces the amount of punishment imposed.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: each punishment point reduces B's payoff by 2 MUs (IC, IF, NC). In symbolic punishment (IS), punishment has no effect on B's payoff.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Subjects know they will play seven rounds, each with a different B.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Player A knows the identity of B in the sense of the experimental role, but all interactions are anonymous; B is informed of punishment, but not the identity of A beyond the game structure.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a trust game with punishment, not a standard public goods game; results may not generalize directly to PGGs.
- The main outcomes are neural activation and punishment behavior, not group efficiency or total welfare.
- Small sample size (15 subjects, 14 analyzed).
- No analysis of long-term effects or repeated interactions with the same partner.
- No reward mechanism is present, so no evidence on reward-punishment interactions.
