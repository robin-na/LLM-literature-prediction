The study does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in the evolutionary ultimatum game on networks, there is a critical value of the population's selfishness degree (the share of the resource claimed by the proposer) above which a fraction of players become pure costly punishers (always reject offers). This critical value is analytically predicted by the inverse of the largest Laplacian eigenvalue of the network. The emergence of costly punishment is thus determined by network topology. The study also shows that pure punishers can have a higher average payoff ratio than others, especially as selfishness increases, but group-level efficiency or welfare is not directly analyzed. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and numerical simulations on various network types (scale-free, small-world, real-world collaboration networks).

## Prediction Guidance
This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants, as it studies the ultimatum game (an adjacent but distinct paradigm) and focuses on the emergence of costly punishment rather than on group efficiency or total payoff. The main outcome is the fraction of pure punishers as a function of network structure and selfishness degree. While the paper provides insight into how network topology affects the threshold for costly punishment, it does not provide data or analysis on how punishment affects group efficiency or welfare in a public-goods-like environment. Thus, its value for the downstream prediction task is limited to theoretical context and mechanism discussion.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Simulations use large networks (e.g., 10,000 players for BA and WS networks, 9,842 for the science collaboration network). Player count is a parameter in the network models.
- number of rounds: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. The evolutionary process is iterated until a stationary state is reached, but the number of rounds is not specified as a fixed parameter; rather, the process continues until convergence.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The accepting strategy is binary (accept or reject), but the offer (proposer's claim) is continuous. Thus, the response is all-or-nothing, but the contribution (offer) is not.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is costly in the sense that rejection results in zero payoff for both players. The cost is implicit in the structure of the ultimatum game.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the fact that rejection results in both players receiving nothing. The impact per unit cost is not parameterized but is inherent in the game structure.

## Important Limitations
- The study focuses on the ultimatum game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to the public goods game; findings may not transfer directly.
- The main outcome is the emergence of costly punishment (fraction of pure punishers), not group efficiency or total welfare.
- Payoff-related outcomes are reported only for subgroups (punishers vs. others), not as overall group efficiency.
- Theoretical and simulation-based; no empirical or experimental data from human subjects.
- No analysis of how punishment affects efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
