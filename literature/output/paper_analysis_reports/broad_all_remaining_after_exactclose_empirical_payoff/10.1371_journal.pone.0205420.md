# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Punishing liars-How monitoring affects honesty and trust
Authors: Behnk, S., Barreda-Tarrazona, I., & García-Gallego, A.
Journal: Plos One
Year: 2018

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- rate of honest messages (truth-telling)
- rate of deceptive messages
- rate of payoff-equalizing messages
- receiver trust (acceptance rate of messages)
- punishment enforcement rate
- beliefs about others' behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are behavioral: honesty (truth-telling), deception, trust (acceptance), and punishment behavior. No direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare is reported. Payoff consequences are discussed in the context of individual sender/receiver payoffs, but not as aggregate efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that a severe, cost-free punishment mechanism for dishonesty in a sender-receiver game leads to a large increase in honest messages and trust by receivers, compared to a baseline with no punishment. This effect persists even when the probability of detecting dishonesty is reduced to 10%, except at a moderate detection probability (25%), where honesty is lower. The deterrence effect of punishment is less pronounced when the sender's potential gain from deception is high. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, but the behavioral results indicate that punishment can sustain high levels of honesty and trust with minimal monitoring.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a principal-agent (sender-receiver) environment with asymmetric information, the availability of severe, cost-free punishment for dishonesty can dramatically increase honest behavior and trust, even with low monitoring (detection) probabilities. However, the game is not a public goods game or a direct variant, and efficiency or group payoff is not measured. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in PGG-like environments, the results suggest that punishment mechanisms with high severity and low cost may have strong positive effects on pro-social behavior, but the transfer to efficiency outcomes in PGGs is indirect. The findings are most relevant for settings where the main concern is honesty/truth-telling under asymmetric information, rather than contribution to a public good.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game is played in pairs (2 players: sender and receiver).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each subject plays only one round as sender or receiver.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication is limited to a structured message from sender to receiver; no free-form chat or discussion.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sender chooses one of three discrete messages; not a continuous contribution.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is cost-free for the enforcer (receiver).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is severe: reduces sender's payoff to 2 euros, which is lower than any other possible payoff.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is one-shot; number of rounds is clear.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a sender-receiver (principal-agent) game, not a public goods game or direct variant; results may not generalize to PGG settings.
- No direct measurement or reporting of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes; only behavioral outcomes (honesty, trust, punishment) are analyzed.
- Punishment is cost-free and severe, which may not reflect real-world or PGG-like punishment mechanisms.
- One-shot game design; no repeated interactions or reputation effects.
- Small group size (pairs only); effects in larger groups are not tested.
- Low rates of actual punishment enforcement, possibly due to psychological costs, may limit external validity.
