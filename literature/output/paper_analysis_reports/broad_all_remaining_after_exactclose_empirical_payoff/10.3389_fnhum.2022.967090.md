## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper argues that the human tendency to punish non-cooperators, even at a personal cost, is rooted in evolutionary and neural mechanisms. It reviews evidence from public goods games and other economic experiments showing that punishment can increase cooperation and, in some cases, average group profit. The author discusses the adaptive value of punishment, the neural reward mechanisms involved, and the importance of institutionalizing punishment to maintain social order and cooperation. The paper also highlights complexities and limitations, such as the costliness of punishment, the potential for retaliation, and the need for centralized enforcement in large societies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical and review-based support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation in public goods games and, under some conditions, improve group outcomes. It references empirical studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, Gächter et al., 2008) that find punishment can increase average profit, but also notes limitations and boundary conditions (e.g., costliness, retaliation, institutional context). For the prediction task, this paper supports the general mechanism by which punishment can improve efficiency, but does not provide direct quantitative evidence or dimension-specific effect sizes. It is most useful for contextualizing why and when punishment might be effective, rather than for precise prediction.
