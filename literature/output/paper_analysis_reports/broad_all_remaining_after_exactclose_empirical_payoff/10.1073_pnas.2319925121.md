## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the structure of the strategy set and the microeconomic assumptions in evolutionary models of cooperation (including public goods games with punishment) can have a large impact on the predicted prevalence of cooperation. For example, allowing for antisocial punishment or for loners to be punished can lead to the collapse of cooperation, while more restrictive strategy sets can support high levels of cooperation. The paper emphasizes that these modeling choices can bias results and that more systematic, unbiased inclusion of strategies is needed for robust predictions. However, the paper does not report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on the abundance of cooperation and the evolutionary stability of different strategies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides important theoretical caution for interpreting the effects of punishment in public goods games: the predicted effect of punishment on cooperation (and by extension, efficiency) can depend strongly on the details of the strategy set and microeconomic assumptions. If a model or experiment restricts who can punish or be punished, or omits antisocial punishment, it may overestimate the positive effect of punishment on cooperation. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not provide direct quantitative support for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of modeling assumptions and the potential for qualitative shifts in cooperation prevalence, which may translate to efficiency in some settings.

## Important Limitations
- The paper is theoretical and does not report empirical or experimental data.
- Efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are not directly analyzed or reported; only cooperation prevalence and strategy abundance are shown.
- The models are highly abstract and may not capture all features of real-world public goods games or experiments.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly inferred from the reported outcomes.
- Parameter sweeps (e.g., for punishment cost, effectiveness, or MPCR) are not systematically explored in relation to efficiency.
