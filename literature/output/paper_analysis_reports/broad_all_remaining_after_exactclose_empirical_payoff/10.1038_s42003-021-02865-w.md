## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The model demonstrates that introducing a costly institution (with a participation fee) alongside a free institution in a public goods game can increase cooperation and group efficiency. For low enhancement factors (low profitability), only the costly institution supports cooperation, and average payoffs remain close to the entrance cost (i.e., not much higher than the baseline). For higher enhancement factors, cooperation emerges in both institutions, and group payoffs increase above zero. The costly institution is more likely to be populated by cooperators, as defectors avoid the entrance cost unless there are enough cooperators to exploit. The model also shows that the effect of the entrance cost is context-dependent: it is beneficial for cooperation and efficiency at low profitability, but can be detrimental at high profitability if the cost is too high, as it reduces participation. The results hold in both well-mixed and structured populations, though spatial structure removes some bistability and further supports cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that introducing a costly institution (entrance fee) in a public goods game can increase efficiency and group payoff, especially when the baseline profitability (mpcr/enhancement factor) is low. The entrance cost acts as a filter, keeping defectors out and allowing cooperators to achieve higher payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly institution (or, by analogy, a punishment mechanism with a cost to participate) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such cost, particularly when the marginal per-capita return is low. However, if the cost is too high and the game is already highly profitable, the effect may reverse. The model does not include explicit punishment, but the costly institution's effect is adjacent to punishment in that it deters free-riding through a cost barrier. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where institutional participation costs or costly precommitment mechanisms are present, rather than direct peer punishment.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The model is iterated over many time steps (generations), but there is no explicit finite round structure; the dynamics are evolutionary and ongoing.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: individuals choose to cooperate (contribute c=1) or defect (contribute 0).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Enhancement factor r (mpcr) is systematically varied. Higher r increases the likelihood of cooperation and efficiency, especially in the presence of a costly institution.

## Important Limitations
- The model is theoretical and does not include empirical or experimental data.
- No explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled; the costly institution acts as a filter rather than a reactive punishment.
- The model assumes binary contribution (cooperate/defect) and does not consider continuous contribution levels.
- No communication, reputation, or information-sharing mechanisms are included.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with explicit peer punishment, rewards, or more complex institutional designs.
- The evolutionary dynamics assume infinite or large populations and may not capture small-group idiosyncrasies.
