# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Inherited Institutions: Cooperation in the Light of Democratic Legitimacy
Authors: Langenbach, P., & Tausch, F.
Journal: Journal of Law Economics & Organization
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public good
- beliefs about others' contributions
- voting behavior
- satisfaction with procedure
- perceived legitimacy
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the amount contributed to the public good. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as a share of the social optimum is reported. Payoff is discussed only as a function of the game, not as an outcome variable.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment uses a one-shot, three-player linear public goods game with or without a centralized, non-deterrent punishment (automatic fine for less-than-full contribution). The institutional setting (punishment or not) is determined either by majority vote (Endo) or random draw (Exo) in the first generation, and this setting is inherited by a matched group in the second generation. In the first generation, contributions are higher when punishment is democratically adopted and lower when it is democratically rejected, compared to random assignment. In the second generation, the positive effect of democratic adoption disappears, but the negative effect of democratic rejection persists (marginally). The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or total earnings as a share of the social optimum, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized, non-deterrent punishment mechanism can increase contributions if it is democratically adopted, but only in the generation that participates in the decision. The effect does not persist to subsequent generations who inherit the institution. The negative effect of democratic rejection may persist. However, since the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, only contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is informative for mapping design to expected contribution behavior, but not for direct efficiency prediction. If contribution is used as a proxy for efficiency (assuming linearity and no punishment cost to the group), the results suggest that democratic adoption of punishment increases efficiency in the first generation, but not in the second.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 3 players per public goods game, fixed across all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. One-shot game; each group plays a single round.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 20 points; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.5 (each point contributed returns 0.5 to each of 3 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is automatic: 4 points deducted from the punished player for under-contribution; no cost to any punisher (centralized, not peer).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is a fixed deduction of 4 points per under-contributor; no cost to others; non-deterrent (not enough to make full contribution payoff-maximizing).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism exists; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; number of rounds is clear.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are not informed about others' contributions before making their own decisions. After the game, they are asked to guess others' contributions for incentivized beliefs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is automatic and centralized; no identification of a punisher is possible or relevant.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff; no direct efficiency or welfare analysis is reported.
- Punishment is centralized and automatic, not peer-to-peer; results may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- The sanction is non-deterrent by design; effects may differ with stronger punishment.
- The experiment is one-shot; repeated game effects are not studied.
- No information about social history (previous generation's contributions) is provided to the second generation; only procedural history is manipulated.
- Generations are fully separated; no overlapping generations or adaptation to observed behavior.
- External validity is limited due to laboratory abstraction and artificial institutional design.
