## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a social penalty (punishment) for non-cooperative actions in already-cooperative societies universally increases cooperation and group welfare in theoretical models of the hawk-dove and prisoner's dilemma games. The penalty reduces the payoff of non-cooperators, shifting the equilibrium toward more cooperation. The effect is robust to the size of the penalty: larger penalties can lead to full cooperation. The results are generalized to non-cooperative games, with the claim that social penalties always promote cooperation and never reduce it. The paper also discusses the implications for real-world legal systems and public goods provision, but does not model or simulate public goods games directly.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that introducing or increasing punishment for non-cooperation will increase group efficiency and average payoff in non-cooperative games, including those adjacent to public goods games. The results are derived analytically and show that the effect of punishment is always non-negative for cooperation and group welfare. However, the models are not public goods games per se, and do not address multi-player, repeated, or more complex institutional features. The findings are most directly applicable to settings where punishment is externally imposed and not subject to strategic choice by players. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially as punishment becomes more severe or certain.

## Important Limitations
- Theoretical models are limited to two-player, one-shot games (hawk-dove and prisoner's dilemma); public goods games are only discussed, not modeled.
- Punishment is imposed by an external authority (society), not by players; does not address strategic punishment or second-order free rider problems.
- No empirical or experimental data are presented.
- No analysis of repeated games, group size effects, or multi-player interactions.
- No modeling of reward mechanisms or their interaction with punishment.
- Assumes penalty is always enforceable and does not consider costs to the punisher or enforcement limitations.
