# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: FREE-RIDING ON PRODUCT QUALITY IN COOPERATIVES: LESSONS FROM AN EXPERIMENT
Authors: Bonroy, O., Garapin, A., Hamilton, S. F., & Monteiro, D. M. S.
Journal: American Journal of Agricultural Economics
Year: 2019

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=adjacent

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: non_payoff_behavior
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of free-riding (shirking/action B)
- frequency of cooperation (action A)
- propensity to shirk
- proportion of players choosing A or B
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the frequency of free-riding (shirking), i.e., the proportion of players choosing the low-effort action B. The paper does not directly report group efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare, but the structure of the game means that higher cooperation (action A) would increase group payoff. However, all reported results and statistical analyses are on behavioral outcomes (choices), not on realized payoffs or efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that increasing the probability of exclusion (punishment) significantly reduces free-riding (shirking) in larger groups, and that increasing the base payoff (independent of collective returns) also reduces free-riding. In small groups, free-riding is low regardless of punishment. The main outcome is the frequency of free-riding, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that punishment (exclusion) and higher base payoffs can facilitate cooperation, but the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect on efficiency must be inferred.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that punishment (in the form of exclusion) reduces free-riding in public-goods-like environments, especially in larger groups. However, because the main outcome is behavioral (frequency of free-riding) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be mapped to efficiency outcomes indirectly. The experimental design and manipulation of group size, punishment probability, and base payoff are informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data is a limitation. The results support the prediction that enabling punishment will increase efficiency in larger groups, especially when the probability of punishment is substantial.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Player count is manipulated between 4 and 8. Larger groups have higher free-riding (lower cooperation), so efficiency would be lower in larger groups absent punishment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds is fixed at 20 in all treatments. All subjects know the number of rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is binary: each player chooses either high effort (A) or low effort (B).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is determined by the parameter a=10 and group size N. The collective rent is divided among all members, so mpcr = a/N.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is exclusion from the group rent if detected. The cost to the punisher is not explicit; punishment is implemented as a probabilistic social sanction, not peer punishment.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the probability of exclusion (theta). Higher theta (punishment probability) reduces free-riding.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the exact number of rounds (20) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed of the frequency of A choices in their group (including their own), their own payoff, and their cumulative payoff. No individual identities are revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The main outcome is behavioral (frequency of free-riding), not group efficiency or total payoff. Efficiency effects must be inferred indirectly.
- No direct measurement or reporting of group efficiency, welfare, or total group payoff.
- Punishment is implemented as probabilistic exclusion by the experimenter, not as peer punishment or monetary deduction.
- The setting is a lab experiment with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about reward effects.
