## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that the risk inherent in social exchange relations (mutual dependence) can be reduced by structuring transactions as negotiated (joint decision, binding agreements) or as serially dependent (allowing for contingent action and trust development). Negotiated transactions most directly transform dependence into interdependence, reducing risk and making outcomes more predictable. Serially dependent transactions (such as repeated reciprocal exchanges) also reduce risk, but less so, and rely more on emergent trust and norms. Generalized exchange (indirect reciprocity) is riskier and less amenable to these risk-reducing mechanisms. The paper suggests that these structural features influence the likelihood of stable cooperation, trust, and solidarity, but does not provide empirical evidence or direct predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides theoretical context for how structural features of social exchange (e.g., repeated interaction, negotiation, direct vs. generalized exchange) may moderate the risk of defection and the emergence of cooperation. While it does not address public goods games or punishment directly, it suggests that mechanisms which increase interdependence (such as repeated play or enforceable agreements) should improve group outcomes and stability. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that game designs with more interdependence, repeated interaction, or enforceable agreements are likely to see higher efficiency or more stable cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence. The relevance to punishment is indirect: punishment is mentioned as a possible influence tactic in negotiation, but not as a central mechanism.
