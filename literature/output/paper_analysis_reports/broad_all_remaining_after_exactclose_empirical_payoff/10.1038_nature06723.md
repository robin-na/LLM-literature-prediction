# Analysis Report

## Source
Title: Winners don't punish
Authors: Dreber, A., Rand, D. G., Fudenberg, D., & Nowak, M. A.
Journal: Nature
Year: 2008

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=adjacent; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per round
- total payoff
- group average payoff
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of cooperation
- frequency of defection
- frequency of punishment
- conditional strategies (e.g., tit-for-tat, punishment after defection)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is average payoff (efficiency proxy), with secondary analysis of cooperation, defection, and punishment rates. The paper explicitly compares average payoffs between punishment and no-punishment conditions.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing a costly punishment option in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma increases the frequency of cooperation but does not increase the average payoff of the group. In both parameterizations (benefit/cost ratios of 2 and 3), there is no significant difference in average payoff between the control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment allowed) conditions. Individuals who use punishment more frequently have lower total payoffs, and the most successful players do not use punishment at all. The results suggest that, in repeated dyadic cooperation games, punishment is maladaptive from an efficiency perspective.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated dyadic cooperation games with direct reciprocity, enabling costly punishment does not improve group efficiency compared to a control without punishment. The cost of punishment cancels out the gains from increased cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar repeated dyadic games, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be similar to or lower than the control efficiency, even if cooperation rates rise. The findings are most directly applicable to repeated Prisoner's Dilemma designs, but the mechanism (costs of punishment offsetting cooperation gains) is likely relevant for other repeated social dilemma games with similar structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are dyadic (2 players per game).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pairwise interaction lasted between 1 and 9 rounds, with continuation probability 0.75 per round (randomly determined length, unknown to subjects).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; interactions were anonymous and via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Choices are discrete: cooperate, defect, or punish (no continuous contribution).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Equivalent to benefit/cost ratio: b/c = 2 (C1/T1) or b/c = 3 (C2/T2). In each round, cooperation means paying 1 for the other to get 2 or 3.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 unit to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 unit cost causes 4 unit loss to target (4:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players did not know the exact number of rounds; continuation probability was 0.75 per round (random end).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects saw the other person's choice and both payoff scores.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is always direct and attributable, as the game is dyadic and all actions are revealed after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a repeated dyadic Prisoner's Dilemma, not a multi-person public goods game; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- The punishment option is always available as a third choice, not as a separate stage after contribution decisions as in standard PGG experiments.
- No reward mechanism is present, so findings do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The number of rounds per interaction is random and not known to participants, which may affect strategic behavior.
- The study does not test parameterizations with lower or higher punishment effectiveness or cost, nor does it explore longer time horizons.
