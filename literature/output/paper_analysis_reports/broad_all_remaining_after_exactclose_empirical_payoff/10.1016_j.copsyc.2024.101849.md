## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that group changes, such as the entry of newcomers, tend to reduce cooperation in the short term because newcomers contribute less than incumbents. Over time, newcomers adapt to the incumbents' norms and increase their cooperation. Punishment mechanisms can induce newcomers to conform to group norms, but the review does not report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The evidence is based on both observational data from an online game (Ikariam) and lab experiments with public goods games and peer punishment. The review emphasizes the need for more causal and longitudinal research, especially on the role of normative differences and the potential for two-way adaptation between newcomers and incumbents.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by summarizing how group composition changes and punishment mechanisms affect cooperation and norm conformity in public goods game-like environments. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency is limited. The review suggests that punishment can help align newcomer behavior with group norms, which may support higher cooperation, but the lack of payoff data means that efficiency effects must be inferred indirectly from behavioral outcomes. The paper is most useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment and group dynamics interact, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The review does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or other direct payoff-based outcomes; it focuses on behavioral outcomes such as contribution and cooperation.
- Most evidence discussed is from observational or experimental studies with limited causal identification, especially regarding the mechanisms underlying norm adaptation.
- Key game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, number of rounds) are not systematically reported or analyzed.
- The review highlights the lack of large-scale, longitudinal, and causal studies on group change and cooperation.
