## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (B), where punishment is possible but cannot be avenged (single punishment stage, random IDs each round); (2) Short Feuds (SF), where multiple punishment stages are possible within a round but IDs are randomized each round (so feuds cannot span rounds); and (3) Long Feuds (LF), where multiple punishment stages are possible and IDs are fixed, so feuds can span rounds. The study finds that as the threat of feuds increases (from B to SF to LF), the frequency of altruistic punishment decreases, and so does the threat of punishment for free-riding. In LF, where feuds are most possible, both cooperation and group earnings decline over time relative to the baseline. Severe punishment is used more in SF to preempt counter-punishment, but in LF, punishment is rare and less targeted at free riders. Overall, the possibility of feuds undermines the efficiency gains from punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether punishment can be avenged (i.e., whether feuds are possible). In standard designs where punishment is one-shot and anonymous, punishment can increase or maintain efficiency. However, when punishment can be countered and feuds can develop (especially across rounds), the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is greatly reduced or even reversed, as both cooperation and group earnings decline. For prediction, the presence of counter-punishment opportunities and the ability to identify punishers are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher than with punishment when feuds are possible, due to the breakdown of cooperation and increased costs from feuding.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per session in all treatments.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No communication or chat between players is mentioned or allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose how much (0-20 ECU) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4 (contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing costs 1 ECU per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 2 ECU per 1 ECU spent.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is repeated 10 times (10 periods).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown each group member's contribution and, in further punishment stages, the number of punishment points assigned by each player to each other player.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Players can identify who punished whom in all treatments; in LF, fixed IDs allow for counter-punishment and feuds, which reduces the positive effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability to real-world environments.
- The time horizon is limited to 10 periods, which may underestimate the long-run negative effects of feuds on cooperation and efficiency.
- No reward mechanism is included, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- The design does not vary group size or MPCR, so findings may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- The study focuses on environments where punishment is observable and can be countered; results may differ if punishment is anonymous or unobservable.
