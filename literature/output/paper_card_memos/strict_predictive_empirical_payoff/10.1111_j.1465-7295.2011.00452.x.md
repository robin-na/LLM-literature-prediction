## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a linear public goods game with costly punishment, adding a stage where players can issue non-binding threats to punish (conditioned on others' contribution levels) increases average contributions and, in the latter periods, increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to a baseline with punishment but no threats. However, the cost of punishment initially offsets the benefit of higher contributions, so efficiency only improves in later rounds. If a second-order punishment stage is added (where players can punish those who fail to carry out their threats), threats become less severe, contributions and efficiency fall, and welfare is equal to or below the baseline. The effect of threats is robust to changes in punishment effectiveness, but the efficiency gain is less persistent when punishment is less effective. Overall, threats can improve efficiency, but only under certain conditions and mainly in longer interactions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with punishment, adding a non-binding threat stage can increase efficiency (group payoff) relative to a baseline with punishment but no threats, but only in the latter periods of repeated play. The efficiency gain is modest and depends on the absence of second-order punishment (punishing those who fail to carry out threats). If second-order punishment is possible, efficiency falls to or below baseline. The effect is also sensitive to punishment effectiveness (punishment technology). For prediction, this suggests that enabling threats (structured, non-binding, and observable) can increase efficiency above the control (punishment-only) baseline, but only in longer games and only if threats are not themselves subject to enforcement. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (higher punishment technology). The control efficiency (punishment-only) is a good lower bound; threats can improve on this, but only under the right design conditions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. 20 periods per session; efficiency gains from threats only emerge in later periods.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous from 0 to 20 ECU; not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Two conditions: High Effectiveness (HE) - 1 ECU cost to punisher, 2 ECU loss to target; Low Effectiveness (LE) - 1 ECU cost to punisher, 1 ECU loss to target. Effects of threats on efficiency are stronger in HE than LE.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology (effectiveness) is varied: HE (1:2) and LE (1:1). Efficiency gains from threats are more persistent in HE.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players see the total group contribution and the individual contributions of each other group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency gains from threats only emerge in the latter periods of repeated play; in short games, threats do not improve efficiency.
- The effect of threats is eliminated or reversed if second-order punishment (punishing those who fail to carry out threats) is possible.
- The experiment uses a structured, non-binding threat schedule rather than free-form communication or chat; results may not generalize to richer communication environments.
- Punishment is always anonymous; effects may differ if punishment is identifiable.
- The study uses a fixed group size (4 players) and a specific mpcr (0.4); results may not generalize to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- No reward or positive sanctioning mechanism is present; results may differ if rewards are possible.
- The time horizon is limited (20 rounds); longer or shorter games may yield different results.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects; external validity to field or real-world settings is uncertain.
