# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.leaqua.2023.101695

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=close; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group efficiency (contributions relative to social optimum)
- total group contributions
- leader and follower payoffs
- payoff ratios (leader vs. simultaneous game)
- Gini index (inequality in payoffs/contributions)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rates (leader and followers)
- conditional cooperation
- beliefs about others' contributions
- willingness to lead
- leader selection and motivation
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable in the meta-analysis and regressions is group efficiency (average group contributions relative to the social optimum). The paper also reports on leader/follower payoffs, contribution ratios, and inequality, as well as behavioral outcomes like conditional cooperation and beliefs.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper synthesizes 62 experimental studies (mainly linear public goods games) and conducts meta-analyses and weighted least squares regressions. It finds that sequential play (leadership) increases group efficiency compared to simultaneous play. Centralized punishment and reward (where only the leader can punish or reward) further increase efficiency, with punishment having a stronger effect than reward. Peer punishment/reward (where all can punish/reward) have even larger effects. Rotating leadership reduces efficiency. The marginal per capita return (MPCR) and group size also positively affect efficiency, but to a lesser extent. Leaders contribute more than followers, and followers contribute about 79% as much as leaders. Inequality within groups does not generally increase with leadership. Leaders' payoffs are similar to those of average players in simultaneous games. The paper provides quantitative estimates of effect sizes for each moderator.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on game design dimensions. The meta-regressions include variables for group size, number of rounds, MPCR, presence and type of punishment/reward, and leadership structure. The results show that adding centralized punishment (by a leader) increases efficiency by about 0.26 (on a 0-1 scale of contributions relative to optimum), and peer punishment increases it by about 0.47. The effect of reward is positive but smaller. Rotating leadership reduces efficiency. These findings can be used to adjust predicted efficiency when punishment is enabled, given the control efficiency and game design. The paper is highly relevant for the downstream prediction task.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Group size (player count) is included as a variable in the meta-regressions. The mean group size is about 4, ranging from 3 to 8. The effect of group size on efficiency is positive but small and often not significant.
- number of rounds: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `unclear` under this dimension. Number of rounds is reported (mean ~14, range 4-20) and included as a control in some regressions, but its effect on efficiency is not a main focus and is generally not significant.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. The main focus is on linear public goods games with continuous contribution choices. Binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games are excluded from the main meta-analysis.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR (marginal per capita return) is a key variable, manipulated across studies (mean ~0.48, range 0.33-0.83). Higher MPCR is associated with higher efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit is not systematically varied or reported in detail, but the presence of centralized punishment (leader can punish) is coded and analyzed. The effect of punishment is positive for efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is not directly measured, but the presence of centralized punishment is coded and its effect on efficiency is estimated.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Centralized reward (leader can reward) and peer reward are included as variables in the meta-regressions. Both increase efficiency, but less than punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Games are finitely repeated and the number of rounds is common knowledge in the included studies.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Players observe others' contributions in sequential games (a core feature of leading-by-example). Observability is necessary for leadership effects.

## Important Limitations
- Punishment cost and effectiveness are not systematically varied or reported in detail; only the presence/absence of punishment is coded.
- The meta-analysis focuses on lab experiments with linear public goods games and may not generalize to field settings or games with binary contributions.
- Chat or free-form communication is not included; only structured communication (e.g., leading-by-words) is considered.
- Reward cost and effectiveness are not directly measured.
- Some design features (e.g., default contribution, punishment/reward identification) are not reported or analyzed.
- Most studies use small group sizes (3-8), so results may not generalize to larger groups.
