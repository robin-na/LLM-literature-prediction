## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that introducing a punishment option in a repeated public goods game increases cooperation and group earnings in some societies, but not in others. In societies with high levels of antisocial punishment, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group earnings is diminished or absent. The variation in outcomes is strongly associated with societal-level factors such as norms of civic cooperation and the strength of the rule of law. The study demonstrates that punishment is not universally beneficial for efficiency or cooperation; its effect depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishment, which varies widely across societies.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly context-dependent. When predicting the efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, it is crucial to consider not only the game design dimensions but also the likelihood of antisocial punishment, which may be influenced by societal or group-level norms. In environments similar to those with low antisocial punishment (e.g., Western Europe, US), punishment is likely to increase efficiency. In environments with high antisocial punishment, punishment may have little or even negative effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions in this study (e.g., player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, anonymity) are held constant, so the main moderator is the social context, not the game mechanics. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment) unless antisocial punishment is low.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game was repeated for 10 rounds with fixed group composition.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed; all interactions were computer-mediated and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: each player could choose how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) was 0.4 (each token contributed yielded 0.4 tokens to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point cost the punisher 1 token and reduced the punished player's earnings by 3 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 token spent by punisher reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game would last 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants were informed about the other group members' contributions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; participants were not informed about who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated; group earnings and mean contributions are used as proxies.
- All experiments were conducted with university undergraduates, which may limit generalizability to broader populations.
- Game design dimensions were held constant across societies, so the main source of variation is social context, not game mechanics.
- The study does not manipulate or systematically vary game design parameters such as punishment cost or MPCR.
- Findings on the effect of punishment on efficiency are conditional on the prevalence of antisocial punishment, which is not directly observable from game design alone.
