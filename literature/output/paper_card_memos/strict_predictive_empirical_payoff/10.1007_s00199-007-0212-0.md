The main dependent variables are average individual earnings (welfare), group contributions, and punishment behavior. Efficiency is measured as average earnings relative to the maximum possible (full cooperation, no punishment).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the design of the punishment mechanism. In the Baseline (single-stage punishment) treatment, efficiency is high: contributions and earnings are close to the social optimum. When a second stage of punishment is added and counterpunishment is possible, contributions and welfare fall significantly. The positive effect of sanction enforcement (punishing those who fail to punish free riders) is present but smaller than the negative effect of counterpunishment. In the most extreme case, with five rounds of punishment (6SFI), both contributions and welfare are significantly lower than in the Baseline, and welfare is even lower than in the no-punishment scenario. The results show that punishment can backfire and reduce efficiency if it enables cycles of retaliation (counterpunishment) or is too unconstrained.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the details of the punishment system. If punishment is anonymous and limited to a single round, it increases efficiency. If counterpunishment is possible, or if there are multiple rounds of punishment, efficiency can decrease, sometimes below the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, the presence of counterpunishment opportunities, the number of punishment rounds, and the information structure (anonymity of punishers) are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is high in this design, and adding punishment can either increase or decrease efficiency depending on these moderators.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. 20 periods per session; number of punishment stages per period is a key moderator (1, 2, or 5). More punishment stages reduce efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players can allocate any amount from 0 to 20 ECUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECUs to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point assigned is costly to the punisher; cost function is not numerically specified but is identical across treatments.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 10% of first-stage earnings, up to 100%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know there are 20 periods per session.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown others' contributions and, depending on treatment, punishment assignments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. In some treatments (Full Information, Revenge Only, No Revenge), the identity of punishers is revealed to varying degrees. When punishers can be identified and counterpunishment is possible, efficiency decreases.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit generalizability to real-world public goods dilemmas.
- The punishment cost function is not numerically specified in the paper, only described as costly and identical across treatments.
- No treatment without any punishment is included; the Baseline is a single-stage punishment treatment, not a pure control.
- The study does not vary MPCR or group size, so findings may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is studied, so results are specific to punishment-based systems.
