## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

## Important Limitations
- Limited number of independent groups per treatment, reducing statistical power for some comparisons.
- The experimental environment (full information, fixed groups, repeated interactions) differs from many previous studies, potentially limiting generalizability to less information-rich or more anonymous settings.
- No systematic investigation of the effects of information availability or group size beyond the chosen design.
- Findings may not generalize to environments with imperfect or costly information, or where group membership is unstable.
