## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper experimentally compares two incentive mechanisms for public goods provision: the Falkinger mechanism (which rewards/penalizes deviations from average contributions) and the compensation mechanism (where players can subsidize each other's contributions). Both mechanisms increase contributions and group efficiency compared to the voluntary contribution control. The Falkinger mechanism, particularly with higher tax-subsidy rates (closer to the efficient value), leads to significantly higher and more stable efficiency gains than the compensation mechanism. The compensation mechanism initially boosts contributions, but this effect decays over time, and average efficiency remains lower than under the Falkinger mechanism. The Falkinger mechanism's performance is robust to parameter changes and predicts average contributions well, while the compensation mechanism suffers from suboptimal subsidy rates and coordination issues.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment-and-reward mechanism (Falkinger) in a two-player, repeated, continuous-contribution public goods game with full information and no communication substantially increases efficiency relative to the control. The effect is stronger and more reliable when the punishment/reward parameter is set closer to the efficient value. The compensation (reward-only) mechanism also increases efficiency, but less so, and the effect is less stable. For prediction, the paper supports a positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is well-calibrated. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed pairs, continuous contribution, no chat, and full feedback settings.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 20 rounds of control (VC) and 20 rounds of treatment (mechanism).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-50 points), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 1 (each point to public good increases both players' payoff by 1).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In the Falkinger mechanism, the 'cost' of punishing (taxing) is endogenous: deviations below average are penalized at rate β, and the mechanism is budget-balanced. The cost is not a fixed parameter but is determined by the tax-subsidy rate β.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the tax-subsidy parameter β; higher β increases the strength of punishment/reward for deviations from average. Higher β leads to higher efficiency.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Both mechanisms include rewards: Falkinger rewards above-average contributors; compensation mechanism allows direct subsidies.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the compensation mechanism, the cost to rewarder is the subsidy rate times the other’s contribution; in Falkinger, reward is budget-balanced via β.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness is determined by the subsidy rate (compensation) or β (Falkinger). Higher rates increase the incentive effect.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in each phase (20 rounds per phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are informed of the other’s contribution and their own payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the other’s actions (contributions, subsidy rates), but identities are anonymous; punishment/reward is not personalized but is mechanically determined.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use only two players per group; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- Contribution choices are continuous, not all-or-nothing; effects may differ in discrete settings.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or communication are not tested.
- The mechanisms are implemented in a specific quadratic utility environment; results may differ with other payoff structures.
- The Falkinger mechanism combines punishment and reward; pure punishment or pure reward mechanisms may behave differently.
- The compensation mechanism’s underperformance may be due to learning or coordination problems specific to the experimental design.
- The study does not test peer-to-peer punishment or reward, only mechanism-based incentives.
- Subjects are students from UK universities; external validity to other populations is untested.
