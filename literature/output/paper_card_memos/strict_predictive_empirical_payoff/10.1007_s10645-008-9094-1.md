The main focus is on contribution/cooperation, but group earnings are analyzed as a welfare proxy.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a four-person linear public good game, groups with heterogeneous productivity (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.3, average 0.6) contribute less than homogeneous groups (all MPCR 0.6) when punishment is not allowed. Introducing a costly punishment mechanism (punisher pays 1, target loses 2 per point) in heterogeneous groups significantly increases average contributions to levels similar to homogeneous groups without punishment. However, the cost of punishment means that average group earnings (welfare) are not higher with punishment: over 15 rounds, average earnings are similar across all treatments, and in early periods, punishment reduces earnings. In the final periods, punishment helps maintain earnings by countering free-riding. Punishment is mainly assigned by high-productivity agents to low-productivity agents in successful groups. High-productivity agents are more responsive to punishment when their contributions are below average.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a linear PGG with productivity heterogeneity, enabling costly punishment increases average contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, due to the cost of punishment. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to heterogeneity, enabling punishment will likely increase contributions, but the net efficiency gain may be small or absent unless punishment is cheap or highly effective. The effect of punishment on efficiency is time-dependent: in later rounds, punishment can prevent the collapse of cooperation and thus improve earnings relative to no-punishment, but over the whole game, the cost of punishment can offset these gains. The findings are most relevant for games with 4 players, 15 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, and a punishment technology of 1:2 cost:impact.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was strictly forbidden at all times.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how many of 10 tokens to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. MPCR is manipulated: T1 (all 0.6), T2/T3 (half 0.9, half 0.3, average 0.6). Heterogeneity reduces contributions without punishment; with punishment, contributions increase but efficiency does not.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's earnings by 2 tokens (1:2 cost:impact).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. All 15 rounds count toward final earnings; number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects see the contribution and earnings of every group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: subjects do not know who punished them; random IDs are reassigned each round.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as the ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff; group average earnings are used as a welfare proxy.
- The experiment uses only 4-player groups and 15 rounds; results may not generalize to larger groups or different time horizons.
- Punishment is costly and anonymous; effects may differ with different punishment technologies or if punishment is identifiable.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism is tested.
- The study focuses on productivity heterogeneity (MPCR), not endowment heterogeneity or other forms of asymmetry.
- The sample is limited to university students in a lab setting; external validity may be limited.
