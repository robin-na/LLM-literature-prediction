Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution, ostracism, group size) and payoff-related outcomes (net payoffs, aggregate returns). Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the social optimum, but net payoffs and aggregate returns are reported and compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally studies a dynamic public goods game with endogenous group formation, free migration, and treatments varying the presence of intergroup competition and ostracism (social exclusion by majority vote). The main findings are: (1) Ostracism increases average group size and contributions to the public good, resulting in higher net payoffs and aggregate returns, both with and without intergroup competition. (2) Intergroup competition alone does not increase group size or contributions, and leads to more imbalanced group structures, which reduces aggregate returns. (3) The combination of ostracism and competition still produces higher cooperation and payoffs than competition alone. (4) The threat of ostracism reduces the number of free-agents and increases the desirability of joining groups, outweighing the effect of involuntary exclusion. (5) Intergroup competition reduces the likelihood of ostracism, as groups are less willing to expel members when facing external threats.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling a social sanctioning mechanism (ostracism) in a dynamic, migration-allowed public goods environment increases both cooperation and group-level payoffs (i.e., efficiency), regardless of whether intergroup competition is present. The effect of ostracism is robust and positive for efficiency-related outcomes. Intergroup competition alone does not improve efficiency and may even reduce it by fragmenting groups. The design features—such as endogenous group formation, free migration, and ostracism by majority vote—are important moderators. The paper does not use classic costly punishment (with explicit cost per punishment point), but ostracism functions as a group-level sanction. Thus, for environments with similar group formation and migration, the presence of ostracism is a strong positive predictor for efficiency gains from sanctioning.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each society consists of 12 players, divided into groups that can merge or split.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods per session.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject can allocate any amount of their 20 MUs between private and project account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR varies with group size: from 1.1 (solo) to 0.27 (group of 12). Social optimum is full contribution in grand coalition.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the experiment lasts 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed about individual contributions in their own group and average contributions in all entities.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject has a unique ID letter known to others throughout the experiment, allowing identification of actions (including ostracism votes).

## Important Limitations
- Ostracism is not a classic costly punishment with explicit cost per unit; it is a group-level exclusion by majority vote, so findings may not generalize to individual costly punishment settings.
- Efficiency is not directly calculated as a ratio to the social optimum, but net payoffs and aggregate returns are reported.
- The experiment uses endogenous group formation and free migration, which may limit generalizability to standard fixed-group PGGs.
- Intergroup competition does not involve external monetary profits or losses, and losing groups do not incur negative earnings, which may affect the salience of competition.
- The design does not include a reward mechanism, so no evidence is provided on the interaction of rewards and punishment.
- The experiment is conducted in a finite, closed society of 12 players, which may not capture dynamics in larger or open populations.
