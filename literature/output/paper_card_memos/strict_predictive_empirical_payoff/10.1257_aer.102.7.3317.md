## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In 50-round, three-player public goods games with all-or-nothing contributions, the introduction of costly punishment options has different effects depending on monitoring and punishment severity. With perfect monitoring, both regular and strong punishment increase average net payoffs, with stronger punishment yielding higher efficiency. With imperfect (noisy) monitoring, regular punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline, while strong punishment increases cooperation but only restores efficiency to the no-punishment level (i.e., the gains from increased cooperation are offset by the costs of punishment). The relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is U-shaped under noise: intermediate punishment is worst for efficiency, while strong punishment is neutral. The paper provides direct, quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes for each treatment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, especially as a function of monitoring quality and punishment severity. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive: under imperfect monitoring, standard punishment can reduce efficiency, and only very strong punishment can (at best) restore efficiency to the no-punishment baseline. The results are directly applicable to settings with all-or-nothing contributions, 3 players, 50 rounds, and known MPCR and punishment parameters. The findings highlight the importance of monitoring quality and punishment effectiveness in predicting efficiency outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 3 players, fixed for all 50 rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions use 50 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is all-or-nothing: each player chooses to contribute all (20 tokens) or none each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contributed token benefits each group member by 0.5 tokens (total return per contributed token is 1.5, so MPCR = 0.5).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punisher pays 1 token per deduction point assigned, up to 5 points per other player per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Two punishment technologies: regular (1 cost : 3 damage, capped at earnings), strong (1 cost : 6 damage, uncapped, can go negative).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 50 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown either the true choices (no noise) or a noisy public record of each group member's contribution after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- All games use fixed groups of 3 players and all-or-nothing contributions, which may limit generalizability to larger groups or continuous contribution settings.
- The monitoring noise is limited to a 10% chance of a false 'no contribution' public record for contributors; other forms or levels of noise are not tested.
- Only punishment (not reward) is available, so results may not generalize to settings with positive incentives.
- Punishment is anonymous; effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The findings may not generalize to shorter or much longer games, or to games with changing group composition.
