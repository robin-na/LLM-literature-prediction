## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experiments use 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was forbidden.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players choose any amount between 0 and 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (multiplier 1.6, 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 Taler per reduction point, regardless of role.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: strong player (Type A) reduces target's income by 4 per point; weak player (Type B) reduces by 1 per point. Average effectiveness is 1.75 in most conditions. The paper shows that higher punishment effectiveness combined with instability leads to lower efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown others' contributions, total incomes, and punishment received/assigned after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are identified by randomly assigned numbers each round; punishment is not attributed to a persistent identity, but players know which anonymous player punished them in that round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiments are limited to groups of 4 players and 10 rounds; generalizability to other group sizes or longer games is not tested.
- The negative effect of instability is shown for the specific implementation of punishment power (one strong, three weak); other forms of inequality are not tested.
- The study does not include a no-punishment baseline; all conditions involve some form of punishment.
- The effect of personality variables is largely inconclusive.
- The findings may not generalize to real-world settings with more complex or less transparent power assignment mechanisms.
