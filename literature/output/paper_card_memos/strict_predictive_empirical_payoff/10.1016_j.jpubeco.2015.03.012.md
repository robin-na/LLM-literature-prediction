# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.jpubeco.2015.03.012

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group earnings
- efficiency (ratio of group payoff to maximum possible)
- individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment assigned
- reward assigned
- voting for institution
- cooperation rate
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, reward, voting) and payoff-based outcomes (group earnings, efficiency). Efficiency is discussed explicitly in the results and appendix tables.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Baseline: 10 periods; each of Reward, Sanction, and Vote: 7 periods each.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/withdrawal is continuous from 0 to 20 ECUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.3 (each unit contributed increases each player's payoff by 0.3).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to punisher is 1 ECU per point assigned; cost to target is 2 ECUs per point received.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs 1 ECU to assign and reduces the target's payoff by 2 ECUs (punishment effectiveness = 2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward institution is a treatment arm; in some treatments, subjects can assign costly reward points.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Cost to rewarder is 1 ECU per point assigned; benefit to recipient is 2 ECUs per point received.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each reward point costs 1 ECU to assign and increases the target's payoff by 2 ECUs (reward effectiveness = 2).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of periods in each treatment is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed of each group member's contribution.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Group member IDs are reshuffled each period; punishment/reward is anonymous and not attributable to specific individuals.

## Important Limitations
- The number of periods per treatment is limited (7 for each institution), which may underestimate the long-term efficiency gains from punishment, as the benefits of sanctions take time to develop.
- Groups are fixed for the duration of the experiment, but member IDs are reshuffled each period to prevent reputation effects; this may differ from real-world repeated interactions.
- The sample of police commissioners is mostly newly appointed or still in training; more experienced police might behave differently.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with artificial stakes and anonymity, which may limit external validity.
- The study does not vary key parameters such as MPCR, punishment cost, or group size within the experiment.
