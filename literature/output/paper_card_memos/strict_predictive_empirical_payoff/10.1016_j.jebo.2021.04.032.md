## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- earnings after contribution
- punishment costs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution
- punishment assigned
- beliefs about partner's contribution
- beliefs about being punished
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are contribution and punishment behavior, but the paper also reports average earnings (payoff) by treatment and stage. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated, but average earnings and their components are reported.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that: (1) Homogeneous groups (same nationality) contribute more to the public good than mixed groups, and this is mainly due to Lebanese participants reducing contributions in mixed groups. (2) Punishment is used more in homogeneous groups, and there is substantial antisocial punishment, especially among Lebanese. (3) The opportunity to punish does not increase contributions overall; in fact, average contributions decrease in the punishment stage for Syrians and do not change significantly for Lebanese or mixed groups. (4) Average earnings after the contribution stage are higher in homogeneous groups, but total earnings (including punishment costs) are not significantly different between treatments, as mixed groups assign less punishment and thus incur lower punishment costs. (5) The presence of punishment does not remedy the lower cooperation in mixed groups. (6) Behavior in mixed groups is more conditionally cooperative, while homogeneous groups show more unconditional cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment in a two-player repeated linear public goods game with a 0.75 MPCR and a 1:3 punishment cost-to-impact ratio. The main finding is that the introduction of punishment does not increase efficiency or average earnings; in fact, contributions do not rise and may even fall, especially due to antisocial punishment. While punishment increases costs (especially in homogeneous groups), it does not lead to higher total payoffs. Thus, for similar game designs (small groups, moderate MPCR, punishment with 1:3 cost-impact, no communication), one should not expect punishment to increase efficiency, especially in settings with potential for antisocial punishment. The paper also highlights that group composition (in-group vs. out-group) can moderate both cooperation and punishment, but the overall effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative in this context.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in pairs (2 players per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each pair plays 6 rounds: 3 without punishment, then 3 with punishment.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses how many of 10 tokens to contribute (0-10).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each token contributed increases both players' payoff by 0.75 ECUs; MPCR = 0.75.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs the punisher 1 ECU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the recipient's payoff by 3 ECUs (cost-to-impact ratio 1:3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the total number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants receive written feedback about their partner's contribution (and punishment in the punishment stage).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. Punishment is not anonymous: with only two players and repeated matching, players can infer who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not directly calculated or reported; only average earnings and their components are provided.
- The experiment uses only two-player groups, which may limit generalizability to larger group settings.
- The presence of antisocial punishment may be context-specific and may not generalize to all populations.
- No reward mechanism is included, so findings are limited to punishment-only environments.
- The sample is not representative of the broader population; recruitment was local and not random at the national level.
- The order of games (no-punishment first, then punishment) is not counterbalanced, which may affect behavior in the punishment stage.
