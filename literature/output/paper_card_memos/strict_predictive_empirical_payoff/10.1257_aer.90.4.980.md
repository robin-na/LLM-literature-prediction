Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual payoffs, as well as contribution rates and punishment behavior. Efficiency is operationalized as average group payoff relative to the maximum possible (full cooperation).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Introducing a costly punishment option in a repeated public goods game with groups of four, a known 10-round horizon, and no communication or identification, leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions and, after an initial period of adjustment, to higher group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the no-punishment condition, contributions and efficiency decline toward full free-riding. In the punishment condition, contributions are sustained at high levels (often near full cooperation), and group payoffs in the final periods are 10-20% higher than in the control. Punishment is used primarily against those who contribute less than the group average, and punished individuals tend to increase their contributions in subsequent rounds. The effect is robust across both stranger (random rematching) and partner (fixed group) treatments.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and anonymous interactions. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low (due to declining contributions), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency by 10-20% in the final rounds, with the effect being stronger in fixed groups (partner) than in random groups (stranger). The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known horizon, full feedback, anonymous punishment) closely match the canonical prediction task, making this paper highly informative for forecasting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. All treatments have 10 periods (rounds), with the horizon known in advance.

- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (a) is fixed at 0.4; with n=4, total multiplier is 1.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: assigning 1-10 points to another player costs 1,2,4,6,9,12,16,20,25 tokens, respectively, to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's first-stage payoff by 10% (up to 10 points, i.e., up to 100% reduction).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are publicly informed that each condition lasts exactly 10 periods; the horizon is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed about the total group contribution (no-punishment) or the full vector of individual contributions (punishment).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4) and a specific MPCR (0.4); results may not generalize to other group sizes or return structures.
- Punishment is anonymous and only negative (no rewards); effects of identification or positive incentives are not tested.
- The time horizon is short (10 rounds) and known; longer or indefinite horizons may yield different dynamics.
- Subjects are university students from Switzerland, which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- No communication is allowed; the effect of chat or discussion is not addressed.
- The initial periods of the punishment condition show lower efficiency due to high punishment costs; only after adjustment does efficiency surpass the control.
