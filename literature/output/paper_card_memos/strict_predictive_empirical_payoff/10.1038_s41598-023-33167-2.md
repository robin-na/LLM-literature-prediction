# Paper Memo: 10.1038_s41598-023-33167-2

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group wealth
- final earnings
- public good provision
- group contribution (as a proxy for group payoff)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- frequency of free-riding
- frequency and cost of punishment assigned
- discriminatory punishment (punishment by subgroup)
- affiliation ratings
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, discrimination) and payoff-based outcomes (group wealth, final earnings, public good provision). Group contribution is used as a proxy for group payoff/efficiency, and group wealth is explicitly analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: groups of 4; Experiment 2: groups of 6; Experiment 3: groups of 3 (uniform) and 6 (pluriform).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: two blocks of 20 rounds each (repeated game); Experiments 2 and 3: one-shot PGG.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; participants were seated in individual cubicles and interacted only via computer.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: binary contribution (all or nothing, 20 MU or 0); Experiments 2 and 3: continuous in steps of 10 MU (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiment 1: multiplier 1.6, 4 players, so MPCR = 0.4; Experiments 2 and 3: multiplier 1.5, 6 players, so MPCR = 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 MU per decrement point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each decrement point reduces target's earnings by 3 MU (punishment effectiveness: 1:3 cost-impact ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the number of rounds in each block; in Experiment 1, the second block was not announced until after the first block.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants were informed about each other's choices (contribution decisions) and the total number of punishment points received by each group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous in Experiment 1 (only total DP received shown, not who punished); in Experiments 2 and 3, punishment was assigned anonymously but with group member identity and academic background visible (arbitrary number and background).

## Important Limitations
- The main manipulation of group structure (uniform vs pluriform) is based on academic background (psychology vs pedagogy students), which may not generalize to other, more salient or conflictual group differences.
- The sample consists of first-year university students from a single institution, limiting generalizability.
- The study does not test other cooperation-enforcing institutions (e.g., rewards, centralized authorities) in the same design.
- The effect of group structure is not manipulated within groups, so individual-level mechanisms are inferred from between-group comparisons.
- The design does not allow for direct communication or reputation building in the repeated game (Experiment 1), which may affect generalizability to real-world settings.
