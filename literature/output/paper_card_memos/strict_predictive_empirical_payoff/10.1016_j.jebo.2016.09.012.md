## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that in a public goods game with heterogeneous, private or observable incentives, peer punishment (sanctions) does not significantly increase group contributions or efficiency above the baseline. This holds both when group members' types are private and when they are observable. In the private information setting, punishment is rarely used except against unambiguous free-riders, and the magnitude is too low to change incentives. When types are observable, punishment is more accurately targeted but still fails to reliably increase contributions, as some high-demanders resist increasing their contributions even when punished. In contrast, both unrestricted communication and a centralized bonus/fine mechanism (Falkinger) substantially increase efficiency, with communication allowing groups to coordinate on the socially optimal contribution vector. The results suggest that punishment is a weak tool for promoting efficiency in heterogeneous groups, especially when contribution norms are ambiguous.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous and possibly private incentives, enabling peer punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff above the baseline. The effect of punishment is weak or null, even when punishment is relatively effective (3:1 impact/cost ratio) and types are observable. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar heterogeneous PGG-like environments, one should expect little or no increase in efficiency relative to the control. The results also highlight the importance of communication and centralized mechanisms as more effective alternatives. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 3 players, 10 rounds, no chat (in punishment treatments), continuous contributions, and a non-linear payoff structure with interior optima.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 3 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games lasted 10 periods/rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Chat/communication was only allowed in the communication treatments, not in baseline or punishment treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0-4 tokens per round). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is 10 points per token contributed by others; the payoff structure is non-linear and differs by type, but the social benefit per token is 20 points (10 to each of 2 others).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 point to reduce another's earnings by 3 points (3:1 impact/cost ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point spent reduces target's earnings by 3 points).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of periods (10) and constant matching were common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all group members saw each other's contributions (but not types in private info treatments).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a non-linear payoff structure with interior optima and only two types (High and Low Demanders), which may limit generalizability to standard linear PGGs.
- All groups are of size 3, which may not capture dynamics in larger groups.
- The study does not test the effect of punishment in the presence of communication or in combination with other mechanisms.
- The findings may not generalize to settings with more than two types or with overlapping optimal contributions.
- The experiment is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
