Outcome notes:
The paper is a selective survey of experimental studies on linear public goods games, with a strong focus on both behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation, punishment) and payoff-based outcomes (group earnings, efficiency, welfare). Many studies discussed report both types of outcomes, and the survey explicitly discusses the distinction between increased cooperation and increased efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This survey synthesizes experimental evidence on the effects of punishment in linear public goods games. It finds that costly punishment opportunities generally increase contributions and cooperation, but do not always increase efficiency or group earnings. Efficiency improvements are only robust when punishment is highly effective (i.e., each unit of punishment inflicts a much larger cost on the target than on the punisher) or when the game is played for many rounds, allowing cooperation to stabilize and punishment costs to fall. In many standard settings, punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to the costliness of punishment. The presence of anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) and counter-punishment can further undermine efficiency. The effect of punishment is also moderated by group composition, information, and cultural context.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this survey provides strong evidence that the effect is not universally positive. The efficiency gain from punishment depends crucially on the punishment technology (cost and impact), the number of rounds (longer games allow efficiency to recover as punishment use declines), and the presence or absence of anti-social punishment. If punishment is low-cost and high-impact, and/or the game is long, efficiency is likely to increase. If punishment is costly relative to its impact, or if anti-social punishment is prevalent, efficiency may decrease even as cooperation rises. Control efficiency (without punishment) is a necessary baseline, but the effect of punishment is highly sensitive to these design dimensions. The survey also highlights that communication, sorting, and non-monetary sanctions can sometimes achieve similar or better efficiency gains than monetary punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Most studies reviewed use groups of 3-5 players; some studies vary group size (e.g., Carpenter 2007a finds that larger groups with sufficient monitoring can sustain high cooperation with punishment). The effect of player count on punishment's efficiency effect is discussed but not always directly manipulated.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Longer games (more rounds) allow punishment to be more effective at increasing efficiency, as shown in Gächter et al. (2008): efficiency gains from punishment are only robust in long time horizons.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (chat, face-to-face, chatroom) is shown to strongly increase cooperation and efficiency, sometimes more than punishment. Bochet et al. (2006) directly compare chat and punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Most studies use continuous contribution choices; all-or-nothing is not a focus but is mentioned in passing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. MPCR is varied in several studies; higher MPCR increases cooperation and can moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is systematically varied in studies (e.g., Anderson and Putterman 2006, Carpenter 2007b). Lower punishment cost increases use of punishment but does not always increase efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is a key moderator: only high-impact punishment reliably increases efficiency (Nikiforakis and Normann 2008, Egas and Riedl 2008).
- reward availability: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Some studies include reward as an alternative or supplement to punishment (e.g., Gürerk et al. 2006, Sefton et al. 2007). Rewards can increase cooperation and efficiency, especially when combined with punishment.
- reward cost: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward cost is described in some studies (e.g., Gürerk et al. 2006, Sefton et al. 2007), but not systematically analyzed for efficiency effects.
- reward technology: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. Reward effectiveness (impact per unit cost) is described but not systematically analyzed for efficiency effects.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Some studies use known, fixed number of rounds; others use random or unknown end. The effect on punishment's efficiency impact is discussed (e.g., longer, known horizon allows efficiency to recover).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Most studies show participants the contributions and/or payoffs of others, which is necessary for targeted punishment and for conditional cooperation.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Some studies manipulate whether the identity of punishers is revealed (e.g., Cinyabuguma et al. 2005, Denant-Boemont et al. 2007). Revealing identities can increase anti-social punishment and reduce efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- As a survey, the paper synthesizes results from many studies with varying designs, so findings are not always directly comparable or generalizable to all public goods game variants.
- The efficiency effects of punishment are highly sensitive to the punishment technology, group composition, and cultural context, which may limit the ability to generalize to new settings.
- Many studies reviewed use student samples and laboratory settings, which may not fully capture real-world dynamics.
- The survey notes that anti-social punishment and counter-punishment are more prevalent in some cultures, which can undermine the generality of positive punishment effects.
- Some dimensions (e.g., default contribution, all-or-nothing choices) are not systematically studied in the reviewed literature.
