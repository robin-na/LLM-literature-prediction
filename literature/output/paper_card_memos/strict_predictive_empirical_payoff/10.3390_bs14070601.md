## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff per group
- average payoff per role (leader/follower)
- total tokens earned
- payoff differences between conditions
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution
- frequency and amount of reward/punishment
- correlation between leader/follower contributions
- behavioral preferences for reward vs punishment
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average payoff (group and role), average contribution, and use of reward/punishment. Payoff is reported in tokens and as CNY equivalents. Efficiency is not explicitly calculated as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but group average payoff is directly reported and can be compared to the maximum possible.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use. The group average payoff in the punishment and reward conditions is higher than in the control and leader-only conditions, indicating a clear positive effect of punishment on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a 4-player, 32-round linear PGG with a 0.4 MPCR and no communication, enabling leader-administered punishment (with a 1:3 cost/impact ratio) increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to both a control (no leader, no punishment) and a leader-only (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players, fixed for all rounds.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups played 32 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; explicitly prohibited in instructions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0-20 tokens per round, integer values.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (public pot multiplied by 1.6, divided by 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token to the leader per 3 tokens deducted from a follower (1:3 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3 tokens lost by target per 1 token spent by leader.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available in the RLR group; leader can spend tokens to increase follower's payoff (1:3 ratio).
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost is 1 token to the leader per 3 tokens added to a follower (1:3 ratio).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3 tokens gained by target per 1 token spent by leader.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed the experiment consisted of 32 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all participants saw the total group contribution and their own payoff. In RL/RLR/RLP, followers saw the leader's contribution before choosing.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment/reward is always administered by the leader, whose identity (as leader) is known for the round, but participants are otherwise anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- All participants were Chinese university students; results may not generalize to other populations.
- Group size, MPCR, and punishment/reward technology are fixed; effects may differ with other parameters.
- Only leader-administered punishment/reward was tested; peer-to-peer or decentralized punishment was not studied.
- No communication or reputation mechanisms were included.
- Efficiency is reported as average payoff, not as a normalized efficiency ratio; maximum possible efficiency must be calculated from reported payoffs.
