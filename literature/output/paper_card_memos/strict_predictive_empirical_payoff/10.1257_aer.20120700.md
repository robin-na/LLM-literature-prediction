## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study uses a field experiment with real-world leaders of Ethiopian forest user groups, combining a third-party punishment public goods game (TPPG) with observational data on group performance in commons management. Leaders are classified by their punishment motives: non-punishers, equality-driven, equality-and-efficiency-driven, and antisocial. The main finding is that groups led by equality-and-efficiency-driven punishers achieve substantially higher efficiency (as measured by young trees per hectare) than those led by non-punishers, while groups led by antisocial punishers perform significantly worse. Equality-only punishers have no significant effect in the first assessment but a positive effect in the second. The effect sizes are large relative to the mean outcome. The results are robust to a wide range of controls and alternative specifications. The study highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it depends on the leader's punishment motive and implementation.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not universally positive or negative, but depends on the nature of punishment. When punishment is targeted at free riders (motivated by equality and/or efficiency), efficiency increases. When punishment is antisocial or indiscriminate, efficiency decreases. The experimental design (two-player linear PGG with third-party punishment, MPCR=0.75, punishment cost=1, punishment impact=3, no chat, continuous contributions) is close to standard PGGs, but the real-world outcome is a field measure of group welfare. The findings suggest that the presence of punishment can increase or decrease efficiency depending on the implementation and motives, and that heterogeneity in punishment type is a key moderator. For prediction, the effect of enabling punishment should be conditioned on whether punishment is likely to be prosocial (targeting defectors) or antisocial (targeting cooperators or indiscriminately).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experimental public goods game uses groups of 2 players (plus a leader as third party punisher). Real-world groups are larger (mean group size ~26), but the experimental measure of punishment motive is from the 2-player game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experimental game is one-shot (single round).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players in the experimental game.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: players can contribute 0, 2, 4, or 6 Birr.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.75 (contributions are multiplied by 1.5 and split between 2 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 Birr to the leader per deduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the punished member's payoff by 3 Birr (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game is one-shot; the number of rounds is known and fixed.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Leaders observe the contribution decisions of both players (but not their identities). Players know their own and the other's contribution in the public goods game.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: punished players do not know which leader punished them, and leaders do not know the identity of the punished player. No identification of punisher to punished.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental game uses two-player groups, which may not fully capture dynamics in larger real-world groups.
- The main efficiency outcome is a real-world proxy (number of young trees per hectare), not direct monetary payoff from the experimental game.
- Leader punishment motives are measured in a one-shot, anonymous experimental context, which may differ from repeated or reputational settings.
- The link between experimental punishment behavior and real-world group performance is correlational, though the paper uses extensive controls to address endogeneity.
- The study is set in a specific cultural and institutional context (Ethiopian forest user groups), which may limit generalizability.
- No direct manipulation of punishment institution at the group level; variation comes from leader type, not from random assignment of punishment ability.
