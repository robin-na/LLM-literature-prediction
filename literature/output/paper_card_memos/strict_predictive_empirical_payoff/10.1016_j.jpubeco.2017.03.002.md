# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.jpubeco.2017.03.002

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group wealth (sum of endowments at start of each period)
- total group earnings
- efficiency (as share of maximum possible group wealth)
- Gini coefficient (inequality in payoffs)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution amount
- share of endowment contributed
- punishment assigned (pro-social and anti-social)
- punishment frequency
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group wealth (total tokens), efficiency (as share of maximum possible), and Gini coefficient (inequality). Contribution and punishment behaviors are also reported but are not the primary focus for efficiency.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a dynamic public good game with endogenous endowments, contributions and group wealth increase over time even without punishment. Introducing punishment does not increase group wealth or efficiency; in some cases, it reduces them, particularly in longer games. Punishment increases contributions but destroys resources, leading to lower or unchanged group wealth. Early inequality is strongly negatively correlated with later group wealth in the punishment condition, and anti-social punishment (shirkers punishing contributors) is more prevalent in unequal groups, further reducing efficiency. Removing inequality experimentally restores the positive effect of punishment on contributions and wealth.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that in dynamic public good games with endogenous endowments, enabling punishment does not improve and may reduce efficiency compared to control (no punishment). The negative effect is especially likely when early inequality is high, as anti-social punishment becomes more common and destructive. For prediction tasks, if the game design includes endogenous endowments and dynamic wealth accumulation, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, especially as the number of rounds increases. The effect of punishment is context-dependent: in standard (static, exogenous endowment) PGGs, punishment increases efficiency, but in dynamic, endogenous settings, it does not.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main treatments use fixed groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Main treatments use 10 or 15 periods. The negative effect of punishment on wealth is more pronounced in the 15-period games.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players can allocate any number of tokens between private and group accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 1.5/4 = 0.375 (group account return 1.5, split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token to subtract 3 tokens from another player (cost ratio 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment technology: 1 token spent removes 3 tokens from target.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed of the total number of periods (10 or 15) at the start.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants see endowments and contributions of all group members; in PUNISH, also see punishment received and end-of-period wealth.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see who punished them, but group member labels are randomized each period to reduce identification.

## Important Limitations
- The dynamic public good game with endogenous endowments differs from the standard PGG; results may not generalize to static or exogenous-endowment settings.
- No communication or chat is allowed, so findings may not apply to settings with communication.
- The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent and may not hold in other dynamic or hybrid designs.
- The study does not explore longer horizons beyond 15 periods, so long-term effects are unknown.
- The effect of punishment may depend on the initial distribution of endowments and early group dynamics, which are not manipulated independently.
