## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper reports a field-lab experiment with Chilean artisanal fishers playing a 20-round common pool resource game. In the first 10 rounds (open access, no enforcement), overharvesting increased and efficiency declined, especially among low-performance unions and nonunionized fishers. In rounds 11-20, a weak enforcement regime was introduced: two players per round were randomly inspected, and if caught overharvesting, their entire round's harvest was confiscated. This sanction led to a sharp reduction in overharvest and a large increase in group earnings (efficiency) among unionized fishers, especially those from high-performance unions, who sustained cooperation and high efficiency through the end of the game. Low-performance unions showed a temporary improvement but cooperation eroded over time. Nonunionized fishers did not respond to enforcement and remained inefficient. The paper provides direct evidence that even weak sanctions can improve efficiency in CPR settings, but the effect is strongly moderated by group social capital and prior cooperative norms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment/sanction mechanism (even if weak and probabilistic) in a repeated CPR game with continuous contribution/extraction choices and no communication can substantially increase group efficiency, especially when baseline efficiency is not already at floor. The effect is strongest in groups with high social capital or prior cooperative norms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment in similar CPR/Pgg-like games will increase efficiency, with the magnitude depending on group composition and baseline cooperativeness. The design features—5 players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous extraction, no rewards, and a probabilistic, high-impact punishment—are all relevant for mapping to prediction. The control (no punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while the treatment (punishment enabled) yields much higher efficiency for unionized groups.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All experimental groups consisted of 5 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 20 rounds: 10 baseline (no enforcement), 10 with enforcement.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication among group members was allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/extraction was continuous: players could overharvest from 0 to 50 units per round.
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each player was assigned a default quota of 100 units per round; overharvest was an explicit additional choice.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. For each unit overharvested, each of the other 4 players lost 0.5 units. This is a negative externality, not a standard MPCR, but functionally similar for prediction.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a probabilistic external sanction: if caught, the entire round's harvest is confiscated. There is no cost to other players; the cost is borne by the punished individual.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is highly effective if triggered: 100% of the round's harvest is confiscated if caught. Probability of inspection is 2/5 per round.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was present in the experiment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were informed about the number of rounds; instructions and procedures made the round structure clear.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players saw the group's average harvest, their own losses due to others' overharvest, and their own profit. They did not see individual others' choices.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were told if they themselves were inspected and punished, but not who else was inspected or punished. No identification of punisher, as punishment was external and random.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is external, probabilistic, and not peer-administered, which may limit generalizability to peer punishment settings.
- The sample is limited to Chilean artisanal fishers, and group composition (unionized vs. nonunionized) strongly moderates the effect of punishment.
- No communication was allowed, which may differ from real-world settings where discussion is possible.
- The experiment uses a continuous extraction (overharvest) design, not a standard binary or linear public goods game.
- No reward mechanism was present, so results do not inform about reward-punishment interactions.
- The enforcement regime is relatively weak (2/5 chance of inspection), but the punishment is severe (full confiscation), which may not match all real-world sanctioning systems.
