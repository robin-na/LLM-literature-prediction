## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated linear public goods games with four players and 20 rounds, the study compares four treatments: (1) standard VCM (no punishment), (2) exogenous universal punishment (StdPun), (3) endogenous punishment with zero cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP0), and (4) endogenous punishment with a small or large cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5). When the right to punish is costless (CTP0), participation is high enough that group contributions and efficiency are as high as in the exogenous punishment treatment (StdPun), and both are significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline (VCM). When there is any cost to acquiring the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5), participation drops, antisocial punishment increases, and group contributions and efficiency fall to levels not significantly different from the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the size of the cost: even a negligible cost eliminates the efficiency gains from punishment. The number of persistent punishers is a key moderator: at least two are needed for punishment to be effective. The study provides direct evidence that the institutional details of how punishment rights are provided (costless vs. costly, endogenous vs. exogenous) are critical for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend critically on whether the right to punish is costless and universally available, or must be acquired at a cost. When punishment is exogenously provided or costlessly available to all, efficiency increases substantially relative to the no-punishment baseline. When even a small cost is required to acquire the right to punish, participation drops, antisocial punishment rises, and efficiency gains disappear. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence and cost of acquiring punishment rights are key moderators: if the game design includes a cost to acquire punishment rights, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. If punishment is costless and universally available, expect large efficiency gains. The number of persistent punishers is also important: at least two are needed for effectiveness in this four-player design.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use 20 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous: players can contribute any integer amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5 (a=0.5, n=4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1 token per unit assigned; effectiveness is 3 tokens lost by target per 1 token spent (1:3 technology). In CTP treatments, there is also a cost to acquire the right to punish (0, 1, or 5 tokens). Even a minimal acquisition cost eliminates efficiency gains.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment technology is 1:3 (1 token spent reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward mechanism does not exist; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 20 rounds; this is common knowledge.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are shown the group's total contribution, individual contributions of others (in descending order), and their own earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous: players know how much punishment they received, but not who assigned it.

## Important Limitations
- The study uses fixed groups of four players and 20 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- Punishment is always anonymous; effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
- No reward mechanism is included; only punishment is studied.
- The acquisition cost for punishment is manipulated, but only three levels are tested (0, 1, 5 tokens); effects at other cost levels are unknown.
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- Efficiency is measured as group earnings relative to the social optimum, but the paper does not report efficiency as a percentage; only mean earnings are given.
- The study does not test the effect of communication or reputation mechanisms.
