## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- group payoff
- individual payoff
- Gini coefficient (inequality)
- relative contributions (as a proxy for group welfare)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- relative contribution
- punishment assigned
- willingness to pay for secrecy
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are relative contributions (contribution/endowment), group and individual payoffs, and Gini coefficient (inequality). Efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome is not directly reported, but group payoff and relative contributions are closely related. Non-payoff outcomes include willingness to pay for secrecy and punishment behavior.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across two lab experiments using a four-player linear public goods game with unequal endowments (determined by merit and luck), the paper finds that: (1) Peer punishment increases contributions and group payoffs, but only when endowments are transparent. (2) When endowments are secret, punishment has a limited effect on cooperation and does not reduce inequality; advantaged participants contribute a lower fraction of their endowment and earn much higher payoffs. (3) When endowments are made public, punishment becomes much more effective at increasing contributions and reducing inequality, especially among the advantaged. (4) Willingness to pay for secrecy is higher among advantaged participants, especially when their advantage is due to luck. (5) Overall, transparency is necessary for punishment to improve efficiency and mitigate the negative effects of inequality.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on whether endowments (wealth) are transparent. In games with secret endowments, punishment has little effect on group payoff or efficiency; in games with public endowments, punishment significantly increases contributions and group payoffs, especially among the advantaged. For prediction, the presence of transparency (showing endowments or relevant summaries) is a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when transparency is high. The game uses continuous contributions, no chat, and a fixed MPCR of 0.4. Punishment is costly (cost 1, impact 3), and there is no reward option. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar design features.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds per game (10 with secret endowments, 10 with public endowments in Study 2).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: players can contribute any part of their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each ECU contributed returns 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 ECU per point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 3 ECU (cost/impact ratio 1:3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of rounds in advance (20 rounds).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see the contributions of each group member and their own payoff after each round. In some treatments, endowments are also revealed.

## Important Limitations
- Laboratory setting may not capture the full complexity of real-world social and economic interactions; external validity is limited.
- Inequality is generated only via endowment differences from merit and luck, not other forms (e.g., political, social, educational).
- Efficiency is not reported as a direct ratio to the fully cooperative outcome; group payoff and relative contributions are used as proxies.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment is available.
- No communication or chat is allowed, so findings may not generalize to settings with communication.
- Punishment is always anonymous; effects of identifiable punishment are not tested.
- The effect of transparency is operationalized as endowment visibility, not broader forms of transparency.
