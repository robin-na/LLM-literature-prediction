Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average contributions and average payoffs, which are linear transformations of each other in this design. The paper also reports on behavioral outcomes such as willingness to contribute and conditional cooperation, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Joint decision-making in pairs, where members can communicate, leads to higher and more sustained efficiency (measured by average group payoff/contributions) than individual decision-making in finitely repeated two-player public goods games. This effect is strongest under partner matching (fixed pairs), where efficiency remains high until the end-game. Under stranger matching (random rematching), efficiency declines more rapidly, but is still higher than individual decision-making in early rounds if social distance is close. When compared to individual decision-making with informal punishment (where subjects can punish at a cost), joint decision-making achieves similar efficiency and average payoffs. The effect of joint decision-making is driven by communication and, to a lesser extent, by close social distance within pairs. Removing social distance (anonymous pairs) reduces efficiency, especially under stranger matching. The paper provides direct evidence on the efficiency impact of punishment institutions versus joint decision-making, and indirect evidence on the role of communication, matching protocol, and social distance.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player finitely repeated public goods games, enabling informal punishment (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases efficiency to a level comparable to that achieved by joint decision-making in pairs with communication. The efficiency boost from punishment is robust under both partner and stranger matching, though the effect is stronger under partner matching. The design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency with punishment enabled are: player count (2), number of rounds (15), MPCR (0.8), punishment cost (0.5 per point), punishment tech (1.5 per point), and matching protocol (partner vs stranger). Communication within pairs (chat) is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially under partner matching. The paper's direct comparison of control (no punishment), punishment, and joint decision-making treatments makes it highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are two-player (individual) or two-pair (each pair is a decision-making unit, but the group is always two units).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use 15 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Pairs in joint-decision treatments can communicate via electronic chat for one minute per round; no chat in individual or punishment treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (non-negative integers from 0 to 40), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.8 in all treatments.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. In punishment treatments, each punishment point costs the punisher 0.5 points.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punished player's payoff by 1.5 points.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is finitely repeated for 15 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed of their own and their counterpart's contributions and payoffs after each round.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is limited to two-player (or two-pair) groups; results may not generalize to larger groups.
- The punishment institution is standard but does not include positive rewards or more complex sanctioning mechanisms.
- The study is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- The paper does not systematically vary punishment cost or effectiveness beyond the standard setting.
- The analysis of chat content is limited and not the main focus; mechanisms behind communication effects are not fully explored.
