Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- average group payoff
- average individual earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- extraction/appropriation levels
- contribution levels
- voting behavior
- trust game results
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is group efficiency (payoff relative to social optimum). Extraction/contribution levels are also reported, but efficiency is the primary outcome for the prediction task.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment involved groups of 5 players managing a common-pool resource over 20 rounds, with a baseline (open access) phase followed by an institution phase (external penalty, external reward, or communication) chosen by group vote. Efficiency (group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum) increased from 79.6% in the baseline to 88.6% on average after introducing institutions. However, the effect of punishment (penalty) was highly contingent: when the penalty rule was imposed with low group support (low self-determination), or in high-trust Namibian communities, efficiency actually decreased (crowding-out effect), with final extraction levels as high or higher than the baseline. In contrast, in low-trust South African communities, punishment increased efficiency. Reward (external enabling intervention) did not produce crowding-out in any context and generally improved or maintained efficiency. Communication alone (no external intervention) produced stable, moderate efficiency. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is mixed and moderated by self-determination and social trust.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments is not universally positive. Instead, punishment can reduce efficiency (crowding-out) when imposed without group support or in high-trust, norm-driven communities. In low-trust settings, punishment can increase efficiency. Reward mechanisms do not crowd out cooperation and are generally safe for efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; the effect depends on the degree of self-determination (e.g., whether punishment is voted in or imposed) and the baseline level of trust in the group. Control efficiency (baseline) is a strong predictor, but the effect of enabling punishment depends on these moderators. Game design dimensions such as group voting, trust, and the nature of the intervention (controlling vs. enabling) are critical for accurate prediction.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a common-pool resource (CPR) game, which is closely related but not identical to a standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to all PGG settings.
- Punishment and reward were externally administered, not peer-to-peer; the effects of decentralized, endogenous punishment may differ.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent, moderated by group voting (self-determination) and baseline trust, which may be difficult to measure or replicate in other settings.
- The cost structure of punishment and reward to the external authority is not specified, which may limit direct comparison to settings with endogenous costs.
- The study is conducted in specific cultural and ecological contexts (southern Africa), which may limit external validity to other populations or environments.
- The number of rounds (20) and group size (5) are fixed; effects may differ with other parameters.
