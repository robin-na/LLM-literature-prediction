## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally compares the effects of punishment and gossip on cooperation and efficiency in a four-round public goods game. Punishment (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3) did not significantly increase overall cooperation and significantly reduced individual earnings (efficiency) compared to control. Gossip (costless) increased both cooperation and efficiency. When both mechanisms were present, gossip offset the negative efficiency impact of punishment. The study also finds that gossip, but not punishment, has positive spillover effects on trust and trustworthiness in a subsequent trust game. The results suggest that punishment, at moderate cost, is less efficient than gossip for promoting cooperation, and that gossip can mitigate the efficiency losses from punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, four-round PGG with a punishment mechanism (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3, anonymous, no chat, continuous contributions), enabling punishment reduces efficiency (total/individual earnings) compared to control. The negative effect is robust, and the presence of a costless gossip mechanism can counteract this loss. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG designs, especially when punishment is moderately costly and anonymous. The results also suggest that the efficiency cost of punishment is not offset by increased cooperation, except possibly in the final rounds, and that gossip is a more efficient mechanism for promoting cooperation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each PGG group had 4 players; this is fixed and directly reported.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The PGG was played for 4 rounds; this is fixed and directly reported.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No direct chat or communication between group members; only gossip (indirect notes to future partners) was allowed in relevant conditions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: participants could contribute any amount from 0 to 10 points.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total group contributions were doubled and divided equally among 4 players. MPCR = 2/4 = 0.5.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 point to punisher per deduction point; each deduction point reduces target's earnings by 3 points (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point cost to punisher reduces target by 3 points (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3).
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism was implemented; only punishment and gossip were manipulated.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the game was four rounds; this was specified in instructions.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants were informed of each member's contribution and earnings.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous: participants only learned the total number of deduction points received, not who assigned them.

## Important Limitations
- Group efficiency (total group earnings) could not be directly analyzed due to the use of experimenter strategy for missing participants, potentially introducing noise.
- The experiment was limited to four rounds of the PGG, which may not capture long-term effects of punishment or gossip.
- Gossip was costless and did not allow for partner selection or ostracism, which may limit generalizability to other settings.
- Punishment was peer-based and anonymous; results may differ with centralized or identified punishment.
- The online, anonymous environment may differ from face-to-face or real-world group interactions.
- No reward mechanism was included, so findings do not address the effects of positive incentives.
