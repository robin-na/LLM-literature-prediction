## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper shows that the effectiveness of peer punishment in public goods games is highly heterogeneous and depends critically on the composition of punishment types within groups. Using a finite mixture model, the authors classify subjects into three types: non-punishers (Type N), antisocial/free rider punishers (Type AF), and free rider-only punishers (Type F). The presence of Type AF subjects, who punish both low and high contributors, is strongly associated with lower group earnings and reduced efficiency. In contrast, groups with only Type F and Type N subjects achieve much higher efficiency. The number of Type AF subjects in a group explains a large share of the variance in group earnings, more than baseline cooperativeness or period effects. Cross-cultural differences in the prevalence of Type AF subjects explain much of the observed variation in the effectiveness of punishment across societies.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform: it depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishers (Type AF) in the group or population. In environments where antisocial punishment is common, enabling punishment can substantially reduce efficiency. In environments where only free rider punishment occurs, enabling punishment can increase or sustain efficiency. The paper provides quantitative evidence (e.g., groups with four Type AF subjects earn 68.7% less than groups with only Type F/N) and regression models showing that the number of Type AF subjects is a strong predictor of group earnings. Thus, for prediction, the cultural or group composition regarding punishment types is a critical moderator, and control game efficiency alone is insufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering this heterogeneity.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played in groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each game is played over 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (0-20 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return is 0.4 (each token contributed yields 0.4 tokens per group member).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 token per unit assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens (1:3 punishment technology).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects observe all group members' contributions before punishment decisions.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; subjects do not know who punished them.

## Important Limitations
- The analysis is based on secondary data from Herrmann et al. (2008); the original experiment was not designed to test the finite mixture model approach.
- The findings are specific to lab settings with university student samples and may not generalize to other populations or real-world settings.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the prevalence of antisocial punishers, which may not be observable in advance in new contexts.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The classification of punishment types is somewhat fragile for antisocial punishers (Type AF), especially over time, indicating possible instability in type assignment.
- The study does not manipulate game design dimensions such as punishment cost or technology, so cannot directly estimate their moderating effects.
