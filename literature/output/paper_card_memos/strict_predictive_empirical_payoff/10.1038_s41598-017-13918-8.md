## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds per group; analyses focus on rounds 8-14.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; participants made decisions in isolated booths.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution and support choices were continuous (0-20 for PGG, 0-9 for support), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided by 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 1:1 in Experiment 1 (PR=1), 1:3 in Experiment 2 (PR=3). The cost to the system is equal to the tokens removed from punished players; the cost to individuals is the tokens they contribute to the punishment fund.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is manipulated via the punishment ratio (PR): PR=1 (low tech), PR=3 (high tech). Higher PR means more punishment per unit support.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the total number of rounds (15) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, all members were informed who had been punished and by how much, and PGG results were provided to all members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was administered by the system, not by individuals; all members were informed who had been punished and by how much, but not who initiated punishment (since it was a pool system).

## Important Limitations
- The study uses a laboratory setting with undergraduate students, which may limit generalizability to real-world public goods problems.
- The efficiency gains from punishment are limited by the high cost of maintaining cooperation; punishment increases cooperation but not necessarily group profit.
- The control (no-punishment) condition is not directly reported, so the baseline efficiency without punishment is not available for direct comparison.
- The study only examines pool punishment systems, not peer punishment or other institutional forms.
- The effect of group size, communication, and other design features is not explored.
