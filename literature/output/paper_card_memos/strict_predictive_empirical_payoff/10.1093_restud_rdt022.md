## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, groups could vote between no sanctions (NS), informal sanctions (IS: peer punishment), and formal sanctions (FS: centralized punishment with fixed and variable costs, deterrent or non-deterrent). Both IS and deterrent FS substantially increased group efficiency and earnings relative to NS. IS were highly cost-effective, yielding higher returns per point spent on sanctions than FS in most conditions. IS were especially effective when chosen by vote, with group earnings and efficiency about 30% higher than when IS were imposed exogenously. Non-deterrent FS also increased efficiency when chosen by vote, but less so than IS. High fixed costs for FS reduced both their efficiency and their popularity. Reducing the effectiveness of IS (increasing cost per unit punishment) decreased their efficiency somewhat, but IS remained popular and relatively effective. Allowing IS and FS to coexist did not substantially change these patterns. Overall, IS can be as or more effective than FS in promoting efficiency, especially when adopted democratically.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with 5 players, enabling informal peer punishment (IS) can increase group efficiency to levels comparable to or exceeding those achieved by formal sanctions, especially when IS are chosen by vote. The efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost-effectiveness of the sanction regime, the fixed and variable costs of punishment, and whether the regime is adopted endogenously. High fixed costs for FS reduce their efficiency advantage. IS are most effective when punishment is relatively cheap and well-targeted. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling IS is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, and that the effect is robust to moderate changes in punishment technology and institutional coexistence. The control (no-sanction) efficiency is typically well below full cooperation, while IS and deterrent FS can approach or reach high efficiency, with IS often being more cost-effective.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players in all main and robustness treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Main treatments: 7 phases of 4 periods each (28 rounds total); each phase corresponds to a regime chosen by vote or imposed.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players is allowed; only voting and observation of others' contributions/punishments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates any amount (0-20) to the group account each round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (group account is doubled and split among 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. IS: 1 point cost to punisher per 4 points deducted from target (main), 1:2 in robustness; FS: fixed cost per period (2 or 8), plus variable cost per unit punished (s=0.4 or 0.8 per unit kept). Lower cost (cheaper punishment) increases efficiency and popularity.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. IS: punishment effectiveness (sigma) is 4 (main) or 2 (robustness); higher effectiveness increases efficiency and popularity. FS: s=0.4 (non-deterrent) or 0.8 (deterrent).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the number of periods per phase (4) and the number of phases (7); the game is finitely repeated and the structure is explained.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players observe the contributions of all other group members each round; in IS, they also see the amount of punishment received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS, players know how much punishment they receive but not who assigned it; punishment is anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use groups of 5; results may not generalize to larger groups or real-world settings.
- No communication or chat is allowed; effects of communication are not tested.
- No reward mechanism is studied; only punishment (sanctions) are considered.
- The study does not allow direct choice between deterrent and non-deterrent FS; only between FS, IS, and NS.
- Cultural and contextual factors are not varied; all subjects are university students in Denmark.
- Efficiency and cost-effectiveness are measured in the lab context; real-world implementation may differ.
- The effect of repeated play and learning is tested, but only over a limited number of rounds and phases.
