## Study Type

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- net pay-off (total earnings after accounting for cost of assigned and received punishment points)
- group earnings
- within-group variation in pay-offs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to group account
- punishment assigned/received
- within-group variation in contributions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is net pay-off (efficiency), with secondary analysis of contributions and punishment behavior. Within-group variation in pay-offs is also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In isolated public goods games, costly punishment increases cooperation but does not significantly increase net pay-off (efficiency), as the costs of punishment offset the gains from higher contributions. When intergroup competition is introduced, groups with punishment opportunities achieve much higher contributions and net pay-offs, both in asymmetric (one group can punish, one cannot) and symmetric (both can punish) competition. In asymmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is dramatic: even the lowest-earning individual in the punishment group outperforms the highest in the no-punishment group. In symmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is positive but smaller and only marginally significant. Punishment also reduces within-group inequality in pay-offs. The paper concludes that intergroup competition is a key moderator of the efficiency effects of costly punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether there is intergroup competition. In the absence of group competition, enabling punishment does not reliably increase efficiency. With group competition, punishment can substantially increase efficiency, especially when only some groups have access to punishment. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of group competition, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (8), number of rounds (30), and the absence of communication or reputation mechanisms. The control efficiency (no punishment) is much lower than the treatment efficiency (with punishment) in the presence of group competition, but not in isolated groups. This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGGs with and without punishment, especially when intergroup competition is a design feature.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 8 players; all treatments used this group size.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games lasted 30 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was explicitly excluded in all treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants allocated any amount of 20 MUs between group and personal accounts.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total group account doubled and divided equally among 8 members. MPCR = 2/8 = 0.25.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point cost the punisher 1 MU.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduced the punished player's earnings by 3 MUs (punishment effectiveness 3:1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Game length (30 periods) was fixed and known to participants.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants were informed about the contributions of their fellow group members and the total contribution made by the competing group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not informed about the individual punishment decisions of other participants; punishment was anonymous.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not include mechanisms such as communication, reputation, or central authority, which may affect the generalizability of results to other PGG settings.
- Punishment was only possible within groups, not between groups.
- The group size (8) is larger than in many PGG studies, which may affect comparability.
- The study does not examine the effects of antisocial punishment in societies where it is common.
- The laboratory setting may not capture all relevant real-world complexities of intergroup conflict.
