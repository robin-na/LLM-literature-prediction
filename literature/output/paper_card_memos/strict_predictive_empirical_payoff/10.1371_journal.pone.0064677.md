Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average payoff per period (group and individual), frequency of cooperation, and the effectiveness of punishment in converting defectors. The paper explicitly analyzes average payoff as a function of punishment and game parameters.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study uses repeated group snowdrift games (a close variant of public goods games) with and without punishment to test the effects of mild versus severe punishment on cooperation and average payoff. In groups of five, with 25 rounds per session, the authors manipulate the cost-to-benefit ratio (r = 0.2 or 0.8) and the punishment fine (p = 2.0 or 4.0, with constant cost to punisher). When cooperation is likely (r = 0.2), both mild and severe punishment increase cooperation, but severe punishment does not further increase cooperation and reduces average payoff compared to mild punishment. When cooperation is unlikely (r = 0.8), only severe punishment substantially increases cooperation and average payoff; mild punishment is less effective and can even reduce average payoff compared to no punishment. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is context-dependent: mild punishment is optimal when cooperation is already likely, while severe punishment is only justified in highly defection-prone environments.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the underlying difficulty of cooperation (cost-to-benefit ratio, analogous to MPCR in PGGs). If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency, adding severe punishment will not improve and may reduce efficiency; mild punishment is sufficient. If the control game has low efficiency (cooperation is rare), only severe punishment can increase efficiency. The cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the baseline cooperation rate, are critical moderators. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in snowdrift- or PGG-like games with peer punishment.

Lower r means higher effective MPCR (easier cooperation), higher r means lower MPCR (harder cooperation). The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive only at high r with severe punishment; at low r, severe punishment reduces efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses the snowdrift game, which is a close but not exact variant of the standard public goods game; results may not fully generalize to PGGs with linear returns.
- Punishment is only available to cooperators and is applied to all defectors if any cooperator chooses to punish, which differs from some PGG punishment protocols.
- The cost of punishment is fixed and does not scale with the fine, which may not reflect real-world or other experimental settings.
- No reward mechanism is present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not tested.
- The study only tests two levels of cost-to-benefit ratio and two punishment fine levels; effects may differ with other parameterizations.
- The experiment is conducted in a single cultural context (Chinese undergraduates), which may limit generalizability.
