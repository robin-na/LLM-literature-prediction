## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- probability of loss avoidance (PLA)
- group-level payoff (implied via PLA and loss structure)
- final payoffs (see supplementary material S1.8)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- individual and group contributions
- sanctioning behavior (prosocial and antisocial)
- retaliation
- conditional cooperation
Outcome notes:
The main reported outcome is the probability of loss avoidance (PLA), which is proportional to group contributions and determines whether a catastrophic loss is avoided. PLA is not a direct measure of efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation), but is closely related. Final payoffs are discussed in the supplementary material, where it is noted that payoffs are higher in no-sanction treatments. The main text focuses on cooperation/contribution and PLA.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment tested the effect of sanctions (costly punishment) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma with German and Russian participants, both in national and international groups. Sanctions increased group cooperation (as measured by the probability of loss avoidance, PLA), especially for Russian participants and in international groups. However, the cost of sanctioning meant that final payoffs were actually higher in the no-sanction treatments, as shown in the supplementary material. The effect of sanctions was positive for increasing cooperation and loss avoidance, but negative for monetary efficiency. The combination of international interaction and sanctions was particularly effective at raising cooperation among low cooperators (Russians), leading to convergence with high cooperators (Germans). The effect of revealing nationality was minimal. Sanctioning was mostly prosocial in international groups, and antisocial sanctioning (punishing high contributors) was reduced. Only a small fraction of the endowment was spent on sanctions, but this was sufficient to increase cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment (sanctions) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma increases group cooperation (PLA) but reduces final payoffs due to the cost of sanctioning. For prediction tasks, this means that the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game may be higher or lower than the control, depending on whether the benefit from increased loss avoidance outweighs the cost of sanctions. In this experiment, the cost of sanctions was high enough that final payoffs were higher in the no-sanction condition, even though cooperation (PLA) was higher with sanctions. The effect is especially pronounced for groups with low baseline cooperation, and in international groups. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, group composition (nationality/culture), and the structure of the risk/loss. The game is a close variant of a PGG, but with a threshold and probabilistic loss, so direct transfer to standard PGGs should be cautious.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 6 players (3 from each location/country in international treatments).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 periods (rounds) per game.
- chat: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No chat or communication between players; interactions were anonymous except for numeric labels.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: up to 50 tokens per round (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard MPCR; instead, group contributions determine the probability of loss avoidance (PLA) via a threshold. The marginal effect of a contribution is proportional to the increase in PLA, not a fixed MPCR.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment (sanctioning) is costly: tokens spent on sanctions are deducted from the punisher's private account. Only 10 tokens per round can be used for sanctions. The cost structure is nonlinear (see supplementary table S6). The cost of sanctioning reduces final payoffs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctions are more-than-proportional: the number of tokens deducted from the target increases more than proportionally with the number of tokens spent by others to sanction that participant. Details in supplementary table S6.
- reward availability: The paper mentions this dimension but gives limited predictive guidance. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment (sanctions) is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants receive information on each group member's contributions in all previous rounds, and the current PLA. In sanction treatments, they also see sanctions assigned by each group member.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are identified by numbers (1-6), and can see which group member assigned sanctions to whom. Numeric labels also indicate location (1-3 vs 4-6).

## Important Limitations
- The game is a collective-risk social dilemma (CRSD), not a standard linear PGG; the outcome is probabilistic and threshold-based, so efficiency is not directly comparable to standard PGGs.
- The main outcome in the paper is probability of loss avoidance (PLA), not direct group payoff or efficiency; final payoffs are discussed only in the supplementary material.
- Sanctioning is costly and reduces final payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is negative, even though cooperation (PLA) increases.
- The experiment is limited to German and Russian university students; generalizability to other populations or cultural groups is uncertain.
- The cost and effectiveness of sanctions are specific to the experimental design (nonlinear, more-than-proportional impact), which may not generalize to other punishment implementations.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not inform about the interaction of punishment and reward.
