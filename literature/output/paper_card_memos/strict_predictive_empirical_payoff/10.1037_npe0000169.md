## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Study 1: one-shot (1 round); Study 2: repeated (10 rounds). The effect of punishment on efficiency differs between one-shot and repeated games.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Oral and written communication were not allowed in Study 2; no mention of chat in Study 1, but instructions indicate independent decision-making.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player can invest any amount between 0 and 100 points.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.5: joint account investments are multiplied by 2 and divided among 4 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Institutional punishment: cost is not paid by peers but is a fixed penalty deducted from under-contributors. Two levels: small and large punishment, with penalty formulas provided.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a deduction from under-contributors, with two levels: small (penalty if below 3/7 of average), large (penalty if below average).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is used.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed of the number of rounds in both studies.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. In repeated PGG, investment information for each group member is fed back to all group members at the end of each period.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is institutional, not peer-based; no identification of individual punishers, as punishment is automatic and rule-based.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments use groups of four; results may not generalize to larger group sizes.
- Participants are undergraduate students from two Chinese universities, limiting generalizability.
- Only institutional punishment is studied; peer punishment and antisocial punishment are not addressed.
- No reward mechanisms are included, so findings do not address reward-punishment interactions.
- The effect of cognitive ability is central, so results may differ in more heterogeneous or representative samples.
- The study does not vary MPCR or other key game parameters beyond punishment magnitude.
