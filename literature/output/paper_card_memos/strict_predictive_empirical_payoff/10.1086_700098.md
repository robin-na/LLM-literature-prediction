## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study implemented a framed field/lab-in-the-field experiment with Namibian forest users, using a threshold public goods/extraction game with two punishment treatments: internal (peer) and external (random monitoring). Both punishment regimes reduced extraction compared to the baseline, but neither increased group or individual earnings (efficiency). Internal punishment was more effective at disciplining high extractors, especially those of low status, due to more targeted and severe peer punishment. However, internal punishment also led to high levels of antisocial punishment, which, together with the direct costs of punishment, resulted in lower overall earnings than both the external punishment and the no-punishment baseline. Ethnic heterogeneity increased antisocial punishment but did not reduce cooperation or prosocial punishment. The study concludes that while self-governance with peer punishment can discipline free riders, it may also generate substantial welfare losses due to costly and vengeful punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a threshold public goods/extraction game with both internal (peer) and external (centralized) punishment, the introduction of punishment mechanisms does not necessarily increase efficiency (group or individual earnings) and may even reduce it, especially when peer punishment is available. The main reason is that the costs of punishment (both direct and indirect, including antisocial punishment) can outweigh the benefits from increased cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar environments (5 players, 20 rounds, MPCR 0.4, threshold/tipping point, no chat, anonymous punishment, no rewards), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when peer punishment is allowed. The effect may be less negative or neutral with centralized punishment, but still not positive. The findings also highlight the importance of group composition (status, ethnicity) and the potential for antisocial punishment to undermine efficiency gains.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted in a specific cultural and ecological context (rural Namibian forest users), which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- The punishment treatments differ not only in the institution (internal vs. external) but also in monitoring probability and punishment severity, making it difficult to isolate the effect of the institution alone.
- No pure control group with punishment disabled in rounds 11-20; comparisons to the baseline (rounds 1-10) may be confounded by time trends and learning effects.
- The presence of antisocial punishment and its drivers (revenge, status, ethnicity) may vary in other settings.
- No reward or positive sanction mechanism was tested, so findings are limited to punishment-based interventions.
- Group size, MPCR, and other parameters were fixed and not varied experimentally.
