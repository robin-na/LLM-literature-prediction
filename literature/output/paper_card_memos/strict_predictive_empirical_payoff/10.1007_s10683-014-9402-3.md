## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This study experimentally manipulates punishment network structure and punishment capacity in a repeated linear public goods game with four players per group. Treatments include no-punishment, complete punishment, pairwise punishment, and untouchable punishment networks, as well as variants with increased punishment capacity. The main findings are: (1) Complete punishment networks lead to higher contributions than no-punishment or pairwise networks, but not necessarily higher group earnings after accounting for punishment costs. (2) Pairwise punishment networks are less effective at increasing contributions and do not improve efficiency. (3) Increasing punishment capacity in incomplete networks does not significantly improve contributions or efficiency. (4) The structure of the punishment network is more important than the absolute punishment capacity for group outcomes. (5) Targeted revenge is a major driver of antisocial punishment. (6) The presence of an 'untouchable' (who cannot punish or be punished) does not significantly reduce the willingness of others to contribute, but untouchables contribute less and earn more.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment network (who can punish whom) is a critical moderator. Enabling punishment in a complete network increases contributions but does not necessarily increase efficiency due to the cost of punishment. Incomplete networks (e.g., pairwise) do not yield efficiency gains and may even reduce efficiency. Increasing punishment capacity without changing network structure does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, it is crucial to account for both the network structure and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless network structure and punishment costs are considered.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players in all treatments.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions have 20 rounds, known in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player allocates 0-10 tokens per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token contributed yields 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token reduces the target's earnings by 3 tokens.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward does not exist; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the exact number of rounds (20) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see all group members' contributions, sanctions imposed, sanctions received, and cumulative earnings after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is fully identified by anonymous ID; players know exactly who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses only four-person groups, which may limit generalizability to larger groups.
- The punishment network structures are stylized and may not capture the full complexity of real-world social networks.
- Efficiency is measured as group earnings after accounting for punishment costs, but does not consider long-term or external effects.
- The study does not include reward mechanisms, so findings may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- All decisions are made anonymously and without communication, which may differ from real-world environments.
