Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings and contributions, both of which are directly related to efficiency. The paper also analyzes behavioral outcomes such as communication, lying, and punishment, but the primary focus is on payoff-based outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with groups of four, the addition of a costly punishment stage increases contributions but not efficiency, as punishment costs offset the gains. Allowing non-binding numerical announcements or promises alone does not improve efficiency or contributions. However, when the ability to make non-binding promises is combined with punishment, both contributions and group earnings (efficiency) increase significantly. This is because the threat of punishment for broken promises makes communication credible, reducing the frequency of lying and the need for actual costly punishment. Thus, the combination of communication (promises) and punishment is more effective for efficiency than punishment alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 10 rounds, and a marginal per capita return of 0.4, the addition of costly punishment increases contributions but not efficiency, due to the cost of punishment. However, if the game also allows for non-binding promise statements (in addition to punishment and numerical communication), efficiency increases significantly, as credible communication reduces the need for costly punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the communication environment: punishment alone does not improve efficiency, but punishment plus credible communication (promises) does. The paper also provides detailed evidence on the cost structure of punishment and the behavioral mechanisms involved.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All sessions used 10 rounds.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous from 0 to 10 experimental dollars.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) is 0.4 (lambda = 1.6, n = 4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is 0.25 per 1 unit reduction in target's earnings.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each 0.25 spent by punisher reduces target's earnings by 1 (punishment effectiveness = 4).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the game would last 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects saw the contributions of each group member. In punishment treatments, they also saw the reductions they received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were identified by randomly assigned letters (B, C, D) that changed each period; punishment was anonymous (recipients did not know who punished them).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted with undergraduate students at a single university, which may limit generalizability.
- The promise treatments explicitly told subjects that promises were non-binding, which may have reduced the effectiveness of promises compared to more naturalistic settings.
- Group size, MPCR, and other parameters were fixed; results may differ with other parameterizations.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so results do not speak to the interaction of punishment and reward.
- Punishment was always anonymous; effects may differ if punishment is identifiable.
