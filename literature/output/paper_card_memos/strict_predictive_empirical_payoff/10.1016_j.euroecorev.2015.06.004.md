Non-payoff outcomes:

- voting behavior (affirmative votes, implementation rates)
- heterogeneity in contributions
- type-specific voting and contribution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (sum of payoffs as a fraction of the maximum), group and individual payoffs, and implementation rates of institutions. Contribution levels and voting behavior are also reported, but efficiency is the primary focus for the prediction task.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with three players, the introduction of an institution formation stage (via unanimity voting) that, if implemented, imposes binding contribution obligations enforced by deterrent sanctions, leads to large increases in efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the maximum possible). For homogeneous groups, a symmetric institution (equal obligations) is almost always implemented and achieves efficiency above 90%. For heterogeneous groups, a symmetric institution is often rejected by low-benefit players due to inequality aversion, but an asymmetric institution (with obligations tailored to equalize payoffs) is much more likely to be implemented, though at a cost to total efficiency. In all cases, efficiency is higher when the institution is implemented than when it is not. The main moderators are the distribution of marginal per-capita returns (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) and the symmetry of institutional obligations. The study does not manipulate punishment cost or technology directly, as the institution, if implemented, is enforced with sufficiently strong sanctions to ensure compliance.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with three players and 20 rounds, the introduction of a binding, deterrent-sanction institution (enabled by unanimity voting) increases efficiency relative to the baseline game without such an institution. The effect is strongest when the institution is both efficient and equitable (i.e., symmetric for homogeneous groups, asymmetric for heterogeneous groups). When the institution is rejected (often due to inequality aversion), efficiency is lower, but still generally higher than in the baseline. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, binding punishment institution (with high compliance) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on group heterogeneity and the design of institutional obligations. The study does not provide evidence on the effect of varying punishment cost or technology, as these are not manipulated; the punishment regime is always fully deterrent. Control efficiency (without institution) is reported and can be used as a baseline for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments are repeated for 20 periods.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication between participants was allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 to 20 units.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 2/3 for homogeneous groups; for heterogeneous groups, two players have MPCR 3/4, one has 1/2.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is implemented as a fully deterrent, centralized sanction; cost to punisher is not directly specified, but institution is enforced with sufficiently strong sanctions to ensure compliance.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Sanctions are 'sufficiently severe to ensure that the prescribed contribution levels are indeed implemented' (i.e., perfect enforcement).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the game consists of 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants are informed about the contributions and payoffs of all group members.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment regime is always fully deterrent and centralized; the study does not vary punishment cost or effectiveness, so results may not generalize to settings with weaker or decentralized punishment.
- All groups are of size 3; effects of larger group sizes are not tested.
- The institution is implemented only if all players vote in favor (unanimity); majority or other voting rules are not studied.
- No reward mechanisms are present, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
- Heterogeneity is limited to differences in marginal per-capita return; other forms of heterogeneity (e.g., endowment, punishment power) are not explored.
