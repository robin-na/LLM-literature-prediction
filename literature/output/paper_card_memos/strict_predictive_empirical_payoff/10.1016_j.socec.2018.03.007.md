Non-payoff outcomes:

- use of rewards and punishments

Outcome notes:
The paper reports both contribution rates and payoff-based outcomes (net payoffs, public goods payoffs). Efficiency is not directly labeled, but net payoffs and public goods payoffs are reported and compared across treatments, which allows for efficiency inference.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the addition of individual punishment or reward mechanisms (either centralized via a leader or decentralized among peers) leads to significantly higher contributions and higher net payoffs compared to the baseline game without such mechanisms. The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment is more effective than reward at sustaining high contributions over time, especially in the peer punishment treatment, where contributions even increase in the second half of the game. Net payoffs are highest in reward treatments (due to the reward mechanism's structure), but also higher in punishment treatments than in the baseline. The presence of a leader (who contributes first and can reward or punish) does not further increase contributions or payoffs compared to peer-based reward or punishment. The effect of punishment on increasing contributions and payoffs is robust, and punishment from leaders has a stronger effect on subsequent contributions than peer punishment. Anti-social punishment is less frequent when punishment is centralized with a leader.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, enabling individual punishment (with a cost/impact ratio of 1:3) increases both contributions and net payoffs (i.e., efficiency) compared to the baseline without punishment. The effect is robust whether punishment is centralized (leader) or decentralized (peers), and is not further enhanced by adding a leader who leads by example. The control (no punishment) efficiency is lower than in the punishment-enabled treatments. The findings support the prediction that, holding game design dimensions constant, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency. The specific cost and impact structure (1 point cost reduces target by 3 points) is important for generalization. Reward mechanisms also increase efficiency, but the structure of the reward mechanism in this experiment is payoff-increasing by design. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar public goods game environments.

## Important Limitations
- The reward mechanism is payoff-increasing by design (each reward point increases total group payoff), so efficiency effects of reward are not directly comparable to punishment or to standard PGGs.
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (4) and a fixed MPCR (0.4), so generalizability to other group sizes or MPCRs may be limited.
- All decisions are made without communication; results may differ in settings with communication.
- The punishment and reward cost/impact ratios are fixed at 1:3; effects may differ with other ratios.
- The study is conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- Efficiency is not directly labeled, but must be inferred from reported net payoffs and public goods payoffs.
