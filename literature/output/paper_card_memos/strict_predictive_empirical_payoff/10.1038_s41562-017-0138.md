# Paper Memo: 10.1038_s41562-017-0138

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- public good provisioning (mean contributions)
- group earnings (implied by public good provisioning and payoff structure)
- loss in points/endowment due to corruption
Non-payoff outcomes:
- frequency of bribe offers and acceptance
- leader decisions (punish, accept bribe, do nothing)
- individual variation by corruption exposure/heritage
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is mean contribution to the public good, which is a proxy for efficiency but not a direct measure of group payoff or total welfare. The paper discusses the economic cost of corruption in terms of lost contributions and points, and interprets these as losses in public good provisioning. Some analyses focus on behavioral outcomes (bribery, punishment choices), but the central results are about how treatments affect public good provisioning.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). In the standard IPGG (control), stronger leaders (higher punishment multiplier) increase contributions and public good provisioning. However, when bribery is possible (bribery game, BG), stronger leaders actually decrease public good provisioning, as they use their power to extract bribes rather than enforce cooperation. Anti-corruption strategies (partial/full transparency, leader investment) can increase contributions in some contexts (strong leaders or high economic potential), but may have no effect or even reduce contributions further in contexts with weak leaders and poor economic potential. The main outcome is mean contribution to the public good, which is a close proxy for efficiency. The results show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be reversed or undermined by corruption opportunities.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the presence of corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) can reverse the usual positive effect of institutional punishment on efficiency. The effect of punishment depends critically on whether leaders can be corrupted, the strength of the leader's punitive power, and the economic potential (mpcr). In standard IPGGs, stronger punishment increases efficiency, but in bribery-enabled games, stronger punishment can decrease efficiency. Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions. When using this paper for prediction, it is important to consider whether the game design allows for corruption/bribery, the strength of punishment, and the economic context. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported directly, but the relative changes are clear: punishment increases efficiency in standard IPGG, but decreases it when bribery is possible.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4-7 players; group size was controlled for in analyses.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Minimum of 10 rounds per treatment; participants did not know when the game would end to avoid end-game effects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous: players could divide their endowment between themselves, the public pool, and (in BG) bribes.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. mpcr (marginal per capita return) was manipulated as economic potential: 0.3 (low) vs 0.6 (high). Higher mpcr (economic potential) generally increased contributions, but the effect of punishment and corruption was moderated by mpcr.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was funded by fixed taxes extracted from all players; leaders could use these taxes to punish, but punishment was not personally costly to the leader.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (punishment multiplier) was manipulated: 1 (weak leader) vs 3 (strong leader). In standard IPGG, higher punishment multiplier increased contributions; in BG (with bribery), higher punishment multiplier decreased contributions.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants did not know when the game would end; after 10 rounds, they were told which round was the last. This was done to avoid end-game effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. In transparency treatments, players could see leader contributions (partial transparency) or all leader actions and anonymized contributions/bribes (full transparency). In other treatments, summary information was more limited.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not measured directly as group payoff or total welfare, but is proxied by mean contribution to the public good.
- The experiment is conducted in a Canadian subject pool, which may limit generalizability to other cultural or institutional contexts.
- The design models institutional punishment with a single leader and fixed taxes, which may differ from other PGG variants.
- Anti-corruption strategies (transparency, leader investment) are operationalized in specific ways that may not capture all real-world interventions.
- The effect of control (no punishment) on efficiency is not reported directly; all comparisons are relative to the standard IPGG (institutional punishment) control.
