## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that the structure of the punishment network strongly moderates the effect of peer punishment on both cooperation and efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group payoffs (efficiency) and higher contributions than the complete punishment network. The complete network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower efficiency, lower contributions, and lower punishment severity, likely due to diffusion of responsibility (bystander effect). The effect of punishment is thus not monotonic in network density: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency. The type of MPCR (high or low) also affects individual contributions, with high-MPCR individuals contributing more. The punishment mechanism is costly and binary (1 token cost, 3 token impact), and the experiment uses stranger matching over 15 rounds. No communication or chat is allowed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment network is crucial for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, especially with heterogeneous returns.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. When predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, it is not sufficient to know only the presence of punishment; the network structure (complete vs. incomplete, and the type of incomplete network) must be considered. In this design, incomplete networks (circle and homo-pairwise) outperform the complete network in both contributions and efficiency. The findings also show that punishment severity is lower in the complete network, likely due to a bystander effect. The experiment uses a four-player, 15-round, linear PGG with binary punishment, no chat, and random reassignment of MPCRs and groupings each round. These details should be matched when using this evidence for prediction. The results are most relevant for settings with heterogeneous returns and similar punishment mechanics.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject allocates 0-20 tokens between private and group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. MPCR is heterogeneous: two players per group have MPCR=0.3 (low), two have MPCR=0.7 (high). MPCR is randomly assigned and changes each round.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is binary: assigning punishment to a player costs 1 token to the punisher.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment assigned reduces the punished player's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is repeated for 15 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see the aggregate group contribution and their own payoff after stage one. In stage two, they see the contributions and MPCRs of connected group members (those they can punish). They do not see contributions of unconnected members or others' final payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed of the total number of punishments they assigned to and received from connected group members, but not the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment only studies four-player groups with specific punishment network structures; results may not generalize to larger groups or other network types.
- MPCR heterogeneity is limited to two values (0.3 and 0.7), and assignments are random and change each round.
- Punishment is binary (0 or 1 per target), not continuous as in some other PGG experiments.
- No communication or reputation effects are possible due to stranger matching and no chat.
- No control (no-punishment) condition is included in this experiment; all treatments involve punishment, so the effect of enabling punishment versus no punishment is not directly estimated.
- Subjects only observe the contributions and MPCRs of connected group members, not the whole group.
- The experiment is conducted in a single cultural and institutional context (National Taipei University, Taiwan), which may limit external validity.
