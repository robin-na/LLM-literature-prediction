Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group efficiency (payoff as a percent of optimal), group/joint returns, and rent accrual. Non-payoff outcomes such as investment/contribution, defection, and sanctioning frequency are also reported, but efficiency is the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper reviews a series of laboratory CPR experiments, including baseline (no punishment), communication, exogenous and endogenous sanctioning, and exogenous rule imposition. In baseline (no punishment) games, groups achieve low efficiency (e.g., 37% or less of optimal). When costly punishment is enabled, groups increase their efficiency (e.g., up to 39% of optimal in one set, and up to 94% in another with strong sanctions), but the net gain is reduced by the cost of punishment. Communication and endogenous rule-making (where groups set their own rules and sanctions) lead to the highest efficiency (up to 93% of optimal). Exogenous rules with imperfect monitoring can crowd out cooperation and reduce efficiency. The effect of punishment is sensitive to its cost and effectiveness, and is most positive when combined with communication or endogenous agreement.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in CPR-like games increases efficiency relative to control (no punishment), but the magnitude depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment. If punishment is costly and not highly effective, net efficiency gains may be modest or even offset by sanctioning costs. If punishment is strong (high impact per cost) or is combined with communication or endogenous rule-making, efficiency can approach optimal levels. For prediction, the presence and parameters of punishment (cost, tech), communication, and endogenous rule-making are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is typically low, and the introduction of punishment generally increases efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and/or combined with communication.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments use groups of 8 players (e.g., baseline, communication, sanctioning), sometimes 6 or 4 in specific designs. Player count is always specified.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds varies between 20 and 30 in baseline; communication and sanctioning treatments often use 10 rounds per phase.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Communication (face-to-face or electronic) is a key treatment variable; enabling chat/communication increases efficiency.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution/investment is continuous (subjects allocate tokens between options), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return is determined by a non-linear transformation function (e.g., F(sum x_i) = 23(sum x_i) - 25(sum x_i)^2); not a fixed MPCR but functionally equivalent.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost per unit is varied ($0.05 to $0.20); lower cost leads to more sanctioning and higher efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (fine per cost) is varied ($0.10 to $0.80 per $0.05-$0.20 cost); higher effectiveness increases efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the number of rounds in advance in most experiments.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Aggregate investment and group outcomes are shown after each round; individual actions are sometimes revealed in sanctioning treatments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. In some sanctioning experiments, the identity of the punished is revealed to the punisher (e.g., inspection), but not always to the group; in Casari and Plott (2003), the identity of the inspector is not revealed.

## Important Limitations
- The experiments use stylized laboratory CPR games, which may not capture all complexities of real-world public goods or CPR environments.
- Punishment is always costly and may not be as effective or as cheap as in some real-world settings.
- Reward mechanisms are not studied, so the results do not inform about the effects of positive incentives.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is sensitive to the cost and effectiveness parameters, which may differ in other settings.
- Most experiments use small groups and repeated interactions; results may not generalize to large or one-shot groups.
- Some efficiency gains from punishment are offset by the cost of sanctioning, so net efficiency may be lower than gross efficiency.
