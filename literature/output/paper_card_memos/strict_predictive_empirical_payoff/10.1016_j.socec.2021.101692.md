Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual payoffs (efficiency), as well as contributions and punishment behavior. Payoff outcomes are reported in detail for both the control (VCM) and punishment (PUN) conditions, in both Turkey and Germany.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 12 or 13 players; community size varies as players migrate between VCM and PUN, but maximum group size is 12 (Germany) or 13 (Turkey). MPCR table provided for n=2 to n=13.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 rounds in Turkey, 30 rounds in Germany. Most analysis focuses on first 20 rounds for comparability.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player chooses an integer amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR varies with group size: MPCR = 1.6/n, where n is the number of players in the community (see Table 2). For n=12, MPCR=0.13; for n=13, MPCR=0.12.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing 1 token costs the punisher 1 token and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 token spent reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment multiplier = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the total number of rounds in advance (20 in Turkey, 30 in Germany).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown individual contributions and payoffs of all members in both communities after each round; identities are anonymous.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players know how much punishment they received and from whom, but not the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment compares endogenous institution choice but does not provide exogenous assignment data for the Turkish cohort; only German exogenous data is available.
- Group sizes can vary within sessions as players migrate between institutions, which may affect MPCR and comparability across rounds.
- The number of rounds differs between countries (20 in Turkey, 30 in Germany), though most analysis is restricted to the first 20 rounds for comparability.
- The subject pools are university students, which may limit generalizability to broader populations.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results are specific to punishment-only environments.
