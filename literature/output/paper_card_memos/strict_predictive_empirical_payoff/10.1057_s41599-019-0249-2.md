## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a within-subjects lab experiment with 76 participants, five public goods game variants were tested: no punishment, peer punishment (individual/shared costs), and democratic punishment (individual/shared costs). All punishment systems increased cooperation rates relative to no-punishment. However, peer punishment (both cost structures) led to lower total payoffs than no-punishment, due to high punishment costs and antisocial punishment. Democratic punishment systems (both cost structures) achieved higher total payoffs than peer punishment, and were not significantly different from the no-punishment system in total payoff. Democratic punishment also reduced antisocial punishment and increased psychological benefits. Thus, punishment can increase efficiency relative to peer punishment, but not relative to no-punishment; only democratic punishment avoids the efficiency loss seen with peer punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends strongly on the institutional design. Peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to punishment costs and antisocial punishment. Democratic punishment systems (with voting) mitigate these costs, resulting in efficiency that matches the no-punishment baseline and exceeds peer punishment. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of voting are all critical moderators. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is high, enabling peer punishment may reduce efficiency, but enabling democratic punishment is likely to maintain efficiency at the control level. The presence of voting and the structure of punishment costs are key design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each participant played 50 periods (10 per system).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were computer-mediated and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: players could choose how many MUs (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Total public good multiplied by 1.6, then split among 4 players; mpcr = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment cost was either individual (punisher pays full cost) or shared (cost split among group members). Peer punishment with shared costs led to more antisocial punishment and lower efficiency.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each MU spent on punishment reduced the target's payoff by 3 MUs (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were fully informed about the duration (10 rounds per system, 50 total).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players were shown the contributions and payoffs of all group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players were informed about how much they had punished and been punished, but not about the identity of the punisher (anonymity maintained).

## Important Limitations
- Within-subjects design with a small sample size (N=76); a between-subjects design would be preferable to avoid carry-over effects.
- All participants were German university students; generalizability to other populations (especially non-WEIRD societies) is uncertain.
- Players remained in the same group of four across all rounds, with only player numbers shuffled; possible group-level carry-over effects.
- No causal inference between psychological constructs (e.g., satisfaction, justice, trust) and behavior can be drawn from the design.
- No individual difference variables (e.g., social value orientation, personality) were measured or analyzed.
