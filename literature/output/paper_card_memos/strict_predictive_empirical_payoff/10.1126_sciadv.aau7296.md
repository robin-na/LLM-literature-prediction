## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when individuals can choose between solving a shared problem collectively (public good) or individually (private solution), many opt for the individual solution even when collective action would be more efficient. This leads to inefficient resource allocation and coordination failure, especially at intermediate cost-benefit ratios. When peer punishment is introduced, it increases the rate of collective solutions but also triggers mutual punishment between individualists and collectivists, which reduces overall group welfare. Punishment does not reduce free riding but instead is used in feuds between incompatible strategies, amplifying inefficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that in public-goods-like games where individual solutions are available, enabling peer punishment can reduce efficiency (group welfare), even if it increases collective action. The effect is driven by wasteful punishment exchanges rather than improved cooperation. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency in similar settings, especially when individual solutions are a viable alternative to collective action. The findings are most relevant when the game allows both individual and collective solutions, and punishment is peer-based, costly, and has a strong impact.

## Important Limitations
- The experimental design is not a classic linear public goods game; it includes both individual and collective solution options, which may limit generalizability to standard PGGs.
- The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is specific to settings where individual solutions are available and may not generalize to classic PGGs without such options.
- The study uses a fixed group size (4) and a fixed number of rounds (10), so effects of these parameters are not tested.
- No communication or chat is allowed, which may affect coordination and punishment dynamics.
- The punishment mechanism is peer-based, costly, and has a strong impact (1:3 cost-to-impact ratio); results may differ with other punishment structures.
