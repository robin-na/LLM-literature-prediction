## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that peer punishment increases cooperation and group earnings in a standard public goods game. When higher-order punishment (counter-punishment) is allowed, it occurs frequently, especially in response to antisocial punishment, but does not significantly reduce the positive effect of punishment on contributions or efficiency. All punishment treatments (first-order and higher-order) result in higher average earnings than the baseline with no punishment. There is no evidence that higher-order punishment deters first-order punishment or reduces efficiency. The presence of higher-order punishment does not provide a net inducement to punish non-punishers.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 15 rounds, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) compared to no-punishment baseline. Adding a second or third stage for higher-order punishment (counter-punishment) does not significantly reduce efficiency or the positive effect of punishment. Thus, for similar game designs, the presence of punishment (even if punishers can be punished) is likely to increase efficiency relative to control. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a standard linear public goods game with four players and 15 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes, payoff structures, or real-world settings.
- The exact marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and punishment cost/effectiveness ratios are not specified in the main text.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- Laboratory setting may not capture all relevant social or psychological factors present in field environments.
