## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with endogenous institution choice, participants could choose between a sanctioning institution (with both positive and negative sanctions) and a sanction-free institution. Initially, most participants preferred the sanction-free institution, but over time, nearly all migrated to the sanctioning institution. In the sanctioning institution, cooperation and efficiency increased steadily, reaching over 95% of the maximum possible group payoff in the final period, while the sanction-free institution's efficiency fell to zero. The presence of punishment (negative sanctions) was the key driver of sustained high cooperation and efficiency. The study demonstrates that sanctioning institutions have a strong competitive advantage and can resolve the social dilemma by attracting and converting even initially noncooperative individuals.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game dramatically increases efficiency compared to a control condition without punishment, even when participants can freely choose between institutions. The design closely matches canonical PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is explicitly measured. The findings support strong positive predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in repeated games with migration or institution choice. The specific punishment cost and effectiveness parameters, as well as the presence of both positive and negative sanctions, are informative for parameterizing prediction models.

## Important Limitations
- Laboratory setting may not fully capture real-world institutional dynamics or long-term evolution.
- Group size per institution varies endogenously as participants migrate, which may affect comparability to fixed-group PGGs.
- The experiment allows both positive and negative sanctions, but only negative sanctions (punishment) are effective; results may differ in settings with only punishment or only reward.
- The exact marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is not numerically specified, limiting precise parameterization.
- Participants are university students, which may limit generalizability.
