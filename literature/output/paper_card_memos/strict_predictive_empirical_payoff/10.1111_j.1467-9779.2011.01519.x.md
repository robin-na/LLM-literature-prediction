Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual payoffs, probability of attack, and investments in X (collective), Y (private), and Z (alternative). The paper directly analyzes welfare and group payoff, and also reports on behavioral outcomes such as contributions and punishment/reward assignments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a laboratory experiment simulating international counterterrorism cooperation as a public good with a stochastic loss, the authors find that, absent institutional incentives, most participants defect and invest in private protection rather than the collective good. Introducing punishment (sanction) or reward institutions significantly increases investment in the collective account. However, while both punishment and reward reduce the probability of a loss event and increase first-stage payoffs, only reward institutions lead to higher overall welfare. Punishment increases cooperation but the direct costs of punishing (borne by both punishers and targets) more than offset the efficiency gains from increased cooperation, resulting in lower final payoffs than the baseline. Reward institutions, by contrast, increase both cooperation and final payoffs. Thus, punishment is not efficiency-enhancing in this environment, while reward is.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public-goods-like environment with costly punishment, enabling punishment increases cooperation but reduces overall efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The costs of punishment (to both punisher and target) outweigh the benefits from increased cooperation. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, one should expect a less positive or even negative effect on group payoff/welfare, especially when punishment is costly and the structure is similar to this experiment (n=4, 40 rounds, costly punishment, no identification of punishers, no chat, continuous contributions). The paper also shows that reward institutions can increase both cooperation and efficiency, in contrast to punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups consisted of 4 players (countries) per session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 40 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication between participants was explicitly prohibited.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0-20 UME per round per account), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. The marginal per-capita return is not linear but is determined by the reduction in probability of a stochastic loss (attack) as a function of total group investment in X. Each token in X reduces the probability of attack by a fixed amount (a/(n*d)).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is costly: e.g., 1 point costs 5 UME to punisher, reduces target's earnings by 20 UME; up to 10 points (max 150 UME cost, 600 UME loss to target).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 point costs 5 UME to punisher, reduces target's earnings by 20 UME (4:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward institution is a separate treatment; not present in baseline or punishment treatments.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is costly: 1 point costs 5 UME to rewarder, increases target's earnings by 20 UME; up to 10 points (max 150 UME cost, 600 UME gain to target).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 point costs 5 UME to rewarder, increases target's earnings by 20 UME (4:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed that the experiment lasted 40 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants were informed about each group member's contribution to X.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment and reward assignments were anonymous; participants could not identify who punished or rewarded them.

## Important Limitations
- The public good is not a standard linear VCM: contributions reduce the probability of a stochastic loss rather than directly increasing a group payoff.
- The marginal per-capita return is nonlinear and depends on group investment in X, making direct comparison to standard PGGs difficult.
- Punishment and reward are both costly, but the cost/impact ratio may differ from other studies.
- Punishment and reward are anonymous; no identification of the punisher/rewarder is possible.
- No communication or chat is allowed between participants.
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4) and 40 rounds; results may not generalize to other group sizes or time horizons.
- The context is framed as counterterrorism/global security, which may affect external validity for other public goods.
