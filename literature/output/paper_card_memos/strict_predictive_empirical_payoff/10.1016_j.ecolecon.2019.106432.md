# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.ecolecon.2019.106432

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=field_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=close; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- social efficiency score (ratio of actual group benefits to group benefits under social optimum)
- welfare gains over control group
- individual and group profits
Non-payoff outcomes:
- pumping hours (extraction behavior)
- user type classification (myopic, individual rational, social optimal, excessive, conservative)
- risk preferences (Holt-Laury lottery)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is pumping hours (extraction), but the paper explicitly calculates and reports social efficiency scores and welfare gains, which are payoff-based outcomes. User type and risk preferences are used as moderators.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 4 players, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each sequence (baseline and treatment) had 5 rounds; total of 10 rounds per group.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication was allowed only in the communication treatment; not allowed in punishment, reward, or control.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution (extraction) was continuous: participants chose between 0 and 8 hours in 0.5 hour steps.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Not a standard linear PGG; marginal returns are determined by a quadratic profit function and group extraction affects future costs. Parameters are specified in Appendix A.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 100 tokens per hour above the optimal extraction, applied with 1/5 probability (random monitoring). The cost is subtracted from the punished individual's profit.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is monetary, 100 tokens per hour above the optimal, with 1/5 chance of being monitored. Effectiveness is high, as shown by efficiency gains.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward treatment exists as a separate arm: 200 tokens for compliance, random monitoring.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is 200 tokens for compliance, paid with 1/5 probability. The cost is to the experimenter, not to other players.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is effective but less so than punishment; 200 tokens per compliant round, random monitoring.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of periods in each sequence was not announced beforehand, but was fixed at 5 rounds per sequence.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, the new groundwater level (group outcome) was announced to all; individual choices were private.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a CPR groundwater extraction game, not a standard linear PGG; results may not fully generalize to all PGG variants.
- Punishment and reward are externally imposed and randomly monitored, not peer-administered; effects may differ from endogenous peer punishment.
- The monitoring probability is low (1/5), and the magnitude of punishment/reward is moderate; effects of higher/lower probabilities or magnitudes are not tested.
- The number of rounds was not announced to participants, which may affect endgame behavior.
- Individual extraction choices are private, and only group-level resource status is public; this may limit social norm effects compared to fully transparent settings.
- Crowding-out of pro-social (social optimal) users is observed, suggesting interventions may reduce intrinsic motivation for some participants.
- The study is conducted in a specific real-world context (Bengaluru, India) with groundwater users; external validity to other populations or resource types may be limited.
