## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab public goods game with four players per group, the introduction of either individual or majority-voting punishment mechanisms increases average contributions relative to the no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether MPCRs are homogeneous or heterogeneous. However, group payoffs (efficiency) do not significantly differ between the no-punishment, individual punishment, and majority-voting punishment conditions. The cost of punishment offsets the gains from increased cooperation. The majority-voting punishment mechanism reduces the frequency of antisocial punishment compared to individual punishment, particularly in homogeneous groups, but antisocial punishment remains present in all conditions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds per condition, and either homogeneous (MPCR=0.5) or heterogeneous (MPCR=0.3/0.5/0.7) returns, enabling punishment (either individual or majority-voting) increases contributions but does not increase efficiency relative to the control. The cost of punishment absorbs the surplus from higher cooperation. Thus, when predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency unless the cost structure or punishment technology is more favorable. The majority-voting mechanism may reduce antisocial punishment but does not improve efficiency over individual punishment. The findings are most applicable to small, anonymous, stranger-matched groups with no communication and known round structure.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each condition (VCM, IP, MP) has 6 rounds (periods).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed; subjects seated with privacy partitions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects choose how many tokens (0-20) to contribute.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Homogeneous: all MPCR=0.5. Heterogeneous: MPCRs are 0.3, 0.5, 0.5, 0.7 per group. MPCR heterogeneity is a main treatment variable.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Individual punishment: 1 token cost per punishment assigned. Majority-voting: 1 token cost per group member (other than punished) if punishment is executed.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Individual punishment: 1 token cost reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens. Majority-voting: if majority votes to punish, punished subject loses 9 tokens (3x3), each other group member pays 1 token.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are told the experiment consists of three parts, each with 6 periods; number of rounds is known.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects are informed of their own payoff and aggregate group contributions. In punishment conditions, individual contributions of all group members are shown.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses stranger matching, so there are no repeated interactions with the same group members, which may limit generalizability to real-world settings with stable groups.
- The punishment mechanisms are implemented in a stylized, laboratory context with fixed costs and impacts; real-world punishment may involve different costs, effectiveness, and social dynamics.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results do not address the effects of positive incentives.
- The study does not consider the time or administrative costs of implementing punishment mechanisms, which may be important in organizational or community contexts.
- The number of rounds per condition is limited (6), which may not capture long-term dynamics.
- The experiment is conducted with university students in Taiwan, which may limit external validity.
