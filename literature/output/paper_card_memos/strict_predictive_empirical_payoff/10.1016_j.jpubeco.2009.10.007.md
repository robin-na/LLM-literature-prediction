## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment compares two versions of a public goods game with punishment: one where punishment is revealed after each round (Observed), and one where punishment is only revealed at the end of 10 rounds (Unobserved). Both treatments use random rematching, groups of 4, 10 rounds per set, and a standard VCM payoff structure (endowment 20, MPCR 0.4). Punishment costs 1 token per point assigned and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) average contributions are significantly higher in the Unobserved treatment than in the Observed treatment; (2) average income (efficiency) is also higher in the Unobserved treatment, though confidence intervals overlap in some periods; (3) the fraction of participants who punish and punishment expenditure are at least as high, or higher, in the Unobserved treatment; (4) punishment is more sensitive to undercontribution in the Unobserved treatment. The results show that costly punishment supports cooperation and efficiency even when it cannot have immediate strategic or reputational effects, suggesting an intrinsic motivation for punishment.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with punishment, the presence of a punishment option increases efficiency (average income) relative to the baseline with no punishment, and that this effect is robust to whether punishment is immediately observed or only revealed at the end. The game design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds per set, no chat, continuous contribution, no default, MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 1, punishment tech 3, no reward, random rematching, and full feedback on others' contributions. The results suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the effect is not dependent on immediate feedback about punishment. The paper is directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in standard PGGs with punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per game, fixed by design.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per set, with two sets per session; results focus on 10-round sets.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication allowed; explicitly prohibited in instructions.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: 0-20 tokens per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each token contributed increases each group member's payoff by 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment costs 1 token per deduction point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each deduction point reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment is available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are told the number of periods (10 per set) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants see the contributions of all group members; in Observed, they also see punishment received after each round.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; participants do not know who punished them, only the total received.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment does not include a no-punishment baseline, so the absolute effect of punishment versus no punishment on efficiency is inferred from prior literature, not directly measured here.
- The study is limited to a specific parameterization (group size 4, MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 1, punishment tech 3) and may not generalize to other settings.
- All participants are from Boston-area universities, which may limit generalizability.
- No reward mechanism is tested, so results do not speak to the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The effect of punishment is only tested under random rematching, not fixed groups or other matching protocols.
