## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab public goods game, groups of four played 10 rounds with or without punishment. In the 'Symmetric' treatment (all players have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment opportunities led to higher contributions and significantly higher group earnings (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the 'Asymmetric' treatment (two players have high, two have low returns), punishment also increased contributions, but the frequency and severity of counter-punishment and feuds rose sharply, leading to high punishment costs. As a result, group earnings in the Asymmetric treatment with punishment were not significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline. The study demonstrates that the efficiency benefits of punishment depend critically on the absence of normative conflict: when such conflict is present, punishment can fail to improve, or even reduce, efficiency due to costly feuding.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games depends on group homogeneity and the presence of normative conflict. In symmetric (homogeneous) groups, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. In asymmetric (heterogeneous) groups with normative conflict, enabling punishment increases cooperation but not efficiency, as punishment costs (especially from feuds) offset the gains. For prediction, the presence of heterogeneity in returns (or other sources of normative conflict) is a key moderator: control efficiency alone is not sufficient—one must also consider whether the game design induces normative conflict. Other design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, technology, player count) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable moderator is the symmetry/asymmetry of returns.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups have 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments have 10 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is continuous: players choose any amount from 0 to 20.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. In Asymmetric: two players have mpcr=0.5, two have mpcr=0.3. In Symmetric: all have mpcr=0.4. In No Feud: same as Asymmetric.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays E$1 per period to assign any number of punishment points; each point reduces recipient's earnings by E$1.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point costs E$1 to assign and reduces recipient's earnings by E$1 (1:1 ratio).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 10 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants see each group member's contribution, type (high/low return), and earnings. During punishment, they see who punished whom and by how much.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are identified by unique IDs; punishment assignments are shown by ID, so players can identify who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world or field contexts.
- The main moderator studied is normative conflict via heterogeneity in returns; other sources of conflict or heterogeneity are not explored.
- No communication is allowed; in real-world settings, communication may help resolve normative conflict and reduce feuding.
- The punishment technology is fixed (1:1 cost-impact ratio); effects may differ with other punishment structures.
- The time horizon is relatively short (10 rounds); longer games or repeated interactions may yield different dynamics.
- No reward mechanism is present; the interaction of rewards and punishment is not studied.
