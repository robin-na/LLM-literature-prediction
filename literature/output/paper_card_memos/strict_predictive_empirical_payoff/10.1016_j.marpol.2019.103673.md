# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.marpol.2019.103673

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- total group earnings
- coefficient of variation in earnings (inequality)
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average extraction (resource use)
- cooperation rate (implied by extraction levels)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average group earnings (payoff), extraction (resource use), and within-group earnings inequality. Extraction is a behavioral outcome, but earnings are directly analyzed and compared across treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 5 players (n=5) in every treatment.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 rounds pre-treatment, 14 rounds post-treatment (total 29 rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Chat/communication was allowed only in the communication treatment (face-to-face, 3 min per round). Not allowed in baseline or punishment treatments.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Extraction choice is continuous: each player chooses any integer between 0 and 20 units per round.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (alpha=20, n=5, b=10; MPCR = (alpha/n)/b = 0.4).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost is 2 Kuruş per unit extracted above quota (quota=1). This is the cost to the punished, not a peer punishment; penalty is imposed by the experimenter.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is perfectly enforced (100% probability), 2 Kuruş per unit above quota. The penalty is low relative to the theoretical optimum.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told in advance how many rounds the game would last, to avoid endgame effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each round, players saw their own extraction, own payoff, and the total group extraction, but not individual extractions of others.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment is imposed by the experimenter (external authority), not by peers; results may not generalize to peer punishment settings.
- Punishment level is low and perfectly enforced; effects of higher or probabilistic punishment are not tested.
- Communication is only face-to-face and only in one treatment; effects of other forms of communication (e.g., written chat) are not tested.
- The sample is small-scale Turkish fishers; generalizability to other populations or larger groups is uncertain.
- The market-based 'purchase of rights' treatment may not fully capture real-world ITQ or market mechanisms.
- Participants did not know the number of rounds, which may affect behavior compared to games with known endpoints.
- No reward or positive incentive treatments are included, so cannot compare punishment to reward.
