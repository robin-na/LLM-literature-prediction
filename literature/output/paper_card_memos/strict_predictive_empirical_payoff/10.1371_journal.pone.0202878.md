Non-payoff outcomes:

Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, exclusion rates, reciprocity) and payoff-based outcomes (mean payoff, total profit). The main efficiency-relevant outcome is mean payoff per treatment.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment compared four treatments in a fixed-order ROSCA game: baseline (no sanctions), punishment rule (forfeiture), voting (exclusion), and both punishment and voting. Exclusion by voting led to significantly higher contribution rates and mean payoffs than the other treatments. The punishment rule, which prevented non-contributors from receiving payouts, did not increase contributions or payoffs relative to the baseline. The combination of voting and punishment was less effective than voting alone. The results suggest that social exclusion based on reputation is a more effective mechanism for sustaining cooperation and improving group efficiency than the explicit punishment rule tested here.

## Prediction Guidance
For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper provides strong evidence that a costless, rule-based punishment (forfeiture of payout for non-contributors) does not improve efficiency or mean payoff compared to a baseline with no punishment. In contrast, exclusion by peer voting (reputation-based exclusion) substantially increases both contributions and efficiency. The game structure is a close variant of a public goods game (ROSCA), with fixed group size, known rounds, and full information about others' actions. The findings suggest that not all punishment mechanisms are equally effective: exclusion based on group voting is effective, but a non-costly, rule-based punishment is not. This distinction is important for predicting the effect of punishment in similar environments.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 4 players per round; 20 subjects per session, randomly assigned to 5 groups of 4 each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 10 rounds per session; each round consists of 4 periods (one for each player to receive payout).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice is binary: contribute 100 points or keep 100 points (all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each contribution of 100 points is given to the scheduled recipient; the effective 'mpcr' is 1 for the recipient, 0 for others, but the structure is not a standard PGG. Discounting applies to payouts depending on period.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment rule (forfeiture) is costless: non-contributors are denied payout, but no cost is paid by others to enforce this.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is highly effective in that it fully denies payout to non-contributors, but is only triggered for non-contribution before own payout. Despite this, it does not increase efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed in advance that there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each payout, all members are informed which members contributed and who received the payout.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All players know who contributed and who did not, and who is denied payout under the punishment rule.

## Important Limitations
- The game is a ROSCA, not a standard public goods game; only one player receives the group benefit per period, and the structure is sequential rather than simultaneous.
- Punishment is costless and rule-based (forfeiture), not costly or peer-administered as in many PGG studies.
- Exclusion is by majority voting, and excluded players can rejoin in future rounds, which differs from some PGG exclusion designs.
- Subjects are university students unfamiliar with real-world ROSCAs; results may not generalize to real ROSCA participants.
- The experiment does not test costly punishment or reward mechanisms.
- The number of rounds and group size are fixed and known, which may affect end-game behavior.
