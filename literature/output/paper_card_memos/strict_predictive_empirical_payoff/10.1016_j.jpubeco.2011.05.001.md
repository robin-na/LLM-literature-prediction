# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.jpubeco.2011.05.001

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average earnings
- group earnings
- efficiency (implied by earnings relative to full cooperation)
- total points earned
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution to public account
- voting behavior on sanction parameters
- conditional contribution schedule
- punishment parameter choices
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group and individual earnings (payoff), and contribution rates. Voting behavior and individual characteristics (IQ, political orientation, gender, cooperative orientation) are also analyzed as predictors of voting and contribution.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The paper finds that when groups in a linear public goods game are allowed to vote on the parameters of a formal sanction scheme (who is penalized, maximum penalty, and exemption level), most groups quickly learn to select efficient penalty rules that induce full cooperation and maximize group earnings. In the baseline (no sanctions), contributions and earnings decline over time as expected. In the penalty treatment, after voting, contributions and earnings rise and are sustained at high levels, with the median group achieving full cooperation and maximum efficiency. The effect is attributed to the ability of groups to endogenously select strong, well-targeted penalties for free-riding. Individual differences (cooperative orientation, IQ, political conservatism, gender) predict voting behavior, but do not prevent most groups from converging to efficient outcomes.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, direct evidence that enabling groups to endogenously design formal sanction schemes in a standard linear public goods game can transform a low-efficiency baseline into a high-efficiency outcome. The effect is robust to group heterogeneity and persists over repeated rounds. For prediction, the presence of a formal, group-voted punishment mechanism with high penalty rates and full exemption for full contributors is highly likely to increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, provided the group can select the parameters. The findings are most applicable to settings with 5 players, 24 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about group outcomes.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups of 5 players were used in all sessions.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 24 periods (rounds) per session, divided into six 4-period phases.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between subjects.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution decisions were continuous (0-20 points per round), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR (alpha) was set at 0.3 (marginal per capita return).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Formal sanctions were implemented as fines deducted from earnings; there was no cost to the punisher (the group or central authority), only to the punished individual.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (penalty per unit below exemption) was directly set by group vote; high penalty rates (a/b >= 0.7) led to full cooperation and high efficiency.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the total number of periods (24) and the phase structure.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects learned group outcomes (vote results, group-level penalty parameters, and summary information about contributions and earnings).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was formal and automatic; subjects knew the group-voted rules but did not know individual votes or who was punished (since punishment was rule-based, not assigned by individuals).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment only studies formal, centralized sanctions with no cost to the punisher; informal or decentralized punishment is not examined.
- The group size is fixed at 5, and results may not generalize to other group sizes.
- No communication or chat was allowed, which may limit external validity for real-world settings with discussion.
- The study does not include a reward mechanism, so cannot compare punishment to positive incentives.
- The penalty scheme is endogenous but constrained to a specific menu; real-world institutional design may be more complex.
- One group failed to converge to efficient rules, indicating possible limits to generalizability.
- The subject pool consists of undergraduates at a single university, which may limit generalizability.
