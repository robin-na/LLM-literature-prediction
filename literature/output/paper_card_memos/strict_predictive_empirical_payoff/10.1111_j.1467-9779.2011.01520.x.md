## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment implements a linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, and multiple endogenous punishment stages per round. Players can punish at a convex cost, and punishment reduces the target's payoff by 10% per point (up to 10 points). Two treatments are compared: BASIC (standard punishment) and OPINION (bystanders can approve/disapprove of punishment, but this has no direct payoff effect). The main findings are: (1) Average payoffs and efficiency remain above the Nash equilibrium and do not differ significantly between treatments or over time; (2) The opportunity for counter-punishment does not lead to a collapse in cooperation or efficiency; (3) Punishment is guided by an absolute full-contribution norm, and both punishers and bystanders apply similar standards; (4) Retaliation occurs but does not undermine overall efficiency; (5) The presence of bystander approval does not materially change efficiency or cooperation patterns. The results suggest that multi-stage punishment with changing groups can sustain cooperation and efficiency, even when counter-punishment is possible.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a four-player, ten-round linear PGG with continuous contributions, full information, and multi-stage punishment (with convex costs and 10% effectiveness per point), the introduction of punishment sustains or increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment Nash equilibrium. Efficiency remains stable and above the Nash level, and does not collapse even when counter-punishment is possible. The presence of bystander approval (OPINION treatment) does not significantly alter efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that in similar designs (with multi-stage punishment, convex costs, and changing groups), enabling punishment is likely to have a more positive effect on efficiency, and the risk of efficiency collapse due to counter-punishment is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in this class of games.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consists of 4 players per round; groups are randomly rematched each round.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. There are 10 rounds (repetitions) of the game.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 to 20 (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.4 (alpha = 0.4), with 4 players.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost is convex: 1 point costs 1, 2 points cost 2, 3 points cost 4, ..., 10 points cost 30 (see Table 1 and instructions).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the target's payoff by 10% of their interim payoff, up to 10 points (i.e., up to 100% reduction).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed that there are 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are shown all individual contributions, payoffs, and punishment assignments after each stage.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are informed about the identity numbers of those who assigned punishment points to them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4) and a specific mpcr (0.4); results may not generalize to other group sizes or mpcr values.
- Punishment is only possible after an explicit announcement, which may affect the frequency and nature of punishment compared to standard designs.
- No reward mechanism is present, so results do not address the interaction of punishment and reward.
- The design uses random rematching each round, which may reduce repeated-game effects compared to fixed groups.
- The OPINION treatment's bystander approval is non-binding and has no direct payoff consequences; effects of real social approval may differ.
- The study is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may limit external validity.
