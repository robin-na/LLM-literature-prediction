Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff

Non-payoff outcomes:

- fairness ratings
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are relative contribution (as a measure of cooperation), profit (group and individual), and subjective ratings. Profit is directly analyzed as a function of punishment system and asymmetry.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 4.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group played 24 periods (rounds), 6 under each of 4 punishment conditions.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; all interactions were computer-mediated and anonymous.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (participants could choose how much to contribute from their endowment).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 1.6/4 = 0.4 (contributions multiplied by 1.6, divided equally among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. For peer and democratic punishment: cost-to-fine ratio is 1:2 (1 MU spent to punish, 2 MUs deducted from target). For central punishment: cost is distributed among other group members, maintaining 1:2 ratio.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 2 MUs deducted per 1 MU spent (cost-to-fine ratio 1:2) for peer and democratic; central punishment is 0.6 MU per MU not contributed, with costs shared among others, maintaining 1:2 ratio.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were informed about the number of periods per punishment system and the total number of periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants were informed about each group member's contribution and income.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could not track individual identities across periods (IDs were shuffled), but could see how much each group member was punished in a given period.

## Important Limitations
- The status of advantaged/disadvantaged was randomly assigned and known to participants; results may differ if endowments are earned.
- The degree of asymmetry was moderate (40 vs 20 MUs); larger or more realistic asymmetries may yield different results.
- The sample consisted of university students from a Western country, limiting generalizability.
- The experiment used a fixed, relatively short number of rounds (24); longer games may yield different efficiency effects.
- No reward mechanism was tested; only punishment systems were compared.
