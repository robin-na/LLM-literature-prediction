## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, both punishment and reward mechanisms can maintain high levels of cooperation and efficiency compared to a control with no targeted interactions. However, reward leads to higher total payoffs than punishment, as punishment is costly and does not increase efficiency beyond what is achieved by reward. When both reward and punishment are available, groups that use reward more achieve higher payoffs, while groups that use punishment more achieve lower payoffs. The results suggest that positive reciprocity (reward) is more effective than negative reciprocity (punishment) for promoting efficiency in repeated public goods games.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with persistent group identity and known group membership, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to a control, but enabling reward is equally or more effective and leads to higher group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment alone should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no-punishment controls, but not as much as reward or combined reward/punishment treatments. The effect of punishment is less positive for efficiency than reward, and when both are available, punishment may even reduce efficiency due to its cost. The findings are most applicable to repeated, fixed-group, non-anonymous settings with continuous contribution choices and no communication.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted in a laboratory setting with groups of four and may not generalize to larger groups or field settings.
- The exact cost and effectiveness per unit of punishment and reward are not specified in the main text.
- Subjects were not told the total number of rounds, which may affect endgame behavior.
- The study does not examine the effect of anonymity or one-shot interactions, so findings may not generalize to those settings.
- The experiment does not allow analysis of whether the opportunity for public goods interaction changes the frequency of bilateral reward or punishment compared to a setting without the public goods game.
