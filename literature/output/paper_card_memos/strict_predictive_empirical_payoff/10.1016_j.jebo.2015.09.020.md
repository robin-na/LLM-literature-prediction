# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.jebo.2015.09.020

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings
- efficiency (group earnings relative to full cooperation)
- total punishment cost
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution
- punishment frequency and targeting
- counter-punishment rates
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are average group earnings (efficiency) and average contributions. Punishment patterns and counter-punishment are analyzed as mechanisms. Efficiency is directly measured as average group earnings relative to the maximum possible (full cooperation).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This paper experimentally studies how the availability of higher-order punishment (punishing punishers or non-punishers) affects cooperation and efficiency in repeated public goods games. The key findings are: (1) When higher-order punishment is enabled with full information (who punished whom), fixed group member IDs, and explicit display of past history, both cooperation and efficiency are significantly higher than in the control with only first-order punishment. (2) When higher-order punishment is enabled but information is ego-centric (subjects only see who punished them), with a dedicated counter-punishment stage and no history, efficiency is significantly lower than in the control. (3) In other treatments (e.g., with only one punishment stage or with history but ego-centric information), efficiency is not significantly different from the control. The main mechanism is that full information allows counter-punishment to be more strongly directed at anti-social or perverse punishers, supporting pro-social punishment and discouraging inefficient retaliation. The results show that the design of information and punishment opportunities moderates the effect of higher-order punishment on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect depends on the information structure and punishment design. If punishment is enabled with full information about who punished whom, fixed IDs, and explicit history, efficiency is likely to increase relative to the control. If punishment is enabled with only ego-centric information, a dedicated counter-punishment stage, and no history, efficiency may decrease. In other cases, the effect may be neutral. The findings suggest that the game design dimensions—especially information about punishment, identifiability, and history—are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between treatments are clear.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use 15 periods (rounds) of play.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: subjects allocate any amount of their endowment to the public good.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each unit contributed gives 0.4 to each of 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 point to reduce target's earnings by 3 points (cost=1 per unit).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (each point spent reduces target's payoff by 3).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the total number of periods (15) in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Varies by treatment: in FULL treatments, subjects see full information about all contributions and punishments; in EGO treatments, only information about own punishments. The effect direction is mixed: full information increases efficiency in some treatments, ego-centric information can reduce it.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Varies by treatment: in FULL treatments, subjects can identify who punished whom; in EGO treatments, only who punished them. Full identifiability supports higher efficiency when combined with history and higher-order punishment.

## Important Limitations
- All experiments are conducted in small groups (n=4) of undergraduate students in a laboratory setting, limiting generalizability to larger groups or field settings.
- The design does not allow for multiple rounds of punishment and counter-punishment within a single period (as in some 'feuding' studies), which may affect the dynamics of retaliation.
- No reward mechanism is studied, so results may not generalize to settings with both punishment and reward.
- The findings are specific to the information structures and punishment opportunities tested; other configurations may yield different results.
- Control efficiency (without any punishment) is not directly reported; all comparisons are relative to first-generation punishment treatments.
