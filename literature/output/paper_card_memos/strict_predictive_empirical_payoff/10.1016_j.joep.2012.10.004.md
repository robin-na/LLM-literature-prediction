## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average payoff
- surplus ratio (efficiency)
- society earnings
- payoff in punishment and non-punishment communities
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency and severity
- institution choice (acceptance of punishment institution)
- speed and stability of full participation in punishment institution
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are payoff-based (average payoff, surplus ratio, efficiency), but the paper also reports on contributions, punishment behavior, and institution choice.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subpopulation (society) consists of 12 participants. Groups are formed endogenously based on institution choice, but the initial pool is 12.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. The experiment consists of 30 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Communication is not allowed during the experiment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each player can contribute any amount from 0 to 20 tokens.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 1.6 divided by group size (number of group members in the community). For a group of 12, MPCR = 0.133; for smaller groups, higher.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token assigned costs the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment token assigned reduces the punished subject's payoff by 3 tokens (punishment effectiveness = 3).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants are informed that the experiment consists of 30 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants receive feedback about contributions, received punishment tokens, and payoffs in both communities. In SHT, additional social history is provided before the experiment.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players cannot identify who punished them. Display order is randomized each round to prevent identification.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment is conducted in a laboratory setting with student participants, which may limit generalizability to real-world social dilemmas.
- The endogenous institution choice design may not capture all aspects of real-world institution formation or enforcement.
- The effect of social history is tested only in the context of peer punishment; no reward or hybrid mechanisms are included.
- The group size is fixed at 12 per subpopulation, and the effect of varying group size is not explored.
- The study does not manipulate the punishment cost or effectiveness parameters; these are fixed throughout.
- The experiment does not allow communication or chat, so findings may not generalize to settings with communication.
