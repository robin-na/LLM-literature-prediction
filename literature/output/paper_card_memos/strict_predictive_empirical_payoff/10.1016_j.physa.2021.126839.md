## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games are played with 4 players per group.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consists of 15 periods (rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each subject allocates 0-20 tokens between private and group account.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Heterogeneous MPCR: two players per group have MPCR=0.3 (low), two have MPCR=0.7 (high). MPCR is randomly assigned and changes each period.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays 1 token per punishment assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Each punishment reduces the punished subject's payoff by 3 tokens per 1 token spent by punisher. The structure of the punishment network (who can punish whom) is systematically varied.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the game is repeated for 15 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects see the aggregate group contribution and their own payoff after stage one. In stage two, they see the allocation decisions and MPCRs of connected group members (as defined by the punishment network). They do not see contributions or punishments of unconnected members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects are informed of the total number of punishments they assigned to and received from connected group members, but not the identity of the punisher.

## Important Limitations
- No control condition without punishment is reported, so the effect of punishment vs. no punishment on efficiency cannot be directly estimated from this paper.
- The experiment uses only four-player groups and a specific set of punishment network structures; results may not generalize to other group sizes or network types.
- MPCR heterogeneity is limited to two values (0.3 and 0.7), and the assignment changes each period, which may affect the development of norms.
- Punishment is binary (0 or 1 per target), not continuous, which may limit the strength of punishment compared to other studies.
- No communication is allowed, which may affect generalizability to settings with communication.
- Subjects do not observe the contributions or punishments of unconnected group members, which may affect information effects.
- The study is conducted in a single cultural and institutional context (National Taipei University, Taiwan).
