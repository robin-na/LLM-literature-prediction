## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- total contributions to the public good
- final payoffs by income quintile
- Gini coefficient (inequality) after the game
Non-payoff outcomes:
- punishment assigned
- reward assigned
- contribution rates (absolute and percentage of income)
- taxation preferences (Experiment 4)
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are patterns of punishment and reward, and how these shift with income transparency. The paper also reports on total contributions, final payoffs by income group, and Gini coefficients, which are closely related to efficiency but does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments 2 and 3 use groups of 5 players in the public goods game.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Experiments 2 and 3 use 10 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous (any amount of income can be contributed).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contributions are doubled and divided equally among 5 players, so MPCR = 2/5 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment: pay 1 unit to reduce another's payoff by 3 units; can spend up to 2 units per other player per round.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (1 unit spent reduces target's payoff by 3 units).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is available in some conditions: pay 1 unit to increase another's payoff by 3 units.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward: pay 1 unit to increase another's payoff by 3 units.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 3:1 (1 unit spent increases target's payoff by 3 units).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts 10 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players see others' contributions and, depending on condition, their incomes. After each round, players see a summary of their payoff and sanctions received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players are identified by random letters that change each round; punishment/reward is anonymous and cannot be traced to a specific individual.

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency is not reported as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome; only total contributions and Gini coefficients are reported.
- Experiments are conducted online with Amazon Mechanical Turk samples, which are not nationally representative.
- The effect of punishment and reward is capped (maximum 2 units per other player per round), which may not reflect real-world sanctioning power.
- Punishment and reward impact is fixed in absolute terms, not proportional to income, which may affect the experience of sanctions across income levels.
- No direct manipulation or analysis of some game design dimensions (e.g., default contribution, chat, all-or-nothing choices).
