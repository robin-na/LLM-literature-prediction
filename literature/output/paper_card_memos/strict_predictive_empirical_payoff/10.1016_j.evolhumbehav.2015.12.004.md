# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.evolhumbehav.2015.12.004

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=close

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:
- amount entrusted in trust game (monetary benefit to punishers)
- earnings in PGG and trust game
- group earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- perceived trustworthiness
- group focus
- worthiness of respect
- liking
- punishment behavior
- contribution behavior
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are trust (amounts entrusted in a trust game after PGG with punishment), perceptions of punishers, and in some studies, actual earnings. Efficiency per se is not directly calculated, but group and individual monetary outcomes are analyzed, especially in relation to punishment and reputation.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Across four studies, the paper finds that (1) punishers are rated as more trustworthy and group focused than nonpunishers; (2) after repeated PGG rounds, punishers are trusted more and receive more money in trust games, but this effect is absent after a single round; (3) justified punishment (punishing free riders) leads to increased trust and monetary benefits, while unjustified punishment leads to reduced trust and earnings; (4) the reputational and monetary benefits of justified punishment can offset or even exceed the cost of punishing, especially in repeated interactions. The paper does not directly report group efficiency ratios, but does show that punishment can increase individual and group monetary outcomes via increased trust and cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated PGGs with punishment, justified punishment can increase the monetary benefits to punishers via enhanced trust and subsequent transfers, partially or fully offsetting the cost of punishment. The effect is contingent on repeated interaction (number of rounds) and the justification for punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in repeated PGGs (with visible identities and reputation) is likely to increase group efficiency and individual payoffs, especially when punishment is justified and recognized as such. In single-round games, these benefits do not materialize. The paper does not provide direct group efficiency ratios, but the monetary outcomes and trust game results are closely related and informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All PGGs were played in groups of 4 participants.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Number of rounds was manipulated: Study 2 used 1 round (no reputational/monetary benefit for punishers), Studies 1, 3, and 4 used 5 rounds (punishers gained trust and monetary benefits).
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: participants could contribute any amount from their endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (total contributions multiplied by 1.6, then divided among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each dollar spent on punishment reduces the target's payoff by $3 (punishment cost to punisher: 1 per 3 impact).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 unit cost to punisher reduces target's payoff by 3 units.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were never told how many PGG rounds to expect.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After contributing, participants found out what each other participant had contributed and kept.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. All players were informed after each round of who had punished whom in that round (except Study 1, where punishment was anonymous).

## Important Limitations
- Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible) is not directly reported; only individual and group monetary outcomes and trust game transfers are analyzed.
- The trust game is one-shot, so reputational benefits may be underestimated compared to repeated dyadic interactions.
- Sample sizes are moderate and drawn from undergraduate populations, limiting generalizability.
- No direct manipulation of some game design dimensions (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost) beyond the fixed values used.
- No reward mechanism is present, so interaction with rewards is not tested.
- Justification for punishment is coded post hoc in Study 4, which may introduce classification ambiguity.
- No direct measure of group-level efficiency or surplus; results are inferred from trust and earnings.
