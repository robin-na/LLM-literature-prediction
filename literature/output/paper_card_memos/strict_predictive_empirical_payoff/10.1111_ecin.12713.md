## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This lab experiment uses a linear public good game with peer punishment to study the effects of heterogeneity in endowment and punishment effectiveness, both separately and jointly. The main findings are: (1) Heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness alone does not affect efficiency or cooperation compared to symmetric groups. (2) Endowment heterogeneity (rich and poor members) reduces efficiency and cooperation, as rich members contribute a lower share of their endowment. (3) When both endowment and punishment effectiveness are heterogeneous (either the rich or the poor is the most effective punisher), efficiency and cooperation are not reduced compared to symmetry, and are higher than under endowment heterogeneity alone. (4) Among groups that achieve high cooperation, efficiency is higher in the 'Rich strong' treatment (richest is most effective punisher) than in the 'Poor strong' treatment, because less punishment is needed to sustain cooperation. (5) Overall, congruent heterogeneities (richest is most effective punisher) do not hinder and may even facilitate efficiency and cooperation compared to endowment heterogeneity alone.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public good games with peer punishment, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency compared to control conditions with endowment heterogeneity alone. However, the efficiency gain from punishment is moderated by the structure of heterogeneity: punishment is most effective (in terms of efficiency) when either all players are symmetric or when the richest player is also the most effective punisher. Endowment heterogeneity without matching punishment effectiveness reduces efficiency, but joint heterogeneities (especially congruent ones) restore efficiency to levels comparable to symmetry. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the alignment of endowment and punishment effectiveness. The control efficiency (without punishment) is lowest under endowment heterogeneity, and the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is largest in this case, especially if punishment effectiveness is also heterogeneous and congruent with endowment.

- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods (rounds) in all treatments.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat allowed between players.

- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness (punishment ratio) is manipulated: in some treatments, all have ratio 2; in others, ratios are 1, 2, or 3, assigned to different members. Heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness alone does not affect efficiency, but when combined with endowment heterogeneity, congruent heterogeneity (rich = strong punisher) increases efficiency among high-cooperation groups.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game lasts exactly 20 periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants are informed about the contributions of each group member and the punishment assigned/received.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is anonymous; players do not know who punished whom.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a linear public good game; results may not generalize to nonlinear public goods or common pool resource games.
- No communication is allowed between players, which may limit external validity for real-world settings where communication is possible.
- The participant pool is German university students; cultural factors may moderate the effects of punishment and heterogeneity.
- The study does not include a no-punishment control; all treatments involve punishment, so the absolute effect of enabling punishment versus no punishment is inferred from the literature, not directly measured here.
- The findings may not generalize to larger groups or to field settings with more complex social structures.
