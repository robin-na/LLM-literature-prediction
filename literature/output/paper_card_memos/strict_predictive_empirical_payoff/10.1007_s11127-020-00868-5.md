Outcome notes:
The main dependent variable is the average profit per period (efficiency), both with and without punishment. Contribution rates and voting behavior are also reported, but the focus is on payoff outcomes.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study replicates and extends classic public goods experiments by allowing subjects to experience both a punishment-enabled and a no-punishment institution, then vote on which to use for a high-stakes final round. Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. Only one session (out of 13) saw a majority vote for the punishment institution, and this was in a small cohort with high returns and high initial cooperation. Statistical analysis shows that subjects' votes are overwhelmingly determined by which institution gave them higher profits, not by which supported higher cooperation. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in matching protocol, order of experience, and marginal per-capita return. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard public goods games with costly voluntary punishment, enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, even though it increases contributions. The effect is robust across a range of game parameters (player count, MPCR, matching protocol, order, and returns). Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups were always of size 2 (pairs) in the main experiment; cohort size varied in random strangers treatments (6-16), but the game itself was always played in pairs.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each subject played 20 rounds (10 with each institution), followed by a high-stakes 21st round after voting.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players was allowed; anonymity was emphasized.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (subjects could choose how many tokens to contribute, not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. MPCR varied: in low return, each token contributed returned 0.6 to self and 0.6 to other (MPCR=0.6); in high return, 0.8 to self and 0.8 to other (MPCR=0.8). Higher MPCR increased contributions but did not reverse the negative effect of punishment on efficiency.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost schedule: 1 token to inflict 10% reduction, up to 30 tokens for 100% reduction. Cost to punisher is linear or convex depending on points assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the other's earnings by 10%; up to 10 points (100% reduction). Punishment effectiveness is thus 1 token cost for 10% reduction.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were told the number of rounds in advance (20 rounds, then a final round after voting).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects were shown a review of their earnings across periods in both environments before voting. During the game, they received feedback on contributions and payoffs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players could not identify who punished them (since games were played in pairs and matching was anonymous).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses pairs (groups of 2), which may not generalize to larger group sizes typical in some public goods games.
- Punishment is always costly and voluntary; no institutional or automatic punishment is studied.
- No reward mechanism is included, so results do not address the effect of positive incentives.
- The design does not allow for communication or reputation-building beyond the matching protocol.
- The findings may not generalize to field settings or to games with different punishment cost/impact structures.
- The study focuses on short- to medium-term laboratory interactions, not long-run or evolutionary dynamics.
