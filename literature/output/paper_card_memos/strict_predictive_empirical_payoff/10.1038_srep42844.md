## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Nine rounds of PGG, plus a one-shot round after interventions removed.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; all interactions were via computer interface.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution was continuous: each player could contribute any amount from 0 to 20 MUs.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR = 0.5 (contributions doubled and split among 4 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 MU to reduce other's earnings by 3 MUs (1:3 ratio).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `less_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1 MU spent reduces recipient by 3 MUs.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants could also reward (add to) others' earnings at same cost/benefit ratio as punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward cost: 1 MU to increase other's earnings by 3 MUs.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness: 1 MU spent increases recipient by 3 MUs.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were not told in advance how many rounds the PGG would last.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, participants could see the contributions and earnings of all group members.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In the public material sanctions condition, participants could see who punished/rewarded them; in the private condition, they could not. No difference found between public and private for main outcomes.

## Important Limitations
- Lab experiment with student participants; generalizability to field or larger groups is uncertain.
- Punishment and reward cost/impact ratios are fixed; effects may differ with other parameterizations.
- Participants did not know the number of rounds in advance, which may affect cooperation dynamics.
- No chat or rich communication; only structured feedback (moral judgment or material sanction) was allowed.
- Efficiency effects are specific to the cost structure of punishment/reward used in this study.
