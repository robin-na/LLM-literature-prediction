Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- welfare (measured as net profits)
- average earnings
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution levels
- punishment assigned
- probability of punishment
- level of punishment (conditional on punishing)
- anti-social punishment
- coordination failures
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are net profits (payoff-based), contributions, and punishment behavior. Efficiency is operationalized as net profits relative to the maximum possible. Anti-social punishment and coordination process are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Groups consisted of either 4 individuals or 4 teams of 3 players each (i.e., 4 decision units per group, but team treatments have 12 participants per group).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each repeated game part had 10 rounds (periods 1-10 without punishment, 11-20 with punishment).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or direct communication allowed between team members or between groups; only structured proposal entry for team coordination.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (integer tokens from 0 to 20), not all-or-nothing.
- default contribution framing: The paper directly informs this dimension. No default contribution; participants had to actively enter their proposal (0-20 tokens).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) was 0.4 per token contributed, with 4 decision units per group.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishing another player/team by reducing their payoff by 3 tokens cost the punisher 1 token.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment technology: 1 token spent reduces target's payoff by 3 tokens (3:1 ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants knew the number of rounds in advance (10 rounds per part, with a surprise restart for the punishment phase).
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, participants received feedback on their own results and anonymized information on the decisions of other individuals/teams.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; players could not identify which individual/team punished them.

## Important Limitations
- No direct communication (chat) was allowed between team members; only structured proposal entry, which may limit generalizability to real-world teams.
- Punishment was only possible at the team level (not to individual team members), so the 'innocent bystander' effect may influence results.
- The experiment was conducted in a lab setting with student participants, which may limit external validity.
- No reward mechanism was included, so results pertain only to punishment, not to positive incentives.
- The team decision process was limited to majority or unanimity rule without face-to-face interaction.
- The surprise restart for the punishment phase may have influenced behavior.
