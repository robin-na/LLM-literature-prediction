## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

## Important Limitations
- The study only examines punishment-based power; no reward or mixed sanctioning systems were tested.
- Leaders were assigned based on measured social value orientation, not elected or selected by group members, which may differ from real-world group dynamics.
- The experiment used a fixed group size (4) and a specific MPCR (0.5); results may not generalize to other group sizes or payoff structures.
- The number of rounds was not known to participants, which may affect endgame behavior.
- No communication was allowed, which may limit generalizability to real-world settings where discussion is possible.
- The study does not examine hybrid systems where both leaders and peers can punish, or where leaders can be sanctioned by the group.
