The paper reports both payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, wealth) and behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment, institution choice). Efficiency is explicitly discussed, especially in the context of punishment and reputation treatments.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Punishment opportunities in public goods games increase cooperation and contributions, but typically reduce efficiency and group payoffs in standard (short) experimental settings due to the destruction of resources through costly punishment. In most experiments with 10 or fewer rounds, average payoffs are lower with punishment than without. However, in longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when punishment is combined with effective reputation mechanisms, efficiency can be higher with punishment. Endogenous institution choice leads most participants to eventually select punishment institutions, which then sustain high cooperation, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed unless the time horizon is long or reputation is leveraged. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed and highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as number of rounds and the presence of reputation mechanisms.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive or negative, but depends critically on the number of rounds (time horizon) and the presence of reputation or endogenous institution choice. For standard short games (10 rounds or fewer), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to control. For longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when combined with reputation mechanisms, punishment can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of endogenous institution choice, which can foster high cooperation and, under the right conditions, higher efficiency. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the number of rounds and the presence of reputation or endogenous choice mechanisms are key moderators.

- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Typical group size is 4 in the main referenced experiments (e.g., Fehr and Gächter 2000, 2002), but some studies vary this. The effect of player count is not directly analyzed for efficiency.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Number of rounds is a key moderator: 10-round games typically see punishment reduce efficiency; 50-round games see efficiency gains from punishment.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous (0 to endowment), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is described as alpha/n, with alpha > 1 and alpha/n < 1. Specific values not always given, but the structure is standard.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment typically costs 1 MU to the punisher and reduces the target's payoff by 3 MUs (e.g., Gürerk et al. 2006). The costliness of punishment is central to the efficiency results.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is typically 3:1 (target loses 3, punisher pays 1). The destructive nature of punishment is key to efficiency outcomes.
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Some experiments allow for positive sanctions (rewards) as well as punishment (e.g., Gürerk et al. 2006). The main efficiency results focus on punishment.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward typically costs 1 MU to the rewarder and gives 1 MU to the recipient (Gürerk et al. 2006).
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward effectiveness is 1:1 (recipient gains 1, rewarder pays 1).
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. In most experiments, the number of rounds is known in advance (e.g., 10, 30, or 50 rounds). Some referenced studies (Yamagishi 1986, Ostrom et al. 1992) did not specify the number of periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Players are informed about the contributions of each group member after each round. Some institution design experiments manipulate the level of information (e.g., only average contribution shown).
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment is typically anonymous, but players know how much each member was punished. In some experiments, reputation mechanisms allow for identification.

## Important Limitations
- Most efficiency results are based on laboratory experiments with student subjects and may not generalize to field settings.
- The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is most robust in short games; longer games or additional mechanisms (reputation, endogenous choice) can reverse this effect.
- The paper synthesizes results from multiple studies, so not all findings are from a single experimental design.
- Some dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR) are not systematically varied for efficiency analysis.
- The interaction between punishment and other institutional features (e.g., communication, framing) is discussed but not always experimentally isolated.
