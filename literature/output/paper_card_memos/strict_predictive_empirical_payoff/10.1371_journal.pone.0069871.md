Primary outcome type: mixed
Payoff-related outcomes:

- profit as proportion of the theoretical optimum
- aggregate benefit of players
Non-payoff outcomes:
- proportion of cooperative moves
- exit proportion
Outcome notes:
The paper reports both behavioral outcomes (cooperation rate, exit rate) and payoff-based outcomes (final profit, efficiency as share of optimum).

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a modified public goods game with six players per group and ten rounds per treatment, the introduction of punishment (a 5 MU fine for non-cooperation) significantly increased cooperation and efficiency compared to the control (no incentives). However, the highest efficiency (profit as a proportion of the theoretical optimum) was achieved when both voluntary participation (exit option) and positive incentives (5 MU reward for cooperation) were present. Punishment alone (No-Neg) led to 67% of the optimum, while reward plus exit (Ex-Pos) led to 69%. The control (No-Null) and punishment plus exit (Ex-Neg) both yielded 58%. Thus, while punishment increases efficiency over the control, it is less effective than the reward-plus-exit combination. The paper also finds that exit alone has little effect, and that the interaction between exit and incentives is important for maximizing efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with six players, ten rounds, no communication, and fixed institutional punishment (5 MU fine per defection), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control (no punishment), but the efficiency gain is smaller than that achieved by combining rewards and voluntary participation. The efficiency effect of punishment is positive but not maximal. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, fixed MPCR, fixed punishment cost/tech, no reward in punishment-only treatments, no identification of punishers, and full information about group outcomes) are all specified, making this paper highly informative for predicting efficiency under similar conditions.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All games were played in groups of 6 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each treatment lasted 10 rounds (after 10 introductory rounds).
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication was allowed between participants.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was binary: help (contribute) or not help (defect).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each help costs 10 MU and gives 20 MU to the other 5 players (4 MU each), so MPCR = 4/10 = 0.4.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is a 5 MU fine for non-cooperation, imposed by an external institution at no cost to other players.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment is perfectly enforced: 5 MU fine per defection, no cost to punisher (external institution).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward (5 MU bonus for cooperation) is present in reward treatments, absent in punishment-only treatments.
- reward cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is provided by an external institution at no cost to other players.
- reward technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Reward is perfectly enforced: 5 MU per cooperation, no cost to others.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants were told the number of rounds in advance.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, the aggregate group result was announced, but not individual identities.

## Important Limitations
- Punishment and reward were administered by an external institution, not by peers, so results may not generalize to decentralized punishment settings.
- The magnitude of incentives was intentionally set below the threshold needed to change the dominant strategy, which may limit generalizability to stronger incentive regimes.
- The contribution decision was binary (help or not), not continuous, which may affect comparability to standard continuous PGGs.
- No communication or reputation mechanisms were allowed, which may limit external validity.
- The experiment used student participants in a lab setting, which may not fully capture real-world commons dilemmas.
