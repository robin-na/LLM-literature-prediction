## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
Peer punishment increases contributions in linear, nonlinear, and CPR public goods games, but does not significantly improve efficiency (payoff relative to Nash and social optimum) in any environment. In nonlinear environments, the effect of punishment on cooperation is weaker and takes longer to emerge. Punishment is less intense in nonlinear settings, likely because it is harder for subjects to identify free riders. In the CPR environment, punishment cannot fully enforce the social optimum, but can help stabilize cooperation at suboptimal levels that reduce the gains from defection. Overall, the costs of punishment erode the potential efficiency gains from increased cooperation.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that, in standard lab public goods and CPR games with 4 players, 10 rounds, and standard peer punishment (cost 1, impact 3, anonymous), enabling punishment increases cooperation but does not reliably increase efficiency. The effect is robust across linear and nonlinear environments, but is weaker and slower in nonlinear settings. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is less costly or more effective than in this design. Control efficiency is a strong predictor of treatment efficiency, as punishment does not reliably improve group payoffs. Game design dimensions such as nonlinearity, complexity, and the ability to identify free riders moderate the effect of punishment on both cooperation and efficiency.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments used groups of 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments used 10 periods (rounds), known in advance.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices were continuous (0-12 tokens), not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Linear VCM: MPCR = 0.5; Nonlinear VCM: MPCR varies (1.25 for first 3 tokens, 0.5 for next 7, 0.2 for last 2); CPR: not directly MPCR, but analogous structure.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point cost the punisher 1 experimental dollar.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the recipient's payoff by 3 experimental dollars.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. The total number of periods (10) was common knowledge and announced in the instructions.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each period, subjects observed the allocations by all individuals in their group.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was anonymous; subjects did not know who assigned deduction points to them.

## Important Limitations
- The experiment uses a fixed group size (n=4), fixed number of rounds (10), and a specific punishment cost/impact structure; results may not generalize to other parameterizations.
- Punishment is anonymous and only negative (no rewards), which may limit external validity for environments with different sanctioning institutions.
- The time horizon is relatively short (10 rounds), which may affect the emergence of long-run efficiency effects.
- The study is conducted in a laboratory setting with student subjects, which may not fully capture field behavior.
- Nonlinear environments are more complex, and the results suggest that complexity reduces the effectiveness of punishment, but the specific mechanisms are not fully disentangled.
