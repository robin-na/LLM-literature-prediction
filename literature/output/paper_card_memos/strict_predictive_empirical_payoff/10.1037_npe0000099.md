## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The study finds that peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public good game compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect holds even when punishment is delayed by five periods, as long as participants receive salient feedback linking punishment to their past contributions. If this feedback is absent (i.e., participants are punished after a delay and are not reminded of their past behavior), punishment still increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, due to increased punishment costs and less targeted norm enforcement. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency thus depends on the informational environment, specifically whether punished individuals can clearly associate punishment with their own prior actions.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with four players and 20 rounds, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, provided that feedback links punishment to past contributions. The effect is robust to a five-period delay in punishment, as long as feedback is salient. If the design omits such feedback, punishment may still increase cooperation but not efficiency, due to higher punishment costs and less effective norm targeting. For prediction, the presence or absence of feedback linking punishment to behavior is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is lower than treatment efficiency (with punishment and feedback), but not when feedback is absent. Other design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are held constant in this study, so the main moderator evidenced is the feedback/identification of norm violation.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All treatments use fixed groups of 4 players (partner matching).
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 20 periods in all treatments. Punishment is only possible in periods 1-15.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution is continuous: each participant decides how many of 20 ECUs to contribute (0-20).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each 1 ECU contributed yields 0.4 ECU to each of 4 group members).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punisher pays a fee for each punishment point assigned: cost schedule is nonlinear (e.g., 1 point = 1 ECU, 2 = 2, 3 = 4, ..., 10 = 30 ECUs).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduces the punishee's contribution-stage payoff by 10%.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants know the game has 20 periods; design is standard repeated game.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Participants are informed about the contributions of their group members before the punishment stage. In some treatments, feedback about past contributions is provided at the time punishment is received (critical for efficiency gains).

## Important Limitations
- All design dimensions except feedback about punishment and delay are held constant; results may not generalize to other MPCRs, group sizes, or punishment cost/tech settings.
- The study uses fixed groups of four and a linear PGG; findings may not extend to other social dilemma structures or group sizes.
- No reward mechanism is tested; only punishment is considered.
- The effect of punishment on efficiency is shown to depend on the presence of salient feedback linking punishment to past contributions, but the study does not systematically vary other informational or institutional features.
- The punishment cost schedule is nonlinear and may not generalize to other punishment cost structures.
