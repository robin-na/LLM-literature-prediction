Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:

- group payoff
- individual payoff
- average stock size (resource sustainability)
- depletion cases
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution/harvest decisions
- communication behavior
- trust and cooperation perceptions
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are efficiency (ratio of actual to optimal group earnings), group and individual payoffs, and resource stock levels. Non-payoff outcomes such as communication and trust are also measured but are not the primary focus.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment demonstrates that introducing a sanctioned quota system (with costly punishment for exceeding quotas) leads to significantly higher efficiency in resource use compared to a baseline with no intervention. Information about ecological thresholds also improves efficiency, but not as much as punishment. Combining both interventions does not yield efficiency gains beyond punishment alone. The results are robust across multiple measures (efficiency, group payoff, resource stock, depletion cases) and are supported by regression analysis controlling for individual and group characteristics. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in CPR games with similar design features.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that enabling punishment (in the form of sanctioned quotas with costly, probabilistic enforcement) increases efficiency in a dynamic, multi-round, continuous-harvest CPR game with communication allowed. The effect is substantial: average efficiency rises from 0.42 (baseline) to 0.71 (punishment) and 0.77 (punishment+information). The design closely matches public-goods-game-like environments, though the context is a renewable resource with dynamic feedbacks. The results suggest that, for similar games with 4 players, communication, and continuous contribution/harvest, enabling punishment is likely to yield a large positive effect on efficiency, even when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The effect is robust to the presence of communication and does not depend on end-game knowledge. The paper also provides evidence that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) and punishment cost/tech are important, but the main result is that punishment is highly effective in this context.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players per session.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Number of rounds was not fixed or known to participants; the game ended after 1 hour or when the resource was depleted. This approximates an infinite horizon.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. Face-to-face communication was allowed at any time except during individual decision-making. All groups used communication.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Harvest/contribution decisions were continuous (participants could choose any number between zero and the current stock). Not all-or-nothing.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each harvested elephant was worth $0.25 to the individual; each unit of pasture was worth $0.10 to the group. The marginal per-capita return is not a single number but is determined by the payoff structure and resource dynamics.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment was probabilistic (2/3 chance if quota violated); if caught, the individual forfeited current harvest and was banned from harvesting for one round (moratorium). No monetary fine. Cost is opportunity cost of lost harvest and moratorium.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. It suggests punishment is `more_positive` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: if caught, the individual loses all current harvest and is banned for one round. Probability of being checked is 2/3 if quota is violated. This is a strong, targeted punishment.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Participants did not know the total number of rounds; the game ended after 1 hour or resource depletion. This was designed to avoid end-game effects.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, group and individual harvests, new stock sizes, and payoffs were communicated to all participants.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was individual and public: if a participant was caught, the group knew who was punished (dice roll determined who was checked).

## Important Limitations
- The experiment was conducted in a specific cultural and ecological context (rural Zimbabwean communities), which may limit generalizability to other populations.
- The CPR game is a dynamic renewable resource game, not a classic linear public goods game; results may differ in static or non-renewable settings.
- Punishment was probabilistic and non-monetary (moratorium and harvest forfeiture), which may differ from monetary punishment in other studies.
- All groups had communication enabled, so the effect of punishment without communication is not tested.
- The number of rounds was not fixed or known to participants, which may affect comparability to fixed-horizon games.
- No reward mechanism was tested, so results do not speak to reward vs. punishment tradeoffs.
