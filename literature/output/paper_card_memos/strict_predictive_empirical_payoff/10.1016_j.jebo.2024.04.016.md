# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.jebo.2024.04.016

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group payoff
- efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation)
- average contribution (as a proxy for efficiency, with explicit mapping to payoff)
- percentage of groups achieving high payoffs
Non-payoff outcomes:
- contribution rate
- punishment frequency
- scheme choice (formal vs informal)
- voting behavior
- pro-social and anti-social punishment
Outcome notes:
The primary dependent variables are group payoff and efficiency, with extensive reporting of average payoffs, efficiency, and the proportion of groups reaching high payoff thresholds. Contribution rates are also reported, but are explicitly linked to payoff via the game’s payoff function. Non-payoff outcomes such as punishment behavior and scheme choice are also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
In a repeated linear public goods game with five players and MPCR 0.4, the introduction of punishment mechanisms (either formal, with a fixed administrative cost and group-voted sanction rate, or informal, with peer-to-peer costly punishment) leads to large and significant increases in group efficiency and payoffs. Without punishment, free riding is prevalent and efficiency is low. With punishment enabled, average group payoffs rise significantly, and a much higher proportion of groups achieve high payoff thresholds. The effect is present regardless of whether participants’ self-regulatory resources are depleted. Formal punishment, when implemented with a deterrent sanction rate, achieves almost full efficiency. Informal punishment also increases efficiency, though not as much as formal deterrent punishment. The act of voting on institutions or sanction rates does not itself account for the efficiency gains; the presence of the punishment mechanism is the key driver.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard repeated public goods game. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward, etc.) are all clearly specified and match the canonical PGG setup. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while enabling punishment (either formal or informal) produces large, robust increases in efficiency and group payoff. The results are robust to manipulations of self-regulatory resources and to whether the punishment institution is chosen endogenously or imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, the findings support a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, with formal deterrent punishment achieving near-full efficiency and informal punishment producing substantial, though somewhat lower, efficiency gains.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All main treatments use groups of 5 players, chosen to avoid tied votes in institutional formation.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Part 2 consists of 24 rounds (6 phases of 4 rounds each); Part 1 is 4 rounds. Total rounds per subject: 28.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat is allowed between players; design is standard for PGG.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choices are continuous from 0 to 20 points (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. MPCR is 0.4 (each contributed point returns 0.4 to each of 5 players).
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS (peer) punishment: 1 point cost to punisher, 3 point loss to punished per unit. In FS (formal) punishment: cost ratio is also 1:3 (with additional fixed administrative cost of 5 points per period for FS).
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is 3:1 (punished:punisher) in both IS and FS schemes. In FS, group votes on sanction rate (0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism is available; only punishment is possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects know the number of rounds/phases in advance; standard repeated game with known horizon.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects are shown all group members’ contributions and, in punishment phases, punishment assignments.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. In IS (peer) punishment, subjects see who punished whom (identities within group are known for punishment assignment). In FS, punishment is impersonal (applies to all free riders).

## Important Limitations
- All results are for groups of 5 players, MPCR 0.4, and 24-round repeated games; generalizability to other parameterizations is not tested.
- No communication is allowed; effects of chat or communication are not addressed.
- No reward mechanism is present; only punishment is studied.
- Cultural and population generalizability is limited (Japanese university students).
- The formal (FS) and informal (IS) punishment schemes differ in cognitive demands; depleted subjects may prefer FS to save mental energy, not just as a commitment device.
- Perfect observability and no enforcement errors are assumed; real-world noise or errors may affect outcomes.
- The study does not vary player count, MPCR, or punishment cost/tech across treatments; only the presence/absence of punishment and self-regulatory resource depletion are manipulated.
