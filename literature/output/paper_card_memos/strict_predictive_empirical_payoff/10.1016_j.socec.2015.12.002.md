# Paper Memo: 10.1016_j.socec.2015.12.002

## Study Type
empirical; empirical subtype=experimental; experimental subtype=lab_experiment

## Task Relevance
PGG or variant=exact; punishment or sanctions=exact; efficiency or payoff outcome=exact

## Outcomes Measured
Primary outcome type: efficiency_or_payoff
Payoff-related outcomes:
- average group earnings (after punishment costs)
- average group earnings (before punishment costs)
- earnings by endowment type (high/low)
- proportion of social optimum achieved
Non-payoff outcomes:
- average contribution
- contribution by endowment type
- punishment assigned
- punishment received
Outcome notes:
The main dependent variables are group earnings (efficiency) and average contributions. Earnings are reported both before and after punishment costs, and by endowment type. Punishment behavior is also analyzed.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
The experiment finds that peer punishment increases contributions and group earnings in public goods games with homogeneous endowments, but not in games with heterogeneous endowments. In homogeneous groups, punishment mitigates the decay of cooperation and raises average contributions and pre-punishment earnings, though post-punishment earnings are lower due to the cost of punishment. In heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase contributions or earnings, and punishment costs further reduce efficiency. The results suggest that normative conflict arising from endowment heterogeneity undermines the effectiveness of peer punishment as a self-governance mechanism for promoting efficiency.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on endowment homogeneity. When endowments are homogeneous, enabling punishment increases contributions and can increase efficiency (though punishment costs may offset gains). When endowments are heterogeneous and endowment information is not available in the punishment stage, enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency, and punishment costs reduce group earnings. For prediction, the presence of endowment heterogeneity (without endowment information in punishment) should be expected to moderate or eliminate the positive effect of punishment on efficiency, even if control efficiency is known. The game is a continuous-contribution, interior-solution PGG with 4 players, 15 rounds, no chat, and punishment cost/impact of 1:3.

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. All groups had 4 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. 15 paid periods (rounds) per session.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No chat or communication allowed; instructions used neutral language.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous (not all-or-nothing).
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Nonlinear (interior solution) PGG: payoff function is quadratic in total contributions. At the Nash equilibrium, marginal per capita return is 1.5 (a=6, n=4, b=0.025), but it decreases as total contributions increase.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost: 1 ED to reduce another's payoff by 3 EDs.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment effectiveness: 1:3 (1 ED spent reduces target by 3 EDs).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism; only punishment was available.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Subjects knew the game would last 15 paid periods.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, subjects saw the individual contributions of each group member (by random ID), but not their endowment type.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment was assigned to random IDs; subjects could see who punished whom by ID, but IDs were randomized each round to mitigate reputation effects.

## Important Limitations
- Endowment heterogeneity was exogenously assigned and relatively modest; results may differ with larger or endogenous heterogeneity.
- No information about endowment type was provided during the punishment stage; this may limit the generalizability to settings where such information is available.
- The experiment used a student sample from a single university, which may limit external validity.
- Punishment costs may outweigh efficiency gains, especially in heterogeneous groups.
- The game used a continuous, interior-solution PGG, which may differ from linear or all-or-nothing PGGs.
