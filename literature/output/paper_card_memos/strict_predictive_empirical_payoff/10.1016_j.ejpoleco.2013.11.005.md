## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This laboratory experiment tests whether adding intermediate contribution targets ('milestones') to a cumulative threshold public goods game increases efficiency, measured by group payoffs and the probability of reaching a final target. The game is played in groups of 5 over 6 rounds, with treatments varying the strictness of milestones, the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), and the probability of group loss if targets are missed. The main findings are: (1) In a scenario with no continuous efficiency gains (i.e., only reaching the target matters), strict milestones increase both contributions and the probability of reaching the target, thus enhancing efficiency. (2) In a scenario with continuous efficiency gains (i.e., contributions always increase payoffs), strict milestones actually reduce average contributions and efficiency, as groups focus on meeting milestones rather than maximizing payoffs. (3) In an intermediate scenario, the positive and negative effects cancel out. The effect of milestones is thus context-dependent, and their efficiency impact is not universally positive.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment-like milestone mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like games is highly sensitive to the underlying payoff structure. When the only way to generate group payoffs is to reach a threshold (no continuous efficiency gains), strict milestones (punishment for missing intermediate targets) can improve efficiency by increasing coordination and contributions. However, when continuous efficiency gains are present (i.e., contributions always increase payoffs), strict milestones can reduce efficiency, as they may distract from maximizing group welfare. For prediction, this means that the presence and structure of milestone/punishment mechanisms, the marginal per-capita return, and the risk of group loss must all be considered. Control efficiency (without milestones/punishment) is a strong moderator: if control efficiency is already high, milestones may not help and can even hurt. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions in cumulative threshold public goods games with milestone/punishment features.

- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat between players is allowed.

The probability of loss (p) is manipulated: 0.5 in B and S, 1/3 in P.
- punishment technology: The paper gives an indirect but usable signal on this dimension. It suggests punishment is `mixed` under this dimension. Punishment effectiveness is determined by the probability of group loss if a milestone is missed (p=0.5 or p=1/3). The impact is all-or-nothing (lose all earnings). The effect of punishment depends on scenario and risk level.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players know the game lasts 6 rounds and when it will end.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each round, players are told how much each member of their group contributed to the joint account.

## Important Limitations
- The punishment mechanism is not individual but group-level: missing a milestone triggers a probabilistic loss for the entire group, not targeted sanctions.
- The experiment does not include communication or negotiation over milestones, which may be important in real-world settings.
- The design does not capture the potential advantages of early investments (e.g., time-dependent returns), which may affect the generalizability to real environmental problems.
- The findings are context-dependent and may not generalize to all public goods or collective-risk settings, especially those with different payoff structures or risk parameters.
- No reward mechanisms are tested, so the interaction of punishment and reward is not addressed.
