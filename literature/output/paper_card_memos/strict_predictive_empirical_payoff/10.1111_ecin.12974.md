The paper reports both payoff-related outcomes (welfare, group earnings) and non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment, emotional state). Efficiency is operationalized as group welfare or group earnings relative to the maximum possible.

## Main Findings Relevant To Prediction
This experiment used a repeated public goods game with costly punishment and stranger matching. In each round, groups of three played five rounds, with either happiness or anger induced via video clips after the contribution stage. The only difference between treatments was the induced emotion. The main findings are: (1) Contributions decay over rounds in the happiness treatment but remain stable in the anger treatment; (2) Final-round welfare and group earnings are higher in the anger treatment than in the happiness treatment; (3) The key mechanism is that antisocial-punished cooperators reduce their contributions in the happiness treatment but not in the anger treatment; (4) Prosocial and antisocial punishment levels do not differ significantly between treatments, but their trends and effects do. Thus, the emotional context moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency: punishment sustains efficiency only when anger is present, not when happiness is induced.

## Prediction Guidance
This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games depends on the emotional environment. With standard parameters (3 players, 5 rounds, MPCR 0.6, costly punishment with 1:3 cost:impact), punishment sustains or increases efficiency when anger is induced, but not when happiness is induced. In the absence of induced happiness, the efficiency effect of punishment is positive or neutral; with induced happiness, the effect is negative. For prediction, the emotional context (incidental happiness vs. anger) is a critical moderator. The control (no-punishment) baseline is not directly reported, but a baseline without emotion induction (see Appendix J) shows similar trends to the anger treatment, suggesting that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is typical unless happiness is induced. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, consider whether the environment is emotionally neutral/angry (expect positive or stable efficiency effect) or happy (expect negative effect).

## Design Dimensions Highlighted In This Paper
- player count: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each group consisted of 3 players.
- number of rounds: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each session consisted of 5 rounds.
- chat: The paper directly informs this dimension. No communication or chat was allowed between players.
- all-or-nothing contribution: The paper directly informs this dimension. Contribution choice was continuous: each subject could choose how much to contribute from their 20-token endowment.
- mpcr: The paper directly informs this dimension. Marginal per capita return (MPCR) was 0.6.
- punishment cost: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment cost was 1 token per punishment point assigned.
- punishment technology: The paper directly informs this dimension. Each punishment point reduced the target's earnings by 3 tokens (1:3 cost:impact ratio).
- reward availability: The paper directly informs this dimension. No reward mechanism was available; only punishment was possible.
- knowing when the game ends: The paper directly informs this dimension. Players knew in advance that there would be 5 rounds.
- seeing peer outcomes: The paper directly informs this dimension. After each stage, players saw others' contributions, earnings, and punishment received, with temporary IDs.
- seeing punisher identity: The paper directly informs this dimension. Punishment received was shown with the temporary ID of the punisher, but IDs changed each round (stranger matching).

## Important Limitations
- The emotional context (happiness vs. anger) was experimentally induced and may not generalize to all real-world settings.
- The experiment used only 5 rounds, so long-run effects of emotion induction on cooperation and punishment are uncertain.
- No pure control (no-punishment) group is reported in the main text; baseline without emotion induction is only in Appendix J.
- Stranger matching was used to avoid strategic effects, which may differ from partner matching or real-world repeated interactions.
- The findings may not generalize to larger groups, different MPCRs, or other punishment/reward structures.
