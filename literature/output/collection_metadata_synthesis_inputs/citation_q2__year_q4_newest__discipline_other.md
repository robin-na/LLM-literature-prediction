- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Dannenberg, A., & Levin, S. A. (2022). Punishment institutions selected and sustained through voting and learning. *Nature Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

- source: Lim, I. S., & Capraro, V. (2022). A Synergy of Institutional Incentives and Networked Structures in Evolutionary Game Dynamics of Multiagent Systems. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment and network structure can interact synergistically to promote prosocial behavior and increase group efficiency (mean payoff) in a trust game. Specifically, the combination of even modest punishment and network structure can achieve full cooperation and maximal efficiency in parameter regions where neither mechanism alone would suffice. The required punishment level to achieve full cooperation is lower in structured populations than in well-mixed populations. The mean payoff is maximized at the threshold punishment level that induces full cooperation; increasing punishment beyond this point does not further increase efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of maintaining the incentive system. The paper provides explicit analytical boundaries for these effects as a function of the trust return (r), network degree (k), and punishment size (p).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a networked trust game (and by extension, in adjacent public-goods-like games) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is not too costly and the network structure is not too sparse. The results suggest that the efficiency gain from punishment is greater in structured populations than in well-mixed ones, and that the combination of network structure and punishment can achieve high efficiency at lower punishment levels than punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the network structure (degree), the effectiveness and cost of punishment, and the underlying return to cooperation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games but are likely to transfer to public goods games with similar strategic structure.

- source: Wang, C. Y., & Cui, W. F. (2022). Supervision for the Public Health Services for Older Adults Under the Background of Government Purchasing: An Evolutionary Game Analysis Framework. *Frontiers in Public Health*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a principal-agent setting with information asymmetry, static punishment or reward mechanisms are insufficient to ensure full compliance (self-discipline) by service providers. Dynamic mechanisms, where the intensity of punishment or reward depends on the observed behavior, can stabilize higher levels of self-discipline. Specifically, higher punishment intensity increases the proportion of self-discipline, while higher reward intensity can decrease it due to moral hazard and adverse selection. The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the results suggest that dynamic sanctioning mechanisms are more effective at promoting compliance than static ones.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (or self-discipline) depends on the design of the sanctioning mechanism, particularly whether it is static or dynamic. While the model is not a public goods game per se, it is structurally similar in that it models a social dilemma with potential for free-riding and the use of sanctions. The findings suggest that, in analogous PGG-like environments, dynamic punishment mechanisms are likely to be more effective at increasing cooperation than static ones, and that increasing punishment intensity generally increases compliance. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. It is most useful for understanding the qualitative effects of punishment design and intensity on compliance behavior.

- source: Chen, W. M., Quan, J., Wang, X. J., & Liu, Y. (2023). Evolutionary dynamics from fluctuating environments with deterministic and stochastic noises. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical model of evolutionary dynamics in the hawk-dove game under fluctuating environments with both payoff and demographic noise. Through simulation, it finds that both deterministic and stochastic fluctuations can promote cooperation, sometimes even reversing selection so that cooperation dominates. Deterministic fluctuations are more effective than stochastic ones at promoting cooperation. The mechanism is that noise can create unfair time-averaged payoffs that favor cooperation. High variance in noise promotes cooperation but makes its maintenance less stable. The results are robust to the type of noise distribution. The paper briefly extends the analysis to the prisoner's dilemma, finding similar qualitative results. No public goods game, punishment, or explicit group efficiency analysis is present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not study public goods games, punishment, or group efficiency. Its findings about noise and cooperation in the hawk-dove and prisoner's dilemma games may be of limited indirect relevance for understanding how environmental uncertainty affects cooperation, but not for the specific prediction task involving punishment and efficiency.

- source: Zhu, P. C., Sun, J. L., Yu, D. X., Liu, C., Zhou, Y. N., & Wang, Z. (2023). Continuous action iterated dilemma under double-layer network with unknown nonlinear dynamics and its convergence analysis. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper demonstrates, through theoretical analysis and simulation, that in continuous-action iterated dilemmas on double-layer networks, agents' strategies converge under a variety of conditions, including when agents have heterogeneous and limited learning abilities and when environmental effects are present. The convergence is robust to different network structures and layer weightings. However, the paper does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or the impact of punishment or reward mechanisms. The findings are relevant for understanding the stability of cooperation dynamics in complex networks but do not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as it does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency outcomes. Its relevance is limited to providing theoretical context for the convergence of strategies in networked social dilemmas with continuous actions and heterogeneous learning abilities. It may be useful for understanding how learning ability and network structure affect the stability of cooperation, but it does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Holubcik, M., Soviar, J., & Lendel, V. (2023). Through Synergy in Cooperation towards Sustainable Business Strategy Management. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=weak | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that strategic management of cooperation between organizations can stimulate the emergence of synergy effects, which include increased competitiveness, innovation, and knowledge sharing. These effects are more likely when organizations set clear goals, establish trust, and manage cooperation dynamically. The research is based on surveys of Slovak enterprises and case studies of global business alliances. While the paper discusses outcomes such as increased economic value and competitiveness, it does not measure efficiency or group payoff in a game-theoretic or experimental sense, nor does it involve punishment or sanction mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or related mechanisms, nor does it use a game-theoretic or experimental design. The outcomes are organizational and strategic, not payoff-based in the sense required for the prediction task. Therefore, this paper should not be used to inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants.
