- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Powers, S. T. (2018). The Institutional Approach for Modeling the Evolution of Human Societies. *Artificial Life*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2018). Rationality alters the rank between peer punishment and social exclusion. *Epl*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary advantage (interpreted as efficiency in promoting cooperation and group payoff) of peer punishment versus social exclusion in PGGs is not fixed, but depends on the intensity of selection (rationality), the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, and whether participation is compulsory or voluntary. Under weak selection, peer punishment can be favored if it is efficient or exclusion is costly. Under strong selection, social exclusion with low cost dominates. The presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not change the condition for which punishment is favored, but reduces the difference in their abundances. The rank between peer punishment and social exclusion can change as rationality changes, and this sensitivity is mapped for a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGGs, especially regarding how the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, group size, and rationality (selection intensity) interact. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive or negative depending on whether punishment is efficient and/or exclusion is costly, and on the rationality of agents. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, theoretical PGGs with the specified parameters. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is conditional on the cost/benefit structure and the rationality of players, and that the presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not fundamentally alter the ranking condition but can affect the magnitude of effects.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment mechanisms (cooperator-driven, defector-driven, or both) into public goods games increases the prevalence of cooperation and raises group efficiency/welfare compared to the baseline with no punishment. In infinite well-mixed populations, a mix of both punishment types is most effective, while in networked populations, cooperator-driven punishment is superior. The effect is sensitive to the synergy factor (mpcr), punishment fine (punishment_tech), and feedback sensitivity, with network structure further moderating the effect. For prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment, the value of mpcr, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the network structure should be considered as key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. The results suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude depending on these design dimensions.

In well-mixed populations, this can transform the game from one dominated by defection to one where full cooperation is stable.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment (with shared cost among cooperators) increases efficiency and group payoff when the fine is sufficiently large relative to the cost. The effect is modulated by group size (player_count), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech, i.e., fine per cost). Structured populations (networked interactions) can allow cooperation to emerge at lower fine thresholds, but as the fine increases, well-mixed populations yield higher efficiency. The findings support the prediction that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective and not too costly. The model is theoretical, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted as qualitative guidance.

For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that enabling punishment and introducing targeted reward mechanisms can shift the system toward higher cooperation and punishment prevalence, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in empirical PGGs. However, the lack of explicit efficiency or payoff reporting means the findings should be used as indirect, mechanistic support rather than direct quantitative evidence.

Although the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the increase in cooperation due to punishment suggests that, in standard PGGs, enabling punishment would increase efficiency, especially when the cost of the public good is not too high. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with two players, continuous contribution choices, and no communication, and may be less directly transferable to finite or empirical settings.
