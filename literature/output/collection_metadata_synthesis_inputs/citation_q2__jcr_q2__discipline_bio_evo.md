- source: Eldakar, O. T., Gallup, A. C., & Driscoll, W. W. (2013). WHEN HAWKS GIVE RISE TO DOVES: THE EVOLUTION AND TRANSITION OF ENFORCEMENT STRATEGIES. *Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment can increase group efficiency (fitness) in public goods game environments, but the pathway depends on the type of punisher. Selfish punishers can invade selfish populations and reduce cheating, thereby increasing group payoff and paving the way for altruistic punishers, who further increase group efficiency. The effectiveness of punishment in raising group payoff is higher when punishment costs are low, group size is small, and the number of rounds (iterations) is high. The model directly analyzes how these design dimensions affect the ability of punishment to increase group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game can increase group efficiency, especially when punishment costs are low, group size is small, and the number of rounds is high. The model is directly based on a standard PGG and manipulates key design dimensions, making its findings highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that the efficiency gain from punishment is larger when punishment is less costly and when group structure allows for stronger group selection (smaller groups, longer association). The findings are most applicable to settings where punishment leads to exclusion or strong consequences for defectors.

- source: Bone, J., Silva, A. S., & Raihani, N. J. (2014). Defectors, not norm violators, are punished by third-parties. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that third-party punishment in a one-shot public goods game is overwhelmingly directed at defectors, regardless of whether defection is the norm in the group. The manipulation of norm violation (defection being typical or atypical) does not affect the likelihood of defectors being punished. Cooperators are rarely punished, even when they are in the minority. The cost structure of punishment is fixed, so punishing multiple players is not more expensive than punishing one. Social rejection patterns mirror punishment: defectors are less preferred as future partners, regardless of norm status. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a one-shot PGG with third-party punishment and a fixed punishment cost, punishment is targeted at defectors rather than norm violators. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design details (one-shot, third-party punishment, fixed cost, no communication, binary contribution) are informative for mapping to other studies, but the lack of payoff outcomes limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction. The findings suggest that, under these design features, punishment is likely to be used against defectors, but the impact on group efficiency remains unmeasured.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, G. R., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Altered Voluntary Cooperative Norms Compliance Under Equal Decision-Making Power. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rDLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects voluntary cooperative norm compliance (measured as contribution) in a linear public goods game with four players, asymmetric endowments, and no punishment or sanction options. Anodal stimulation increases contributions, cathodal decreases them, and sham is intermediate. The effect is observed for both high and low endowment players. The study also finds that participants' own norm perceptions (norm.own) are more predictive of compliance than their perceptions of others' norms (norm.other), and that anodal stimulation changes the relative influence of these norm perceptions. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported, and the game does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its findings are limited to the effect of neural stimulation on voluntary cooperation (contribution) in a standard linear PGG. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are reported and may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform the downstream prediction task regarding the effect of punishment on efficiency. It may be useful as a control or baseline for voluntary cooperation in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Frey, U. J. (2019). Long-term evidence on cooperation and cultural differences in public goods dilemmas. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that in a real-world, online public goods game, average cooperation rates are low and relatively stable over time, with only minor differences between countries. Individual differences are more important than cultural, economic, or religious factors. The results suggest that cooperation in this context is lower than in typical laboratory public goods games, especially those with punishment. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the game, and the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a useful baseline for public goods games without punishment: average contribution rates are much lower than in laboratory experiments, and cultural background has little effect. However, it does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff, as no punishment mechanism is present or analyzed. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment, this paper is only relevant as a control or baseline for no-punishment environments, especially in more naturalistic, long-term, or online settings.

- source: Javaid, A., & Falk, T. (2015). Incorporating local institutions in irrigation experiments: evidence from rural communities in Pakistan. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implemented a sequential common-pool resource (CPR) game with five players per group, modeling irrigation dilemmas with asymmetric access. In the baseline (no institutional intervention), groups achieved high efficiency (average group earnings at 74% of the social optimum, group investment at 98% of the optimum). When external punishment (costly, probabilistic fines) was introduced, group earnings and efficiency significantly decreased (regression coefficient for group earnings: -9.03, p<0.1). The punishment mechanism reduced over-extraction by head-users but did not increase tail-user earnings; instead, it led to more water being wasted and lower group welfare. Communication and traditional authority treatments did not significantly affect group earnings or investment. The high baseline efficiency is attributed to strong local norms and the public revelation of individual extraction decisions. The main effect of punishment was to create additional coordination problems, not to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments with high baseline efficiency and strong social information (public feedback on individual actions), introducing an external punishment mechanism can reduce group efficiency and welfare. The punishment treatment in this design (costly, probabilistic, not directly linked to rule violations) led to more resource wastage and lower group earnings. For prediction tasks, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high and social information is available, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency. The effect is specific to the punishment design used here (costly, probabilistic, not directly rewarding the punisher). Other institutional interventions (communication, traditional authority) had no significant effect. The findings are most applicable to sequential CPR games with asymmetric access, public feedback, and close-knit communities.

- source: Inglis, R. F., West, S., & Buckling, A. (2014). An experimental study of strong reciprocity in bacteria. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in bacterial populations, cooperative punishers (who both produce a public good and punish non-cooperators) can invade a population of cheats only when the traits for cooperation and punishment are tightly linked. When the linkage is broken (by mixing punishing and non-punishing cooperators), invasion fails. Non-punishing cooperators alone also cannot invade. The main outcome is the frequency and survival/extinction of the different strains, not group efficiency or total payoff. The results support theoretical predictions that linkage is necessary for the evolutionary success of strong reciprocity in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and by implication, group efficiency) depends critically on the linkage between cooperation and punishment. However, the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff directly, and the outcomes are evolutionary (strain invasion/extinction) rather than economic payoffs. The findings suggest that in systems where punishment and cooperation are not tightly linked, punishment may not improve group outcomes. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the importance of linkage or coupling between cooperation and punishment, but less so about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency in standard public goods games.

- source: Perez, I., Yu, D. J., Janssen, M. A., & Anderies, J. M. (2015). Social roles and performance of social-ecological systems: evidence from behavioral lab experiments. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that group success in a lab irrigation game is best explained by the presence of certain combinations of emergent social roles, rather than any single role. All groups had leaders, knowledge generators, and followers, but successful groups also had connectors and either moralists or observers. The enforcer role (which involves punishing others' behavior, e.g., via threats to withhold investment) was not necessary for group success and was more common in unsuccessful groups. The presence of enforcers appears to be a symptom of conflict and lack of cooperation, not a cause of improved efficiency. The experiment does not include a formal punishment mechanism (e.g., costly punishment with explicit payoff consequences), so findings about 'enforcer' roles are based on communication and threats rather than actual sanctioning actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling formal punishment mechanisms on efficiency in public goods or common-pool resource games. Instead, it provides evidence that informal enforcement roles (as expressed in chat) are not sufficient or necessary for group success, and that their presence may indicate underlying conflict. For prediction tasks about the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper suggests that the mere presence of enforcement or sanctioning behavior (in the absence of formal mechanisms) does not guarantee improved efficiency. The findings are most relevant for settings with communication and emergent informal roles, not for games with explicit, costly punishment options.

- source: Konishi, N., & Ohtsubo, Y. (2015). Does dishonesty really invite third-party punishment? Results of a more stringent test. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that, even after removing five key methodological confounds (including adding a reward option and ensuring anonymity), a significant minority of third-party observers are willing to incur a personal cost to punish a dishonest trustee, even when the trustee's allocation is fair. The rate of punishment is lower than in previous studies (27% vs. 53%), but still significantly higher in the dishonesty condition than in the honesty condition. Participants were more likely to punish than reward in response to dishonesty. The main outcomes are the frequency and cost of punishment/reward, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that third-party punishment of norm violations (specifically dishonesty) can occur even when methodological confounds are controlled. However, the design is a modified trust game with third-party punishment and reward, not a public goods game or direct group efficiency context. The outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward rates and costs), not efficiency or group payoff. Thus, while the paper supports the robustness of third-party punishment as a behavioral phenomenon, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. Its main value is in showing that punishment can persist even when alternative explanations are minimized, but it does not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency.

- source: Baum, W. M., Paciotti, B., Richerson, P., Lubell, M., & McElreath, R. (2012). Cooperation due to cultural norms, not individual reputation. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a trust game with a conditional fine, trustees return more to investors from their own ethnic group when no fine is available. When a fine is available and imposed, trustworthiness increases in cross-ethnic pairs, eliminating the in-group advantage. In same-ethnic pairs, the fine has no effect or may crowd out trustworthiness. The effect is similar when the fine is available but not used. The study does not report group efficiency or total earnings as a main outcome, but the behavioral results suggest that punishment is more effective at increasing trustworthiness across ethnic lines than within them.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (measured as trustworthiness) depends on group identity: punishment increases trustworthiness in cross-ethnic pairs but not in same-ethnic pairs. However, because the outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The design is a modified trust game, not a public goods game, so transferability to PGGs is limited but informative for settings where group identity and punishment interact. The punishment is costless to the punisher and has a fixed impact, which should be considered when mapping to other designs.

- source: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R. (2010). Interactions Between Sabre-Tooth Blennies and Their Reef Fish Victims: Effects Of Enforced Repeated Game Structure and Local Abundance on Victim Aggression. *Ethology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that resident reef fish species, which cannot avoid repeated interactions with parasitic blennies, are significantly more likely to show aggressive responses (interpreted as punishment) than visitor species, even after controlling for size and territoriality. Aggression is negatively correlated with local abundance (group size), both across species and within the most abundant species, suggesting that the benefits of punishment are diluted in larger groups. The authors interpret these patterns as consistent with theoretical predictions from repeated game and public goods models, but do not directly measure efficiency, group payoff, or the downstream effects of punishment on blenny behavior or group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, ecological evidence relevant to the prediction of punishment effects in public-goods-like settings. It supports the idea that repeated interactions (enforced by residency) increase the likelihood of punishment, and that larger group size dilutes the incentive to punish. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its main value is in supporting the importance of repeated interactions and group size as moderators of punishment behavior, which may inform priors or mechanistic expectations in related experimental PGG studies.

- source: Miraghaie, A. M., Pouretemad, H., Villa, A. E. P., Mazaheri, M. A., Khosrowabadi, R., & Lintas, A. (2022). Electrophysiological Markers of Fairness and Selfishness Revealed by a Combination of Dictator and Ultimatum Games. *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that distinct neural markers (N1, P2, MFN, LPC) are associated with behavioral profiles of fairness and selfishness in economic games. Selfish participants show neural signatures consistent with spiteful punishment (e.g., rejecting low offers in UG, resulting in zero payoffs for both), while fair participants show markers consistent with costly punishment (e.g., rejecting selfish allocations in DG). However, the paper does not report or analyze group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and does not manipulate or compare the presence/absence of punishment as a treatment. The findings are relevant for understanding the neural basis of punishment and fairness, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The games studied (DG and UG) are adjacent to PGGs but lack the group structure, repeated interaction, and explicit public goods provision. Punishment in UG is endogenous (rejection of unfair offers) and always results in zero payoff for both, but the study does not compare efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report group-level payoff outcomes. The main value for prediction is in the mechanistic and neural understanding of individual differences in punishment behavior, not in quantitative efficiency effects.

- source: Powers, S. T., Ekárt, A., & Lewis, P. R. (2018). Modelling enduring institutions: The complementarity of evolutionary and agent-based approaches. *Cognitive Systems Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that participants' choices in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma games are influenced by information about the other player's action and by individual personality traits. The disjunction effect (greater cooperation under uncertainty) is present but weaker than in previous studies and is not explained by a general bias to cooperate. Higher potential payoffs (high variability matrices) reduce the disjunction effect, suggesting more optimal responding when incentives are larger. Reward Responsiveness predicts more optimal (defecting) choices when the optimal strategy is to defect and the other player's action is known. Agreeableness predicts more cooperative choices when the optimal strategy is to cooperate. No evidence is found for a general bias toward cooperation, and the results are similar whether the task is framed as a social dilemma or a neutral card game. The study does not include punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes. Its findings on the influence of payoff variability and personality on cooperation/defection may be contextually informative for understanding individual decision-making in social dilemmas, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects or efficiency.

- source: Smith, K. M. M., Mabulla, I. A. A., & Apicella, C. L. L. (2022). Hadza hunter-gatherers with greater exposure to other cultures share more with generous campmates. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that Hadza participants are more likely to share with campmates they rank higher on generosity and, to a lesser extent, foraging ability. Importantly, the preference for generosity is significantly stronger among Hadza with greater exposure to other cultures. The effect of exposure is specific to generosity and does not extend to foraging ability. The results are interpreted as evidence for the flexibility of partner choice psychology, with socioecological context (exposure to other cultures) shaping the traits valued in social partners. The study does not involve punishment, sanctions, or analyze efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanction mechanisms, nor does it report efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes. Its design (anonymous sharing game with individual allocation decisions) is adjacent to public goods games but lacks the core features of contribution to a group project, punishment, or efficiency analysis. Therefore, it should not be used to inform predictions about punishment effects on efficiency in PGG-like settings.

- source: Bateson, P. (2014). Evolution, epigenetics and cooperation. *Journal of Biosciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The paper reviews and synthesizes evolutionary theories of cooperation, emphasizing the integration of epigenetics and development into these frameworks. It discusses kin selection, mutualism (including reciprocal altruism and punishment as a stabilizer), and group selection, arguing for a pluralistic approach. Punishment is mentioned as a mechanism that can stabilize cooperation, but the discussion is conceptual and not tied to experimental or payoff-based outcomes.
