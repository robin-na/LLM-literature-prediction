- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

  findings: The paper shows that, while inequality aversion can theoretically motivate antisocial punishment under certain conditions, empirical data from several public goods game experiments indicate that most antisocial punishment acts do not fit this explanation. Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The findings suggest that other motives, such as revenge or a desire to harm others, are more likely explanations for antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

  findings: The paper does not present new empirical findings but critiques the interpretability of Kocher and Matzat's experimental results. It notes that subjects' preferences over punishment and reward mechanisms are difficult to interpret due to the multiplicity of possible utility functions and equilibria. The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. The author suggests that simpler experimental designs would make it easier to interpret the effects of institutional choices on cooperation and contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-good games. However, it offers important cautionary notes: (1) the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism (e.g., cost, impact, available choices); (2) subject beliefs and the complexity of institutional choice can obscure the relationship between mechanism and outcome; (3) the presence of multiple equilibria under different utility functions makes it difficult to infer efficiency effects from observed preferences or voting patterns. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

- source: Bolle, F. (2021). Deterrence by Collective Punishment May Work against Criminals but Never against Freedom Fighters. *Games*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that collective punishment (CP) is ineffective or counterproductive in threshold public good games where resistance is individually costly but can produce a public good (e.g., protests, strikes). In these cases, increasing the threat or severity of CP does not increase compliance and may even increase resistance. In contrast, in threshold public bad games (e.g., pollution, crime), CP can sometimes increase compliance, but only when the individual benefit from misbehavior is small compared to the social cost. The paper provides formal equilibrium analysis and discusses equilibrium selection, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for predicting the behavioral effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments, especially regarding when punishment is likely to deter undesirable behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on when collective punishment is likely to be effective in public-goods-like games. It suggests that in threshold public good games (where cooperation is costly and produces a group benefit), punishment is unlikely to increase efficiency or compliance, and may even be counterproductive. In threshold public bad games (where defection is individually profitable but socially costly), punishment can increase compliance and, by implication, efficiency, but only when the individual incentive to defect is not too large. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions for efficiency, but its qualitative results can inform predictions about the likely direction of punishment effects given the structure of the game (public good vs. public bad, cost/benefit ratios). However, because the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff directly, its support for efficiency prediction is indirect and should be supplemented with empirical or simulation evidence.

- source: Friehe, T., & Tabbach, A. (2018). A comparison of simple action-based and outcome-based policies for emergency-like situations. *Mathematical Social Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis shows that action-based punishment/reward policies are superior to outcome-based policies in generalized volunteer's dilemma settings (where more than one volunteer is needed and costs are private). Action-based policies can uniquely induce the efficient (second-best) outcome and prevent the no-participation equilibrium, while outcome-based policies always allow for a no-participation equilibrium, which is stable and potentially focal. The required level of punishment/reward under action-based policies is lower than under outcome-based policies. Combined policies that include punishment for non-participation can also rule out the no-participation equilibrium. The analysis is grounded in social cost minimization and group welfare, not just participation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that punishment (when implemented as action-based or combined with action-contingency) will have a more positive effect on efficiency (group welfare, social cost minimization) than outcome-based punishment in public-goods-like environments where more than one participant is needed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (action-based vs. outcome-based) is a critical moderator: action-based punishment can uniquely induce efficient outcomes and prevent social traps, while outcome-based punishment may fail to improve efficiency if the no-participation equilibrium is selected. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in settings with binary (all-or-nothing) participation, private costs, and no coordination. However, the results are theoretical and based on a generalized volunteer's dilemma, which is adjacent but not identical to standard PGGs.

Specifically, if the cost-to-benefit ratio exceeds a threshold (less than 0.5), punishment is always credible and cooperation is an equilibrium for any population size and discount factor. When the cost of cooperation is endogenous, players can use it as a coordination device to achieve efficient outcomes. The results are entirely theoretical and provide explicit payoff-based conditions for when punishment increases group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with random matching and private monitoring, the introduction of credible punishment (grim trigger) can increase group efficiency to the cooperative level, provided the cost of cooperation is within a specific range. The key moderators are the cost-to-benefit ratio, population size, and discount factor. The results suggest that, for a wide range of parameter values, enabling punishment will move efficiency from the defection baseline to the cooperative optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the cost of cooperation (analogous to MPCR in PGGs), population size, and the presence of punishment. - source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.

  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games (here, a donation game) will increase group efficiency, especially when players are subject to prospect-theoretic biases. The effect is more pronounced when punishment is mild or infrequent, as real human players may overweight the risk of being punished. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the payoff-based outcomes are directly analyzed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (even with low probability or low severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, particularly if the control game has low efficiency due to free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of psychological factors (probability weighting) in moderating the effect of punishment.

- source: Blonski, M., & Spagnolo, G. (2015). Prisoners' other Dilemma. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, the riskiness of cooperation equilibria can be precisely characterized by comparing the Nash-products of cooperation and defection equilibria. The least risky cooperation equilibrium is always the grim trigger strategy. There exists a critical discount factor (delta*) above which cooperation can be supported without strategic risk, and below which all cooperation equilibria are risky. The more patient the players (higher discount factor), the more forgiving the punishment strategies can be while still supporting cooperation. The paper also introduces the concept of 'risk perfection', requiring that not only the equilibrium path but also all subgames (punishment phases) are not risky. The results generalize to asymmetric games and to repeated Cournot duopoly (collusion) games, which are structurally similar to public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on the relationship between punishment harshness, forgiveness, and the viability of cooperation in repeated games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it shows that the ability of punishment to support cooperation depends on the discount factor and the riskiness of the equilibrium. In environments structurally similar to public goods games, the results suggest that harsher punishments (like grim trigger) are less risky and more likely to support cooperation, especially when players are less patient. As patience increases, more forgiving punishments become viable. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the strategic riskiness of the environment and the patience of players, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.
