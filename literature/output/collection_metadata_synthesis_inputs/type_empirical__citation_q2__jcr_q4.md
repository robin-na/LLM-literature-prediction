# Paper Set Evidence Digest

Number of papers in this paper set: 13

Each item below is a compact paper-level analysis digest. Use only this digest.

- source: Kocher, M. G., & Matzat, D. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that, although punishment increases contributions more than reward or the standard VCM, it reduces group efficiency because the costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. The reward institution, despite a lower maximum leverage, is both the most preferred and the most efficient, yielding the highest average profits. The standard VCM yields intermediate efficiency. The effectiveness (leverage) of both punishment and reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency. There is no significant difference in efficiency or institutional choice between partner and stranger matching. Gender differences exist in preferences for institutions, but not in efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency (average group profit) compared to both the control (standard VCM) and the reward institution. The efficiency loss is due to the cost of punishment actions. The reward institution, even with lower maximum leverage, is more efficient than both punishment and the standard VCM. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency, unless the punishment technology is extremely effective and/or punishment is used sparingly. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across partner and stranger matching, and is not moderated by self-selection or communication (which is absent). The paper also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment or reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Campos-Vazquez, R. M., & Mejia, L. A. (2016). Does corruption affect cooperation? A laboratory experiment. *Latin American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that: (1) exposure to a high-corruption environment (low monitoring) reduces subsequent cooperation in a public goods game; (2) allowing a leader to centrally punish increases cooperation (contributions) compared to no punishment, with a stronger effect in low-corruption groups; (3) allowing group members to counter-punish the leader reduces cooperation compared to leader-only punishment. All findings are based on contribution rates, not on efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that punishment can foster cooperation, but its effectiveness is moderated by prior corruption exposure and is undermined by the threat of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that centralized punishment by a leader increases contribution rates in a standard linear public goods game, and that this effect is stronger in groups with less prior exposure to corruption. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified, and the findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling centralized punishment on cooperation (contribution rates), but not directly on efficiency. The results also highlight that the presence of counter-punishment can reduce the positive effect of punishment on cooperation.

- source: Windmann, S., Binder, L., & Schultze, M. (2021). Constructing the Facets of Altruistic Behaviors (FAB) Scale. *Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The FAB scale reliably measures three distinct facets of altruistic behavior, including peer punishment. In a pilot study, the peer punishment subscale correlates with the amount participants spend on punishing free riders in a public goods game with punishment. The public goods game used is a standard design (groups of four, endowment, MPCR=0.75, punishment with 1:3 fee-to-fine ratio), but the only behavioral outcome reported is the amount spent on punishment, not group efficiency or total earnings. The study provides evidence that self-reported peer punishment trait predicts willingness to punish in a PGG, but does not analyze or report the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that self-reported peer punishment trait is associated with actual punishment behavior in a public goods game with punishment. However, it does not provide any evidence about the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are described for the pilot PGG, but no efficiency or payoff outcomes are reported. Therefore, this paper does not directly inform prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. It may be useful for understanding individual differences in punishment behavior, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Noussair, C. N., van Soest, D., & Stoop, J. (2015). Punishment, reward, and cooperation in a framed field experiment. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing punishment or reward opportunities does not increase cooperation compared to baseline: average catch and effort are statistically indistinguishable across treatments. Punishment is directed at non-cooperators, but does not induce greater cooperation in subsequent periods. Reward is used more frequently than punishment, but is not targeted based on recipient cooperation. The results suggest that, in this field setting, peer punishment and reward are ineffective at increasing cooperation, in contrast to many laboratory PGG studies. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported, but the behavioral proxies (catch and effort) do not improve with punishment or reward.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a field setting with a common-pool resource framed as a social dilemma, enabling peer punishment (and reward) does not increase cooperation or its behavioral proxies, compared to a baseline with no sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency observed in many lab PGGs may not generalize to field environments with similar incentive structures but different context, framing, or real-world constraints. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited; it is most informative as a caution about generalizability and as evidence that punishment may not always improve group outcomes, even when the game structure is similar to a PGG.

- source: Becchetti, L., Pelligra, V., & Salustri, F. (2018). The impact of redistribution mechanisms in the vote with the wallet game: experimental results. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The introduction of an ex post redistribution mechanism (taxing defectors and subsidizing cooperators) in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game significantly increases the share of cooperative choices compared to the baseline. In the baseline, cooperation decays over time, but with redistribution, cooperation is stabilized at a higher level. Framing the responsible product as socially beneficial also increases cooperation, but the effect of redistribution is robust across frames. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the effect on efficiency cannot be directly determined, but the increase in cooperation rates suggests likely higher group payoffs under redistribution.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that a centralized, ex post redistribution mechanism (akin to a tax/subsidy rather than peer punishment) can substantially increase cooperation rates in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game with 10 players and no communication. While efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the structure of the payoffs and the increase in cooperation rates imply that group efficiency would also increase under the redistribution mechanism. The findings are most directly relevant for predicting the effect of centralized, automatic sanction/reward mechanisms (not peer punishment) in all-or-nothing, no-communication, 10-player repeated games with known rounds and MPCR of 0.3. The results are less directly applicable to standard peer punishment settings, but suggest that institutionalized redistribution can be highly effective at sustaining cooperation.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Leibbrandt, A., & López-Pérez, R. (2014). Different carrots and different sticks: do we reward and punish differently than we approve and disapprove?. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that monetary punishment is used more often and more intensely than non-monetary disapproval, but monetary reward and non-monetary approval are used at similar rates. The determinants of monetary punishment/reward are primarily payoff-based (inequity aversion), while non-monetary approval/disapproval is more strongly driven by reciprocity and norm-based considerations. Strict payoff equality reduces disapproval and increases approval. The experiment is not a repeated public goods game but a set of one-shot, two-player allocation games, so efficiency or group payoff outcomes are not reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment and reward, distinguishing between monetary and non-monetary forms. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes in a repeated public goods context, nor does it manipulate or report most of the game design dimensions relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. Its main value is in clarifying that the impact of punishment/reward on behavior may depend on whether the mechanism is monetary or non-monetary, and that motivations differ. For direct prediction of efficiency effects in PGGs, this paper is only indirectly informative.

- source: Davis, D. D., & Holt, C. A. (1999). Equilibrium cooperation in two-stage games: Experimental evidence. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the use of contingent punishment/reward strategies in the second stage of a two-stage game is strongly associated with higher rates of cooperation in the first stage. This effect is robust across different treatments, including those where the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium or is Pareto dominated. The structure of the second-stage equilibria does not reliably predict cooperation rates. Instead, initial cohort propensities and first-stage payoff structure are more important. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability and use of punishment/reward strategies can increase cooperation rates in two-stage games, even when the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The findings suggest that behavioral responses to punishment opportunities are important, but the translation to efficiency outcomes is not addressed. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can support cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Chen, M. L. (2012). The Effect of Leader Reward and Punishment Behaviors on Subordinates' Budget Reports. *Engineering Economist*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that hands-off (no reward or punishment) is least effective in reducing budgetary slack. Punishment alone reduces slack more than reward alone, but the combination of reward and punishment (carrot-stick) is most effective. The effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is large relative to the benefit of slack creation. Rewards alone, regardless of size, have little effect. Requiring a factual assertion (honesty) also reduces slack. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (slack/honesty).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment, especially when combined with reward, reduces dishonest behavior (budgetary slack) in a principal-agent reporting game. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it is a one-superior/one-subordinate budget reporting game, not a multi-player public goods game. The findings are most useful for understanding the behavioral impact of punishment and reward on honesty/slack, but less so for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Güth, W., Kliemt, H., Levati, M. V., & von Wangenheim, G. (2007). On the coevolution of retribution and trustworthiness:: An (indirect) evolutionary and experimental analysis. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that most participants behave trustworthily (exert effort) and that a small but significant share of punishment (revenge/suing) persists, even among those without monetary inducement. The share of trustworthiness and punishment does not depend on the number of monetarily induced 'moral' players or the order of treatments. There is a weak positive correlation between observed untrustworthiness and subsequent punishment. Monetary inducement increases the likelihood of trustworthiness and punishment among targeted individuals, but not to the level predicted by rational choice. The main outcomes are behavioral; the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated trust game with costly punishment, the availability of punishment leads to persistent (though low) rates of punishment and high rates of trustworthiness, even without material incentives. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The behavioral findings suggest that punishment can support trustworthiness, but the lack of payoff analysis limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Roberts, S. C., Vakirtzis, A., Kristjánsdóttir, L., & Havlícek, J. (2013). Who Punishes? Personality Traits Predict Individual Variation in Punitive Sentiment. *Evolutionary Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=other_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that individual differences in willingness to punish a transgressor in a public-goods-like scenario are predicted by personality traits, especially lower Agreeableness. Other traits (Conscientiousness, Extraversion) have weaker or more limited associations. The study uses hypothetical scenarios where participants indicate their anger, desire to punish, and willingness to pay to punish a free-rider, but no actual economic game is played and no payoff or efficiency data are collected. The findings are relevant for understanding who is likely to engage in costly punishment, but do not inform the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or variants. It does not manipulate or measure any game design dimensions relevant to efficiency, nor does it report control or treatment efficiency. Its findings are about individual differences in punitive sentiment, not about the consequences of punishment for group outcomes. Therefore, it should not be used to predict efficiency effects of punishment based on game design dimensions.

- source: Przepiorka, W., & Diekmann, A. (2020). Binding Contracts, Non-Binding Promises and Social Feedback in the Intertemporal Common-Pool Resource Game. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a repeated intertemporal common-pool resource game with five players and ten rounds, the study compares the effects of (1) binding contracts, (2) non-binding promises, (3) private social feedback, and (4) public social feedback, against a no-regulation control. Binding contracts, when unanimously signed, lead to the highest group efficiency (average profit per round close to the cooperative optimum). Non-binding promises increase sustainable investment and efficiency initially, but the effect decays over time. Private feedback (where only the recipient sees ratings) does not improve efficiency over the control. Public feedback (where feedback scores are visible to all) significantly increases sustainable investment and group efficiency, with the effect being more stable over time than promises. The study provides direct evidence that public, but not private, social feedback can substitute for formal sanctions in promoting efficiency in CPR dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated CPR games with continuous contribution choices, public social feedback (where feedback is visible to all) can substantially increase group efficiency, even in the absence of formal punishment or monetary sanctions. Private feedback is ineffective. Binding contracts are most effective but require unanimous agreement. For prediction tasks, the presence of public feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = yes) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, while private feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = no) should not. The absence of monetary punishment or reward mechanisms means that the findings are most relevant for settings where social (non-monetary) sanctions are possible. The game design dimensions (player_count = 5, num_rounds = 10, continuous contributions, no chat, known rounds, full feedback on others' choices and payoffs) are well-specified and should be used as reference points for prediction.
