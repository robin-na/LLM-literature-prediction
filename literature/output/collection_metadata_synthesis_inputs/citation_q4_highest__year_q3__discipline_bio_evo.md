- source: Fehr, E., & Schurtenberger, I. (2018). Normative foundations of human cooperation. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. The review emphasizes that the presence of punishment alone is not sufficient for efficiency gains; the structure and normative context of punishment matter greatly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and integrative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff unless punishment is normatively constrained to reduce antisocial punishment and collateral costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the presence of normative or institutional constraints, the prevalence of antisocial punishment, and the cultural context. Game design dimensions that affect the cost, targeting, and normative context of punishment are likely to be critical moderators of efficiency outcomes. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes, but it cautions against assuming that increased cooperation from punishment always translates into higher efficiency.

- source: Alvarez-Rodriguez, U., Battiston, F., de Arruda, G. F., Moreno, Y., Perc, M., & Latora, V. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics of higher-order interactions in social networks. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper shows that in PGGs with higher-order interactions (hypergraphs), the critical synergy factor required for cooperation decreases as group size increases in uniform random hypergraphs, meaning larger groups can foster cooperation more easily (at a given synergy factor). Heterogeneity in group membership (hyperdegree) and group size can either facilitate or hinder cooperation, depending on the structure: scale-free (hub-dominated) hypergraphs inhibit cooperation, while uniform or less heterogeneous structures do not. The framework is validated with empirical data from scientific collaborations, showing that the synergy factor (group benefit) is a non-monotonic function of group size, with an optimal group size for maximum benefit. The paper does not study punishment, rewards, or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency and payoff outcomes in PGG-like environments with varying group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. It is directly relevant for understanding how group size and network heterogeneity affect the critical conditions for cooperation and group payoff, but cannot inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment. Use this paper to inform baseline efficiency predictions in control (no-punishment) conditions as a function of group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but not for treatment effects of punishment.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2018). Punishment and inspection for governing the commons in a feedback-evolving game. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment and inspection mechanisms can promote cooperation and prevent overexploitation in a common-pool resource game, but their ability to improve group efficiency (resource sustainability) depends critically on the intrinsic growth rate of the resource. If the resource renews too slowly, even perfect cooperation enforced by strong punishment leads to resource depletion and low efficiency. At moderate growth rates, strong punishment and inspection can achieve both high cooperation and sustainable resource levels, but weak institutions result in mixed strategy populations and depleted resources. At high growth rates, the resource is always sustainable regardless of punishment, and punishment only affects the fraction of cooperators versus defectors. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is context-dependent and can be absent or even negative if resource dynamics are unfavorable.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like common-pool resource games is highly contingent on the ecological context, especially the resource's intrinsic growth rate. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is crucial to consider not only the game design dimensions (such as punishment cost and effectiveness) but also the underlying dynamics of the resource. In settings where the resource cannot recover quickly, punishment may not improve efficiency even if it increases cooperation. In more favorable ecological conditions, punishment can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, but only if it is sufficiently effective (high detection probability and/or fine). Thus, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universal and must be interpreted in light of resource dynamics.

- source: Ågren, J. A., Davies, N. G., & Foster, K. R. (2019). Enforcement is central to the evolution of cooperation. *Nature Ecology & Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that enforcement mechanisms—defined as actions that reduce selfishness within cooperative alliances—are predicted by evolutionary models to evolve across a wide range of biological systems, from genomes to human societies. The models show that, in the absence of enforcement, selfishness undermines cooperation and reduces group fitness. When enforcement is possible, it evolves under many parameter regimes and leads to higher cooperation and improved collective outcomes. Empirical examples from animal societies, mutualisms, and cellular systems support the theoretical predictions. The effectiveness of enforcement depends on its cost and the structure of the system, but its presence is generally associated with higher group efficiency or fitness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment or enforcement in public-goods-like environments will increase group efficiency or collective payoff, relative to control conditions without enforcement. The models are not direct PGGs but are structurally analogous, and the mechanisms (punishment, policing, sanctions) map closely to punishment in PGGs. The effect is generally more positive, but the magnitude depends on the cost of enforcement and system details. The paper also highlights that enforcement is most critical when relatedness is low and that high enforcement costs can limit its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment will increase efficiency, especially when enforcement is not prohibitively costly.

- source: Chowdhury, S. N., Kundu, S., Banerjee, J., Perc, M., & Ghosh, D. (2021). Eco-evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in the presence of policing. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model shows that the introduction of punishment (policing) as a third strategy in a repeated PD game can lead to stable coexistence or oscillatory dominance cycles among cooperators, defectors, and punishers, depending on ecological and game parameters. The strength of punishment, temptation to defect, and ecological benefits (from 'free space') all modulate which strategies persist. Moderate punishment can suppress defectors and promote cooperation, but high temptation to defect can still undermine cooperation. The model also demonstrates that initial conditions and parameter choices can lead to multistability, with different attractors (e.g., dominance by one strategy, coexistence, or extinction). The focus is on the eco-evolutionary dynamics of strategy frequencies, not on group efficiency or total welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that punishment can promote the persistence of cooperation in social dilemma settings, but the results are framed in terms of population dynamics (strategy frequencies and coexistence) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The model is based on a repeated PD game, not a public goods game, and does not report efficiency as defined by total group payoff relative to full cooperation. Therefore, while the findings support the idea that punishment can help sustain cooperation under certain conditions, they do not provide direct quantitative guidance for predicting efficiency changes in PGG-like environments with or without punishment. The results may be useful for understanding qualitative moderators (e.g., the importance of punishment strength, temptation, ecological context), but are not directly transferable to the efficiency prediction task.

- source: West, S. A., Cooper, G. A., Ghoul, M. B., & Griffin, A. S. (2021). Ten recent insights for our understanding of cooperation. *Nature Ecology & Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists
  findings: The paper synthesizes recent advances in the study of cooperation, emphasizing that relatedness (kin selection) is a primary driver of cooperation within species, while enforcement mechanisms (including punishment and rewards) are important for stabilizing cooperation, especially in mutualisms between species. It highlights that enforcement can take the form of sanctions or rewards, but these are discussed as evolutionary strategies rather than as manipulable variables in economic games. The paper also notes that direct fitness benefits and ecological context can influence cooperation, but relatedness is often the most detectable factor. Economic games are mentioned as a tool for studying human cooperation, but the paper is critical of over-interpreting their results for evolutionary theory. No quantitative or experimental results on efficiency or payoff are provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical context for the importance of enforcement mechanisms (punishment, sanctions, rewards) in stabilizing cooperation, but does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It may inform the general expectation that enforcement can increase cooperation, but it does not specify under what game design conditions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) this effect is positive, negative, or neutral for efficiency. For prediction tasks requiring quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes, this paper is not directly informative.

- source: Bradley, A., Lawrence, C., & Ferguson, E. (2018). Does observability affect prosociality?. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The meta-analysis finds that making prosocial actions observable increases prosocial behavior (r = 0.141), with stronger effects in repeated interactions, social dilemmas (including public goods games), and when decisions have consequences for the actor. The effect is also stronger in laboratory settings and with passive observers. However, the outcome is always a behavioral measure (e.g., contribution, donation, volunteering) or intention, not efficiency or group payoff. The analysis does not directly address punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that observability (e.g., making contributions public) can increase prosocial behavior in public-goods-game-like environments, especially in repeated games and social dilemmas. However, it does not report on efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of punishment or sanctions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or the effect of punishment, this paper is only weakly informative, suggesting that increasing observability may increase contributions, which could in turn affect efficiency, but without direct evidence. It does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment, nor does it report the relevant game design dimensions in a way that would allow direct mapping to efficiency outcomes.
