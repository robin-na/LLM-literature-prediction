  findings: In a spatial PGG with influential individuals, both punishment and reward mechanisms (administered by influential individuals) can promote cooperation and increase mean payoff compared to the baseline with no such interventions. Rewarding cooperators is more effective than punishing defectors in terms of both promoting cooperation and increasing mean payoff, especially at low multiplication factors (low MPCR). The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. The model is theoretical/simulation-based, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted with caution.

- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Punishment (peer or pool) can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, especially when participation is voluntary. (2) Pool punishment with second-order punishment is particularly effective, leading to stable, high-efficiency cooperative regimes. (3) Peer punishment can also support cooperation, but is less robust and can be undermined by second-order free riders. (4) Compulsory participation (no option to abstain) undermines the effectiveness of punishment, resulting in dominance by defectors and low efficiency. (5) The costs of punishment (especially pool punishment) can reduce efficiency if not balanced by increased cooperation, but with sufficient rounds or strong imitation, efficiency gains are realized. (6) The structure of the punishment mechanism (peer vs. pool, presence of second-order punishment) and the option for voluntary participation are key moderators of the efficiency effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when participation is voluntary and when pool punishment with second-order punishment is available. The model shows that the design dimensions—such as player count, number of rounds, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and whether participation is voluntary—critically shape the efficiency outcome. In particular, pool punishment with second-order punishment and voluntary participation are predicted to yield the highest efficiency. Peer punishment can also increase efficiency, but is less stable and more vulnerable to free-riding. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency, enabling punishment (especially with the above features) is likely to substantially increase efficiency. However, if punishment is costly and not well-targeted, or if participation is compulsory, efficiency gains may be limited or negative.

  findings: Adaptive punishment in spatial public goods games enables the spontaneous emergence of rare, localized punishers who respond to the spread of defection. This mechanism maintains smooth interfaces between cooperators and defectors, invigorates spatial reciprocity, and prevents the emergence of cyclic dominance that would otherwise allow defectors to persist. As a result, adaptive punishment can achieve full cooperation (pure C phase) in parameter regions where, without punishment, only defectors would survive. The cost of punishment, when adaptively deployed, is globally negligible, making the mechanism highly efficient. Compared to steady (non-adaptive) punishment, adaptive punishment is more effective at eliminating defectors and does so with lower cost.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that adaptive, context-sensitive punishment mechanisms in spatially structured public goods games can substantially increase the likelihood of full cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency, even when punishment is costly. The effect is especially pronounced when punishment is inexpensive, but remains positive even for costly punishment. The findings suggest that models or interventions that allow for adaptive, rather than fixed, punishment are likely to yield higher efficiency than those with no punishment or with steady punishment. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, so predictions should be based on the qualitative pattern that adaptive punishment expands the parameter space for high efficiency.

For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment can improve efficiency in structured populations, but the mapping to efficiency must be inferred from cooperation levels.
