- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Murase, Y., & Baek, S. K. (2021). Friendly-rivalry solution to the iterated n-person public-goods game. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the CAPRI-n strategy achieves full cooperation and maximal efficiency in the iterated n-person public-goods game, even with implementation errors. When all players use CAPRI-n, the group achieves the cooperative payoff in the limit of small error probability. The strategy is defensible (cannot be exploited), efficient (achieves full cooperation), and distinguishable (can exploit unconditional cooperators). Evolutionary simulations show that CAPRI-n dominates the population across a wide range of benefit-to-cost ratios and population sizes, outperforming other memory-one strategies. The results are robust for n=2 and 3, and supported by Monte Carlo simulation for n=4.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public-goods games with error, a well-designed punishment/reciprocity strategy (CAPRI-n) can achieve near-maximal efficiency, even as group size increases. The findings suggest that, when the game allows for memory-based contingent punishment and recovery, enabling such mechanisms can dramatically increase efficiency relative to baseline. The results are most directly applicable to settings with continuous contributions, no explicit punishment cost, and no external punishment/reward mechanism, but where strategies can implement contingent retaliation. The paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated PGGs with endogenous, strategy-based punishment/reciprocity, especially as group size increases.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of cooperation in costly institutions exhibits Red Queen and Black Queen dynamics in heterogeneous public goods. *Communications Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The model demonstrates that introducing a costly institution (with a participation fee) alongside a free institution in a public goods game can increase cooperation and group efficiency. For low enhancement factors (low profitability), only the costly institution supports cooperation, and average payoffs remain close to the entrance cost (i.e., not much higher than the baseline). For higher enhancement factors, cooperation emerges in both institutions, and group payoffs increase above zero. The costly institution is more likely to be populated by cooperators, as defectors avoid the entrance cost unless there are enough cooperators to exploit. The model also shows that the effect of the entrance cost is context-dependent: it is beneficial for cooperation and efficiency at low profitability, but can be detrimental at high profitability if the cost is too high, as it reduces participation. The results hold in both well-mixed and structured populations, though spatial structure removes some bistability and further supports cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that introducing a costly institution (entrance fee) in a public goods game can increase efficiency and group payoff, especially when the baseline profitability (mpcr/enhancement factor) is low. The entrance cost acts as a filter, keeping defectors out and allowing cooperators to achieve higher payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly institution (or, by analogy, a punishment mechanism with a cost to participate) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such cost, particularly when the marginal per-capita return is low. However, if the cost is too high and the game is already highly profitable, the effect may reverse. The model does not include explicit punishment, but the costly institution's effect is adjacent to punishment in that it deters free-riding through a cost barrier. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where institutional participation costs or costly precommitment mechanisms are present, rather than direct peer punishment.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Freedom to choose between public resources promotes cooperation. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that allowing individuals to choose between multiple public goods games (resources) with different enhancement factors can promote cooperation. Defectors tend to cluster in the highest quality resource, while cooperators can 'shelter' in lower quality resources, avoiding exploitation. Cooperation is maximized when the two highest quality resources are similar in quality. The model is analyzed both analytically (replicator-mutator dynamics) and via simulation, showing robust results across parameter ranges. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present. The main outcome is the density of cooperators, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly relevant for understanding how choice between public resources affects cooperation rates, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. The model does not include punishment, reward, or sanctions, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome. Therefore, it should not be used to inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency, but may be useful for understanding the impact of resource choice and group structure on cooperation.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Wang, L. C., Liu, Y. Y., Guo, R. Q., Zhang, L., Liu, L. J., & Hua, S. J. (2024). The paradigm of tax-reward and tax-punishment strategies in the advancement of public resource management dynamics. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that both tax-funded punishment and reward mechanisms can promote cooperation and sustain public resources, but only if the natural growth rate of the resource pool is sufficiently high. When the resource growth rate is low, even universal cooperation (driven by strong punishment or reward) cannot prevent resource depletion, so efficiency remains low. For moderate or high growth rates, the system can reach stable states with high cooperation and sustainable resources, but the tax-punishment model can exhibit bistability, meaning the final outcome depends on initial conditions. The magnitude of the tax (which determines the strength of punishment or reward) also affects whether the system stabilizes at full cooperation, coexistence, or defection. The models provide explicit, parameterized predictions for group efficiency and resource sustainability as a function of player count, tax size, and resource growth rate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, parameterized theoretical predictions for the effect of punishment (and reward) on group efficiency and resource sustainability in public-goods-like resource management games. The models are not standard linear PGGs, but are close variants with dynamic, renewable resources and tax-based centralized punishment/reward. The findings show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by the natural growth rate of the resource pool and the magnitude of the tax (punishment strength). For low growth rates, punishment does not improve efficiency; for moderate/high growth rates, punishment can improve efficiency, but outcomes may be bistable. The results are most applicable to settings where resource dynamics and centralized sanctioning are key features. The paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions in such environments.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

- source: Grimalda, G., Belianin, A., Hennig-Schmidt, H., Requate, T., & Ryzhkova, M. (2022). Sanctions and international interaction improve cooperation to avert climate change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment tested the effect of sanctions (costly punishment) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma with German and Russian participants, both in national and international groups. Sanctions increased group cooperation (as measured by the probability of loss avoidance, PLA), especially for Russian participants and in international groups. However, the cost of sanctioning meant that final payoffs were actually higher in the no-sanction treatments, as shown in the supplementary material. The effect of sanctions was positive for increasing cooperation and loss avoidance, but negative for monetary efficiency. The combination of international interaction and sanctions was particularly effective at raising cooperation among low cooperators (Russians), leading to convergence with high cooperators (Germans). The effect of revealing nationality was minimal. Sanctioning was mostly prosocial in international groups, and antisocial sanctioning (punishing high contributors) was reduced. Only a small fraction of the endowment was spent on sanctions, but this was sufficient to increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment (sanctions) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma increases group cooperation (PLA) but reduces final payoffs due to the cost of sanctioning. For prediction tasks, this means that the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game may be higher or lower than the control, depending on whether the benefit from increased loss avoidance outweighs the cost of sanctions. In this experiment, the cost of sanctions was high enough that final payoffs were higher in the no-sanction condition, even though cooperation (PLA) was higher with sanctions. The effect is especially pronounced for groups with low baseline cooperation, and in international groups. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, group composition (nationality/culture), and the structure of the risk/loss. The game is a close variant of a PGG, but with a threshold and probabilistic loss, so direct transfer to standard PGGs should be cautious.

- source: de Melo, G., & Piaggio, M. (2015). The perils of peer punishment: Evidence from a common pool resource framed field experiment. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Nonmonetary peer punishment (social disapproval) reduces resource extraction (increases cooperation) in mixed-community groups but has no significant effect in in-group (same community) groups. The effect is measured as a reduction in the average number of nets used per person. There is substantial antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators), which can undermine the effectiveness of punishment. The study does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff, but lower extraction implies higher efficiency. The presence of antisocial punishment and lack of a unique social norm limit the positive effect of punishment. The findings suggest that the context (group composition) moderates the effect of punishment on cooperation, and that coordination is needed for punishment to be effective.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that enabling peer punishment (social disapproval) can reduce resource extraction (increase cooperation) in common pool resource games, especially in mixed-group settings. However, the effect on efficiency is not directly measured; it must be inferred from extraction levels. The presence of antisocial punishment and lack of norm consensus can limit or even reverse the positive effect of punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency in mixed-group contexts than in homogeneous groups, but the risk of antisocial punishment and unclear norms can reduce or negate efficiency gains. The specific game design (field context, nonmonetary punishment, no communication, continuous extraction choice) should be considered when generalizing.

- source: Ringsmuth, A. K., Lade, S. J., & Schlüter, M. (2019). Cross-scale cooperation enables sustainable use of a common-pool resource. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

  findings: The paper finds that even weak social sanctioning (punishment) between communities can prevent the collapse of cooperation that would otherwise occur due to biophysical coupling (resource sharing). When only biophysical coupling is present and communities do not communicate or sanction each other, increased resource access can undermine cooperation in one community. However, if communities can sanction defectors in both communities, or if they communicate when setting norms, cooperation is more robust. The model identifies parameter regions where cooperation or defection collapses, depending on the strength of biophysical and social coupling and on whether communities collaborate in norm setting. The findings highlight the importance of cross-community sanctioning and communication for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource systems.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (social sanctioning) in public-goods-like environments can prevent the collapse of cooperation that may occur due to resource sharing or biophysical coupling. However, the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so its relevance to predicting efficiency outcomes is indirect. The results suggest that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms, as well as opportunities for communication and norm setting, are important moderators of cooperation stability. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that punishment will have a more positive effect on cooperation (and likely efficiency) when social coupling is present, especially if communities can communicate and set norms collaboratively. However, quantitative predictions about efficiency cannot be extracted from this paper.

- source: Sääksvuori, L. (2014). Intergroup conflict, ostracism, and the evolution of cooperation under free migration. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: This paper experimentally studies a dynamic public goods game with endogenous group formation, free migration, and treatments varying the presence of intergroup competition and ostracism (social exclusion by majority vote). The main findings are: (1) Ostracism increases average group size and contributions to the public good, resulting in higher net payoffs and aggregate returns, both with and without intergroup competition. (2) Intergroup competition alone does not increase group size or contributions, and leads to more imbalanced group structures, which reduces aggregate returns. (3) The combination of ostracism and competition still produces higher cooperation and payoffs than competition alone. (4) The threat of ostracism reduces the number of free-agents and increases the desirability of joining groups, outweighing the effect of involuntary exclusion. (5) Intergroup competition reduces the likelihood of ostracism, as groups are less willing to expel members when facing external threats.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling a social sanctioning mechanism (ostracism) in a dynamic, migration-allowed public goods environment increases both cooperation and group-level payoffs (i.e., efficiency), regardless of whether intergroup competition is present. The effect of ostracism is robust and positive for efficiency-related outcomes. Intergroup competition alone does not improve efficiency and may even reduce it by fragmenting groups. The design features—such as endogenous group formation, free migration, and ostracism by majority vote—are important moderators. The paper does not use classic costly punishment (with explicit cost per punishment point), but ostracism functions as a group-level sanction. Thus, for environments with similar group formation and migration, the presence of ostracism is a strong positive predictor for efficiency gains from sanctioning.

- source: Joshi, J., Brännström, & Dieckmann, U. (2020). Emergence of social inequality in the spatial harvesting of renewable public goods. *Plos Computational Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that when harvesting efficiency is low, all agents evolve to be sedentary and harvest at low rates, resulting in equitable but inefficient resource use. As harvesting efficiency increases, agents become mobile, and resource extraction approaches the maximum sustainable yield, achieving both efficiency and equity. Further increases in harvesting efficiency or reductions in dispersal cost lead to overexploitation and the emergence of social inequality, with a minority of mobile, overexploitative agents and a majority of frugal, sedentary agents. Rapid social learning (impatience) and local information (myopia) further aggravate inequality and inefficiency. The model identifies key moderators of efficiency and equity but does not include or analyze punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for understanding how efficiency in spatial public goods–like environments depends on ecological and behavioral parameters such as harvesting efficiency, dispersal cost, consumer density, and information structure. However, it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctioning, as these mechanisms are absent from the model. Therefore, while the findings are useful for predicting efficiency in control (no-punishment) conditions and for understanding the role of mobility and social learning, the paper does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to the introduction of punishment. It is best used for baseline efficiency estimation and for understanding moderators of overexploitation and inequality in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Yaman, A., Leibo, J. Z., Iacca, G., & Wan Lee, S. (2023). The emergence of division of labour through decentralized social sanctioning. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

This leads to substantially higher average group payoff compared to groups without sanctioning, or with only altruistic or selfish learning.
