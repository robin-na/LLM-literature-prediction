- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Shinada, M., & Yamagishi, T. (2008). Bringing Back Leviathan into Social Dilemmas. *New Issues and Paradigms in Research on Social Dilemmas*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The chapter synthesizes evidence that punishment opportunities in public goods games reliably increase cooperation and contributions, but the net effect on group efficiency or total payoff is not always positive. The net benefit of sanctions depends on game parameters such as MPCR and the punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When the MPCR is high and/or the punishment is efficient (high fine-to-fee ratio), the net benefit of punishment is more likely to be positive. However, in many experiments, the total costs of punishment (including both the cost to punishers and the fines paid by punished) exceed the extra benefits from increased cooperation, resulting in a net loss in group welfare. The chapter also reviews evidence that sanctions can have negative psychological effects, such as reducing intrinsic motivation and trust, and that the indirect effect of sanctions (changing expectations about others' behavior) can be as important as the direct deterrent effect.
  prediction_guidance: This chapter provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on structural game parameters, especially MPCR and punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When predicting the efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, it is crucial to consider whether the increased cooperation from punishment will be sufficient to offset the costs of administering punishment. If the control game (without punishment) already has high efficiency, or if the punishment is costly and/or the MPCR is low, enabling punishment may reduce overall efficiency. Conversely, with high MPCR and efficient punishment, enabling punishment may increase efficiency. The chapter also highlights the importance of considering psychological and indirect effects, such as changes in trust and expectations, which may moderate the long-term impact of punishment on efficiency.

  findings: The paper shows that in simulated public goods games, a reputation system based on punitive actions (punishment scoring) allows for the evolution and maintenance of high cooperation frequencies in large groups, even when errors in reputation assessment are present. In contrast, reputation based on generosity (contributions) or rewards is much less effective: generosity-based reputation rarely leads to cooperation in large groups, and reward-based systems are unstable due to invasion by non-rewarders. The model assumes binary contribution and punishment choices, with payoffs used for evolutionary selection, but does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff as outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment-based reputation mechanisms are more effective than generosity- or reward-based reputation for sustaining cooperation in public goods games, especially as group size increases and errors in reputation assessment become more likely. However, because the main outcome is cooperation frequency rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting the likely positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, especially in large groups and error-prone environments. The model assumes all-or-nothing contribution and punishment choices, no communication, and full observability of actions (except for errors in reputation memory). The results are most informative for predicting the effect of punishment in large, anonymous, repeated PGGs with reputation tracking, but do not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency.

For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.
