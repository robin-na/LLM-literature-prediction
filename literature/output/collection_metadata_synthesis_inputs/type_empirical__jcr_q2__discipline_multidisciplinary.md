- source: Liao, Y. L., Zhang, L., Lei, S. Y., Song, M. Z., Deng, W. K., & Hu, D. F. (2021). RETRACTED: Third-Party Punishment Mechanism and Corporate Cooperation in Environmental Investment: Experiments on Public Goods Game (Retracted Article). *Discrete Dynamics in Nature and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares a threshold public goods game with and without third-party punishment (TPP), using groups of three participants (framed as enterprises) making resource investment decisions toward a group threshold. The TPP mechanism automatically punishes group members whose investment rate is below the group mean. The presence of TPP leads to higher mean resource investment amounts, higher individual and group investment rates, and a substantially higher success rate of cooperation (from 55.57% to 76.85%). Regression analysis confirms that TPP has a significant positive effect on investment behavior, even after controlling for individual characteristics and measured preferences for interaction and equity (IEP).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a third-party punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods game increases group investment and the probability of achieving the public good. For prediction tasks, the presence of TPP (punishment enabled) should be expected to yield higher efficiency or group payoff than the control (punishment disabled), especially in small groups (n=3) with a threshold structure and automatic, impersonal punishment. The results are most directly applicable to threshold PGGs with automatic third-party punishment and no communication. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency-related outcomes under these design conditions.

- source: Sadowski, J., Spierre, S. G., Selinger, E., Seager, T. P., Adams, E. A., & Berardy, A. (2015). Intergroup Cooperation in Common Pool Resource Dilemmas. *Science and Engineering Ethics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=weak | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The studies found that, in a complex, multi-group common-pool resource game with real stakes and open communication, groups were able to achieve cooperative outcomes and avoid the tragedy of the commons, even in the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms. Cooperation was facilitated by the emergence of ethical leaders and effective intergroup communication. In both studies, groups coordinated to ensure equitable resource distribution, resulting in moderate but sustainable payoffs for all. The game design allowed for a variety of strategies, but did not include punishment or reward options. The findings suggest that leadership and communication can substitute for formal sanctions in promoting cooperation and efficiency, but do not provide evidence on the effect of punishment per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods or common-pool resource games, as it does not include or manipulate punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. It does, however, provide evidence that, in the absence of punishment, cooperation and moderate efficiency can be achieved through communication and leadership. For prediction tasks, this paper may help estimate baseline efficiency in control conditions without punishment, especially in games with communication and group identity. It does not provide evidence on how efficiency would change if punishment were enabled.

- source: Schroeder, K. B., Pepper, G. V., & Nettle, D. (2014). Local norms of cheating and the cultural evolution of crime and punishment: a study of two urban neighborhoods. *Peerj*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in a 3PP game, residents of a high-crime, low-trust neighborhood (B) are more likely to steal and less likely to punish theft than those in a low-crime, high-trust neighborhood (A). Perceived cheating (descriptive norm violation) is higher in B and predicts both more theft and less expectation of punishment. Experimentally manipulating perceived norms in B increases expectation of punishment. The cost of punishment (subjective value of £2) and trust both moderate willingness to punish. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but mean payoffs are similar across neighborhoods. The results support the idea that local norms and trust shape antisocial behavior and punishment, with potential feedback loops.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. While it does not use a standard public goods game or directly analyze efficiency, it shows that punishment is less likely to be used in low-trust, high-norm-violation environments, and that expectation of punishment can be increased by manipulating perceived norms. The cost of punishment and trust are important moderators. For prediction, this suggests that punishment may be less effective at increasing efficiency in environments with low trust and high perceived norm violation, and that the subjective cost of punishment matters. However, because efficiency is not directly measured, the evidence is indirect and should be used with caution.
