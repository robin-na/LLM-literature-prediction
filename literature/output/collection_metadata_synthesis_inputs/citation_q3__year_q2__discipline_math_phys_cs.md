- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Oya, G., & Ohtsuki, H. (2017). Stable polymorphism of cooperators and punishers in a public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in well-mixed populations, punishment does not maintain cooperation or improve efficiency; free riders dominate unless initial conditions favor punishers, and even then, cooperation is lost. In spatially structured populations (Wright's island model and stepping stone model), if punishment is sufficiently efficient and the cost of cooperation is less than the cost of punishment, a stable coexistence of cooperators, punishers, and free riders can emerge, either as a limit cycle or a stable equilibrium (especially with mutation). In these cases, the presence of punishment can support higher average payoffs and group welfare than in the absence of punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: it can be positive in structured populations with the right parameters, but is neutral or negative in well-mixed populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on population structure, the relative costs of cooperation and punishment, and the efficiency of punishment. In well-mixed (random matching) environments, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency or maintain cooperation. In spatially structured environments, punishment can support higher efficiency and stable coexistence of strategies, but only if punishment is efficient and costly enough relative to cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive only in structured populations with high punishment efficiency and when cooperation is less costly than punishment. The model also highlights the importance of mutation and the inability to both cooperate and punish in the same round. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in theoretical or agent-based models, and provide mechanistic insight for interpreting empirical results.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: The paper demonstrates that in threshold public goods games relevant to climate governance, the presence of local (polycentric) sanctioning institutions leads to higher efficiency and more robust cooperation than global (centralized) institutions. Efficiency is measured by the average group payoff relative to the maximum possible. The model shows that local punishment institutions are more effective at sustaining cooperation, especially under high risk, and can prevent the collapse into full defection. Larger group sizes, higher threshold uncertainty, and high homophily among wealth classes reduce efficiency. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling local (polycentric) punishment institutions in public goods games will increase efficiency relative to global punishment or no punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in smaller groups and under high risk perception. The model directly analyzes efficiency and group payoff, making its results highly relevant for predicting the impact of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions. However, the results are theoretical and may need empirical validation for specific parameterizations.

- source: Zhang, J. L., Chu, T. G., & Weissing, F. J. (2013). Does insurance against punishment undermine cooperation in the evolution of public goods games?. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with punishment and an additional option for defectors to buy insurance that covers their punishment costs. It shows that if insurance is cheaper than the expected punishment, cooperation can be destabilized, leading to lower group efficiency and welfare. However, if insurance is realistically priced (at or above the expected fine), cooperation remains stable and efficient. The results are presented as a set of scenarios, with the stability of cooperation, defection, or speculation depending on the relative costs of cooperation, punishment, and insurance. The main implication is that punishment remains an effective mechanism for sustaining cooperation and efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games are robust to the introduction of insurance against punishment, as long as the insurance is not priced below the expected punishment cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in standard public goods games with punishment, efficiency should remain high unless there are additional mechanisms (like cheap insurance) that allow defectors to avoid punishment at a lower cost. The model also clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment (and thus its impact on efficiency) depends on the relationship between the cost of cooperation, the expected punishment, and any available insurance. If the control game (without punishment) is inefficient, introducing punishment should increase efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.

- source: Dercole, F., De Carli, M., Della Rossa, F., & Papadopoulos, A. V. (2013). Overpunishing is not necessary to fix cooperation in voluntary public goods games. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both peer-punishment (overpunishing) and shared-punishment (fixed sanction per defector, cost shared among punishers) in voluntary PGGs can lead to the fixation of cooperation and high group efficiency, provided antisocial punishment is absent. The main difference is quantitative: overpunishing increases the fraction of initial conditions leading to full cooperation, but shared-punishment is sufficient to achieve efficient outcomes in most cases, especially when stochasticity (finite populations) is considered. The likelihood of efficient cooperation increases with group size, sanction severity, and the public goods multiplier. The paper provides explicit payoff formulas and analyzes the basins of attraction for efficient (cooperative) equilibria under a wide range of parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in voluntary PGGs increases group efficiency, even when punishment is mild (shared-punishment). The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, round numbers, and parameter values. The paper gives explicit formulas for payoffs and the conditions under which cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved. For prediction tasks, the presence of voluntary participation, absence of antisocial punishment, and the ability to punish defectors (even with fixed, shared-cost sanctions) are sufficient to expect a large increase in efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is stronger with larger groups, higher punishment severity, and higher public goods multipliers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGG-like games with similar design features.

- source: Archetti, M., & Scheuring, I. (2013). Trading public goods stabilizes interspecific mutualism. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with non-linear (sigmoid or step) benefit functions, stable mutualism and high group efficiency can be achieved without punishment, even in large groups and with high costs. Punishment is only necessary to maintain mutualism and efficiency in the special case of linear benefits (the N-person prisoner's dilemma). When punishment is introduced in the linear case, it drives the system to full cooperation and maximal efficiency. In non-linear cases, punishment is not required for high efficiency, as the non-linearity itself stabilizes cooperation at high levels. The paper provides analytical and numerical results for the equilibrium frequency of cooperators and the conditions for stable mutualism.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the shape of the benefit function. If the game is linear (standard PGG), enabling punishment will have a strong positive effect on efficiency, driving the group to full cooperation. If the game is non-linear (e.g., threshold, sigmoid, or step), high efficiency can be achieved without punishment, and enabling punishment adds little or no additional benefit. Therefore, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to know whether the benefit function is linear or non-linear. Other game design dimensions (group size, cost/benefit ratio) interact with this, but the linearity of the benefit is the key moderator identified by this paper.

- source: Villatoro, D., Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Nardin, L. G., Sabater-Mir, J., & Conte, R. (2014). The Norm-Signaling Effects of Group Punishment: Combining Agent-Based Simulation and Laboratory Experiments. *Social Science Computer Review*.

The authors attribute this to the norm-signaling function of group punishment, which is hypothesized to activate both instrumental and normative motivations for compliance. Agent-based simulations support the idea that norm salience, as modeled in the EMIL-I-A architecture, can explain the observed human behavior.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of punishment (specifically, whether it is coordinated by the group or uncoordinated) can significantly affect cooperation rates in public goods games, even when the material incentives are held constant. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that group punishment may lead to higher efficiency than uncoordinated punishment, but this is inferred from contribution rates rather than directly measured. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of group punishment structure and norm-signaling on cooperation, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency when combined with other sources.

Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

- source: Hintze, A., & Adami, C. (2015). Punishment in public goods games leads to meta-stable phase transitions and hysteresis. *Physical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that allowing punishment in public goods games shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to emerge to lower values, making cooperation possible under less favorable conditions. Punishment acts as a catalyst for cooperation, even if it is used sparingly, and can stabilize cooperation below the critical point. However, punishment can also stabilize defection above the critical point if defectors reach a critical density. The system exhibits meta-stable phase transitions and hysteresis: once cooperation is established, it can persist even as conditions worsen, and vice versa. The main reported outcomes are the probabilities of cooperation and punishment, and the critical values of the synergy parameter, not direct efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and parameters of punishment (cost, effectiveness) in a public goods game can substantially lower the threshold (synergy r) at which cooperation emerges. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the probability of cooperation is a strong proxy for efficiency in this context, as full cooperation would maximize group payoff. The findings suggest that, for a given game design, enabling punishment (especially if it is effective and not too costly) will increase the likelihood of high cooperation and thus higher efficiency, particularly near the critical synergy threshold. However, the paper does not provide quantitative efficiency values, so its support is indirect and best used for qualitative prediction or mechanistic understanding.
