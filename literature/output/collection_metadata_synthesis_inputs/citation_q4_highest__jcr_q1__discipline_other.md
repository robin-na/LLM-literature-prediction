- source: Grechenig, K., Nicklisch, A., & Thöni, C. (2010). Punishment Despite Reasonable Doubt-A Public Goods Experiment with Sanctions Under Uncertainty. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard repeated public goods game with and without punishment, and with varying levels of noise in information about others' contributions, the paper finds that: (1) Punishment increases efficiency and cooperation only when information is accurate; (2) With moderate or high noise, punishment does not improve cooperation and reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline; (3) Subjects continue to punish under noise, but punishment is less targeted and more frequent, leading to more antisocial punishment and welfare loss; (4) The efficiency loss from punishment under noise is substantial and persistent, even though subjects could choose not to punish. The results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing mechanism is highly contingent on the accuracy of information about others' behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency benefit of enabling punishment in public goods games depends critically on the accuracy of information about others' contributions. When information is accurate, punishment increases efficiency; when information is noisy, punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring is a key moderator: if the game design includes noisy or imperfect information about contributions, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency. If information is accurate, punishment is likely to increase efficiency. Other design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable moderator is information accuracy.

- source: Kraak, S. B. M. (2011). Exploring the 'public goods game' model to overcome the Tragedy of the Commons in fisheries management. *Fish and Fisheries*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods game experiments, the option for costly punishment increases cooperation and, after initial costs, leads to higher average payoffs and group efficiency. When punishment is combined with mechanisms for reputation-building (e.g., non-anonymity, indirect reciprocity), cooperation is further enhanced and the use of punishment becomes more targeted and less frequent, reducing its destructive impact. The author argues that these findings are directly relevant for designing fisheries management institutions: enabling peer monitoring, punishment, and communication can foster cooperation and improve collective outcomes. However, the paper also cautions that externally imposed sanctions may undermine intrinsic motivation to cooperate, and that institutional legitimacy and stakeholder involvement are crucial.
  prediction_guidance: This paper should inform predictions that enabling punishment in public goods game-like environments (including fisheries) generally increases efficiency and group payoff, especially when combined with mechanisms for reputation and communication. The evidence is based on a synthesis of experimental studies, not new data, but the translation to fisheries is conceptually strong. The paper highlights that the effect of punishment is most positive when it is peer-driven, combined with transparency and communication, and not imposed externally. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment, reputation, and communication mechanisms should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control games without these features.

- source: Zhu, P. C., Guo, H., Zhang, H. L., Han, Y., Wang, Z., & Chu, C. (2020). The role of punishment in the spatial public goods game. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that peer punishment leads to higher levels of cooperation than pool punishment in spatial public goods games. When the threshold for switching to pool punishment is high (i.e., more peer punishment), cooperation is more prevalent. The coexistence of both punishment types is explored, and the transition between regimes is mapped. The main findings are based on the frequencies of cooperators, defectors, and punishers, not on efficiency or group payoff. The ratio of fine to cost is higher for peer punishment, which is suggested as a reason for its greater effectiveness in promoting cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that peer punishment is more effective than pool punishment in increasing the prevalence of cooperation in spatial PGGs. However, because the outcomes are reported in terms of strategy frequencies rather than efficiency or group payoff, the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. The model and parameterization are clearly described, so the paper can inform mechanistic expectations about how punishment type and its implementation affect cooperation, but not directly about efficiency or welfare outcomes.

- source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in the public goods game with pool exclusion strategies. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper shows that introducing prosocial pool exclusion into a PGG leads to higher average payoffs and can stabilize cooperation through periodic oscillations or fixation to full cooperation, depending on whether second-order exclusion is present. Antisocial exclusion can destabilize cooperation but does not fully prevent the effectiveness of prosocial exclusion. Compared to punishment, prosocial pool exclusion is a more robust and effective mechanism for sustaining high efficiency in the population, even in the presence of antisocial strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in infinite well-mixed populations, enabling a prosocial pool exclusion mechanism (where excluders pay a fixed cost to exclude defectors from the public good) increases group efficiency relative to the standard PGG without exclusion. The effect is robust to the presence of antisocial exclusion, and even more pronounced when second-order exclusion is allowed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a pool exclusion mechanism should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment, especially when exclusion is institutionalized and second-order exclusion is possible. However, the results are derived from deterministic replicator dynamics and may not directly translate to finite or experimental settings.

- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Santos, F. C., & Pacheco, J. M. (2013). A bottom-up institutional approach to cooperative governance of risky commons. *Nature Climate Change*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that local, group-level sanctioning institutions are much more effective than global, population-level institutions at promoting successful collective action in a threshold public goods game with risk of collective loss. Local institutions increase the fraction of groups that reach the public good threshold (group achievement), especially when the perceived risk of failure is low. Global institutions are unstable and provide only marginal improvements. The effect is stronger in smaller groups. The model does not report efficiency or total payoff, but the results strongly suggest that local punishment institutions increase the likelihood of group success, which is closely related to efficiency in this context.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling local, group-level punishment institutions in threshold public goods games (with risk of collective loss) will increase the rate of successful group outcomes compared to no punishment or global punishment. While the model does not report efficiency or total payoff, the 'group achievement' outcome is a close proxy for efficiency in threshold settings. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is local, groups are small, and risk perception is low. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so its value is mainly in qualitative prediction and mechanism understanding.

- source: Raihani, N., & Aitken, D. (2011). Uncertainty, rationality and cooperation in the context of climate change. *Climatic Change*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper argues that cooperation in large-scale social dilemmas like climate change can be promoted by mechanisms that increase the net benefit of cooperation relative to free-riding, such as punishment, rewards, communication, and reputation. It highlights that uncertainty and irrational responses (e.g., loss aversion, framing effects) can inhibit cooperation. The authors review evidence that punishment and reward can both increase cooperation in public goods games, but that punishment is costly and may only be effective once cooperation is already established, while rewards may be more effective in early stages. Communication and information about others' actions also increase cooperation. The paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, but suggests that these mechanisms can indirectly improve collective outcomes by increasing cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based support for the idea that punishment, reward, communication, and framing can increase cooperation in public goods games and climate change contexts. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when combined with rewards and communication) is likely to increase cooperation, but the effect on efficiency is not directly addressed. The review highlights that punishment is more effective when cooperation is already high, and that rewards may be more effective in low-cooperation settings. The paper is useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators but does not provide direct empirical input for efficiency prediction.

- source: Bicchieri, C., Duffy, J., & Tolle, G. (2004). Trust among strangers. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in one-shot trust games, strategies that do not trust or reciprocate dominate, resulting in low average payoffs. When the game is repeated for more rounds (increasing the shadow of the future), conditional strategies that punish defection (e.g., grim trigger, hopeful) become viable and eventually dominate the population, leading to the emergence of a norm of trust and reciprocation and higher average payoffs. The transition from non-cooperation to cooperation occurs as the number of rounds increases beyond a threshold (typically r > 2 or 3). The stable outcome is a polymorphic population of conditional strategies supporting trust/reciprocation. The results are robust to initial conditions and to the inclusion of stochasticity in the evolutionary process.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated trust-game-like environments, the length of the repeated interaction (number of rounds) is a critical moderator of the emergence of cooperation and higher efficiency. When the game is one-shot or very short, efficiency remains low due to dominance of non-cooperative strategies. As the number of rounds increases, conditional strategies that implement punishment by withholding trust/reciprocation after defection can support the emergence of trust and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, in the absence of explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, repeated interaction alone can enable the emergence of efficient, cooperative outcomes if conditional strategies are available. The model does not directly manipulate or analyze explicit punishment cost or technology, but the logic of conditional strategies is analogous to endogenous punishment via future play. The results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated trust games as a function of the number of rounds and the available strategy set.

- source: Jagers, S. C., Harring, N., Löfgren, Sjöstedt, M., Alpizar, F., Brülde, B., Langlet, D., Nilsson, A., Almroth, B. C., Dupont, S., & Steffen, W. (2020). On the preconditions for large-scale collective action. *Ambio*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper argues that as collective action problems become larger in scale (more actors, greater spatial/temporal distance, more complexity), spontaneous cooperation becomes less likely due to increased stressors such as anonymity, lack of accountability, heterogeneity, and uncertainty. Third-party interventions (which may include sanctions, incentives, or other mechanisms) become increasingly necessary to generate and sustain collective action. The framework is intended to guide future empirical research and policy design, but does not itself provide new empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. However, it offers theoretical context: it suggests that in larger-scale settings, informal mechanisms (like peer punishment) are less effective, and formal third-party interventions (which may include sanctions or rewards) become more important for sustaining cooperation. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may diminish as group size, spatial/temporal distance, and complexity increase, unless punishment is institutionalized or externally enforced. The paper does not provide parameter-level guidance for the specific game design dimensions required for quantitative prediction.

- source: Dugatkin, L. A. (2002). Cooperation in animals: An evolutionary overview. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in animals can be explained by several evolutionary mechanisms, including kin selection, reciprocity, byproduct mutualism, and group selection. Punishment is highlighted as an important mechanism that can promote and stabilize cooperation, especially in larger groups where cooperation would otherwise be difficult to sustain. The discussion of punishment draws on theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and qualitative animal examples (e.g., macaques, vervet monkeys, wasps), but does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or group payoff. The paper suggests that the presence of punishment or retribution in models makes cooperation more likely, but does not quantify the effect in public-goods-game-like terms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation and stabilize prosocial behavior in group settings, including those analogous to public goods games. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff outcomes with and without punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation, especially in larger groups, but does not inform the magnitude or specific moderators of efficiency effects in experimental public goods games.

- source: Clavien, C., & Chapuisat, M. (2013). Altruism across disciplines: one word, multiple meanings. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that the term 'altruism' is used inconsistently across disciplines, leading to confusion and overstated claims, especially when transferring findings between fields like experimental economics and evolutionary biology. It argues for a clear taxonomy distinguishing psychological, reproductive, behavioural, and preference altruism. The authors show that much of the experimental economics literature (including public goods games with punishment) is best understood in terms of behavioural or preference altruism, not reproductive altruism. The paper cautions against conflating outcome-based and motivation-based definitions, and against assuming that findings about punishment or cooperation in economic games directly inform evolutionary or moral questions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. However, it is useful for clarifying that many studies in experimental economics (including those on punishment in public goods games) are about behavioural or preference altruism, and that their outcomes (such as increased cooperation or punishment behavior) should not be conflated with broader evolutionary or moral claims. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention should be paid to the definitions and outcome measures used in empirical studies, and that efficiency or payoff outcomes are distinct from measures of cooperation or punishment frequency.

- source: Wang, L., Jia, D. Y., Zhang, L., Zhu, P. C., Perc, M., Shi, L., & Wang, Z. (2022). Levy noise promotes cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma game with reinforcement learning. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game with reinforcement learning, introducing Lévy noise into the payoff matrix increases the fraction of cooperators in the population. The effect is stronger for Lévy noise (lower stability index α) than for Gaussian noise (α=2) or no noise. The mechanism is that noise accumulates in the Q-value updates, making cooperation more attractive over time. The results are robust to initial conditions. However, the paper does not analyze group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, and does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment, reward, or efficiency outcomes, and the game is a spatial prisoner's dilemma with Q-learning and noise. The findings may be of limited indirect relevance for understanding how stochastic payoff fluctuations affect cooperation rates, but not for the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency.
