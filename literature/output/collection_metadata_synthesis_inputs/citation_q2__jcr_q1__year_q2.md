- source: Hetzer, M., & Sornette, D. (2013). An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, evolutionary dynamics lead to two regimes: (1) a defection regime with low cooperation and low group payoff when punishment is weak or absent, and (2) a coordination regime with higher cooperation and higher group payoff when punishment is sufficiently strong and driven by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model predicts the empirically observed propensity to punish in lab experiments. The presence of punishment transforms the social dilemma into a coordination problem, enabling higher efficiency. Disadvantageous inequity aversion is shown to be evolutionarily dominant, and populations with this trait invade and outcompete purely self-regarding populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency (group payoff) when punishment is sufficiently strong and is motivated by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model gives explicit formulas for the threshold propensity to punish and shows that, above this threshold, populations coordinate on higher contributions and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar design parameters (e.g., n=4, r=3, MPCR=0.4), enabling punishment will shift the group from low to high efficiency, provided the punishment regime is effective. The paper also predicts how efficiency and punishment propensity depend on group size and punishment effectiveness, which can inform predictions for other parameterizations. However, the paper does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency change, so predictions should be interpreted as strong theoretical expectations rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: Roberts, G. (2013). When Punishment Pays. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, punishment can be self-interested and increase group efficiency when the cost to punishers is outweighed by the benefits of increased cooperation. The key condition is that the cost to the punisher (p) is less than the product of the punishment impact (q), the benefit per cooperative act (b), divided by the net cost of cooperation (c): p < q*b/c. Simulations confirm that with typical lab parameters (e.g., group size 3, k=2, p=1, q=3), punishment leads to high stable cooperation and high group payoffs. The effect is robust to group size and number of rounds, provided the parameter condition holds. The paper also explains why punishment sometimes fails to increase payoffs (e.g., when p is too high or q is too low), and why punishment is more effective in repeated than one-shot games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for predicting that, in repeated public goods games with punishment enabled and with favorable punishment parameters (low p, high q), efficiency will be substantially higher than in control games without punishment. The key moderators are the punishment cost (p), punishment effectiveness (q), group size (g), and the number of rounds (m). The paper gives explicit formulas for when punishment will increase efficiency, and shows that the effect is robust across a range of typical lab parameters. If the control game has low efficiency and the punishment parameters satisfy p < q*b/c, enabling punishment should lead to a large increase in efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Ye, H., Chen, S., Luo, J., Tan, F., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2016). Increasing returns to scale: The solution to the second-order social dilemma. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a PGG with increasing returns to scale, the presence of punishment leads to a dramatic increase in group efficiency and average payoff, provided the IRS parameter (alpha) is above a threshold (about 1.2–1.3). In standard PGGs (alpha=1), punishment does not stably increase efficiency, as defectors can invade and dominate. With strong IRS (alpha > 1.3), punishers can resist invasion by both defectors and second-order free-riders, leading to stable high cooperation and high group payoff. The effect of punishment is thus highly contingent on the returns-to-scale structure of the public good. The results are supported by both analytical evolutionary models and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment in a PGG depends critically on the degree of increasing returns to scale in the production function. In standard linear PGGs, punishment may not improve efficiency, but in games with strong IRS, punishment can lead to near-maximal efficiency by stabilizing cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence and strength of IRS (or analogous nonlinear production functions) should be considered a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Other game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are also discussed, but the IRS effect is dominant.

- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

- source: Paál, T., & Bereczkei, T. (2015). Punishment as a Means of Competition: Implications for Strong Reciprocity Theory. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 10-round, 4-player PGG with a competitive, rank-based payoff and costly punishment enabled from round 6 onward, average contributions declined steadily and did not increase after punishment was introduced. Punishment was not targeted at low contributors but at those with higher accumulated profits, indicating strategic rather than norm-enforcing use. There was no association between contributions and punishment received or imposed. The results suggest that in competitive environments, punishment can become a tool for rivalry rather than for sustaining cooperation, and does not reverse the decline in cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that in a PGG with a competitive, tournament-style payoff structure and full information about others' profits, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency or group payoff. Instead, punishment is used for strategic competition, and cooperation continues to decline. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent: in competitive, rank-based settings, punishment may not improve and may even undermine group efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so this inference is based on contribution behavior and the described use of punishment.

- source: Kubena, A. A., Houdek, P., Lindová, J., Príplatová, L., & Flegr, J. (2014). Justine Effect: Punishment of the Unduly Self-Sacrificing Cooperative Individuals. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a 12-player PGG, the introduction of punishment leads to a U-shaped relationship between contribution and punishment received: both the lowest and highest contributors are punished, with the most severe punishment falling on the highest contributors (the 'Justine Effect'). Antisocial punishment is primarily carried out by free riders and low contributors. While the paper reports that average contributions nearly double when punishment is available, it does not analyze or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, nor does it compare these outcomes between the punishment and no-punishment conditions. The focus is on the behavioral dynamics of punishment, not on payoff-based outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence about the behavioral dynamics of punishment in large-group PGGs, especially regarding antisocial punishment and its targets. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, which are central to the downstream prediction task. The finding that punishment increases average contributions suggests a possible positive effect on efficiency, but the presence of antisocial punishment (which reduces the payoffs of high contributors) could offset these gains. Without explicit efficiency or payoff data, the paper's value for predicting treatment efficiency is limited to indirect inference based on contribution patterns and punishment behavior.

- source: Toriumi, F., Yamamoto, H., & Okada, I. (2016). Exploring an Effective Incentive System on a Groupware. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of public-goods-like meta-sanction games, systems with first-order punishment and appropriate second-order sanctions (such as punishing non-punishers or rewarding punishers) can achieve and robustly sustain high cooperation rates. In contrast, systems that allow first-order rewards without second-order incentives for rewarding behavior can actually impede the formation of cooperative regimes. The cooperation rate is highly sensitive to the cost/benefit ratio of sanctions and the discount factor for higher-order sanctions. The results are reported in terms of cooperation rates, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct implications for efficiency are not provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task: it shows that the structure of the punishment and reward system (including the presence or absence of second-order incentives) strongly affects the cooperation rate in public-goods-like environments. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that enabling punishment (with appropriate cost/benefit ratios and possibly second-order sanctions) can increase cooperation rates, but the translation to efficiency is not quantified. The results also warn that adding rewards without second-order incentives can reduce cooperation, which may imply lower efficiency, but this is not directly measured.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Jacobs, G. S., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2014). Nonequivalence of updating rules in evolutionary games under high mutation rates. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, the choice of updating rule (BP, DP, LD) can dramatically change the long-run frequencies of defectors, cooperators, and punishers. For the same parameter values, the birth-death process leads to a population dominated by defectors, while the death-birth process results in a majority of cooperative strategies (cooperators and punishers). Link dynamics yields intermediate results. These differences arise even in well-mixed populations and are especially pronounced when mutation rates are not negligible and when more than two strategies interact. The paper does not report group efficiency or payoff, but the shift from defector to cooperator/punisher dominance implies a likely increase in group welfare under some updating rules.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the updating rule (a feature of evolutionary dynamics, not typically manipulated in lab experiments) can be a major moderator of the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, on efficiency. In public goods games with punishment, the same game design (including punishment parameters) can yield very different long-run outcomes depending on the updating rule: punishment may be ineffective (defector-dominated) under one rule and highly effective (cooperator/punisher-dominated) under another. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as indicating that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the evolutionary dynamics assumed, especially in theoretical or agent-based models. For empirical prediction, this suggests caution in generalizing across studies with different evolutionary assumptions.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2016). Institutionalize Reciprocity to Overcome the Public Goods Provision Problem. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a repeated four-person public goods game, embedding a reciprocal strategy as an institution (institutionalized reciprocity, IR) leads to significantly higher group efficiency (total profit) than a standard control game. The effect is strongest when the institution enforces a strict upper bound on contributions (S-IR), which enables reciprocators to coordinate against free riders and prevents excessive cooperation. In S-IR, group profits are significantly higher than in both the control and a less restrictive IR variant (NL-IR). The IR mechanism operates as a collective punishment system: if any player fails to meet the target, the group's future benefit is reduced for all. The study does not implement standard peer punishment, but the IR mechanism provides a collective sanction that is effective at increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutionalized, rule-based collective punishment (via reduction of future group benefit after a deviation) can substantially increase efficiency in repeated public goods games, especially when the institution strictly limits contributions to a target. While the study does not test standard peer punishment, it shows that collective, institutionally enforced sanctions can outperform standard repeated games without punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a credible, enforced institutional sanction (even if not peer punishment) can raise efficiency well above control, particularly when the rules are clear and binding. The results are most directly applicable to games with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions (in increments), and full feedback on others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Bravo, G., & Squazzoni, F. (2013). Exit, Punishment and Rewards in Commons Dilemmas: An Experimental Study. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a modified public goods game with six players per group and ten rounds per treatment, the introduction of punishment (a 5 MU fine for non-cooperation) significantly increased cooperation and efficiency compared to the control (no incentives). However, the highest efficiency (profit as a proportion of the theoretical optimum) was achieved when both voluntary participation (exit option) and positive incentives (5 MU reward for cooperation) were present. Punishment alone (No-Neg) led to 67% of the optimum, while reward plus exit (Ex-Pos) led to 69%. The control (No-Null) and punishment plus exit (Ex-Neg) both yielded 58%. Thus, while punishment increases efficiency over the control, it is less effective than the reward-plus-exit combination. The paper also finds that exit alone has little effect, and that the interaction between exit and incentives is important for maximizing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with six players, ten rounds, no communication, and fixed institutional punishment (5 MU fine per defection), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control (no punishment), but the efficiency gain is smaller than that achieved by combining rewards and voluntary participation. The efficiency effect of punishment is positive but not maximal. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, fixed MPCR, fixed punishment cost/tech, no reward in punishment-only treatments, no identification of punishers, and full information about group outcomes) are all specified, making this paper highly informative for predicting efficiency under similar conditions.

- source: van Miltenburg, N., Przepiorka, W., & Buskens, V. (2017). Consensual punishment does not promote cooperation in the six-person prisoner's dilemma game with noisy public monitoring. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three punishment institutions (individual decision rule, CDR2, CDR3) in a six-person prisoner's dilemma, with and without 20% noise in public monitoring of contributions. In the absence of noise, all punishment institutions increase cooperation and earnings relative to the baseline, with collective decision rules (especially CDR3) performing best. The main reason is that noise leads to both mistaken punishment of cooperators and missed punishment of defectors, so resources are wasted and cooperation is not effectively enforced. Thus, punishment does not promote efficiency in noisy environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a six-person all-or-nothing prisoner's dilemma with 20% noise in public monitoring, enabling punishment (regardless of whether it is individual or requires consensus) reduces group efficiency compared to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect is robust across different punishment decision rules. The findings suggest that, when noise is present, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be lower than the control efficiency. The results are directly relevant for settings with similar group size, binary contribution, and noisy monitoring, and less so for linear PGGs or settings with perfect information.

- source: Wang, Y. F., Vasilakos, A. V., & Ma, J. H. (2015). VPEF: A Simple and Effective Incentive Mechanism in Community-Based Autonomous Networks. *Ieee Transactions on Network and Service Management*.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling voluntary costly punishment in a public-goods-like environment with voluntary participation and a small entry fee can dramatically increase efficiency, driving the system to near-full cooperation (punisher state) and minimizing efficiency loss. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values, provided the multiplexing/diversity factor is above 1 and defectors can be reliably detected. The model is most applicable to dynamic, anonymous, and voluntary participation settings, and less so to settings with continuous contribution levels or where punishment/reward is not voluntary.
