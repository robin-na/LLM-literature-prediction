- source: Sutter, M., Haigner, S., & Kocher, M. G. (2010). Choosing the Carrot or the Stick? Endogenous Institutional Choice in Social Dilemma Situations. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that enabling groups to endogenously choose their institution (via voting) leads to higher contributions and higher group profits (efficiency) than exogenously imposed institutions, holding the institution constant. Both punishment and reward options increase contributions and profits compared to the standard VCM, but the effect is much stronger when the punishment/reward technology is highly effective (high leverage). However, punishment is rarely chosen when groups can choose, likely due to its costliness; groups prefer reward when it is effective. In exogenous treatments, high-leverage reward yields the highest profits, followed by high-leverage punishment, with the standard VCM lowest. The efficiency gains from punishment are offset by the cost of punishing, while reward can be efficiency-enhancing if its leverage is high. The positive effect of endogenous choice is not explained by selection of more cooperative types into certain institutions, but rather by the act of participation itself.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players and 10 rounds, enabling punishment (especially with high effectiveness) increases group efficiency (average profit) compared to the standard VCM, but the efficiency gain is limited by the cost of punishment. Reward, when highly effective, yields even higher efficiency than punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive but smaller than the effect on contributions, due to the costliness of punishment. Endogenous institutional choice (voting) further increases efficiency for any given institution. For prediction, the presence and effectiveness of punishment/reward, the cost/impact ratio, and whether the institution is chosen endogenously or exogenously are all important moderators. Control efficiency (standard VCM) is always lower than with effective punishment or reward, but the size of the efficiency gain depends on the punishment/reward technology and institutional choice procedure.

- source: Sefton, M., Shupp, R., & Walker, J. M. (2007). The effect of rewards and sanctions in provision of public goods. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and ten rounds per sequence, the introduction of costly sanctioning (punishment) opportunities after an initial baseline phase leads to sustained higher contributions and, after an initial efficiency loss due to sanctioning costs, to higher group efficiency compared to the baseline. Reward opportunities alone do not maintain high contributions or efficiency, as the use of rewards declines quickly. The combination of sanctions and rewards produces the highest average contributions and earnings, but the effect is primarily due to the sanctioning mechanism. The cost structure is linear: each token used for punishment costs the punisher 10 cents and reduces the target's earnings by 10 cents (total group loss: 20 cents per token). The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5. Players cannot identify who punished or rewarded them, and all information is anonymous. No communication is allowed. The number of rounds is known in advance. The findings are robust across groups, though there is heterogeneity in group-level outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and known round structure. The results show that enabling costly punishment (with a 1:1 cost-impact ratio) increases efficiency over the baseline, but only after an initial period where the direct costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. Over time, as the threat of punishment sustains cooperation and the use of punishment declines, efficiency improves. The effect is robust to the absence of communication and anonymity of punishment. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs (4 players, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.5, linear punishment, no chat, anonymous), enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, especially in later rounds. The cost structure of punishment is critical: with a 1:1 cost-impact, the efficiency gain is positive but moderate; more effective punishment (higher impact per cost) would likely yield larger efficiency gains. The paper also shows that reward mechanisms alone are insufficient to sustain efficiency. These results should be directly used for prediction in similar lab PGG settings.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: Introducing a costly punishment option in a repeated public goods game with groups of four, a known 10-round horizon, and no communication or identification, leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions and, after an initial period of adjustment, to higher group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the no-punishment condition, contributions and efficiency decline toward full free-riding. In the punishment condition, contributions are sustained at high levels (often near full cooperation), and group payoffs in the final periods are 10-20% higher than in the control. Punishment is used primarily against those who contribute less than the group average, and punished individuals tend to increase their contributions in subsequent rounds. The effect is robust across both stranger (random rematching) and partner (fixed group) treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and anonymous interactions. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low (due to declining contributions), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency by 10-20% in the final rounds, with the effect being stronger in fixed groups (partner) than in random groups (stranger). The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known horizon, full feedback, anonymous punishment) closely match the canonical prediction task, making this paper highly informative for forecasting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled.

- source: Ambrus, A., & Greiner, B. (2012). Imperfect Public Monitoring with Costly Punishment: An Experimental Study. *American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In 50-round, three-player public goods games with all-or-nothing contributions, the introduction of costly punishment options has different effects depending on monitoring and punishment severity. With perfect monitoring, both regular and strong punishment increase average net payoffs, with stronger punishment yielding higher efficiency. With imperfect (noisy) monitoring, regular punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline, while strong punishment increases cooperation but only restores efficiency to the no-punishment level (i.e., the gains from increased cooperation are offset by the costs of punishment). The relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is U-shaped under noise: intermediate punishment is worst for efficiency, while strong punishment is neutral. The paper provides direct, quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes for each treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, especially as a function of monitoring quality and punishment severity. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive: under imperfect monitoring, standard punishment can reduce efficiency, and only very strong punishment can (at best) restore efficiency to the no-punishment baseline. The results are directly applicable to settings with all-or-nothing contributions, 3 players, 50 rounds, and known MPCR and punishment parameters. The findings highlight the importance of monitoring quality and punishment effectiveness in predicting efficiency outcomes.

In LAW, legal sanctions are nondeterrent in isolation but, when combined with social punishment, they increase cooperation (mean contribution rises from 11.8 to 15.1 out of 20) and total efficiency (mean payoff rises from 18.59 to 24.65, a 33% increase). The effect is driven by higher contributions and lower social punishment costs, even though legal sanctions partially substitute for social ones. The design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar game designs.

- source: Fudenberg, D., & Pathak, P. A. (2010). Unobserved punishment supports cooperation. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares two versions of a public goods game with punishment: one where punishment is revealed after each round (Observed), and one where punishment is only revealed at the end of 10 rounds (Unobserved). Both treatments use random rematching, groups of 4, 10 rounds per set, and a standard VCM payoff structure (endowment 20, MPCR 0.4). Punishment costs 1 token per point assigned and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) average contributions are significantly higher in the Unobserved treatment than in the Observed treatment; (2) average income (efficiency) is also higher in the Unobserved treatment, though confidence intervals overlap in some periods; (3) the fraction of participants who punish and punishment expenditure are at least as high, or higher, in the Unobserved treatment; (4) punishment is more sensitive to undercontribution in the Unobserved treatment. The results show that costly punishment supports cooperation and efficiency even when it cannot have immediate strategic or reputational effects, suggesting an intrinsic motivation for punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with punishment, the presence of a punishment option increases efficiency (average income) relative to the baseline with no punishment, and that this effect is robust to whether punishment is immediately observed or only revealed at the end. The game design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds per set, no chat, continuous contribution, no default, MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 1, punishment tech 3, no reward, random rematching, and full feedback on others' contributions. The results suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the effect is not dependent on immediate feedback about punishment. The paper is directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in standard PGGs with punishment.

- source: Casari, M., & Luini, L. (2009). Cooperation under alternative punishment institutions: An experiment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares three peer punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game: Baseline (standard, individual punishment), Sequential (punishment with information about others' punishment actions), and Consensual (punishment only enacted if at least two group members request it). All treatments use groups of 5, 20 rounds (10 without punishment, 10 with), no communication, and full information about group contributions and payoffs (but not identities). The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment costs 1 token per point and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) Punishment opportunities increase contributions in all treatments, but only the Consensual institution increases net earnings (efficiency) above the no-punishment baseline. In Baseline and Sequential, punishment reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. (2) The Consensual institution achieves higher cooperation and efficiency with less punishment, as it censors anti-social punishment (punishment of high contributors) and requires consensus to punish. (3) Providing more information about others' punishment actions (Sequential) does not improve coordination or efficiency. (4) The form of the punishment institution is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the institutional details of the punishment mechanism. In standard and sequential punishment settings (where any individual can punish unilaterally), punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. In contrast, a consensual punishment rule (requiring at least two agents to agree to punish) both increases contributions and improves efficiency, even surpassing the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be inferred from control efficiency and game structure alone; the specific punishment institution (especially whether it censors anti-social punishment and requires consensus) is a key moderator. The paper also shows that increasing information about others' punishment actions does not necessarily improve efficiency. Thus, for games with similar structure, expect punishment to reduce efficiency unless the institution filters out anti-social punishment and requires consensus.

Contribution feedback leads to the highest efficiency and contributions, earnings feedback leads to lower efficiency and contributions, and providing both types of feedback leads to the lowest efficiency due to increased punishment and lack of convergence. For prediction tasks, this means that even with identical game design dimensions and control efficiency, the realized efficiency with punishment can vary dramatically depending on whether feedback is about contributions, earnings, or both. Contribution feedback supports higher efficiency, while earnings feedback undermines it. If both are shown, efficiency can be even lower than with earnings feedback alone. Thus, the 'show_other_summaries' dimension (and its content) is a critical moderator for predicting treatment efficiency.

In fact, average earnings (a direct measure of efficiency) were substantially lower in the punishment condition than in the no-punishment condition across all subject pools. The findings highlight the importance of considering the prevalence of antisocial punishment and the social context when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency.

Thus, for the prediction task, the efficiency effect of enabling punishment should be predicted primarily based on the average punishment effectiveness, not on whether punishment power is symmetric or asymmetric.
