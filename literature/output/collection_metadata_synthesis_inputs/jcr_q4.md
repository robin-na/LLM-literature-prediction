- source: Kocher, M. G., & Matzat, D. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that, although punishment increases contributions more than reward or the standard VCM, it reduces group efficiency because the costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. The reward institution, despite a lower maximum leverage, is both the most preferred and the most efficient, yielding the highest average profits. The standard VCM yields intermediate efficiency. The effectiveness (leverage) of both punishment and reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency. There is no significant difference in efficiency or institutional choice between partner and stranger matching. Gender differences exist in preferences for institutions, but not in efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency (average group profit) compared to both the control (standard VCM) and the reward institution. The efficiency loss is due to the cost of punishment actions. The reward institution, even with lower maximum leverage, is more efficient than both punishment and the standard VCM. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency, unless the punishment technology is extremely effective and/or punishment is used sparingly. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across partner and stranger matching, and is not moderated by self-selection or communication (which is absent). The paper also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment or reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Carpenter, J., Kariv, S., & Schotter, A. (2012). Network architecture, cooperation and punishment in public good experiments. *Review of Economic Design*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies public goods games with costly punishment under eight different network architectures, varying completeness, connectedness, and directedness. All games have four players, 15 rounds, and fixed MPCR and punishment cost. The main finding is that network structure crucially moderates the effect of punishment on both cooperation and efficiency. Complete and some connected networks achieve high efficiency because they elicit high contributions with relatively low punishment expenditures. In contrast, directed and disconnected networks often see more punishment and lower efficiency, despite sometimes higher contributions. The marginal benefit of adding monitoring edges is non-linear: adding edges to sparse networks increases efficiency, but in denser networks, more edges can actually reduce efficiency due to over-punishment. The paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment is not uniform but depends on the monitoring/punishment network.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that network architecture (who can punish whom) is a critical moderator. In games with complete or well-connected networks, punishment increases contributions and can improve efficiency, especially when punishment expenditures are low. In directed or disconnected networks, punishment is more frequent and severe, often reducing efficiency due to high punishment costs. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and other game parameters are held constant, so the findings isolate the effect of network structure. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one must account for the network's connectedness, completeness, and directedness, as these determine whether punishment will be efficiency-enhancing or not. The results suggest that simply enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the network context is decisive.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

  findings: The paper finds that implementing rare but severe concerted punishment in a Public Goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable and increase group efficiency. The threshold probability for punishment to be effective decreases as the severity of punishment increases and as the population size increases. The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Ishikawa, B. Y. S., & Fontanari, J. F. (2025). Revisiting institutional punishment in the N-person prisoner's dilemma. *Theory in Biosciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

Selfish punishment by a powerful minority can prevent the collapse into pure selfishness when power is highly asymmetric, but this comes at the cost of reduced group productivity compared to scenarios where altruistic punishment prevails. In environments with high power asymmetry, punishment may maintain some cooperation but at a lower efficiency due to the persistence of selfish punishers. In more egalitarian settings, punishment is more likely to be altruistic, leading to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with symmetric power and distributed punishment opportunities will see greater efficiency gains from punishment, while those with concentrated or asymmetric power may see only partial gains or even efficiency losses if selfish punishment dominates.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based guidance that punishment mechanisms in public goods games can increase cooperation rates, but often at a cost that may reduce group efficiency or average payoff, especially in peer punishment settings. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment is likely to increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, and in some cases may reduce it due to the costs of punishment.
