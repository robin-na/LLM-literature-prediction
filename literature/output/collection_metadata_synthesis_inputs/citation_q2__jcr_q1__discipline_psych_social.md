- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., & Reddmann, L. (2022). Shadow of conflict: How past conflict influences group cooperation and the use of punishment. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a lab experiment with groups of four, participants either experienced an asymmetric intergroup conflict (attacker-defender game) or not, and then played a repeated public goods game with peer punishment. The main findings are: (1) Prior conflict does not significantly reduce average cooperation rates, but does reduce group earnings (efficiency) due to less effective punishment. (2) When previous conflict roles are identifiable, punishment is less likely to be targeted at free-riders and is less effective at increasing cooperation, especially when administered by previous attackers. (3) Hiding previous conflict roles (resetting group tags) increases both cooperation and group earnings over time, and punishment regains its effectiveness. (4) The negative effect of conflict on efficiency is not due to lower cooperation per se, but to maladaptive punishment patterns that reduce the welfare gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with peer punishment, the presence of a prior asymmetric conflict (with identifiable roles) reduces group efficiency (earnings) even if average cooperation rates are not significantly lower. The reduction in efficiency is due to less effective punishment: free-riders are punished less, and punishment by previous attackers is less effective. If prior conflict roles are hidden, efficiency increases and punishment regains its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is moderated by the social context—specifically, whether there is a 'shadow of conflict' and whether past roles are identifiable. In standard PGG designs without such conflict history, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, identifiability) are all well specified, and the control efficiency is directly comparable.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: van Aaken, A. (2019). Experimental Insights for International Legal Theory. *European Journal of International Law*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that experimental evidence from public goods games challenges the strong free-rider and sanctioners' dilemma assumptions of rationalist theory. It highlights that individuals often cooperate and punish defectors even at a cost to themselves, and that mechanisms such as communication, framing, trust, and legitimacy can enhance cooperation. Punishment (including decentralized, third-party, and symbolic forms) is shown to be effective in sustaining cooperation, especially when perceived as fair. Outcasting (exclusion) and endogenously chosen rules (consent) are also identified as effective mechanisms.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and related mechanisms (communication, framing, trust, legitimacy) can increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in public goods games and similar environments. It is useful for understanding which design dimensions (e.g., presence of punishment, communication, framing, endogenous rule choice) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct empirical evidence on efficiency outcomes, so it should be used as a source of mechanism-based priors and qualitative guidance rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Eichenseer, M. (2023). Leading-by-example in public goods experiments: What do we know?. *Leadership Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The paper synthesizes 62 experimental studies (mainly linear public goods games) and conducts meta-analyses and weighted least squares regressions. It finds that sequential play (leadership) increases group efficiency compared to simultaneous play. Centralized punishment and reward (where only the leader can punish or reward) further increase efficiency, with punishment having a stronger effect than reward. Peer punishment/reward (where all can punish/reward) have even larger effects. Rotating leadership reduces efficiency. The marginal per capita return (MPCR) and group size also positively affect efficiency, but to a lesser extent. Leaders contribute more than followers, and followers contribute about 79% as much as leaders. Inequality within groups does not generally increase with leadership. Leaders' payoffs are similar to those of average players in simultaneous games. The paper provides quantitative estimates of effect sizes for each moderator.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on game design dimensions. The meta-regressions include variables for group size, number of rounds, MPCR, presence and type of punishment/reward, and leadership structure. The results show that adding centralized punishment (by a leader) increases efficiency by about 0.26 (on a 0-1 scale of contributions relative to optimum), and peer punishment increases it by about 0.47. The effect of reward is positive but smaller. Rotating leadership reduces efficiency. These findings can be used to adjust predicted efficiency when punishment is enabled, given the control efficiency and game design. The paper is highly relevant for the downstream prediction task.

- source: Zschache, J. (2012). Producing public goods in networks: Some effects of social comparison and endogenous network change. *Social Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on networks, the ability of agents to change their social ties in response to social comparison (i.e., to seek out like-minded cooperators and avoid defectors) can sustain or increase cooperation and thus the production of public goods. Low network density and low costs of network change (low cohesion) allow cooperators to persist by forming supportive subgroups, leading to higher efficiency. High initial cooperation and low segregation also promote full cooperation. When network change is costly (high cohesion), cooperation collapses and efficiency is low. The model shows that both forward-looking and backward-looking (adaptive) agents can achieve high efficiency under favorable network conditions, but backward-looking agents require even more favorable starting conditions. Explicit costly punishment is not modeled; instead, social comparison provides a form of endogenous, non-costly social pressure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that endogenous network change and social comparison can substitute for explicit costly punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that game designs allowing for dynamic partner selection or network rewiring (low cohesion/costs) will see higher efficiency, especially when initial cooperation is high and the network is not highly segregated. However, the absence of explicit costly punishment means the results are most applicable to settings where social comparison or endogenous social pressure is the main incentive, not where formal punishment is enabled. The results are directly informative for predicting efficiency in networked PGGs with endogenous tie change, but less so for classic PGGs with explicit punishment options.

- source: Stoop, J., van Soest, D., & Vyrastekova, J. (2018). Rewards and cooperation in social dilemma games. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that net-positive rewards (where the recipient gains more than the sender loses) dramatically increase cooperation and efficiency in standard public goods games (VCM), with most groups reaching high contribution levels when rewards are available. In contrast, in the Claim game—where players can undo others' contributions by taking from the public pool—rewards have only a marginal effect on cooperation, and average net contributions are not significantly different from zero. In the Claim game, rewards are often exchanged bilaterally and are not strongly linked to cooperative behavior in the social dilemma. The results suggest that the effectiveness of rewards in promoting efficiency depends critically on the structure of the social dilemma, particularly whether others can undo cooperative actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of reward mechanisms in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends on whether the game allows for negative actions (i.e., taking from the public pool) and whether the MPCR is certain. In standard VCMs (linear PGGs), rewards can substantially increase efficiency, but in environments where contributions can be undone (as in the Claim game or CPR games), rewards are much less effective. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a 'take' option or uncertain MPCR is a critical moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency with rewards if the game allows for undoing others' contributions. The findings do not inform about punishment effects, as punishment is not studied.

- source: Freytag, A., Güth, W., Koppel, H., & Wangler, L. (2014). Is regulation by milestones efficiency enhancing? An experimental study of environmental protection. *European Journal of Political Economy*.

  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether adding intermediate contribution targets ('milestones') to a cumulative threshold public goods game increases efficiency, measured by group payoffs and the probability of reaching a final target. Control efficiency (without milestones/punishment) is a strong moderator: if control efficiency is already high, milestones may not help and can even hurt. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions in cumulative threshold public goods games with milestone/punishment features.
