- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y. (2013). Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.

  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.
