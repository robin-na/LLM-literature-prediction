- source: Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2016). Peer discipline and incentives within groups. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment, when feasible and not prohibitively costly, enables groups to sustain full contribution to public goods and achieve high efficiency, even in large groups. The model provides conditions under which peer discipline is feasible and optimal, and shows that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the cost and effectiveness of the punishment and audit technology. The model also shows that, in the absence of peer punishment, only small groups can achieve efficient outcomes, while large groups fail to contribute. The results are robust to various extensions, including renegotiation, spillover costs, and generalized auditing procedures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling peer punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that punishment and audit costs are not too high. The model gives explicit formulas for group utility as a function of game parameters, including player count, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in larger groups and when the punishment technology is effective and not too costly. The model also highlights the importance of the cost structure of punishment and auditing, and the quality of monitoring signals, for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment.

- source: Wolitzky, A. (2013). Cooperation with Network Monitoring. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. For pure public goods, efficiency is typically increasing in group size; for divisible public goods, it is typically decreasing. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: Hwang, S. H. (2017). Conflict technology in cooperation: The group size paradox revisited. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that coordinated punishment in public goods games can lead to a concentration effect, making it easier to sustain cooperation and achieve high group efficiency as group size increases. When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run. This result is robust to several variations, including the presence of unconditional punishment costs and some decay in coordination as group size increases. The model provides a theoretical explanation for empirical findings that larger groups can sometimes achieve higher cooperation and efficiency, contrary to standard free-rider logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

- source: Thöni, C. (2014). Inequality aversion and antisocial punishment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that, while inequality aversion can theoretically motivate antisocial punishment under certain conditions, empirical data from several public goods game experiments indicate that most antisocial punishment acts do not fit this explanation. Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The findings suggest that other motives, such as revenge or a desire to harm others, are more likely explanations for antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

- source: Spitzer, M. L. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper does not present new empirical findings but critiques the interpretability of Kocher and Matzat's experimental results. It notes that subjects' preferences over punishment and reward mechanisms are difficult to interpret due to the multiplicity of possible utility functions and equilibria. The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. The author suggests that simpler experimental designs would make it easier to interpret the effects of institutional choices on cooperation and contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-good games. However, it offers important cautionary notes: (1) the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism (e.g., cost, impact, available choices); (2) subject beliefs and the complexity of institutional choice can obscure the relationship between mechanism and outcome; (3) the presence of multiple equilibria under different utility functions makes it difficult to infer efficiency effects from observed preferences or voting patterns. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

  findings: The model demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with rewards (where giving rewards is costly but beneficial to the receiver), the emergence of a social institution prescribing prosocial behavior (contribute and reward contributors) can lead to efficient public good provision, provided that rewards are only given to contributors and the network structure evolves quickly enough to allow exclusion of non-contributors. If rewards are also given to defectors, or if players update strategies too quickly, the system converges to low contribution and low efficiency. However, it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment, nor does it compare reward to punishment directly. For prediction tasks focused on punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that endogenous exclusion (a form of sanction) can substitute for explicit punishment, but does not quantify the effect of punishment per se. The model's results are most applicable to games with dynamic partner selection and reward, not to standard fixed-group punishment treatments.
