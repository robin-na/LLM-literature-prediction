- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Li, S. L., Du, C. P., Li, X. X., Shen, C., & Shi, L. (2024). Antisocial peer exclusion does not eliminate the effectiveness of prosocial peer exclusion in structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in structured populations, prosocial peer exclusion can uphold cooperation even in the presence of antisocial peer exclusion, except when exclusion costs are low. Antisocial peer exclusion alone suppresses cooperation, but when both types coexist, prosocial exclusion remains effective at sustaining cooperation through complex coexistence and cyclic dominance patterns. The results are reported in terms of cooperation rates and strategy frequencies, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings are robust across different network sizes and parameter sweeps, but are based on theoretical simulations rather than empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that prosocial peer exclusion can maintain high levels of cooperation in spatial public goods games, even when antisocial exclusion is possible, except at low exclusion costs. However, because the outcomes are reported as cooperation rates and not as efficiency or group payoff, the direct quantitative effect of exclusion on efficiency cannot be extracted. The results suggest that, in structured populations, enabling prosocial exclusion (a form of targeted sanctioning) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that the negative impact of antisocial exclusion does not fully undermine this effect. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect support: higher cooperation rates likely correspond to higher efficiency, but the mapping is not explicit. The findings are most relevant for settings with spatial structure, peer-based exclusion, and variable exclusion costs.

- source: Wang, L. C., Liu, Y. Y., Guo, R. Q., Zhang, L., Liu, L. J., & Hua, S. J. (2024). The paradigm of tax-reward and tax-punishment strategies in the advancement of public resource management dynamics. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that both tax-funded punishment and reward mechanisms can promote cooperation and sustain public resources, but only if the natural growth rate of the resource pool is sufficiently high. When the resource growth rate is low, even universal cooperation (driven by strong punishment or reward) cannot prevent resource depletion, so efficiency remains low. For moderate or high growth rates, the system can reach stable states with high cooperation and sustainable resources, but the tax-punishment model can exhibit bistability, meaning the final outcome depends on initial conditions. The magnitude of the tax (which determines the strength of punishment or reward) also affects whether the system stabilizes at full cooperation, coexistence, or defection. The models provide explicit, parameterized predictions for group efficiency and resource sustainability as a function of player count, tax size, and resource growth rate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, parameterized theoretical predictions for the effect of punishment (and reward) on group efficiency and resource sustainability in public-goods-like resource management games. The models are not standard linear PGGs, but are close variants with dynamic, renewable resources and tax-based centralized punishment/reward. The findings show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by the natural growth rate of the resource pool and the magnitude of the tax (punishment strength). For low growth rates, punishment does not improve efficiency; for moderate/high growth rates, punishment can improve efficiency, but outcomes may be bistable. The results are most applicable to settings where resource dynamics and centralized sanctioning are key features. The paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions in such environments.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

- source: Grimalda, G., Belianin, A., Hennig-Schmidt, H., Requate, T., & Ryzhkova, M. (2022). Sanctions and international interaction improve cooperation to avert climate change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment tested the effect of sanctions (costly punishment) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma with German and Russian participants, both in national and international groups. Sanctions increased group cooperation (as measured by the probability of loss avoidance, PLA), especially for Russian participants and in international groups. However, the cost of sanctioning meant that final payoffs were actually higher in the no-sanction treatments, as shown in the supplementary material. The effect of sanctions was positive for increasing cooperation and loss avoidance, but negative for monetary efficiency. The combination of international interaction and sanctions was particularly effective at raising cooperation among low cooperators (Russians), leading to convergence with high cooperators (Germans). The effect of revealing nationality was minimal. Sanctioning was mostly prosocial in international groups, and antisocial sanctioning (punishing high contributors) was reduced. Only a small fraction of the endowment was spent on sanctions, but this was sufficient to increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment (sanctions) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma increases group cooperation (PLA) but reduces final payoffs due to the cost of sanctioning. For prediction tasks, this means that the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game may be higher or lower than the control, depending on whether the benefit from increased loss avoidance outweighs the cost of sanctions. In this experiment, the cost of sanctions was high enough that final payoffs were higher in the no-sanction condition, even though cooperation (PLA) was higher with sanctions. The effect is especially pronounced for groups with low baseline cooperation, and in international groups. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, group composition (nationality/culture), and the structure of the risk/loss. The game is a close variant of a PGG, but with a threshold and probabilistic loss, so direct transfer to standard PGGs should be cautious.

- source: Yaman, A., Leibo, J. Z., Iacca, G., & Wan Lee, S. (2023). The emergence of division of labour through decentralized social sanctioning. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that decentralized social sanctioning, implemented as a matrix of role-based rules for reward and punishment, enables groups of self-interested agents to learn and maintain efficient divisions of labour. This leads to substantially higher average group payoff compared to groups without sanctioning, or with only altruistic or selfish learning. The effect is robust across different environmental conditions and is close to the theoretical upper bound. Complexity regularization can promote the evolution of simpler, convergent social norms without sacrificing group payoff. The model shows that sanctioning transforms the incentive structure, making it individually rational to take on critical but otherwise unrewarding roles, thus solving the social dilemma.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling decentralized social sanctioning (punishment and/or reward) in public-goods-like environments can substantially increase group efficiency, especially when critical roles are otherwise unrewarding. The effect is robust across a range of environmental parameters and is close to the optimal achievable by global optimization. The model is not a direct PGG but is structurally adjacent, with payoff interdependence and social dilemmas. The findings support the prediction that, all else equal, enabling punishment (and/or reward) will increase efficiency relative to a control with sanctioning disabled, especially in settings where division of labour is required. The model also highlights the importance of norm complexity and the potential for simple sanctioning rules to be highly effective.

- source: Batistoni, T., Barclay, P., & Raihani, N. J. (2022). Third-party punishers do not compete to be chosen as partners in an experimental game. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment and helping are both used as signals of cooperative intent, but helping is a stronger and less ambiguous signal than punishment. Observability increases investments in both, but the possibility of partner choice does not further escalate investments. Higher investments in punishment or helping increase the likelihood of being chosen as a partner and being trusted, but this effect is much stronger for helping. Investments in both are positively associated with actual trustworthiness in a Trust Game. The study does not address group efficiency or total payoff in a public goods game, but provides evidence on the reputational and signaling functions of punishment and helping.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent to PGGs, using a Dictator Game with third-party punishment/helping and a subsequent Trust Game. The main outcomes are individual-level signaling, partner choice, and trust/trustworthiness, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment is a weaker and more ambiguous signal of cooperative intent than helping, and that reputational incentives may increase costly punishment/helping, but do not escalate under partner choice. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in PGGs, this paper provides only indirect, contextual evidence about the social and reputational motivations for punishment, not about its effect on group payoff or efficiency.

  findings: The study finds that in repeated networked games (trust game, ultimatum bargaining, and two-stage prisoner's dilemma with punishment), oxytocin administration to central (hub) players increases their willingness to enforce cooperation norms via costly punishment. This enforcement, rather than mere cooperation or trust, is what enables cooperation to spread throughout the network. Manipulating peripheral nodes' punishment or cooperation has much weaker effects. The main outcomes are cooperation and punishment rates, not group efficiency or total payoff, though some payoff equality measures are reported in the ultimatum game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and effectiveness of costly punishment, especially when wielded by central/influential players, is a key moderator of cooperation in repeated networked social dilemmas. The findings suggest that, in networked repeated games with punishment, enabling effective punishment by central players can substantially increase cooperation rates, and by extension, may improve efficiency, but this is not directly measured. The results are most relevant for predicting behavioral outcomes (cooperation, norm enforcement) rather than efficiency as defined by group payoff relative to full cooperation. The design dimensions most strongly supported are network structure (heterogeneity, centrality), punishment existence and effectiveness, and repeated interaction. The study does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency change due to punishment, so its use for predicting efficiency outcomes is indirect and should be supplemented with studies that report group payoff or efficiency directly.

The design is adjacent to PGGs, focusing on third-party responses to unfairness rather than first-party cooperation or group provision. However, it provides informative evidence about the psychological and neural mechanisms that may underlie punishment and compensation choices, and how prior experiences and mood can shift the likelihood of punishment versus compensation. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address how punishment affects group payoff or efficiency.

- source: Wang, Y. F., & Meng, X. Z. (2023). Evolutionary game dynamics of cooperation in prisoner?s dilemma with time delay. *Mathematical Biosciences and Engineering*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a 2-player prisoner's dilemma, adding a penalty for mutual defection (punishment) leads to stable coexistence of cooperators and defectors when the penalty exceeds the cost of cooperation (a > c). The equilibrium proportion of cooperators increases with the penalty size. When time delays are introduced, the system can undergo a Hopf bifurcation: for small delays, the equilibrium is stable, but for delays above a critical threshold (which decreases as penalty increases), the system oscillates between cooperation and defection. Adding mutation (random switching between strategies) slightly reduces the equilibrium proportion of cooperators, but does not fundamentally change the qualitative dynamics. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on the frequency of cooperation and the stability of these frequencies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that increasing the severity of punishment for defection in a prisoner's dilemma increases the equilibrium proportion of cooperators, which is typically associated with higher group payoff in such games. However, the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence. The results suggest that, in similar 2-player games, enabling punishment (with sufficient severity) will increase cooperation rates, and thus likely improve efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. However, if time delays are present and large, the system may become unstable and oscillate, which could reduce average efficiency. The findings are most relevant for settings with 2 players, no communication, continuous contribution/strategy choice, and deterministic evolutionary dynamics. The results are less directly transferable to standard public goods games with more players, explicit payoff reporting, or experimental data.

- source: Murase, Y., & Baek, S. K. (2023). Grouping promotes both partnership and rivalry with long memory in direct reciprocity. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The main finding is that group structure, when combined with the availability of longer-memory strategies (which allow for friendly rival strategies), leads to the evolution of nearly full cooperation and maximal efficiency in the repeated donation game. In well-mixed populations, longer memory does not have this effect. The results are robust to the benefit-to-cost ratio and to details of the evolutionary process. The paper does not study public goods games or explicit punishment mechanisms, but the repeated donation game is adjacent to PGGs, and friendly rival strategies function as a form of endogenous sanctioning (never allowing the co-player to earn more). Efficiency is measured as the fraction of time mutual cooperation is achieved, which is equivalent to group payoff relative to the maximum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player repeated donation games (adjacent to PGGs), the combination of group structure and the availability of longer-memory strategies (enabling friendly rival strategies) can dramatically increase efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise be difficult. While there is no explicit punishment mechanism, friendly rival strategies act as endogenous enforcers of cooperation by never allowing the co-player to earn more. For prediction tasks, this suggests that in environments with group structure and sufficient strategic complexity, efficiency can be much higher than in well-mixed or memory-limited settings, even without explicit punishment. However, the results may not transfer directly to multi-player PGGs or to settings with explicit costly punishment.
