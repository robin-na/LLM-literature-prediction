- source: Pleasant, A., & Barclay, P. (2018). Why Hate the Good Guy? Antisocial Punishment of High Cooperators Is Greater When People Compete To Be Chosen. *Psychological Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that when participants in a public goods game with punishment are competing to be chosen for a subsequent trust game, antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is much higher than in a standard PGG with punishment. This effect is present from the first round and persists across rounds. In the control condition, contributions increase over rounds (as is typical when punishment is available), but in the markets condition, contributions do not increase, suggesting that antisocial punishment suppresses the escalation of cooperation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral pattern implies that the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment may be undermined or reversed in contexts with high antisocial punishment driven by partner competition.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the context of partner competition (biological markets) can substantially increase antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, which in turn suppresses the usual increase in cooperation seen with punishment. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings imply that in environments where partner choice or competitive altruism is salient, enabling punishment may not improve and may even harm group efficiency, as antisocial punishment undermines cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of partner competition or similar social comparison incentives is a critical moderator: in such settings, the efficiency benefit of punishment is likely reduced or reversed, even if control efficiency is known. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits quantitative prediction.

- source: Lindström, B., Jangard, S., Selbing, I., & Olsson, A. (2018). The Role of a Common Is Moral Heuristic in the Stability and Change of Moral Norms. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across nine experiments, participants observing one-shot PGGs judged common behaviors (whether altruistic or selfish) as more moral and less deserving of punishment than rare behaviors. This effect was robust across lab and online samples, and held for both preexisting moral norms (cooperation/selfishness) and arbitrary behaviors. In one experiment, participants could spend real money to punish, and commonness reduced costly punishment of selfishness. The effect of commonness was explained by a formal model of social influence (Social Impact Theory). Agent-based simulations showed that the CIM heuristic can generate both stability and sudden change in group-level moral norms. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived commonness of behaviors in PGGs shapes moral and punishment judgments, and that common selfishness is punished less than rare selfishness. It also shows that costly punishment is reduced when selfishness is common. However, because the experiments are one-shot and do not measure group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, the findings do not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled. The results are most useful for understanding psychological and behavioral responses to norm violation and punishment, rather than for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions in repeated or strategic PGGs.

- source: van Dijk, E., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2021). Experimental Games and Social Decision Making. *Annual Review of Psychology, Vol 72*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The review highlights that the inclusion of punishment and reward mechanisms in repeated public goods games and other social dilemmas enables partner control, which can promote cooperation and the development of group norms. It notes that punishment is typically costly to the punisher but more costly to the target, and that such mechanisms can shape future cooperation through reputation and indirect reciprocity. The review also discusses moderators such as group heterogeneity, intergroup competition, and cross-cultural variation in norm enforcement. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative synthesis on how punishment affects efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and reward mechanisms can promote cooperation in public goods games and related environments, especially in repeated interactions. It identifies key design features (e.g., repeated play, sanctioning options, partner selection) that are relevant to predicting the impact of punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to calibrate the magnitude of punishment's effect on efficiency. Its value for prediction is mainly in identifying which game dimensions are theoretically important moderators.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schurtenberger, I. (2018). Normative foundations of human cooperation. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. The review emphasizes that the presence of punishment alone is not sufficient for efficiency gains; the structure and normative context of punishment matter greatly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and integrative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff unless punishment is normatively constrained to reduce antisocial punishment and collateral costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the presence of normative or institutional constraints, the prevalence of antisocial punishment, and the cultural context. Game design dimensions that affect the cost, targeting, and normative context of punishment are likely to be critical moderators of efficiency outcomes. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes, but it cautions against assuming that increased cooperation from punishment always translates into higher efficiency.

- source: Henrich, J., & Muthukrishna, M. (2021). The Origins and Psychology of Human Cooperation. *Annual Review of Psychology, Vol 72*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The review argues that mechanisms such as punishment, reputation, and signaling are crucial for sustaining cooperation in large-scale human societies, especially where kinship and direct reciprocity are insufficient. It highlights that the willingness to punish norm violations (including in public goods and related games) varies dramatically across societies and is associated with community size and institutional context. The review synthesizes evidence that punishment can sustain cooperation, but also that the form and prevalence of punishment are culturally variable. It also notes that mechanisms supporting cooperation can stabilize both prosocial and maladaptive costly norms. However, the review does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment mechanisms can sustain cooperation in public goods game-like environments, especially in large, anonymous societies. It also highlights that the effectiveness and prevalence of punishment depend on cultural, institutional, and demographic factors such as community size. However, because the review does not report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency given game design dimensions and control efficiency. Its main value is in identifying which dimensions (e.g., community size, presence of punishment, cultural context) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation, and in providing context for interpreting empirical findings from other studies.

- source: Alvarez-Rodriguez, U., Battiston, F., de Arruda, G. F., Moreno, Y., Perc, M., & Latora, V. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics of higher-order interactions in social networks. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper shows that in PGGs with higher-order interactions (hypergraphs), the critical synergy factor required for cooperation decreases as group size increases in uniform random hypergraphs, meaning larger groups can foster cooperation more easily (at a given synergy factor). Heterogeneity in group membership (hyperdegree) and group size can either facilitate or hinder cooperation, depending on the structure: scale-free (hub-dominated) hypergraphs inhibit cooperation, while uniform or less heterogeneous structures do not. The framework is validated with empirical data from scientific collaborations, showing that the synergy factor (group benefit) is a non-monotonic function of group size, with an optimal group size for maximum benefit. The paper does not study punishment, rewards, or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency and payoff outcomes in PGG-like environments with varying group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. It is directly relevant for understanding how group size and network heterogeneity affect the critical conditions for cooperation and group payoff, but cannot inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment. Use this paper to inform baseline efficiency predictions in control (no-punishment) conditions as a function of group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but not for treatment effects of punishment.

- source: Barclay, P., & Barker, J. L. (2020). Greener Than Thou: People who protect the environment are more cooperative, compete to be environmental, and benefit from reputation. *Journal of Environmental Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that (1) people who engage in more pro-environmental behaviors are more likely to cooperate in a continuous Prisoner's Dilemma; (2) people donate more to an environmental charity when their donations are observed, and even more when competing to be chosen as a partner; (3) observers preferentially choose and cooperate more with those who donated more to the environment; (4) the correlation between environmentalism and cooperation is partially mediated by concern for others. The study does not include punishment or sanctions, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide informative evidence about the effects of observability and partner choice (reputation/competition) on cooperative and pro-environmental behavior in a continuous Prisoner's Dilemma. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly relevant. For prediction tasks involving the effects of observability or partner choice on cooperation, the findings suggest that such mechanisms can increase cooperative behaviors, but no efficiency or payoff data are available.

- source: Horne, C., & Mollborn, S. (2020). Norms: An Integrated Framework. *Annual Review of Sociology, Vol 46*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper reviews and synthesizes sociological theories of norms, focusing on why and how people enforce norms (including through punishment and reward), and the implications for social order and inequality. It distinguishes between consequentialist (cooperation-focused), relational (coordination-focused), and agentic (contextual/individual-focused) approaches. The review highlights that sanctions (punishment and reward) can increase norm compliance, but also that enforcement can reinforce inequalities and sometimes undermine collective welfare, depending on the structure of interests and relationships. The paper does not report new empirical findings or analyze efficiency or group payoff in experimental games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical context for understanding the role of punishment and reward in norm enforcement and social order, but does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative findings that can be directly used to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games or similar experimental settings. It may inform the interpretation of why punishment can sometimes promote or undermine cooperation, but does not offer parameter-level guidance for the prediction task.

- source: Dhaliwal, N. A., Patil, I., & Cushman, F. (2021). Reputational and cooperative benefits of third-party compensation. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The main finding is that third-party compensation leads to greater reputational and partner choice benefits than third-party punishment, as judged by observers in hypothetical scenarios and vignettes. Compensators are seen as more moral, trustworthy, and are more often chosen as partners in economic games (Trust Game, Dictator Game). These effects are robust across different contexts, including workplace and real-life vignettes. The paper also finds that compensators tend to have lower Dark Triad personality traits. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and does not provide evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or third-party punishment games. Its findings are relevant for understanding reputational and partner choice consequences of punishment versus compensation, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes. The game design dimensions are described in detail for the hypothetical games, but the dependent variables are not payoff-based. Therefore, this paper should not be used as primary evidence for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency.

- source: Pedersen, E. J., McAuliffe, W. H. B., & McCullough, M. E. (2018). The Unresponsive Avenger: More Evidence That Disinterested Third Parties Do Not Punish Altruistically. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Across five lab experiments, participants who were directly harmed (insulted) by another person reliably punished the insulter using an aversive sound blast. Participants who witnessed a friend being insulted also punished the insulter, but less strongly. However, participants who witnessed a stranger being insulted generally did not punish the insulter more than they punished uninvolved bystanders, except in one experiment where social censure may have been a factor. The results suggest that third-party punishment is not a robust, general tendency, but is contingent on welfare interdependence (e.g., self or friends). The findings cast doubt on the external validity of economic game results that report high rates of third-party punishment, suggesting those may be inflated by experimental demand. No efficiency or payoff-based outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. Its main contribution is to question the generalizability of third-party punishment effects observed in economic games, suggesting that such effects may be overestimated due to experimental demand. For the downstream prediction task, this paper signals that the presence of punishment opportunities does not guarantee increased norm enforcement or efficiency, especially when punishment is not personally relevant or when welfare interdependence is low. However, because no efficiency or payoff outcomes are measured, the paper cannot inform quantitative predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.

- source: Okada, I. (2020). A Review of Theoretical Studies on Indirect Reciprocity. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper synthesizes theoretical advances in indirect reciprocity, showing that cooperation can be maintained in social dilemma settings when reputation mechanisms and justified punishment are present. It details how the refinement of assessment functions (e.g., leading eight norms) resolves issues like the scoring and punishment dilemmas, enabling stable cooperation under certain conditions. However, it notes that these models are not directly applicable to public goods games, as selective cooperation (the core of indirect reciprocity) is not possible in standard PGGs. The review also highlights the importance of the benefit-cost ratio, error rates, and the distinction between public and private reputation systems in determining the stability of cooperation. While punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed, the focus is on their role in norm stability and cooperation, not on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and reputation mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in social dilemmas, but it does not offer direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its models are adjacent to PGGs but differ in key structural ways (e.g., selective cooperation vs. public goods provision). The review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment and norm refinement can support cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment depends on norm structure, reputation information, and error rates, but it does not allow for direct mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency outcomes in PGGs.

- source: Vonasch, A. J., Reynolds, T., Winegard, B. M., & Baumeister, R. F. (2018). Death Before Dishonor: Incurring Costs to Protect Moral Reputation. *Social Psychological and Personality Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that people value their moral reputation extremely highly, often above wealth, success, or even life itself. In hypothetical scenarios, large proportions of participants preferred jail, amputation, or death to acquiring a bad reputation. In lab experiments, many participants chose to endure pain or disgust (cold pressor, touching worms) rather than have information suggesting they were racist shared with others. The motivation to avoid reputation damage is strong and can drive costly behavior. However, the paper does not use public goods games, does not manipulate or measure punishment or sanctions in a game-theoretic sense, and does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does, however, support the broader claim that reputation concerns can motivate costly prosocial or norm-compliant behavior, which is a mechanism often invoked in explaining why punishment can sustain cooperation. The findings may be useful for contextualizing why individuals might comply with norms or avoid free-riding, but they do not inform quantitative predictions about efficiency or the effects of specific game design dimensions. For the prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant and should not be weighted heavily for estimating efficiency outcomes.

  findings: The study finds that age is associated with perceived costs of the pandemic (older people perceive higher health risks, younger people perceive more daily life disruption and job insecurity), but age is not robustly associated with prosocial COVID-19 behaviors (such as social distancing or staying at home) or support for behavioral regulations (such as mandatory quarantine or vaccination). - source: Ember, C. R., Skoggard, I., Ringen, E. J., & Farrer, M. (2018). Our better nature: Does resource stress predict beyond-household sharing?. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that beyond-household food and labor sharing is nearly universal in nonindustrial societies. Societies experiencing higher resource stress (e.g., famine, natural hazards, chronic scarcity) are more likely to have frequent and extensive sharing customs, especially seasonal sharing. However, increased resource stress does not predict broader social or geographic scope of sharing (i.e., with non-relatives or beyond the community); in some cases, sharing with non-relatives is less common under high resource stress. The study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and does not involve experimental manipulation or public goods game paradigms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not involve experimental games, punishment, or payoff-based outcomes. However, it provides contextual evidence that cooperation (in the form of sharing) is more frequent in environments with higher resource stress, suggesting that environmental risk can increase cooperative customs. This may be indirectly relevant for understanding baseline cooperation in environments with or without punishment, but does not inform the effect of punishment or game design dimensions on efficiency.
