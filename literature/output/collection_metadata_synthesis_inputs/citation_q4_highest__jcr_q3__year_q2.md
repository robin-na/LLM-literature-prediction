# Paper Set Evidence Digest

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2013). Enforcement of contribution norms in public good games with heterogeneous populations. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in the absence of punishment, public good groups (both homogeneous and heterogeneous) rapidly converge to low contributions and low efficiency, regardless of the normative appeal of efficiency or fairness rules. When punishment is enabled, contributions and efficiency increase sharply in all group types. About 40% of groups achieve full efficiency (maximal contributions by all) with punishment, while the rest enforce different relative contribution norms depending on group heterogeneity (e.g., equal contributions, proportional to endowment). The increase in efficiency from punishment is substantial and robust, though the exact efficiency level and norm enforced depend on the group structure. The paper provides direct evidence that punishment enables groups to overcome the collective action problem and coordinate on efficient or near-efficient outcomes, even in the presence of heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in linear public good games with three players and ten rounds leads to a large increase in group efficiency, regardless of whether the group is homogeneous or heterogeneous in endowments or marginal benefits. The effect is robust: without punishment, efficiency is low due to free-riding; with punishment, efficiency rises substantially, with many groups achieving full efficiency. The specific norm enforced (equal contributions, proportionality, etc.) depends on the group’s heterogeneity, but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar lab PGG designs, enabling punishment will significantly increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by group heterogeneity and the specific norm that emerges.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Nowak, M. A., & Rand, D. G. (2014). Punishment does not promote cooperation under exploration dynamics when anti-social punishment is possible. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both compulsory and voluntary public goods games, when all forms of punishment are allowed (including anti-social punishment), the introduction of punishment does not increase the average payoff or efficiency of the group under high mutation rates. Analytical results and agent-based simulations show that the average payoff of cooperators is not increased by punishment when anti-social punishment is possible; in fact, the outcome is equivalent to the no-punishment baseline. Only when punishment is restricted to pro-social forms (punishing defectors only) does punishment increase efficiency. These findings are robust to changes in mutation rate and selection intensity.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in PGG-like environments with high exploration/mutation and the possibility of anti-social punishment, enabling punishment will not increase group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative when anti-social punishment is possible, regardless of other game parameters such as player count, rounds, or selection intensity. If the game design allows anti-social punishment, do not expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

- source: Schoenmakers, S., Hilbe, C., Blasius, B., & Traulsen, A. (2014). Sanctions as honest signals - The evolution of pool punishment by public sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and public visibility of a punishment institution in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to control conditions without punishment. The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2014). Optimal distribution of incentives for public cooperation in heterogeneous interaction environments. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2013). Effectiveness of conditional punishment for the evolution of public cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that conditional punishment, where the severity of punishment is proportional to the number of punishers in a group, is more effective than unconditional punishment in promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when punishment is costly. Conditional punishers can maintain cooperation at lower fines and costs, and are more efficient in terms of cooperation per unit cost. Unconditional punishers only outperform conditional punishers when punishment is very cheap. The results are robust across different values of the multiplication factor (mpcr), and the findings are explained through the mechanism of indirect territorial competition and pattern formation on the spatial grid.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the design of the punishment mechanism (conditional vs. unconditional) and the cost-to-fine ratio are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperation and cost-effectiveness. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the results suggest that conditional punishment is more likely to yield higher cooperation per unit cost, especially when punishment is not cheap. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency as a fraction of the maximum possible payoff, so predictions about absolute efficiency should be made with caution. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and no communication.

- source: Seip, E. C., Van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2014). Anger motivates costly punishment of unfair behavior. *Motivation and Emotion*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across three laboratory experiments, the paper finds that (1) participants assign more costly punishment to less cooperative (unfair) players in a PGG, (2) anger mediates the relationship between perceived unfairness and punishment in both PGG and STG settings, and (3) experimentally induced anger increases costly punishment of unfair players in a subsequent STG. The studies do not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment is motivated by anger in PGG and STG settings, and that non-cooperation reliably triggers costly punishment. However, it does not provide any data or analysis on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, which are the primary outcomes for the downstream prediction task. Therefore, while the paper supports the behavioral mechanism by which punishment is enacted, it does not inform whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency under any game design dimension. The design details (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are reported and may be useful for mapping to other studies, but no efficiency outcomes are available.

- source: Han, T. A., Pereira, L. M., & Lenaerts, T. (2017). Evolution of commitment and level of participation in public goods games. *Autonomous Agents and Multi-agent Systems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a commitment mechanism—where players can propose binding agreements before the PGG, with compensation for defection—substantially increases both cooperation and average payoffs, provided the cost of arranging commitment is not too high. The effect is robust across group sizes and compensation levels, as long as compensation is above a threshold. The optimal participation threshold (number of committers required) depends on the severity of the dilemma and the cost of commitment. The model shows that commitment mechanisms can transform the efficiency of the PGG, making group payoffs much closer to the fully cooperative optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling credible commitment mechanisms in PGG-like environments can substantially increase efficiency, especially when the cost of arranging commitment is low relative to the benefit of cooperation. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a commitment mechanism (even if not a classic punishment) should be expected to increase efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the cost of commitment, the mpcr, and the participation threshold. The results are robust across group sizes and compensation levels (above a threshold). However, the model does not directly analyze classic punishment (costly peer punishment), so direct transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be done with caution.

- source: Wang, Z., Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2014). Rewarding evolutionary fitness with links between populations promotes cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that rewarding players whose utility exceeds a threshold by granting them external links to another population can significantly promote cooperation in both public goods and prisoner's dilemma games on structured networks. The effect is maximized at intermediate threshold values, where rewarded players are rare but able to percolate, acting as catalysts for cooperation. If the threshold is too low or too high, the effect diminishes. The main reported outcome is the fraction of cooperators; efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed. The results are robust across network types and game details. Punishment is not modeled, only discussed as background.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It models a reward mechanism (external links for high-fitness players) and analyzes its effect on cooperation rates, not on efficiency or payoff. While it discusses the relative merits of reward and punishment in the introduction, all results pertain to reward. Therefore, it is not directly informative for the downstream prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency, except as context for how reward mechanisms can promote cooperation under certain network and threshold conditions.

- source: Hilbe, C., Wu, B., Traulsen, A., & Nowak, M. A. (2015). Evolutionary performance of zero-determinant strategies in multiplayer games. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that repeated interactions (direct reciprocity) can sustain cooperation and high efficiency in small groups, but not in large groups. As group size increases, the evolutionary dynamics favor defectors and extortioners, leading to a sharp drop in average group payoff. The results are robust across both restricted (ZD strategies only) and general (all memory-one strategies) strategy spaces. The paper provides explicit formulas for payoffs and demonstrates, both analytically and via simulation, that group size is a critical moderator of efficiency in repeated public goods games without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, increasing group size in repeated public goods games leads to lower efficiency, even when direct reciprocity is possible. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that the baseline efficiency in control games without punishment will be high for small groups but will decline as group size increases. The paper does not provide evidence about the effect of enabling punishment, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency change when punishment is enabled. However, it does highlight that, without additional mechanisms (like punishment), large groups are unlikely to sustain cooperation or high efficiency.

- source: Martin, J. M., Gonzalez, C., Juvina, I., & Lebiere, C. (2014). A Description-Experience Gap in Social Interactions: Information about Interdependence and Its Effects on Cooperation. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that providing more information about interdependence in a two-player, 200-round IPD increases both mutual cooperation and joint payoffs (efficiency). Four information conditions were tested: No-Info (players unaware of the other), Min-Info (aware of other but cannot see their actions), Mid-Info (can see other's actions and outcomes), and Max-Info (can also see the full payoff matrix). Joint performance (sum of points) was lowest in No-Info and Min-Info, higher in Mid-Info, and highest in Max-Info. The effect of information on performance is fully mediated by mutual cooperation. Punishment (defection after defection) is high in all conditions and does not vary with information. The results suggest that awareness and transparency of interdependence are key to achieving higher efficiency in repeated social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated dyadic social dilemmas structurally similar to the IPD, increasing the amount of information about interdependence (especially showing others' actions and the payoff matrix) leads to higher efficiency (joint payoff). For prediction, if a public-goods-like game allows players to see others' actions and/or the payoff structure, one should expect higher efficiency than in games where such information is hidden. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, simultaneous-move games without explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, but the mechanisms (reciprocation, not punishment) may generalize to other settings. The paper does not manipulate punishment cost or technology, nor does it include explicit costly punishment or reward options, so its direct predictive value is limited for games with those features.

- source: Hilbe, C., Traulsen, A., & Sigmund, K. (2015). Partners or rivals? Strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper provides a comprehensive theoretical analysis of memory-one strategies in the IPD, showing which strategies can enforce mutual cooperation (maximal efficiency), competitive advantage, or linear payoff relationships (ZD strategies). It proves that partner strategies (including Grim and Tit-for-Tat under certain conditions) can enforce mutual cooperation, and that generous ZD strategies can promote cooperation by aligning payoffs. The existence and effectiveness of these strategies depend on the discount factor (patience) and the payoff matrix. The paper does not analyze the effect of enabling or disabling punishment as a separate game feature, but it shows that strategies implementing contingent punishment (e.g., Grim) are critical for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in repeated games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for understanding how strategy design in repeated games can enforce efficient outcomes, but it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling punishment as a separate game feature in public goods games. Its results are most relevant for two-player repeated games (IPD) and show that strategies implementing contingent punishment (like Grim or Tit-for-Tat) are necessary for sustaining cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment in PGG-like environments, the paper suggests that the ability to implement credible punishment (via strategy) is essential for efficiency, but it does not address institutional punishment mechanisms or group settings directly. Thus, its value is mainly theoretical and mechanistic, not empirical or directly predictive for PGGs with explicit punishment options.

- source: Corgnet, B., Espín, A. M., & Hernán-González, R. (2015). The cognitive basis of social behavior: cognitive reflection overrides antisocial but not always prosocial motives. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab studies (US and Spain), individuals with higher CRT scores are more likely to make choices consistent with 'mild' altruism (increasing others' payoffs at low/no cost), while those with lower CRT scores are more likely to act out of egalitarian or spiteful motives. The studies use non-strategic, anonymous allocation tasks (not public goods or social dilemma games) and do not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are directly measured or reported. The findings suggest that cognitive reflection is associated with a specific pattern of social preferences, but do not inform about the effects of punishment or efficiency in public goods contexts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or efficiency/welfare outcomes, nor does it use a public goods or social dilemma design. The findings are about individual cognitive style and social motives in non-strategic allocation games. Therefore, it should not be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency in public goods games with or without punishment.

- source: Diekmann, A., Przepiorka, W., & Rauhut, H. (2015). Lifting the veil of ignorance: An experiment on the contagiousness of norm violations. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when subjects are informed about the extent of others' norm violations (cheating in a dice game), their own rate of norm violation increases in subsequent rounds, compared to a control group with no such information. This effect is robust to whether the information is about an external group or the current session. The effect is not due to punishment, as the design eliminates any possibility of sanctions. The effect is moderated by subjects' prior beliefs: underestimators of norm violations increase cheating when informed, while overestimators may decrease it. The study provides evidence for the contagiousness of norm violations via conditional norm compliance, but does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes in a public goods context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as there is no punishment, no group payoff, and no public good. The findings are relevant for understanding how information about norm violations can erode compliance, but not for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and outcomes are adjacent to the prediction task, offering insight into norm dynamics but not into payoff-based outcomes or the role of sanctions.
