- source: Wu, Z. W., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2014). Punishment Mechanism with Self-Adjusting Rules in Spatial Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

- source: Zhang, C. Y., Zhang, J. L., & Weissing, F. J. (2014). The role of emotions in the maintenance of cooperative behaviors. *Epl*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a stress-based cost for defectors in a public goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable if the stress intensity exceeds a calculable threshold. This threshold is lower for larger populations and higher public goods multipliers, and higher for larger group sizes. The model provides explicit formulas for the fixation probability of cooperation and the conditions under which cooperation can invade and dominate a population of defectors. The findings are directly relevant to efficiency, as they show under what conditions the group can achieve higher payoffs through sustained cooperation, even in the absence of explicit external punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that internalized costs for defection (modeled as stress or moral emotions) can substitute for explicit punishment in promoting cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games. While it does not study explicit punishment, it shows that any mechanism that imposes a cost on defection (whether internal or external) can shift the evolutionary dynamics toward cooperation, provided the cost is above a threshold determined by game parameters (group size, population size, and MPCR). For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of any effective cost for defection—whether via punishment or internalized emotion—can increase efficiency, and that the required cost decreases with larger populations and higher MPCR. However, the model does not address explicit punishment technology, cost, or identification, so its direct applicability to those dimensions is limited.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: McBride, M., Kendall, R., D'Orsogna, M. R., & Short, M. B. (2016). Crime, punishment, and evolution in an adversarial game. *European Journal of Applied Mathematics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that, unlike standard social dilemma games (such as the public goods game), the adversarial game studied here can have multiple equilibria, including both efficient (no-crime, high-payoff) and inefficient (high-crime, low-payoff) outcomes. The presence of punishment (reporting and conviction) transforms the game from a pure social dilemma into a coordination game, where the effectiveness of punishment depends on the collective willingness to report. The Informant strategy (defectors who also report) is critical: under imitation dynamics, any presence of Informants is sufficient to reach efficiency, but under best response dynamics, Informants are necessary but not sufficient. The model shows that too severe punishment or high retaliation risk can undermine the emergence of efficient equilibria. Thus, the design of punishment institutions and the social context (e.g., support for reporting) are crucial moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like adversarial games is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism, the available strategy set (especially the presence of Informants), and the evolutionary dynamics. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under certain parameter regimes: moderate punishment severity, low retaliation risk, and the availability of strategies that combine defection and punishment. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will always improve efficiency; instead, the effect is conditional and can be negative or null if punishment is too harsh or if key strategies are unavailable. The results are most directly applicable to adversarial games with crime and reporting, but the mechanism insights may transfer to public goods games with similar punishment structures.

- source: Luo, J. S., & Zhao, M. (2013). Proportional cost for punishment enhances spatial reciprocity in evolutionary games. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that proportional punishment (where the cost is distributed across a proportion of the population and defectors are fined based on their payoff) enhances both cooperation and average payoff in spatial prisoner's dilemma and snowdrift games. As the proportion of the population bearing punishment cost (p) and the strength of punishment (beta) increase, the average payoff rises and more individuals achieve higher payoffs. The effect is robust across both game types. The paper provides detailed analysis of how payoff distributions shift with punishment parameters, showing that punishment reduces the number of individuals with very low or very high payoffs, concentrating payoffs in a higher, narrower range.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial pairwise games (PDG and SD), introducing a proportional punishment mechanism increases average payoff (efficiency) compared to the baseline without punishment. The effect is stronger as the proportion of the population involved in punishment and the punishment strength increase. While the setting is not a public goods game, the adjacent nature of the games and the explicit analysis of average payoff and its distribution make the findings relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar environments. The results suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment (especially with higher coverage and strength) will increase efficiency relative to the control. However, the model is theoretical and does not address all PGG design dimensions (e.g., no chat, no reward, no explicit round structure), so direct quantitative transfer should be cautious.

- source: Larson, J. M. (2016). The evolutionary advantage of limited network knowledge. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with gossip-based punishment, the ability to sustain full cooperation (and thus maximize group efficiency/fitness) depends on both the structure of the social network and the precision of individuals' knowledge about that structure. When all individuals have precise knowledge of the network, it is harder to sustain full cooperation because some individuals can be identified as weakly connected and thus are tempting targets for defection. When individuals have only coarse knowledge (e.g., know only the average neighborhood size), it is easier to sustain full cooperation, as targeting is not possible. The minimum discount factor required to sustain cooperation is lower under coarse knowledge than under precise knowledge, making full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) more robust. The model thus predicts that limited network knowledge facilitates the enforcement of cooperation through punishment, leading to higher group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the information structure—specifically, the precision of network knowledge. In environments where individuals have only coarse knowledge of the network, punishment is more effective at sustaining full cooperation and thus maximizing efficiency. If a public goods game or similar environment allows only limited information about the network or about others' positions, the model predicts a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency. Conversely, if players have precise knowledge of the network, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency may be reduced. The paper does not provide direct empirical estimates but offers clear theoretical mechanisms and comparative statics relevant to prediction.

- source: Nasrallah, W. F., & Cheaib, K. A. (2016). An equilibrium model of how regulative and normative institutions influence micro-economic and organizational behavior. *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The theta-pi model demonstrates that both punishment (regulative institutions) and empathy (normative institutions) can increase the efficiency of economic interactions by expanding the set of transactions that are mutually beneficial and deterring predation. The model provides explicit conditions under which markets are efficient (all cooperate), collapse (all defect or abstain), or are path-dependent/oscillatory. Punishment (pi) is especially effective in high-temptation, low-sucker-loss environments, while empathy (theta) is more effective when losses from being cheated are high. When some actors are exempt from punishment (elites), efficiency declines and can collapse if the exempt group is too large. Overlapping institutions and partial coverage can lead to complex dynamics, including cycles of cooperation and predation. The model quantifies the marginal value of increasing institutional strength and provides phase diagrams for different transaction types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (increasing pi) in a public-goods-like environment will increase efficiency, especially when the temptation to defect is high and the cost to victims is not overwhelming. The model clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its universality and the structure of payoffs, and that partial or selective enforcement can undermine efficiency. The findings suggest that, all else equal, increasing the probability or effectiveness of punishment will shift the equilibrium from market collapse or low efficiency to high efficiency, provided the institutional coverage is broad. The model also highlights that empathy-based mechanisms can substitute for punishment in some environments, but are less effective when temptation is high and losses are low. For prediction tasks, the model's phase diagrams and threshold conditions can be mapped to game design dimensions such as punishment cost, effectiveness, and universality, supporting the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency in public-goods-like games.

The effect is stronger for punishment than for reward. The mechanism works both by increasing beliefs in others' cooperation and, for punishment, by directly increasing cooperative choices. In contrast, when the underlying game remains a prisoner's dilemma (i.e., the punishment/reward is too small to change the equilibrium), revealing the mechanism does not increase cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in mutual cooperation implies higher group welfare in the context of the stag hunt game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that, in two-player, repeated stag hunt games with centralized punishment or reward, making the institutional mechanism transparent increases cooperation rates, especially when punishment is highlighted. While efficiency is not directly measured, the increase in mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant outcome) suggests that efficiency would also increase. The results are most relevant for coordination games adjacent to public goods games, rather than standard PGGs. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment and making its mechanism salient is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such information, especially when the punishment is strong enough to change the equilibrium structure.

- source: Qu, X. L., Zhou, C. L., Cao, Z. G., & Yang, X. G. (2016). Conditional dissociation as a punishment mechanism in the evolution of cooperation. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
