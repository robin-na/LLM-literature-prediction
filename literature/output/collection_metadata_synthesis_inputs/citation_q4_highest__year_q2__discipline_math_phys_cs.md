- source: Perc, M., Jordan, J. J., Rand, D. G., Wang, Z., Boccaletti, S., & Szolnoki, A. (2017). Statistical physics of human cooperation. *Physics Reports-review Section of Physics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that enabling punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency and sustain cooperation, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defectors. However, the effect is not universally positive: high punishment costs, the presence of antisocial punishment, or poorly tuned parameters can lead to lower efficiency or even the collapse of cooperation. Adaptive and probabilistic punishment mechanisms can improve efficiency by reducing unnecessary costs. The spatial structure of interactions (e.g., lattices, networks) is critical: it allows for clustering of cooperators and punishers, which can support higher efficiency at lower multiplication factors (mpcr) than in well-mixed populations. The paper provides detailed phase diagrams showing the regions where punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on efficiency, depending on the interplay of punishment cost, effectiveness, group size, and synergy factor.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, mpcr, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control only when punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective, and when the spatial structure allows for clustering of cooperators/punishers. If punishment is costly or antisocial punishment is possible, efficiency gains may be lost or reversed. The paper's phase diagrams and model descriptions can be used to map specific game designs to expected efficiency outcomes, given the control efficiency. However, the results are based on theoretical models and simulations, not direct empirical measurement, so predictions should be interpreted with caution and in the context of the model's assumptions.

  findings: The study finds that in a spatial public goods game, punishment is generally the most effective strategy for promoting cooperation and group efficiency, outperforming both reward and the combined punishment+reward strategy except in very narrow parameter regions. The combined strategy (B) can survive and even outperform elementary strategies only when the cost of punishment/reward is extremely low relative to their impact, and/or when the synergy factor is low (i.e., cooperation is otherwise hard to sustain). The results suggest that adding reward to punishment does not generally increase group efficiency beyond what punishment alone achieves, and that the evolutionary advantage of combining positive and negative reciprocity is limited.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, enabling punishment (with or without reward) generally increases group efficiency compared to no punishment, especially when the cost of punishment is not prohibitively high. However, adding reward to punishment does not further increase efficiency except in very narrow, low-cost parameter regions. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with reasonable cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the marginal benefit of also enabling reward is likely negligible except under harsh conditions (low MPCR, low cost). The results are most directly applicable to spatial (networked) PGGs with group size 5, continuous contributions, and symmetric cost/impact for punishment and reward.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2017). Second-Order Free-Riding on Antisocial Punishment Restores the Effectiveness of Prosocial Punishment. *Physical Review X*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible. When the synergy factor is low, prosocial punishment can still restore cooperation and efficiency, because nonpunishing defectors (second-order free-riders) undermine antisocial punishers, allowing punishing cooperators to dominate. The result is that, across a wide range of parameters, the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency, even in the presence of antisocial punishment. The paper also identifies parameter regions with stable coexistence of strategies, but the main result is that prosocial punishment is robustly effective in structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both compulsory and voluntary public goods games, when all forms of punishment are allowed (including anti-social punishment), the introduction of punishment does not increase the average payoff or efficiency of the group under high mutation rates. Analytical results and agent-based simulations show that the average payoff of cooperators is not increased by punishment when anti-social punishment is possible; in fact, the outcome is equivalent to the no-punishment baseline. Only when punishment is restricted to pro-social forms (punishing defectors only) does punishment increase efficiency. These findings are robust to changes in mutation rate and selection intensity.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in PGG-like environments with high exploration/mutation and the possibility of anti-social punishment, enabling punishment will not increase group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative when anti-social punishment is possible, regardless of other game parameters such as player count, rounds, or selection intensity. If the game design allows anti-social punishment, do not expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

- source: Adami, C., Schossau, J., & Hintze, A. (2016). Evolutionary game theory using agent-based methods. *Physics of Life Reviews*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. The effect is quantitatively predicted by mean-field theory and confirmed by simulations: higher probability or density of punishment lowers the threshold for cooperation. However, because punishment is costly, it creates a second-order dilemma, and the evolutionary dynamics can lead to meta-stable states and hysteresis. When punishment is allowed to evolve, its effect is strongest near the critical point, and in highly cooperative or highly defective regimes, punishment is not maintained by selection. The findings are robust across different group sizes and mutation rates, and the paper provides explicit equations and simulation results for the effect of punishment on group payoff and cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (payoff) in the dilemma region by shifting the critical point for cooperation to lower values of the synergy parameter (r). The effect size and direction depend on the punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness), group size, and whether punishment is fixed or can evolve. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.

- source: Schoenmakers, S., Hilbe, C., Blasius, B., & Traulsen, A. (2014). Sanctions as honest signals - The evolution of pool punishment by public sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and public visibility of a punishment institution in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to control conditions without punishment. The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

  findings: The paper finds that probabilistic sharing of punishment duties in a public goods game can solve the problem of costly punishment and promote cooperation, even when punishment is expensive. In well-mixed populations, probabilistic sanctioning transforms the game into a coordination game with two stable equilibria (full cooperation and full defection). In structured populations, sharing punishment duties leads to high cooperation rates, but only if the severity and frequency of punishment are carefully balanced; both too little and too much punishment can reduce cooperation. The main reported outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. Theoretical payoff expressions are provided, and the analysis explains why intermediate punishment is optimal for cooperation, but efficiency is not directly measured or reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the way punishment is implemented (shared probabilistically or periodically) can have a major impact on cooperation rates in public goods games. While the main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, the derived payoff expressions and analysis of invasion dynamics suggest that intermediate levels of punishment severity and frequency can maximize group welfare by supporting high cooperation at lower cost. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the population and the sharing of punishment duties, are important moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, because efficiency is not directly reported, quantitative predictions about efficiency must be inferred indirectly from the cooperation rates and payoff formulas.

Conditional punishers can maintain cooperation at lower fines and costs, and are more efficient in terms of cooperation per unit cost. Unconditional punishers only outperform conditional punishers when punishment is very cheap. The results are robust across different values of the multiplication factor (mpcr), and the findings are explained through the mechanism of indirect territorial competition and pattern formation on the spatial grid.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the design of the punishment mechanism (conditional vs. unconditional) and the cost-to-fine ratio are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperation and cost-effectiveness. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the results suggest that conditional punishment is more likely to yield higher cooperation per unit cost, especially when punishment is not cheap. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency as a fraction of the maximum possible payoff, so predictions about absolute efficiency should be made with caution. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and no communication.

However, it does highlight that, without additional mechanisms (like punishment), large groups are unlikely to sustain cooperation or high efficiency.

- source: Li, L. F., & Xiao, E. (2014). Money Talks: Rebate Mechanisms in Reputation System Design. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when feedback reporting is costly, market efficiency (proportion of efficient trades and participant earnings) declines. Introducing a rebate mechanism, where sellers can offer a partial rebate to buyers for leaving feedback, increases efficiency—especially when sellers frequently offer rebates. The rebate acts as a signal of seller cooperativeness, increasing buyers' willingness to purchase and sellers' willingness to ship. The effect is robust even when the rebate does not fully cover the reporting cost. The more often sellers offer rebates, the higher the efficiency and earnings for both buyers and sellers. If sellers do not offer rebates, efficiency can be even lower than in the no-rebate, high-cost condition. Over time, sellers learn to offer rebates more frequently, further improving efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a mechanism for sellers to precommit to (partially) covering buyers' feedback costs increases efficiency in a repeated trust game with feedback, especially when sellers frequently use the mechanism. The effect is strongest when the rebate is offered often, and the mechanism works even if the rebate is less than the full reporting cost. For prediction, if a game design allows for a precommitment rebate mechanism and sellers are likely to use it, efficiency should be higher than in a comparable game with high feedback costs and no rebate. The control efficiency (with high feedback cost and no rebate) is a lower bound; the treatment efficiency (with rebate) will be higher, approaching the efficiency of the low-cost or automatic feedback conditions as rebate frequency increases. The findings are most directly relevant to trust/reputation games with feedback as a public good, and less directly to standard PGGs or punishment games, but the mechanism is analogous to reward/precommitment interventions in PGGs.
