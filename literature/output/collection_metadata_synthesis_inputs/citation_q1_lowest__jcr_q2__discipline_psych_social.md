- source: Zhang, Z. Y., Elvis, N. T., Wang, J. W., & Hou, G. L. (2024). Can Leading by Example Alone Improve Cooperation?. *Behavioral Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use. The group average payoff in the punishment and reward conditions is higher than in the control and leader-only conditions, indicating a clear positive effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a 4-player, 32-round linear PGG with a 0.4 MPCR and no communication, enabling leader-administered punishment (with a 1:3 cost/impact ratio) increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to both a control (no leader, no punishment) and a leader-only (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Joseph, G., Miquel-Florensa, J., Pahuja, S., Hoo, Y. R., & Tebekew, T. (2025). Motivating Public Sector Employees: Public Good Contributions in Addis Ababa Water and Sewerage Authority. *Economic Development and Cultural Change*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compared three public good game variants: (1) standard, (2) threshold (group must reach a minimum contribution or all is lost), and (3) monitor (one randomly chosen, anonymous group member can punish others at a cost). Both the threshold and monitor games increased individual contributions compared to the standard game, with the threshold game increasing contributions the most. However, because about one-quarter of groups failed to meet the threshold and lost all contributions, the monitor game produced the highest average group payoff (efficiency). The punishment mechanism was effective: monitors punished in about 73% of groups, and punishment was targeted at low contributors. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to order effects and individual characteristics. The study provides direct evidence that introducing costly punishment in a one-shot, anonymous, five-player public good game increases group efficiency relative to the control.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a five-player, one-shot public good game with continuous contributions, no communication, and a known MPCR of 0.4. The monitor (punishment) treatment increased group efficiency/payoff compared to the control. The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

- source: Botelho, A., Harrison, G. W., Pinto, L. M. C., Ross, D., & Rutström, E. E. (2022). Endogenous choice of institutional punishment mechanisms to promote social cooperation. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study replicates and extends classic public goods experiments by allowing subjects to experience both a punishment-enabled and a no-punishment institution, then vote on which to use for a high-stakes final round. Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. Only one session (out of 13) saw a majority vote for the punishment institution, and this was in a small cohort with high returns and high initial cooperation. Statistical analysis shows that subjects' votes are overwhelmingly determined by which institution gave them higher profits, not by which supported higher cooperation. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in matching protocol, order of experience, and marginal per-capita return. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard public goods games with costly voluntary punishment, enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, even though it increases contributions. The effect is robust across a range of game parameters (player count, MPCR, matching protocol, order, and returns). Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.

- source: Chessa, M., & Loiseau, P. (2024). Enhancing voluntary contributions in a public goods economy via a minimum individual contribution level. *Public Choice*.

  findings: The paper demonstrates that introducing a minimum individual contribution level (while still allowing for full free riding) in a voluntary public goods game can strictly increase the total contribution and social welfare at equilibrium, provided the minimum is not set too high. However, it is highly informative about the effects of restricting the contribution set by imposing a minimum, which is a different intervention. - source: Whitmeyer, J. M. (2004). The group control catastrophe. *Sociological Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. Instead, for intermediate monitoring efficacy, compliance can collapse to zero, creating a bifurcation with two possible equilibria (full compliance or full defection), depending on initial conditions (e.g., the number of initial cooperators). The cost to the agent of obtaining compliance can be minimized by using only one type of sanction (individual or collective) and by leveraging initial cooperation to reach the high-compliance equilibrium, especially at moderate monitoring efficacy. However, the model does not directly report efficiency in the sense of group payoff relative to the maximum possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on group compliance and payoffs in public-goods-like environments is highly non-linear and can depend critically on monitoring efficacy, initial conditions, and the structure of sanctions. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.
