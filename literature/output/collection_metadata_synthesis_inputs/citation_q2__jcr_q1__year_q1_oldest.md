- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Wang, Y. F., Nakao, A., Vasilakos, A. V., & Ma, J. H. (2011). On the effectiveness of service differentiation based resource-provision incentive mechanisms in dynamic and autonomous P2P networks. *Computer Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: In dynamic, anonymous P2P environments modeled as public goods games, traditional punishment- and reward-based service differentiation mechanisms fail to sustain cooperation or high efficiency due to the presence of 'cooperator' mutants who contribute but do not punish or reward. Introducing a small entry fee and allowing voluntary participation enables the punishment-based scheme to achieve high average payoff and system efficiency, with punishers dominating the population most of the time. Reward-based schemes, even with entry fees, are less effective and more unstable, with defectors occupying the system more frequently. The effect is robust to group size, total population, and punishment cost. The average payoff in the punishment-based scheme approaches the ideal (but unfeasible) case of a reward-based scheme where rewarders can selectively reward only other rewarders and incur zero cost, but such selective rewarding is not feasible in anonymous, dynamic settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods game environments with anonymous, dynamic, and voluntary participation, punishment-based mechanisms with a small entry fee are much more effective at sustaining high efficiency (average payoff) than reward-based mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the control (no-punishment) condition is dominated by defectors. The results suggest that, if the game allows for voluntary entry and a small entry fee, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of group size, population size, or moderate changes in punishment cost. However, if there is no entry fee, neither punishment nor reward mechanisms are effective. The paper's results are most applicable to settings with anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, and may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.

- source: Bodnar, T., & Salathé, M. (2012). Governing the Global Commons with Local Institutions. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment to maintain high efficiency in a public goods game depends critically on the spatial reach of the sanctioning institution relative to the interaction range of the commons. Both peer and pool punishment can maintain the commons (i.e., prevent the tragedy of the commons and sustain high group payoff/efficiency) if the coverage coefficient (probability that two players can punish each other) is above a threshold. There are sharp phase transitions: small increases in the reach of punishment can lead to large increases in efficiency. Pool punishment can solve the second-order free rider problem and maintain the commons even when peer punishment cannot, but only if the cost of contributing to the punishment pool is low and the punishment imposed on defectors is high. The model also shows that in some parameter regimes, peer punishment can be more effective than pool punishment, especially when the reach of the sanctioning system is intermediate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, provided that the punishment system has sufficient reach (coverage) and the cost/impact parameters are favorable. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not linear but can exhibit threshold effects: if the punishment system is too local or weak, it may have little effect, but once a critical coverage is reached, efficiency can increase dramatically. The model also distinguishes between peer and pool punishment, showing that institutionalized (pool) punishment can maintain efficiency even in the face of second-order free riding, but only if the cost structure is appropriate. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the spatial reach and cost-effectiveness of punishment are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment, and that the absence of sufficient reach can nullify the effect of punishment.

- source: Rosas, A. (2008). The return of reciprocity: a psychological approach to the evolution of cooperation. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. Exclusion-based punishment (TPR) is more stable and efficient, provided defectors cannot exploit cooperators. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency. Theoretical predictions are made for experimental settings, suggesting that people will avoid over-punishment and switch methods when possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Rusch, H. (2012). An evolutionary perspective on the long-term efficiency of costly punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

- source: Goette, L., & Huffman, D. (2007). Affect and the motivational foundations of social capital. *Review of General Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, cooperation typically declines over time due to negative affect and conditional cooperation. However, when a punishment option is introduced, cooperation rates remain high, as punishment deters defection. The motivation to punish is linked to affective responses, such as anger at defectors and satisfaction from punishing. The paper notes that punishment can sometimes be misapplied (e.g., punishing cooperators), which can undermine cooperation. The findings are based on a synthesis of prior experimental studies, not new empirical data, and focus on behavioral and affective outcomes rather than direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong qualitative support for the idea that introducing punishment in public goods games can sustain high cooperation rates and prevent the typical decline seen in repeated games without punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report new experimental results. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is likely to increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency, provided punishment is targeted at defectors. The review also cautions that punishment can be counterproductive if misapplied. The paper is most useful for understanding the mechanisms and moderators of punishment effects, but less so for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

It highlights that punishment power is often used to enforce cooperation, but can also provoke retaliation and cycles of revenge, potentially undermining group outcomes. Delegation of punishment power and the use of centralized or third-party punishment mechanisms can help sustain cooperation. The review also notes that reward can be as effective as punishment in some contexts, but is less well understood. The paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative findings about efficiency or group payoff, but it synthesizes behavioral mechanisms that are relevant to the prediction of such outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual and mechanistic context for how punishment and reward power affect cooperation in public goods games, but does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff outcomes. It is useful for understanding the potential pathways through which punishment may increase or decrease efficiency, such as by deterring free-riding or provoking retaliation. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be moderated by factors such as the structure of punishment (individual vs. centralized), the possibility of retaliation, and the availability of reward. However, it does not provide data that can be directly used for quantitative prediction.

- source: Zschache, J. (2012). Producing public goods in networks: Some effects of social comparison and endogenous network change. *Social Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on networks, the ability of agents to change their social ties in response to social comparison (i.e., to seek out like-minded cooperators and avoid defectors) can sustain or increase cooperation and thus the production of public goods. Low network density and low costs of network change (low cohesion) allow cooperators to persist by forming supportive subgroups, leading to higher efficiency. High initial cooperation and low segregation also promote full cooperation. When network change is costly (high cohesion), cooperation collapses and efficiency is low. The model shows that both forward-looking and backward-looking (adaptive) agents can achieve high efficiency under favorable network conditions, but backward-looking agents require even more favorable starting conditions. Explicit costly punishment is not modeled; instead, social comparison provides a form of endogenous, non-costly social pressure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that endogenous network change and social comparison can substitute for explicit costly punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that game designs allowing for dynamic partner selection or network rewiring (low cohesion/costs) will see higher efficiency, especially when initial cooperation is high and the network is not highly segregated. However, the absence of explicit costly punishment means the results are most applicable to settings where social comparison or endogenous social pressure is the main incentive, not where formal punishment is enabled. The results are directly informative for predicting efficiency in networked PGGs with endogenous tie change, but less so for classic PGGs with explicit punishment options.

Three incentive conditions are tested: (1) standard PGG, (2) no-loss guarantee for cooperators, and (3) equalized bonus for all in successful rounds. Gifted adolescents show higher gain sensitivity and lower loss sensitivity, and are more strategic in maximizing payoffs. Average adolescents are more loss-averse, less strategic, and more influenced by social adaptation behaviors. The study provides detailed evidence on how group composition (gifted vs. average) and incentive structure affect efficiency and cooperation, but does not manipulate or analyze punishment or sanctioning.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on efficiency and payoff outcomes in repeated binary PGGs without punishment or reward. It is directly informative for predicting efficiency in control (no-punishment) conditions with known game design parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.). However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or discussed in the experimental design. Thus, it is useful for establishing baseline efficiency but not for predicting the effect of punishment.

  findings: The experiment shows that a blind, probabilistic punishment mechanism based on group performance can significantly increase average group quality (success ratio) in a cooperative production setting, but only when the cost of contributing high quality is not too high. In the LC treatment, average group quality rises from about 40% to about 70% of the maximum after punishment is introduced, while in the HC treatment, there is no significant improvement. The main outcome is group quality, which is closely related to efficiency, though not reported as explicit group payoff.

For prediction tasks, the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive when the marginal cost of cooperation is low relative to the group benefit, and negligible when costs are high. The design features—no chat, fixed group size (n=4), 20 rounds per block, no individual monitoring, and punishment based on group outcome—are all relevant for mapping to prediction settings. The main limitation is that the outcome is group quality, not direct group payoff, but the two are tightly linked in this design.

- source: Galan, J. M., Latek, M. M., & Rizi, S. M. M. (2011). Axelrod's Metanorm Games on Networks. *Plos One*.

  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment and metapunishment to sustain cooperation in a public-goods-like environment is highly sensitive to the underlying network structure. Specifically, higher clustering and interconnectedness (more triplets per agent) make it more likely that the population will spend most of its time in a cooperative, norm-enforcing state, which is associated with higher expected payoffs. In contrast, higher average degree (more neighbors per agent) can make norm collapse (defection, low punishment) more likely, reducing group welfare. The results are derived from both mathematical analysis of expected payoffs and extensive agent-based simulations, but the main simulation outcomes are behavioral (frequency of norm establishment/collapse) rather than explicit efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, especially when considering the role of network structure. It suggests that punishment (and metapunishment) can lead to higher efficiency (more time in cooperative states) when the network has high clustering and interconnectedness, but may be less effective or even counterproductive in networks with high average degree and low clustering. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff values, its support for quantitative prediction is limited to qualitative or relative effects based on network features. The findings are most relevant when the game design includes networked interactions, costly punishment, and possibly metapunishment.

For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that punishment is only effective in structured (non-well-mixed) populations, and that the effectiveness of punishment depends on network density and punishment strength.

- source: Brick, K., & Visser, M. (2010). Meeting a national emission reduction target in an experimental setting. *Climate Policy*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Voluntary cooperation alone is insufficient to meet a group emission reduction target in a public good game with heterogeneous players. Allowing communication increases average contributions and the proportion of groups meeting the target (from 35% to 50%), but also increases polarization between full cooperation and free-riding. Introducing a tax-based sanction (automatic fine for under-contribution) leads to near-universal compliance with the target (88–91% of groups meet the target), but also crowds out voluntary contributions above the minimum required. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of institutional (not peer) punishment on group compliance and contributions, especially in settings with asymmetric player types and marginal returns. However, because the study does not report efficiency in the standard sense (group payoff as a share of maximum possible), its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. Still, the high compliance rates under tax-based sanctions suggest that efficiency would be much higher with punishment enabled than in the voluntary or communication-only conditions.

- source: Langlois, C. C., & Langlois, J. P. P. (2004). Provisions for noncompliance and treaty value: A game theoretic perspective. *International Studies Quarterly*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated prisoner's dilemma environments with possible unintentional noncompliance (noise), the design of punishment schemes has a large effect on long-run efficiency (treaty value). Probabilistic trigger schemes, which are analogous to mutual punishment phases after a deviation, are self-punishing and reduce efficiency because both the perpetrator and the victim suffer during punishment. Contrite tit-for-tat (CTFT) schemes, which allow for redress (compensation to the victim) and delay retaliation (generosity), achieve much higher efficiency by minimizing the time spent in mutual defection and ensuring that only the guilty party is punished. The paper provides explicit formulas and comparative statics for how efficiency declines with noise under each scheme, and shows that CTFT dominates trigger schemes in most of the relevant parameter space.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the structure of the punishment scheme (especially whether punishment is self-punishing or allows for redress) is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment in repeated social dilemma games. If the punishment mechanism is costly to the punisher and involves mutual punishment phases (as in standard trigger schemes), efficiency is substantially reduced, especially as noise increases. If the punishment mechanism allows for redress (compensation to the victim) and avoids mutual punishment (as in CTFT), efficiency is much higher. The paper also shows that the speed of retaliation (trigger probability) and the expected duration of punishment phases are important: swifter, shorter punishments are more efficient, but only if they still deter defection. These insights should inform predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment based on the punishment technology, cost structure, and whether redress is possible.

- source: Thomadsen, R., & Bhardwaj, P. (2011). Cooperation in Games with Forgetfulness. *Management Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that the effect of forgetfulness on efficiency in repeated games with punishment is fundamentally ambiguous: it can either reduce or increase the set of discount factors and game parameters under which full cooperation (and thus maximum efficiency) is sustainable. When the stage game is such that the cooperative action always yields nonnegative payoffs, forgetfulness weakens punishment and makes cooperation harder to sustain, reducing efficiency. However, when the stage game allows for the possibility that a forgetful player can be punished below the minimax, forgetfulness can make severe punishments credible and thus expand the range of parameters supporting cooperation, increasing efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas and graphical illustrations for these effects in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and a franchising effort game. The results are theoretical and depend on the structure of the stage game and the probability of forgetfulness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games is not monotonic and can be moderated by cognitive or institutional features like forgetfulness. In environments where punishment is only as severe as the stage minimax, introducing forgetfulness will generally reduce the efficiency gains from punishment. However, in environments where forgetfulness allows for below-minimax punishments, punishment can become more effective at sustaining cooperation, potentially increasing efficiency beyond what is possible with perfect recall. For prediction tasks, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined solely from standard game design dimensions; one must also consider whether the environment allows for forgetfulness and whether the stage game payoffs permit below-minimax punishments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates but gives clear theoretical boundaries and mechanisms.

- source: Duéñez-Guzmán, E. A., & Sadedin, S. (2012). Evolving Righteousness in a Corrupt World. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a small benefit to punishers (such as status or a payment from cooperators) destabilizes corrupt equilibria and enables the evolution and stability of 'righteousness'—a state where cooperative punishers dominate and both corruption and defection are suppressed. Righteousness is associated with higher collective payoffs (efficiency/wellbeing) than either corruption or defection. Power inequalities that favor corrupt punishers stabilize corruption, but removing or reducing these inequalities (and/or providing small benefits to punishers) can shift the population to righteousness.
