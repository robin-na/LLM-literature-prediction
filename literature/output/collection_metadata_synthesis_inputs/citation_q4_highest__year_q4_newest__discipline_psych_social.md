- source: Hua, S. J., & Liu, L. J. (2024). Coevolutionary dynamics of population and institutional rewards in public goods games. *Expert Systems with Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional rewards, if the reward strength is adaptively increased when cooperation is low and decreased when cooperation is high, the system can reach stable equilibria with sustained cooperation and group welfare. The model shows that: (1) a stable coexistence of cooperation and reward strength is possible; (2) full cooperation can be achieved with minimal reward intensity if the per capita incentive is above a threshold; (3) moderate cooperation can be sustained with either low or high reward intensity, depending on parameter values; (4) in some cases, even high reward intensity cannot prevent full defection if incentives are too low. The model provides explicit conditions for these outcomes in terms of group size, marginal per-capita return, and reward parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with institutional rewards, but not for games with punishment. It provides theoretical predictions for how adaptive reward mechanisms affect group payoff and cooperation, given game parameters such as group size, marginal per-capita return, and reward strength. However, it does not address punishment, so it does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are useful for understanding the efficiency impact of reward-based interventions, especially when reward strength is allowed to adapt to cooperation levels.

- source: Van Lange, P. A. M., & Rand, D. G. (2022). Human Cooperation and the Crises of Climate Change, COVID-19, and Misinformation. *Annual Review of Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, show_other_summaries
  findings: The review synthesizes evidence that mechanisms such as reputation, reciprocity, and social preferences (including egalitarianism and parochialism) are central to sustaining cooperation in social dilemmas. It highlights that in repeated public goods games, cooperation can be stabilized by making contributions observable and by enabling accountability through punishment or gossip. The review notes that punishment can promote cooperation, but also that gossip may be as effective as punishment and more efficient due to lower costs. The paper discusses the challenges of scaling cooperation to large groups and complex real-world dilemmas, emphasizing the importance of observability, local interactions, and psychological proximity. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions or direct evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment, reputation, and observability can promote cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It provides conceptual support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation, but also cautions that gossip and reputation mechanisms may be equally or more efficient due to lower costs. The review does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency effects, so it should be used for contextual and mechanistic understanding rather than for parameterizing predictive models of efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the inclusion of observability, reputation, and punishment as important moderators, but does not allow for direct estimation of their effects on efficiency.

- source: Boyd, R., & Richerson, P. J. (2022). Large-scale cooperation in small-scale foraging societies. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that large-scale cooperation (hundreds of individuals) in the production of public goods (e.g., communal hunts, construction of drivelines, warfare, habitat improvement) was common in Holocene and likely Pleistocene foraging societies, even at low population densities. Mechanisms such as direct sanctions (ostracism, denial of aid, physical punishment), social norms, and leadership roles were important for sustaining cooperation beyond the scale where mutualistic or reciprocal incentives suffice. The evidence challenges the view that ancestral human cooperation was limited to small groups. However, the paper does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes in a way that would directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides contextual and theoretical support for the idea that punishment and social sanctions can sustain large-scale cooperation in collective action problems, especially as group size increases and individual incentives to free-ride grow. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it analyze the effect of specific game design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, player count) on efficiency. Thus, while it supports the plausibility of punishment as a mechanism for sustaining cooperation in large groups, it does not provide data that can be directly used to predict efficiency changes in public-goods-game-like experiments. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of sanctions and institutions for large-scale cooperation and the likely need for additional mechanisms as group size increases.

- source: Gross, J., & Vostroknutov, A. (2022). Why do people follow social norms?. *Current Opinion in Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper reviews mechanisms underlying norm compliance, such as internalization, social and self-image concerns, and social learning. It highlights that people may follow norms even in the absence of external punishment, and that norm compliance is influenced by beliefs about others' behavior, group identity, and situational salience. The review discusses experimental tasks for measuring norm abidance and notes that rule-following tendencies can predict cooperative behavior in economic games. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative findings about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and contextual background on norm compliance and its measurement, which may inform the interpretation of why punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games. However, it does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates relevant to predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions or control efficiency. Its value for the prediction task is limited to contextualizing the psychological mechanisms that may underlie observed effects in empirical studies.
