- source: Wu, Z. W., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2014). Punishment Mechanism with Self-Adjusting Rules in Spatial Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing manipulators—who both punish defectors and reward cooperators—into a spatial public goods game can sustain cooperation and increase average group payoff, even when the synergy factor (r) is low and cooperation would otherwise fail. The model shows that for a range of parameter values (punishment/reward efficiency, manipulation cost), manipulators and cooperators can coexist, and the group achieves higher payoffs than in the absence of punishment/reward.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-signal evidence that enabling punishment (and reward) in a spatial public goods game increases group efficiency and average payoff, especially when the control (no punishment/reward) condition would result in low cooperation and low payoff. The effect is moderated by the efficiency of punishment (punishment impact per cost), the cost to punishers/rewarders, and the synergy factor (mpcr equivalent). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment (especially when combined with reward) will generally increase efficiency, provided the punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective. The model also highlights that the presence of defectors and the structure of the population (spatial lattice) are important, but the main prediction is robust across a range of parameter values. This paper should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar spatial PGG designs.

- source: Huang, S. T., Dong, Y. L., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). THE OPTIMAL INCENTIVE IN PROMOTING COOPERATION: PUNISH THE WORST AND DO NOT ONLY REWARD THE BEST. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

The results also indicate that the effect of punishment is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the network topology. The paper's detailed mapping of efficiency and strategy frequencies across parameter spaces can be used to inform quantitative predictions of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions.

  findings: Redistributing fines from defectors to cooperators and punishers in a spatial public goods game generally increases the persistence of cooperation and raises the total system payoff, especially when punishment costs are high. When punishment costs are low, the standard pool punishment model (without redistribution) can achieve higher cooperation and higher total payoff, as punishers dominate and defectors are eliminated. The effect of fine redistribution is thus mixed: it is beneficial for efficiency and cooperation under high punishment cost, but can reduce efficiency and cooperation when punishment cost is low. The distribution parameter (alpha) also matters: allocating more fines to punishers (lower alpha) increases cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the cost of punishment and the mechanism for handling fines. If fines are redistributed to cooperators and punishers, efficiency losses from punishment can be mitigated, especially when punishment is costly. However, in settings where punishment is cheap, standard punishment (without redistribution) may yield higher efficiency by allowing punishers to eliminate defectors. For prediction, the presence or absence of fine redistribution, the punishment cost, and the fine distribution parameter (alpha) are important moderators. The results suggest that enabling fine redistribution is likely to increase efficiency when punishment is expensive, but may not always do so when punishment is cheap.

  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods and multi-player PD games with heterogeneous cost-benefit ratios, increasing genetic relatedness does not always increase cooperation or efficiency. For low relatedness, direct reciprocity (punishment via GRIM) can sustain cooperation and high efficiency. For high relatedness, cooperation is a dominant strategy and efficiency is high. The findings are supported by both subgame perfect equilibrium analysis and agent-based evolutionary simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public goods games with heterogeneity in cost-benefit ratios, the effect of mechanisms analogous to punishment (i.e., direct reciprocity via GRIM) on efficiency depends critically on the population structure and relatedness. When relatedness is low, punishment can sustain high efficiency; when relatedness is high, cooperation is self-sustaining. However, at intermediate relatedness, the threat of punishment is undermined, and efficiency drops. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity and the degree of relatedness (or analogous parameters) can cause non-monotonic effects of punishment on efficiency, and that punishment is most effective at low relatedness. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated PGG-like games with heterogeneity, but less so for one-shot games or games with explicit costly punishment mechanisms.
