- source: Kube, S., & Traxler, C. (2011). THE INTERACTION OF LEGAL AND SOCIAL NORM ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares two treatments: BASE (only social punishment) and LAW (social plus mild legal punishment). In LAW, legal sanctions are nondeterrent in isolation but, when combined with social punishment, they increase cooperation (mean contribution rises from 11.8 to 15.1 out of 20) and total efficiency (mean payoff rises from 18.59 to 24.65, a 33% increase). Social punishment is partially crowded out (mean punishment points drop by 57%), but the overall cost of norm enforcement falls and group welfare rises. The marginal incentive to cooperate is higher in LAW due to the additive effect of legal and social sanctions. The results are robust to both between- and within-subject comparisons.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, continuous contributions, and no communication, adding a mild legal punishment mechanism to existing social punishment increases group efficiency substantially. The effect is driven by higher contributions and lower social punishment costs, even though legal sanctions partially substitute for social ones. The design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar game designs.

- source: Nikiforakis, N. (2010). Feedback, punishment and cooperation in public good experiments. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard public good game with peer punishment, the format of feedback (contribution feedback, earnings feedback, or both) after each round has a large effect on group efficiency and cooperation. Contribution feedback leads to the highest efficiency and contributions, earnings feedback leads to lower efficiency and contributions, and providing both types of feedback leads to the lowest efficiency due to increased punishment and lack of convergence. The punishment mechanism itself (cost and effectiveness) is held constant across treatments. The results show that the same punishment institution can have very different efficiency outcomes depending on how information is presented to participants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is not only a function of the punishment parameters, but also of the information environment—specifically, the feedback format. For prediction tasks, this means that even with identical game design dimensions and control efficiency, the realized efficiency with punishment can vary dramatically depending on whether feedback is about contributions, earnings, or both. Contribution feedback supports higher efficiency, while earnings feedback undermines it. If both are shown, efficiency can be even lower than with earnings feedback alone. Thus, the 'show_other_summaries' dimension (and its content) is a critical moderator for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Eckel, C. C., Fatas, E., & Wilson, R. (2010). Cooperation and Status in Organizations. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with a star network (one central, three peripheral players), the introduction of costly punishment increases contributions and efficiency only when the central player is low-status (assigned by lowest trivia quiz score). In high-status groups (central player is highest scorer), punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency. Peripheral players are more likely to mimic high-status central players, but high-status central players do not set higher contribution levels. Punishment is used more in low-status groups, and low-status central players are punished more and earn less. Efficiency, measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible, increases with punishment only in low-status groups. The number of rounds does not affect efficiency trends, which remain flat over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the status of a central player in a star network. If the central player is low-status, enabling punishment increases efficiency; if high-status, punishment has little or no effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, network structure, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) are all clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, in games with a central leader and status manipulation, the status of the leader is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across treatments, but the efficiency gain from punishment is present only in low-status central player groups.

- source: Tyran, J. R., & Feld, L. P. (2006). Achieving compliance when legal sanctions are non-deterrent. *Scandinavian Journal of Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a one-shot, linear public goods game with groups of three, the authors compare no law, exogenous mild law (non-deterrent sanction), exogenous severe law (deterrent sanction), and endogenous (voted) versions of mild and severe law. Exogenous mild law does not significantly increase efficiency or compliance compared to no law (net efficiency: 33% vs. 30%). Exogenous severe law achieves near-maximal efficiency (89%). When mild law is enacted by group vote, net efficiency rises to 62%, nearly double the exogenous mild law and about three times the no law condition. Endogenous severe law achieves 96% net efficiency. The increase in efficiency under endogenous mild law is not due to selection effects but to the causal effect of the law: voting for mild law signals commitment, raises expectations of cooperation, and activates conditional cooperation. If mild law is rejected, efficiency is even lower than in the no law condition. The results show that the effectiveness of mild punishment depends critically on whether it is self-imposed or externally imposed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends not only on the severity of the sanction but also on whether the punishment regime is endogenously chosen (via group voting) or exogenously imposed. For prediction tasks, the presence of endogenous (voted) mild punishment should be expected to yield much higher efficiency than exogenous mild punishment, even when the punishment is non-deterrent. Severe punishment (deterrent) is highly effective regardless of endogeneity. The game is one-shot, with no communication, group size of 3, and a marginal per-capita return of 0.5. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar lab PGGs with and without punishment, especially when considering the mode of law enactment (voted vs. imposed).

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2013). Enforcement of contribution norms in public good games with heterogeneous populations. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in the absence of punishment, public good groups (both homogeneous and heterogeneous) rapidly converge to low contributions and low efficiency, regardless of the normative appeal of efficiency or fairness rules. When punishment is enabled, contributions and efficiency increase sharply in all group types. About 40% of groups achieve full efficiency (maximal contributions by all) with punishment, while the rest enforce different relative contribution norms depending on group heterogeneity (e.g., equal contributions, proportional to endowment). The increase in efficiency from punishment is substantial and robust, though the exact efficiency level and norm enforced depend on the group structure. The paper provides direct evidence that punishment enables groups to overcome the collective action problem and coordinate on efficient or near-efficient outcomes, even in the presence of heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in linear public good games with three players and ten rounds leads to a large increase in group efficiency, regardless of whether the group is homogeneous or heterogeneous in endowments or marginal benefits. The effect is robust: without punishment, efficiency is low due to free-riding; with punishment, efficiency rises substantially, with many groups achieving full efficiency. The specific norm enforced (equal contributions, proportionality, etc.) depends on the group’s heterogeneity, but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar lab PGG designs, enabling punishment will significantly increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by group heterogeneity and the specific norm that emerges.

- source: Anderson, C. M., & Putterman, L. (2006). Do non-strategic sanctions obey the law of demand? The demand for punishment in the voluntary contribution mechanism. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment used a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with three players per group, five rounds, and random rematching (perfect stranger design). After each contribution stage, subjects could punish others at randomly assigned costs per unit of punishment. The main findings are: (1) punishment is used even when costly and even when there is no strategic benefit; (2) the amount of punishment is strongly negatively related to its cost (law of demand); (3) punishment is mainly directed at free riders, but a significant amount of punishment is also directed at high contributors by low contributors; (4) higher contributions are observed when punishment is available and especially when it is cheap, but efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is actually lower when punishment is cheap, due to the direct costs of punishment. Efficiency is highest when punishment is more expensive, as less punishment is used. Thus, the availability and cost of punishment have a non-monotonic effect on efficiency: punishment can sustain cooperation, but its costs can outweigh its benefits for group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the cost of punishment. When punishment is cheap, it is used more, leading to higher contributions but lower efficiency due to the costs incurred by both punishers and targets. When punishment is expensive, it is used less, contributions are somewhat lower, but efficiency is higher. For prediction tasks, this means that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are key moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's design and results allow inference about the relative efficiency across different punishment cost regimes. The findings suggest that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the net effect depends on the balance between increased cooperation and the direct costs of punishment. Game designs with low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment are likely to see the largest increases in contribution but also the largest efficiency losses.

- source: Denant-Boemont, L., Masclet, D., & Noussair, C. N. (2007). Punishment, counterpunishment and sanction enforcement in a social dilemma experiment. *Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the design of the punishment mechanism. In the Baseline (single-stage punishment) treatment, efficiency is high: contributions and earnings are close to the social optimum. When a second stage of punishment is added and counterpunishment is possible, contributions and welfare fall significantly. The positive effect of sanction enforcement (punishing those who fail to punish free riders) is present but smaller than the negative effect of counterpunishment. In the most extreme case, with five rounds of punishment (6SFI), both contributions and welfare are significantly lower than in the Baseline, and welfare is even lower than in the no-punishment scenario. The results show that punishment can backfire and reduce efficiency if it enables cycles of retaliation (counterpunishment) or is too unconstrained.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the details of the punishment system. If punishment is anonymous and limited to a single round, it increases efficiency. If counterpunishment is possible, or if there are multiple rounds of punishment, efficiency can decrease, sometimes below the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, the presence of counterpunishment opportunities, the number of punishment rounds, and the information structure (anonymity of punishers) are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is high in this design, and adding punishment can either increase or decrease efficiency depending on these moderators.

- source: Aoyagi, M., & Fréchette, G. (2009). Collusion as public monitoring becomes noisy: Experimental evidence. *Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that, in both homogeneous and heterogeneous groups, contributions decline over time in the absence of punishment, but increase over time when punishment is available. The opportunity to punish leads to higher contributions, with no significant difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. The origin of endowment (effort vs. windfall) does not affect contribution levels or punishment patterns. Punishment is used to enforce the equal-contribution rule, and both effort and windfall subjects contribute similarly. There is no evidence of a normative conflict arising from endowment heterogeneity. The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in contributions with punishment is consistent with higher efficiency, subject to the cost of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public good game with 4 players, 12 rounds, and a punishment stage, enabling punishment increases contributions regardless of whether endowments are earned or windfall. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions is a strong proxy for increased efficiency, though the cost of punishment may offset some gains. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward, anonymity of punishment, feedback on others' contributions) are all specified and match standard PGG lab designs. For prediction, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency (proxied by contributions) in similar game designs, and that endowment origin does not moderate this effect.

- source: Anderson, L. R., & Stafford, S. L. (2003). Punishment in a regulatory setting: Experimental evidence from the VCM. *Journal of Regulatory Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that introducing probabilistic punishment for free riding in a public goods game increases group contributions, and that increasing the expected cost of punishment (probability × severity) further increases compliance. Punishment severity has a larger marginal effect on contribution than punishment probability. In repeated games, past punishment can actually decrease subsequent compliance, possibly due to a 'lightning doesn't strike twice' belief. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so its findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than welfare or efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard VCM public goods game, introducing and increasing the expected cost of externally imposed punishment increases contribution rates. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The findings are highly relevant for predicting the direction of contribution changes when punishment is enabled, and suggest that both punishment probability and severity matter, with severity being more influential. For efficiency prediction, the results imply that efficiency is likely to increase with punishment (since contributions increase), but the magnitude and any possible over-punishment effects on group payoff are not quantified.

- source: Carpenter, J. P. (2007). Punishing free-riders: How group size affects mutual monitoring and the provision of public goods. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that allowing punishment in public goods games sustains high contribution rates, especially when participants can monitor and punish a larger fraction of the group. Increasing group size from 5 to 10 does not reduce contributions and may even increase them, contrary to standard theory. However, when the ability to monitor/punish is restricted (e.g., Single monitoring), contributions fall to levels similar to the no-punishment control. The effect of punishment is thus strongly moderated by the monitoring technology. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the high contribution rates in the Full and Half monitoring conditions suggest that efficiency would be higher with punishment enabled, especially when monitoring is not restricted.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, enabling punishment (especially with broad monitoring) increases contribution rates, and likely efficiency, relative to control. The effect is robust to group size (5 vs 10) and is strongly moderated by the monitoring fraction: punishment is only effective when players can monitor/punish at least half the group. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, predictions about efficiency must be inferred from contribution rates. The findings are most informative for settings where the main outcome of interest is contribution/cooperation, and less directly for efficiency or total payoff prediction.

- source: Seip, E. C., Van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2014). Anger motivates costly punishment of unfair behavior. *Motivation and Emotion*.

  findings: Across three laboratory experiments, the paper finds that (1) participants assign more costly punishment to less cooperative (unfair) players in a PGG, (2) anger mediates the relationship between perceived unfairness and punishment in both PGG and STG settings, and (3) experimentally induced anger increases costly punishment of unfair players in a subsequent STG. Therefore, while the paper supports the behavioral mechanism by which punishment is enacted, it does not inform whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency under any game design dimension. The design details (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are reported and may be useful for mapping to other studies, but no efficiency outcomes are available.

- source: Galbiati, R., & Vertova, P. (2008). Obligations and cooperative behaviour in public good games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that, for a given structure of low, fixed marginal incentives (probabilistic monitoring, penalty, and reward), increasing the minimum required contribution (the 'obligation') leads to higher average contributions in a repeated public good game. This effect is significant only when the obligation is set high (80% of endowment); a low obligation (40%) does not significantly increase contributions relative to no obligation. Contributions decline over time in all conditions, but the rate of decline is slower with higher obligations. Unexpected increases in the minimum contribution obligation cause a temporary jump in contributions, while decreases do not reverse the downward trend. The study does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, so the effect on efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public good games with low-probability, low-magnitude punishment and reward, increasing the minimum required contribution (obligation) can increase average contributions, especially when the obligation is set high. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes. The results suggest that, under similar game parameters (6 players, 10 rounds, continuous contribution, no chat, fixed MPCR, probabilistic monitoring, anonymous play), introducing or raising a minimum contribution obligation (with fixed marginal incentives) will likely increase contributions, but may not sustain them over time. The effect on efficiency would depend on the cost of monitoring and the penalty/reward structure, which are not analyzed in terms of group payoff. The findings are most relevant for predicting contribution behavior, not efficiency.

- source: Benard, S. (2012). Cohesion from Conflict: Does Intergroup Conflict Motivate Intragroup Norm Enforcement and Support for Centralized Leadership?. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.

  findings: The experiments show that intergroup conflict increases the likelihood that group members will punish noncontributors (enforce norms) and support centralized leadership, but only when the outgroup is actively competing (i.e., high outgroup contribution). - source: Ahn, T. K., Isaac, R. M., & Salmon, T. C. (2008). Endogenous group formation. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that group formation rules significantly affect both contribution behavior and efficiency in a public goods environment. Restricted entry (a form of social punishment) leads to higher contributions in mid-sized groups but smaller group sizes, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to free entry/exit or restricted exit. Restricted exit leads to declining contributions over time and does not improve efficiency. The ability to punish by denying entry is more effective at increasing contributions than the ability to punish by reducing one's own contribution. However, the efficiency gains from higher contributions in restricted entry are offset by the smaller group sizes, so overall earnings are lower. The study highlights that the specific form of the punishment or sanction mechanism is crucial for its effect on cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the design of group formation and sanction mechanisms (such as voting to deny entry) can have substantial effects on both contribution behavior and efficiency in public goods games. However, the effect on efficiency is not always positive: mechanisms that increase contributions may also reduce group size, lowering overall group earnings. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and type of punishment/sanction mechanism, as well as group formation rules, should be considered alongside standard game parameters. The findings caution that not all punishment-like mechanisms increase efficiency, and their effects may depend on how they interact with group size and formation dynamics.

- source: Houser, D., Xiao, E., McCabe, K., & Smith, V. (2008). When punishment fails: Research on sanctions, intentions and non-cooperation. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that threats of punishment (a $4 fine for not meeting the requested back-transfer) do not increase cooperation and can even reduce it, especially when the requested return is high relative to the sanction. This effect is the same whether the punishment is intentionally imposed by the investor or randomly assigned by nature, indicating that incentives, not intentions, drive the effect. When the sanction is used to enforce a low request, it increases full cooperation; when used to enforce a high request, it increases complete defection. The main outcomes are the percentage of the tripled amount returned and the distribution of cooperation/defection types. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, so direct implications for efficiency are not available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation in trust/investment games depends on the relationship between the sanction size and the requested return. Punishment can reduce cooperation when the sanction is small relative to the demand. However, because the study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, it does not directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral responses (cooperation/defection) to punishment, especially in one-shot, two-player, trust-like games with continuous contribution and fixed punishment cost/effectiveness. The lack of efficiency or group payoff data limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Engle-Warnick, J., & Slonim, R. (2006). Inferring repeated-game strategies from actions: evidence from trust game experiments. *Economic Theory*.

The trust game is not a standard PGG, but the findings are likely to transfer to other repeated social dilemmas with similar strategic structure. The presence of punishment strategies is associated with more positive efficiency effects in indefinite games, but not in finite games.

- source: Fonseca, M. A., & Peters, K. (2018). Will any gossip do? Gossip does not need to be perfectly accurate to promote trust. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
