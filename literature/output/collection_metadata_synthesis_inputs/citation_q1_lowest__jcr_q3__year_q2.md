- source: Zhosan, D., & Gardner, R. (2013). Problems of the commons: group behavior, cooperation and sanctioning in a two-harbor experiment. *Environment and Development Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a laboratory experiment simulating a two-commons (two-harbor) resource dilemma, groups of 8 participants (students and professional fishermen) repeatedly chose how to allocate effort between two commons and a safe alternative. The experiment varied institutional features across blocks: baseline (no restrictions), enforcement (costly movement between commons), communication, and communication plus sanctioning. Efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) was the primary outcome. Communication led to large, significant increases in efficiency for all groups, with average net efficiency rising from ~0.8 to ~0.98. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings, then monetary penalties for repeated defection) further increased efficiency in most groups, sometimes reaching 1.0. The effect of sanctioning was generally positive but smaller than the effect of communication, and in one group, sanctioning led to a breakdown in cooperation. The results show that communication and sanctioning can both be effective in increasing efficiency in commons dilemmas, with communication having the largest and most consistent effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling communication among group members in a commons dilemma can dramatically increase efficiency, even before any formal or monetary sanctioning is introduced. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings and monetary penalties) yields further, though more modest, efficiency gains. For prediction tasks, the presence of communication and/or sanctioning should be expected to increase efficiency substantially relative to a control with neither. The effect of communication is robust and large; the effect of sanctioning is positive but may be context-dependent and sometimes less pronounced. The experiment's design and reporting allow for direct mapping to game design dimensions relevant for prediction.

- source: Hsu, L. C. (2013). TAX AUDITING AS A PUBLIC GOOD GAME: AN EXPERIMENTAL STUDY ON PUNISHMENT AND COMPLIANCE. *Pacific Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The endogenous-audit (collective punishment) treatment produces much higher compliance rates than both the no-audit and random-audit treatments. In the final 10 rounds, compliance rates are 0.297 (no audit), 0.758 (random audit), and 0.921 (endogenous audit). However, average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment (85.2 points) than in the random-audit treatment (92.1 points), due to the distribution of fines and discovered taxes. Audit productivity (e.g., reported tax per audit) is higher in the endogenous-audit treatment in the latter half of the game. The main effect of punishment is to increase compliance, but the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous due to the cost of monitoring and the way fines are pooled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a collective punishment mechanism (endogenous audit) in a public-goods-like tax game increases compliance rates compared to both no-punishment and exogenous punishment (random audit) conditions. However, the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous, as average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment than in the random-audit treatment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment can increase compliance but may not always increase efficiency, depending on the cost structure and how punishment/fines are redistributed. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit cost structure, etc.) are well specified and can be mapped to the prediction task, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Antoci, A., & Zarri, L. (2015). Punish and perish?. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary success and efficiency impact of punishment in a prisoner's dilemma with explicit punishment and reward options depend on the type of strong reciprocity present. Altruistic punishment of defectors (SNR) can support cooperation and high average payoff, but only if punishers are sufficiently numerous; otherwise, defectors dominate and efficiency collapses. Altruistic rewarding (SPR) leads to persistent coexistence of types and fluctuating efficiency. Symmetric strong reciprocity (punishing defectors and rewarding cooperators) is maladaptive: it leads to the extinction of cooperators and punishers, resulting in universal defection and minimal efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (PNP) or both defectors and non-punishing cooperators (HSNR) can robustly enforce cooperation and maintain high efficiency, with the PNP strategy being especially robust. The presence of unconditional cooperators who do not punish can undermine the evolutionary stability of punishment-based cooperation. The model shows that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universally positive and can be negative or fragile depending on the sanctioning structure and population heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments is highly contingent on the details of the punishment and reward mechanisms and the behavioral heterogeneity of the population. In prediction tasks, the presence of explicit reward options, the type of punishment (who is punished), and the initial population composition are critical moderators. Punishment of defectors alone can increase efficiency if enough punishers are present, but this equilibrium is fragile. Adding reward can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (second-order free riders) is more robust in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is essential to consider not just the presence of punishment but also the structure of available sanctions and rewards, and the possibility of second-order free riding. The findings caution against assuming that enabling punishment will always increase efficiency; under some conditions, it can be maladaptive.

- source: Laclau, M., & Tomala, T. (2017). Repeated games with public deterministic monitoring. *Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, reward_exists, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper provides a general theoretical framework for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms can be used to sustain efficient outcomes in repeated games with public monitoring. It shows that the set of sustainable payoffs is characterized by admissibility and joint rationality, and that explicit punishment phases (using approachability) can deter deviations. However, the efficiency achievable in equilibrium is limited by the monitoring structure: if deviations are not detectable or not attributable, efficiency may be strictly less than the social optimum. The paper gives explicit constructions of equilibrium strategies with main (cooperation), punishment, compensation, and reward phases, and proves that for sufficiently patient players, any payoff in the interior of the admissible and jointly rational set can be supported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the efficiency of repeated games with punishment, especially in settings with imperfect public monitoring. It provides a theoretical upper bound on efficiency as a function of the monitoring structure and the ability to implement punishment phases. The explicit construction of punishment and reward phases shows how punishment can be used to sustain cooperation, but also highlights that efficiency is limited by the possibility of undetectable or unattributable deviations. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the monitoring structure: when monitoring is sufficiently informative, punishment can support high efficiency; when it is not, efficiency is bounded away from the optimum. The results are general and apply to a wide class of repeated games, including but not limited to public goods games.

- source: Olcina, G., & Calabuig, V. (2015). COORDINATED PUNISHMENT AND THE EVOLUTION OF COOPERATION. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the long-run efficiency of a team trust game with coordinated punishment depends critically on the institutional parameters of punishment: the effectiveness of coordinated punishment (damage inflicted if enough punishers act), the cost of punishment, and the strength of peer punishment (peer pressure on non-punishers). If the effectiveness of coordinated punishment is high enough (damage parameter λ ≥ 0.5) and peer pressure is strong (peer punishment cost to defectors is high relative to cost of coordinated punishment), then the only stable long-run outcome is a fully cooperative, efficient culture with no observed punishment (because the threat is credible). If these conditions are not met, the system converges to a non-cooperative, inefficient culture. The model also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment, lowering its cost, or increasing peer pressure all make the efficient outcome more likely. The results are robust to the dynamics of preference transmission, and the model provides explicit boundaries for the basins of attraction of efficient vs inefficient cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling effective, coordinated punishment (with sufficient effectiveness and/or low enough cost) and strong peer punishment will increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, even when the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the mechanisms (threshold punishment, peer punishment, endogenous preference evolution) are highly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The explicit parameter boundaries (e.g., λ ≥ 0.5 for effectiveness, peer punishment cost vs coordinated punishment cost) can inform quantitative predictions about when punishment will be effective. The model also highlights that if punishment is not sufficiently effective or peer pressure is weak, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency. The results are most directly applicable to trust/investment games with team-based punishment, but the logic is transferable to PGG variants with threshold or coordinated punishment.

- source: Celik, G., & Peters, M. (2016). Reciprocal relationships and mechanism design. *Canadian Journal of Economics-revue Canadienne D Economique*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in environments where players cannot enforce punishments for non-cooperation, reciprocal contracting (where players can make commitments contingent on others' commitments) allows for a larger set of efficient outcomes than centralized mechanism design with the same punishment constraints. The key mechanism is that players can use contract offers to signal private information, which can lower the reservation utility of deviators and thus make more efficient outcomes individually rational and incentive compatible. The analysis is theoretical, with the Cournot duopoly serving as a worked example. The results show that, under certain conditions (private values, single crossing, transferable utility), the incentive compatibility constraints can be simplified, and efficient outcomes can be implemented with appropriate transfer schemes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that, even in the absence of enforceable punishment, mechanisms that allow for reciprocal commitments and information revelation can achieve higher efficiency than mechanisms that do not. For the downstream prediction task, the main implication is that the absence of punishment does not necessarily preclude efficient outcomes if the game design allows for reciprocal contracting and signaling. However, the results are derived in a general mechanism design context (e.g., Cournot duopoly, collusion, marriage, etc.) rather than in standard public goods games. The findings suggest that, in environments structurally similar to public goods games but with richer contracting and signaling possibilities, efficiency can be higher than predicted by models that only consider enforceable punishment or centralized mechanisms. The paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency changes when punishment is enabled or disabled in standard PGGs, but it does clarify the theoretical boundaries of what is possible in related environments.

- source: Povey, R. (2014). Punishment and the potency of group selection. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that embedding a punishment system within a group selection framework reduces the evolutionary success of altruism. While punishment can statically deter selfishness and improve immediate social welfare, it simultaneously weakens the group selection mechanism that would otherwise promote the evolution of intrinsic altruism. This leads to a lower equilibrium level of altruism and, in the long run, can reduce dynamic social welfare. The model shows that the requirements for group selection to sustain altruism become more stringent when punishment is available, and that the use of punishment may have negative dynamic welfare implications, even if it improves static outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling punishment in a group environment may increase static efficiency (short-term group payoff) but can reduce dynamic efficiency (long-run welfare) by undermining the evolution of intrinsic cooperation. The model is not a standard public goods game but is adjacent, focusing on sequential punishment and group selection. The findings imply that, in environments where intrinsic motivation is important for long-run efficiency, introducing punishment may have ambiguous or even negative effects on overall efficiency, especially over evolutionary or cultural timescales. The paper is most informative for settings where the persistence of cooperation and welfare over time is of interest, rather than immediate, one-shot efficiency gains.

- source: Larson, J. M. (2016). The evolutionary advantage of limited network knowledge. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with gossip-based punishment, the ability to sustain full cooperation (and thus maximize group efficiency/fitness) depends on both the structure of the social network and the precision of individuals' knowledge about that structure. When all individuals have precise knowledge of the network, it is harder to sustain full cooperation because some individuals can be identified as weakly connected and thus are tempting targets for defection. When individuals have only coarse knowledge (e.g., know only the average neighborhood size), it is easier to sustain full cooperation, as targeting is not possible. The minimum discount factor required to sustain cooperation is lower under coarse knowledge than under precise knowledge, making full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) more robust. The model thus predicts that limited network knowledge facilitates the enforcement of cooperation through punishment, leading to higher group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the information structure—specifically, the precision of network knowledge. In environments where individuals have only coarse knowledge of the network, punishment is more effective at sustaining full cooperation and thus maximizing efficiency. If a public goods game or similar environment allows only limited information about the network or about others' positions, the model predicts a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency. Conversely, if players have precise knowledge of the network, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency may be reduced. The paper does not provide direct empirical estimates but offers clear theoretical mechanisms and comparative statics relevant to prediction.

- source: Myers, C. D. (2016). Participation and punishment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that when punishment is possible, participation (turnout) increases dramatically (from 60% to 88%). Punishment is overwhelmingly directed at abstainers, and abstainers incur a net loss from being punished. Egoist subjects are more responsive to punishment than pro-social subjects, leading to a more egoist composition among participants when punishment is available. However, because participation is costly and does not increase the value of the prize, increased participation does not improve group efficiency or welfare. The study does not report treatment effects on efficiency or group payoff, and the design makes clear that increased participation is not efficiency-enhancing.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment increases participation in a binary-choice, team-based contest game where participation is individually costly and does not increase group welfare. However, it does not provide evidence that punishment increases efficiency or group payoff; in fact, the design ensures that increased participation is efficiency-reducing. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment depends critically on the structure of the game: in games where participation is not efficiency-enhancing, punishment may increase costly participation without improving (and possibly reducing) efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding behavioral responses (participation/cooperation) to punishment, not for predicting efficiency outcomes in standard public goods games.

However, punishment is not strictly necessary for eventual correctness if other conditions (such as sufficient reward relative to aspiration and cost) are met. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, it shows that punishment reduces the average cheating probability and accelerates convergence to a fully cooperative state. This is due to an equilibrium substitution effect: as immigrants are more strongly incentivized to cooperate, the overall level of cooperation rises above its 'natural' level, making defection more attractive for citizens. The model shows that the net effect of punishment is smaller than would be predicted if equilibrium effects were ignored. The paper does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but expected payoffs for different agent types are derived. The results are robust to heterogeneity in agent preferences.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that punishment targeted at a subset of players (immigrants) can increase overall cooperation rates, but may also induce more defection among the untargeted group (citizens), reducing the net effect on cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff may be less positive than expected if substitution effects are present. However, the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so its quantitative value for predicting efficiency is limited. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it is based on a networked prisoner's dilemma with binary (all-or-nothing) choices and dynamic linking, rather than a standard PGG. The findings are most relevant for understanding equilibrium behavioral responses to targeted punishment policies.

- source: Inaba, M., Takahashi, N., & Ohtsuki, H. (2016). Robustness of linkage strategy that leads to large-scale cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that a linkage strategy—where defectors in a social dilemma are excluded from indirect reciprocity benefits—can be evolutionarily stable and maintain high group efficiency, but only when there are implementation errors in the social dilemma and no perception errors. In the absence of errors, linkage and non-linkage strategies are neutral, allowing non-linkage strategies to drift in and eventually enable defectors to invade, collapsing cooperation and efficiency. Perception errors (misjudging others' actions) undermine the stability and efficiency of linkage strategies, as they disrupt the ability to assortatively cooperate and exclude defectors. The results highlight that the effectiveness of exclusionary sanctions (linkage) for maintaining efficiency is highly sensitive to the type and prevalence of errors in the system.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-like exclusion mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that exclusionary sanctions (linkage) can increase efficiency, but only if players can reliably observe and agree on others' actions (i.e., low perception error) and if there is some noise in implementation (so that linkage strategies can distinguish themselves from non-linkage cooperators). If perception errors are common, the efficiency benefit of punishment/exclusion is undermined. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is implemented as exclusion from future benefits rather than direct costly punishment, and where the information environment is well-specified. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with explicit costly punishment, but the mechanisms and moderators (error types, information) are highly relevant for prediction.
