- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Bodnar, T., & Salathé, M. (2012). Governing the Global Commons with Local Institutions. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment to maintain high efficiency in a public goods game depends critically on the spatial reach of the sanctioning institution relative to the interaction range of the commons. Both peer and pool punishment can maintain the commons (i.e., prevent the tragedy of the commons and sustain high group payoff/efficiency) if the coverage coefficient (probability that two players can punish each other) is above a threshold. There are sharp phase transitions: small increases in the reach of punishment can lead to large increases in efficiency. Pool punishment can solve the second-order free rider problem and maintain the commons even when peer punishment cannot, but only if the cost of contributing to the punishment pool is low and the punishment imposed on defectors is high. The model also shows that in some parameter regimes, peer punishment can be more effective than pool punishment, especially when the reach of the sanctioning system is intermediate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, provided that the punishment system has sufficient reach (coverage) and the cost/impact parameters are favorable. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not linear but can exhibit threshold effects: if the punishment system is too local or weak, it may have little effect, but once a critical coverage is reached, efficiency can increase dramatically. The model also distinguishes between peer and pool punishment, showing that institutionalized (pool) punishment can maintain efficiency even in the face of second-order free riding, but only if the cost structure is appropriate. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the spatial reach and cost-effectiveness of punishment are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment, and that the absence of sufficient reach can nullify the effect of punishment.

- source: Chung, D., Yun, K., Kim, J. H., Jang, B., & Jeong, J. (2011). Different Gain/Loss Sensitivity and Social Adaptation Ability in Gifted Adolescents during a Public Goods Game. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that gifted adolescents are more cooperative and earn higher total payoffs than average adolescents in a repeated binary public goods game with five players and no punishment or reward mechanisms. Three incentive conditions are tested: (1) standard PGG, (2) no-loss guarantee for cooperators, and (3) equalized bonus for all in successful rounds. Gifted adolescents show higher gain sensitivity and lower loss sensitivity, and are more strategic in maximizing payoffs. Average adolescents are more loss-averse, less strategic, and more influenced by social adaptation behaviors. The study provides detailed evidence on how group composition (gifted vs. average) and incentive structure affect efficiency and cooperation, but does not manipulate or analyze punishment or sanctioning.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on efficiency and payoff outcomes in repeated binary PGGs without punishment or reward. It is directly informative for predicting efficiency in control (no-punishment) conditions with known game design parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.). However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or discussed in the experimental design. Thus, it is useful for establishing baseline efficiency but not for predicting the effect of punishment.

- source: Galan, J. M., Latek, M. M., & Rizi, S. M. M. (2011). Axelrod's Metanorm Games on Networks. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment and metapunishment to sustain cooperation in a public-goods-like environment is highly sensitive to the underlying network structure. Specifically, higher clustering and interconnectedness (more triplets per agent) make it more likely that the population will spend most of its time in a cooperative, norm-enforcing state, which is associated with higher expected payoffs. In contrast, higher average degree (more neighbors per agent) can make norm collapse (defection, low punishment) more likely, reducing group welfare. The results are derived from both mathematical analysis of expected payoffs and extensive agent-based simulations, but the main simulation outcomes are behavioral (frequency of norm establishment/collapse) rather than explicit efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, especially when considering the role of network structure. It suggests that punishment (and metapunishment) can lead to higher efficiency (more time in cooperative states) when the network has high clustering and interconnectedness, but may be less effective or even counterproductive in networks with high average degree and low clustering. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff values, its support for quantitative prediction is limited to qualitative or relative effects based on network features. The findings are most relevant when the game design includes networked interactions, costly punishment, and possibly metapunishment.

- source: Duéñez-Guzmán, E. A., & Sadedin, S. (2012). Evolving Righteousness in a Corrupt World. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a small benefit to punishers (such as status or a payment from cooperators) destabilizes corrupt equilibria and enables the evolution and stability of 'righteousness'—a state where cooperative punishers dominate and both corruption and defection are suppressed. Righteousness is associated with higher collective payoffs (efficiency/wellbeing) than either corruption or defection. The model demonstrates that egalitarian and harsh punishment (i.e., similar and sufficiently high penalties for defectors and corrupt punishers) are key to achieving righteousness. Power inequalities that favor corrupt punishers stabilize corruption, but removing or reducing these inequalities (and/or providing small benefits to punishers) can shift the population to righteousness. Once established, righteousness is highly stable and resistant to invasion by defectors or corrupt punishers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public-goods-like environments, enabling punishment (especially when punishers receive even small additional benefits) can increase group efficiency/wellbeing compared to control conditions without punishment or with only corrupt punishment. The model suggests that the design dimensions most relevant for maximizing efficiency gains from punishment are: (1) ensuring punishers are not disadvantaged relative to cooperators (e.g., via small payments or status), (2) making punishment of defectors and corrupt punishers sufficiently harsh, and (3) minimizing power inequalities that allow corrupt punishers to avoid punishment. While the model is not an exact PGG, its structure and outcomes are highly relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like games, especially regarding the role of corruption, punishment severity, and benefits to punishers.

- source: Jaffe, K. (2008). Evolution of Shame as an Adaptation to Social Punishment and its Contribution to Social Cohesiveness. *Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that shame can evolve as an adaptation to social punishment, increasing pro-social behavior (generosity) after punishment. When punishment is present (especially if costless to the punisher) or when shame is present, populations maintain higher levels of generosity and a balance between punishers and non-punishers. However, the model does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff, focusing instead on the evolutionary dynamics of behavioral traits. The results suggest that shame and punishment can stabilize cooperation for long periods, but with fluctuations between cooperative and selfish phases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that punishment (and shame) can increase pro-social behavior in resource-sharing environments, which may translate to higher efficiency in public goods games. However, because the model is not a direct PGG and does not report efficiency or group payoff, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The main relevance is in showing that punishment and shame can stabilize cooperation, especially when punishment is not costly to the punisher. The findings are more about evolutionary stability and trait frequencies than about efficiency per se.

- source: Hock, K., & Fefferman, N. H. (2011). Violating Social Norms when Choosing Friends: How Rule-Breakers Affect Social Networks. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The study finds that when individuals in a network break social conventions for choosing partners, the effects depend on the convention in use. In 'intermediary-based' (betweenness) networks, rule-breakers primarily affect their own social position, and only at higher frequencies do they impact group organization. In 'popularity-based' (in-degree) networks, rule-breakers do not affect their own or others' individual positions, but as their frequency increases, they significantly alter the overall network structure (decentralization). The paper discusses the potential for rule-breaking to transform social systems, but does not measure or report efficiency, payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or measure punishment, efficiency, or payoff, nor does it use a PGG or close variant. Its findings on how rule-breaking alters network structure may be contextually relevant for understanding the broader consequences of norm violation, but do not inform quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The paper may be useful for mechanism discussion or for understanding the importance of social structure, but not for direct prediction in the target task.

- source: Kümmerli, R. (2011). A Test of Evolutionary Policing Theory with Data from Human Societies. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that (1) higher similarity among citizens is associated with lower crime rates (interpreted as higher cooperation), (2) lower similarity is associated with higher policing effort, and (3) increases in policing effort over time are associated with reductions in crime rates. These findings support evolutionary policing theory in a real-world context. However, the outcomes are not directly efficiency or payoff-based, but rather use crime rate as a behavioral proxy for defection/cooperation. The study does not use a public goods game or experimental design, and does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that increased punishment (policing) can reduce defection (crime) in real-world settings, especially in less homogeneous populations. However, because it does not use a public goods game, does not manipulate punishment experimentally, and does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it is of limited direct use for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. Its main value is in supporting the general plausibility of punishment reducing defection, but it does not inform the quantitative relationship between game design dimensions and efficiency.

- source: Tucker, W. T., & Ferson, S. (2008). Evolved altruism, strong reciprocity, and perception of risk. *Strategies for Risk Communication: Evolution, Evidence, Experience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that human cognitive architecture is shaped by evolutionary pressures to maintain cooperation and punish free riders in social contract networks, such as those modeled by public goods games. It reviews evidence that people are willing to incur personal costs to punish defectors, supporting the concept of 'strong reciprocity.' The authors suggest that these evolved mechanisms are central to risk perception and cooperation, and that they help explain why people often resist narrowly self-interested, rational-maximizing behavior in favor of fairness and contract maintenance. However, the paper does not provide new empirical data or direct analysis of efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and contextual support for the idea that punishment mechanisms are evolutionarily grounded and play a key role in sustaining cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It supports the expectation that punishment can increase cooperation and deter free riding, but does not provide quantitative or direct empirical evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of punishment is likely to increase cooperation and potentially efficiency, but does not provide parameter-specific or design-dimension-specific guidance. Its value is mainly in supporting the plausibility and generality of punishment effects, not in calibrating their magnitude or moderators.

- source: Tobeña, A. (2009). Lethal Altruists Itineraries along the Dark Outskirts of Moralistic Prosociality. *Values, Empathy, and Fairness Across Social Barriers*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that strong altruism (including costly punishment) and parochialism are important temperamental traits that can drive both prosocial and antisocial behaviors, such as martyrdom and suicide terrorism. It reviews evidence that some individuals are predisposed to punish norm violators at personal cost, and that these tendencies are influenced by genetic, neurobiological, and temperamental factors. The paper references behavioral economic games (ultimatum, dictator, third-party punishment) as tools for measuring these traits, but does not report new experimental results or analyze efficiency or group payoff. The main contribution is a conceptual framework for understanding the interplay of individual traits and group dynamics in extreme forms of moralistic prosociality.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it discusses the existence of costly punishment and its association with certain temperamental and genetic traits, it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. The references to behavioral games are used to illustrate individual differences in punishment and altruism, not to quantify their effects on group efficiency. Therefore, this paper offers only indirect, contextual support for the idea that costly punishment is a stable human trait, but does not inform the quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in experimental game settings.

- source: Ma, N., Li, N., He, X. S., Sun, D. L., Zhang, X. C., & Zhang, D. R. (2012). Rejection of Unfair Offers Can Be Driven by Negative Emotions, Evidence from Modified Ultimatum Games with Anonymity. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that rejection rates of unfair offers are significantly above zero even when anonymity is strictly enforced, suggesting that negative emotions alone can drive costly punishment-like behavior. Rejection rates are higher in public (experimenter-observed) or 'informed' conditions, indicating that reputational concerns and cognitive processing of consequences further increase rejection. However, the study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, and the game is a modified ultimatum game rather than a public goods game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its main contribution is to clarify the psychological motivations for costly punishment (rejection) in ultimatum-like games, distinguishing between emotional and reputational drivers. It does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot be used to calibrate the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like settings. It may be useful for understanding the psychological underpinnings of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Szilagyi, M. N., & Somogyi, I. (2010). A Systematic Analysis of the N-person Chicken Game. *Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that in the N-person chicken game, the final proportion of cooperators depends sensitively and sometimes non-monotonically on the payoff function parameters (R and S). For certain parameter ranges, small changes can lead to drastic shifts in cooperation rates. The system stabilizes to a constant or oscillating cooperation rate, but the group efficiency or total payoff is not directly analyzed. No punishment, reward, or sanctioning mechanisms are included in the model.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. However, it does provide insight into how the structure of payoff functions (e.g., the relative rewards for cooperation and defection) can lead to abrupt changes in cooperation rates in N-person social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address punishment, efficiency, or payoff outcomes in the presence or absence of sanctions.

- source: Clavien, C., Tanner, C. J., Clément, F., & Chapuisat, M. (2012). Choosy Moral Punishers. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that the propensity to punish a norm violator (by voting against her) varies by socio-professional category: future teachers are significantly more likely to punish than future police officers or high school students, despite all groups recognizing the immoral behavior. The punishment is costless and anonymous, and the context is a simulated music competition, not an economic or public goods game. The findings challenge the idea of a universal, context-independent motivation to punish norm violators. No efficiency, payoff, or group welfare outcomes are measured or discussed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games or similar economic games. Its findings are about context-specific willingness to punish in a non-economic, real-world scenario, and do not inform the relationship between punishment and efficiency or payoff. It may be relevant for understanding moderators of punishment motivation, but not for the downstream prediction task focused on efficiency in PGG-like environments.
