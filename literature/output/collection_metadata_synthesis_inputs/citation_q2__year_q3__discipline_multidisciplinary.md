- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Hintze, A., Staudacher, J., Gelhar, K., Pothmann, A., Rasch, J., & Wildegger, D. (2020). Inclusive groups can avoid the tragedy of the commons. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment in public goods games lowers the critical synergy threshold required for cooperation to emerge, thereby increasing the likelihood of cooperation and improving group-level payoff (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. However, the cost of punishment reduces the total group payoff, so the efficiency gain from increased cooperation is partially offset by punishment costs. The effect is stronger when punishment is more efficient (higher fine per unit cost). The paper also shows that inclusiveness (redistribution of payoffs) is an even more effective way to promote cooperation and efficiency, often making punishment unnecessary if inclusiveness is high enough.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is efficient (high fine, low cost). The effect is moderated by the costliness of punishment: higher punishment costs reduce the efficiency benefit. The findings also highlight that the structure of payoff redistribution (inclusiveness) can be a more powerful moderator than punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that, all else equal, adding punishment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the degree of payoff redistribution.

Monetary punishment and non-monetary reward are the most effective. After incentives are removed, contributions remain higher than baseline for several rounds, indicating some long-lasting effect, but eventually decline. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results are highly informative for predicting contribution behavior, but less so for efficiency unless one assumes a direct mapping from contribution to efficiency (which is not always valid, especially when punishment is costly). The design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is useful for understanding the behavioral response to punishment and reward mechanisms in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Chapkovski, P. (2021). Strike one hundred to educate one: Measuring the efficacy of collective sanctions experimentally. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Collective sanctions are not effective at increasing cooperation or contributions in a public good game, and may even reduce them compared to individual sanctions, especially when peer punishment is available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions overall, but under collective sanctions, contributions are significantly lower than under individual sanctions with peer punishment. Average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions, but this difference is not statistically significant. There are notable gender differences: men contribute more under collective sanctions, women less. Perceptions of fairness mediate these effects, with collective sanctions seen as less fair, especially by women.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard PGG with 3 players, 15 rounds, continuous contributions, and a fixed threshold for external punishment, enabling collective sanctions (punishing the whole group for one member's under-contribution) does not improve efficiency or group payoff compared to individual sanctions, and may even reduce cooperation and contributions, especially when peer punishment is also available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions, but collective sanctions reduce this effect. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that switching from individual to collective sanctions is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, particularly in small groups with peer punishment. Gender composition may moderate effects.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of cooperation in costly institutions exhibits Red Queen and Black Queen dynamics in heterogeneous public goods. *Communications Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The model demonstrates that introducing a costly institution (with a participation fee) alongside a free institution in a public goods game can increase cooperation and group efficiency. For low enhancement factors (low profitability), only the costly institution supports cooperation, and average payoffs remain close to the entrance cost (i.e., not much higher than the baseline). For higher enhancement factors, cooperation emerges in both institutions, and group payoffs increase above zero. The costly institution is more likely to be populated by cooperators, as defectors avoid the entrance cost unless there are enough cooperators to exploit. The model also shows that the effect of the entrance cost is context-dependent: it is beneficial for cooperation and efficiency at low profitability, but can be detrimental at high profitability if the cost is too high, as it reduces participation. The results hold in both well-mixed and structured populations, though spatial structure removes some bistability and further supports cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that introducing a costly institution (entrance fee) in a public goods game can increase efficiency and group payoff, especially when the baseline profitability (mpcr/enhancement factor) is low. The entrance cost acts as a filter, keeping defectors out and allowing cooperators to achieve higher payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly institution (or, by analogy, a punishment mechanism with a cost to participate) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such cost, particularly when the marginal per-capita return is low. However, if the cost is too high and the game is already highly profitable, the effect may reverse. The model does not include explicit punishment, but the costly institution's effect is adjacent to punishment in that it deters free-riding through a cost barrier. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where institutional participation costs or costly precommitment mechanisms are present, rather than direct peer punishment.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of cooperation and consistent personalities in public goods games. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in two-round public goods games, both reward-based and assortative mechanisms can promote the evolution of cooperation and the emergence of consistent cooperative or defective personalities. In the 'reward dilemma' scenario, only first-round cooperators are allowed to play a second PGG, which can incentivize cooperation if the second game's enhancement factor is high enough. In the 'assortative' scenario, both cooperators and defectors play a second PGG in separate groups, and competition between these groups can further promote cooperation and personality consistency. The model shows that cooperation is maximized at intermediate values of the second-round enhancement factor, and that consistent personalities (cooperating or defecting in both rounds) naturally evolve. However, the paper does not model punishment or sanctions, and does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly relevant for understanding how reward and assortment mechanisms (and the possibility of repeated or conditional interaction) can promote cooperation and personality consistency, but it does not address punishment, sanctions, or their efficiency effects. The findings may be indirectly informative for prediction tasks involving reward or assortment, but not for punishment. The model's outcomes are primarily behavioral (cooperation rates, personality consistency), not efficiency or group payoff, so it should not be used as a primary source for predicting efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Koike, S., Nakamaru, M., Otaka, T., Shimao, H., Shimomura, K. I., & Yamato, T. (2018). Reciprocity and exclusion in informal financial institutions: An experimental study of rotating savings and credit associations. *Plos One*.

  findings: The experiment compared four treatments in a fixed-order ROSCA game: baseline (no sanctions), punishment rule (forfeiture), voting (exclusion), and both punishment and voting. Exclusion by voting led to significantly higher contribution rates and mean payoffs than the other treatments. The punishment rule, which prevented non-contributors from receiving payouts, did not increase contributions or payoffs relative to the baseline.   prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper provides strong evidence that a costless, rule-based punishment (forfeiture of payout for non-contributors) does not improve efficiency or mean payoff compared to a baseline with no punishment. In contrast, exclusion by peer voting (reputation-based exclusion) substantially increases both contributions and efficiency. The game structure is a close variant of a public goods game (ROSCA), with fixed group size, known rounds, and full information about others' actions. The findings suggest that not all punishment mechanisms are equally effective: exclusion based on group voting is effective, but a non-costly, rule-based punishment is not.
