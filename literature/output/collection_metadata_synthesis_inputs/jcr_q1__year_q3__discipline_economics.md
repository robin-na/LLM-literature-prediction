- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

- source: Ambrus, A., & Greiner, B. (2019). Individual, Dictator, and Democratic punishment in public good games with perfect and imperfect observability. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper experimentally compares three punishment institutions (individual, democratic, dictator) in repeated 5-person public goods games, under both perfect and imperfect monitoring. Democratic punishment, where a majority vote is required to punish, results in significantly higher average net profits (efficiency) and higher contributions than individual or dictator punishment. This is true in both monitoring conditions, though the efficiency advantage is somewhat reduced under noise. The improvement is due to a sharp reduction in anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators), while pro-social punishment (punishing defectors) is maintained. Dictator punishment does not outperform individual punishment in terms of efficiency. The study provides direct, quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment institution and monitoring noise on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games. It shows that the institutional design of punishment (democratic vs. individual vs. dictator) and the observability of contributions (perfect vs. noisy) are key moderators. Democratic punishment substantially increases efficiency compared to individual punishment, especially by reducing anti-social punishment. However, the efficiency gain is somewhat sensitive to monitoring noise. The paper provides exact values for average net profits under each condition, allowing for quantitative calibration of prediction models. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, observability, etc.) are all clearly specified, making the results highly transferable to similar prediction tasks.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

- source: Bühren, C., & Dannenberg, A. (2021). The demand for punishment to promote cooperation among like-minded people. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games is highly contingent on group type and information. Highly cooperative groups achieve near-optimal payoffs without punishment, and introducing punishment reduces efficiency due to its cost. Uncooperative groups only benefit from severe punishment if they are informed about their own composition and implement the institution early; otherwise, punishment is often misapplied or subverted, leading to low efficiency. Middle cooperators benefit from punishment only in the absence of information about group composition. Information about group cooperativeness allows groups to better match their institution choice to their needs, increasing overall efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment is not uniform and is moderated by group composition and information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games is not a simple function of the game design dimensions alone, but is critically moderated by group composition (cooperativeness) and information about that composition. For prediction tasks, this means that the same punishment institution can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency depending on whether the group is composed of high, middle, or low cooperators, and whether they know this. In the absence of such information, punishment is often over- or under-used, reducing efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction from this paper are the presence and cost of punishment, the severity/technology of punishment, the availability of information about group composition, and the baseline cooperativeness of the group. Control efficiency (no punishment) is a strong predictor: if control efficiency is high, adding punishment reduces efficiency; if control efficiency is low, adding severe punishment with information can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of comprehension and early institution choice for realizing efficiency gains from punishment.

- source: Langenbach, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Inherited Institutions: Cooperation in the Light of Democratic Legitimacy. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot, three-player linear public goods game with or without a centralized, non-deterrent punishment (automatic fine for less-than-full contribution). The institutional setting (punishment or not) is determined either by majority vote (Endo) or random draw (Exo) in the first generation, and this setting is inherited by a matched group in the second generation. In the first generation, contributions are higher when punishment is democratically adopted and lower when it is democratically rejected, compared to random assignment. In the second generation, the positive effect of democratic adoption disappears, but the negative effect of democratic rejection persists (marginally). The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or total earnings as a share of the social optimum, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized, non-deterrent punishment mechanism can increase contributions if it is democratically adopted, but only in the generation that participates in the decision. The effect does not persist to subsequent generations who inherit the institution. The negative effect of democratic rejection may persist. However, since the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, only contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is informative for mapping design to expected contribution behavior, but not for direct efficiency prediction. If contribution is used as a proxy for efficiency (assuming linearity and no punishment cost to the group), the results suggest that democratic adoption of punishment increases efficiency in the first generation, but not in the second.

- source: Albrecht, F., Kube, S., & Traxler, C. (2018). Cooperation and norm enforcement - The individual-level perspective. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds substantial heterogeneity in both cooperation and punishment patterns. About half of subjects are pro-social punishers, and about 40% are non-punishers. There is only a moderate correlation between cooperation and punishment types: many conditional cooperators do not punish, and some free-riders do punish pro-socially. In repeated public goods games with punishment, groups with more pro-social punishers achieve higher and more stable contribution levels, even after controlling for the number of conditional cooperators. Regression analyses show that the number of pro-social punishers in a group is a stronger predictor of group contribution levels than the number of conditional cooperators. However, the paper does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public goods games with punishment, the presence of pro-social punishers in a group increases contribution levels. This suggests that enabling punishment can increase cooperation, but the paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoffs. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is informative about behavioral mechanisms (contributions and punishment) but not about the net payoff effects of punishment. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the experimental setup matches the canonical PGG with punishment, so the findings are highly relevant for understanding how group composition and punishment opportunities affect cooperation rates, but not directly efficiency.

- source: Gallier, C. (2020). Democracy and compliance in public goods games. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that a non-deterrent contribution rule in a public goods game increases contributions, regardless of whether it is imposed exogenously or chosen democratically. The aggregate increase in contributions is primarily due to the presence of the rule itself, not the democratic process. Democratic participation (voting) does not have a statistically significant effect on contributions at the aggregate level after accounting for the direct effect of the rule, self-selection, and information effects. At the individual level, participants who are overruled in the group vote tend to reduce their contributions, but this negative effect is counteracted by a small democracy premium if the rule is democratically chosen. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with a mild, non-deterrent automatic sanction, the introduction of the rule (punishment) increases contributions, but the effect is not due to democratic participation per se. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of introducing a weak punishment mechanism on contribution rates, but not directly on efficiency or group payoff, as these are not reported. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contribution, etc.) are clearly specified and can inform parameterization of prediction models. However, since efficiency is not reported, the paper's quantitative results cannot be directly used for efficiency prediction, but the qualitative pattern (punishment increases cooperation even when non-deterrent) is robust.

- source: Stoop, J., van Soest, D., & Vyrastekova, J. (2018). Rewards and cooperation in social dilemma games. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that net-positive rewards (where the recipient gains more than the sender loses) dramatically increase cooperation and efficiency in standard public goods games (VCM), with most groups reaching high contribution levels when rewards are available. In contrast, in the Claim game—where players can undo others' contributions by taking from the public pool—rewards have only a marginal effect on cooperation, and average net contributions are not significantly different from zero. In the Claim game, rewards are often exchanged bilaterally and are not strongly linked to cooperative behavior in the social dilemma. The results suggest that the effectiveness of rewards in promoting efficiency depends critically on the structure of the social dilemma, particularly whether others can undo cooperative actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of reward mechanisms in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends on whether the game allows for negative actions (i.e., taking from the public pool) and whether the MPCR is certain. In standard VCMs (linear PGGs), rewards can substantially increase efficiency, but in environments where contributions can be undone (as in the Claim game or CPR games), rewards are much less effective. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a 'take' option or uncertain MPCR is a critical moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency with rewards if the game allows for undoing others' contributions. The findings do not inform about punishment effects, as punishment is not studied.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2020). SUSTAINING COOPERATION: COMMUNITY ENFORCEMENT VERSUS SPECIALIZED ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of the European Economic Association*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the optimal way to sustain cooperation in repeated public-goods-like games depends on the effectiveness of the punishment technology. If specialized enforcers can punish at low cost and high impact, then pure enforcer punishment (with immediate return to cooperation) maximizes efficiency. If specialized enforcement is less effective, then combining enforcer punishment with community enforcement (e.g., temporary withdrawal of cooperation or ostracism) is optimal. The paper provides explicit characterizations of the maximum sustainable cooperation (efficiency) in each regime, and shows that the presence of effective specialized enforcement can increase group welfare compared to pure community enforcement. However, if the punishment technology is very weak, community enforcement alone is nearly optimal. The results generalize to both small and large groups, and to settings with imperfect monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of introducing punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on the effectiveness of the punishment technology and other game design parameters. It shows that, when punishment is highly effective (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the harm inflicted), enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency by supporting higher cooperation. However, if punishment is costly or ineffective, the efficiency gain is limited, and community enforcement may be nearly as good. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency as a function of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and discount factor. The results also highlight that the structure of punishment (one-time vs. repeated, enforcer vs. peer) and the possibility of ostracism or repentance are important moderators.

- source: Chávez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., & Stranlund, J. K. (2018). Managing and defending the commons: Experimental evidence from TURFs in Chile. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common-pool resource game with insiders (who can communicate) and outsiders (who cannot), the introduction of poaching without monitoring or sanctions dramatically reduces insiders' earnings and resource sustainability, nearly eliminating the efficiency gains from cooperation. Adding weak external enforcement (low-probability monitoring and fines) or allowing insiders to invest in monitoring (with the same fine) both significantly reduce poaching and increase insiders' earnings and harvests, but do not fully restore efficiency to baseline (no-poaching) levels. Combining external and local monitoring does not further reduce poaching or increase efficiency. The main constraint is insiders' inability to coordinate sufficient monitoring investment. The results are robust across field (TURF members) and student samples.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with communication among insiders, the introduction of punishment (via monitoring and fines for poaching) increases group efficiency relative to a poaching-only control, but the effect is partial and depends on the ability to coordinate monitoring. The effect is similar whether monitoring is provided externally or by insiders, and combining both does not yield additional efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment (even if weak) should be expected to increase efficiency above the poaching-only baseline, but not to the level of the no-poaching or full-cooperation baseline. The magnitude of the effect is limited by the monitoring probability and the coordination of monitoring investment.

In small groups, free-riding is low regardless of punishment. The main outcome is the frequency of free-riding, not efficiency or group payoff. The experimental design and manipulation of group size, punishment probability, and base payoff are informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data is a limitation. The results support the prediction that enabling punishment will increase efficiency in larger groups, especially when the probability of punishment is substantial.

- source: Cagala, T., Glogowsky, U., Grimm, V., & Rincke, J. (2019). PUBLIC GOODS PROVISION WITH RENT-EXTRACTING ADMINISTRATORS. *Economic Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study introduces a 'public trust game' (PTG), a repeated public goods game with an administrator who can extract rents from the contribution pool. The main finding is that the presence of a rent-extracting administrator reduces contributions and efficiency, but this is entirely explained by the reduction in MPCR (i.e., the efficiency of the public good). When comparing the PTG to a standard PGG with the same (lowered) MPCR, there is no significant difference in contributions or efficiency. The dynamics show that rent-extraction shocks and cooperation shocks only have short-run effects; the system returns to its previous level of cooperation and efficiency. The volatility of contributions is mainly driven by contributors' own behavior, not by the administrator. Groups with higher trust and cooperative attitudes experience less short-run volatility in cooperation after shocks.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-goods-like environments where an administrator can extract rents, the negative effect on efficiency is fully mediated by the reduction in MPCR. If the control game (standard PGG) and the treatment game (with administrator) have the same MPCR, the presence of the administrator does not further reduce efficiency. Thus, for prediction, the key moderator is the effective MPCR: punishment or rent extraction that reduces MPCR will reduce efficiency accordingly, but the mere presence of an administrator or rent-extraction opportunity does not further depress efficiency beyond this. The paper does not study punishment in the standard sense (peer or centralized costly punishment), but its findings are highly informative for cases where punishment or corruption reduces the effective return from cooperation.

- source: Nyborg, K. (2018). Reciprocal climate negotiators. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that reciprocal preferences can, under certain conditions (strong and widespread reciprocity), stabilize the grand coalition in a public-goods-like coalition game, leading to high group welfare (efficiency). If only a minority of countries are reciprocal, or if reciprocity is weak, the only stable coalitions are small and provide little welfare improvement over the non-cooperative baseline. The model demonstrates that the presence of reciprocal preferences can shift the game from a pure Prisoner's Dilemma to a coordination game in utilities, but this does not guarantee high efficiency unless reciprocity is strong and common. The paper does not model explicit punishment or sanctioning institutions, but reciprocity can act as a form of endogenous social sanctioning. The welfare/efficiency effects of reciprocity are thus mixed: potentially large if conditions are right, but often minimal in realistic parameter ranges.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that social preferences (reciprocity) can, in principle, increase efficiency in public-goods-like coalition games, but only if they are strong and widespread. In the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms, reciprocity alone is not a reliable path to high efficiency unless the population is highly reciprocal. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling social preference mechanisms (like reciprocity) may only improve efficiency if the relevant parameters (strength and prevalence of reciprocity) are high. The model does not address explicit punishment, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled games is limited, but it does show that endogenous social preferences can sometimes substitute for formal sanctions in supporting cooperation and efficiency. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is implemented via social preferences rather than institutionalized mechanisms.

The absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative prediction, but the qualitative direction is clear: punishment/reward increases cooperation and likely efficiency, especially among defectors.

- source: De Luca, G., Sekeris, P. G., & Spengler, D. E. (2018). Can violence harm cooperation? Experimental evidence. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment compares two treatments: a 'conflict' treatment where violent appropriation (chance) is a viable option when resources are low, and a 'control' treatment where the cost of appropriation is so high that it is never optimal. In the conflict treatment, participants are significantly less likely to choose the cooperative extraction rate ('low') and more likely to choose the non-cooperative rate ('high') in early rounds, anticipating future conflict. The expectation of future conflict thus undermines cooperation even before conflict occurs. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral shift from cooperation to non-cooperation implies lower efficiency in the conflict treatment. Additional analyses show that experiencing conflict in previous games further reduces cooperation in subsequent games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a credible, destructive appropriation/conflict option in a dynamic CPR game reduces cooperative behavior and likely reduces efficiency, even before conflict is actually chosen. For prediction tasks, the presence of a credible destructive sanction (even if not a standard punishment mechanism) should be expected to reduce efficiency relative to a control with no such option. However, the paper does not study standard punishment (costly peer punishment for norm violations) but rather a destructive appropriation/conflict option, so the results are most relevant for settings where punishment is destructive and ends the game, not for standard public goods games with ongoing punishment. The findings suggest that the threat of destructive sanctions can unravel cooperation and efficiency, especially when the sanction is credible and not prohibitively costly.
