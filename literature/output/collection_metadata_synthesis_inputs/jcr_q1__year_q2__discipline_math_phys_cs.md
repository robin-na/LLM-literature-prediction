- source: Perc, M., Jordan, J. J., Rand, D. G., Wang, Z., Boccaletti, S., & Szolnoki, A. (2017). Statistical physics of human cooperation. *Physics Reports-review Section of Physics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that enabling punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency and sustain cooperation, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defectors. However, the effect is not universally positive: high punishment costs, the presence of antisocial punishment, or poorly tuned parameters can lead to lower efficiency or even the collapse of cooperation. Adaptive and probabilistic punishment mechanisms can improve efficiency by reducing unnecessary costs. The spatial structure of interactions (e.g., lattices, networks) is critical: it allows for clustering of cooperators and punishers, which can support higher efficiency at lower multiplication factors (mpcr) than in well-mixed populations. The paper provides detailed phase diagrams showing the regions where punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on efficiency, depending on the interplay of punishment cost, effectiveness, group size, and synergy factor.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, mpcr, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control only when punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective, and when the spatial structure allows for clustering of cooperators/punishers. If punishment is costly or antisocial punishment is possible, efficiency gains may be lost or reversed. The paper's phase diagrams and model descriptions can be used to map specific game designs to expected efficiency outcomes, given the control efficiency. However, the results are based on theoretical models and simulations, not direct empirical measurement, so predictions should be interpreted with caution and in the context of the model's assumptions.

The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with reasonable cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the marginal benefit of also enabling reward is likely negligible except under harsh conditions (low MPCR, low cost). The results are most directly applicable to spatial (networked) PGGs with group size 5, continuous contributions, and symmetric cost/impact for punishment and reward.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2017). Second-Order Free-Riding on Antisocial Punishment Restores the Effectiveness of Prosocial Punishment. *Physical Review X*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible. When the synergy factor is low, prosocial punishment can still restore cooperation and efficiency, because nonpunishing defectors (second-order free-riders) undermine antisocial punishers, allowing punishing cooperators to dominate. The result is that, across a wide range of parameters, the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency, even in the presence of antisocial punishment. The paper also identifies parameter regions with stable coexistence of strategies, but the main result is that prosocial punishment is robustly effective in structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.

- source: Adami, C., Schossau, J., & Hintze, A. (2016). Evolutionary game theory using agent-based methods. *Physics of Life Reviews*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. The effect is quantitatively predicted by mean-field theory and confirmed by simulations: higher probability or density of punishment lowers the threshold for cooperation. However, because punishment is costly, it creates a second-order dilemma, and the evolutionary dynamics can lead to meta-stable states and hysteresis. When punishment is allowed to evolve, its effect is strongest near the critical point, and in highly cooperative or highly defective regimes, punishment is not maintained by selection. The findings are robust across different group sizes and mutation rates, and the paper provides explicit equations and simulation results for the effect of punishment on group payoff and cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (payoff) in the dilemma region by shifting the critical point for cooperation to lower values of the synergy parameter (r). The effect size and direction depend on the punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness), group size, and whether punishment is fixed or can evolve. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.

  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, the choice of updating rule (BP, DP, LD) can dramatically change the long-run frequencies of defectors, cooperators, and punishers. For the same parameter values, the birth-death process leads to a population dominated by defectors, while the death-birth process results in a majority of cooperative strategies (cooperators and punishers). These differences arise even in well-mixed populations and are especially pronounced when mutation rates are not negligible and when more than two strategies interact. The paper does not report group efficiency or payoff, but the shift from defector to cooperator/punisher dominance implies a likely increase in group welfare under some updating rules.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the updating rule (a feature of evolutionary dynamics, not typically manipulated in lab experiments) can be a major moderator of the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, on efficiency. In public goods games with punishment, the same game design (including punishment parameters) can yield very different long-run outcomes depending on the updating rule: punishment may be ineffective (defector-dominated) under one rule and highly effective (cooperator/punisher-dominated) under another. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as indicating that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the evolutionary dynamics assumed, especially in theoretical or agent-based models. For empirical prediction, this suggests caution in generalizing across studies with different evolutionary assumptions.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support that enabling exclusion-based punishment in public goods games can increase the prevalence of cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency, especially when exclusion is effective and/or costs are not too high. While the paper does not report efficiency as a direct outcome, the dominance of excluders and cooperators over defectors implies higher group payoff. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when exclusion is effective, costs are moderate, and group size is not too large for peer exclusion (but pool exclusion can handle larger groups).
