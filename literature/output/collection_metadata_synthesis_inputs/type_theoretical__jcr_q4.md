- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.

The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

  findings: The model demonstrates that punishment, whether selfish or altruistic, can sustain altruism in public goods games, but the efficiency of this outcome is contingent on power dynamics. Selfish punishment by a powerful minority can prevent the collapse into pure selfishness when power is highly asymmetric, but this comes at the cost of reduced group productivity compared to scenarios where altruistic punishment prevails. When power is more evenly distributed, altruistic punishment dominates, leading to higher levels of altruism and group efficiency. The model also shows that if selfish punishment is not possible, populations are more vulnerable to a collapse into selfishness under power asymmetry. Thus, the presence of punishment generally increases group-level cooperation and productivity, but the efficiency gain is maximized when punishment is altruistic and power is symmetric.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the symmetry and distribution of power. In environments with high power asymmetry, punishment may maintain some cooperation but at a lower efficiency due to the persistence of selfish punishers. In more egalitarian settings, punishment is more likely to be altruistic, leading to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with symmetric power and distributed punishment opportunities will see greater efficiency gains from punishment, while those with concentrated or asymmetric power may see only partial gains or even efficiency losses if selfish punishment dominates. The model also highlights the importance of the severity of punishment consequences and the ability to exclude selfish punishers in determining efficiency outcomes.

  findings: The paper reviews theoretical and empirical literature on the evolution of cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It finds that punishment (both peer and institutional) can promote cooperation, especially in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, but at a cost that can reduce group welfare or average payoff. Peer punishment can curb free-riding but is costly and can lead to second-order free-riding and antisocial punishment, often resulting in lower group payoffs compared to no-punishment conditions. Institutional punishment can overcome some of these issues but is also costly. Reward mechanisms are generally less effective than punishment in moving populations from defection to cooperation, unless the reward is large. The paper emphasizes that combining reward and punishment can have a strong effect on promoting cooperation. Theoretical models predict that the effect of punishment on cooperation and payoff depends on the size and structure of the incentive, and that punishment can eliminate extremely selfish behavior but does not always increase group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based guidance that punishment mechanisms in public goods games can increase cooperation rates, but often at a cost that may reduce group efficiency or average payoff, especially in peer punishment settings. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the game (e.g., peer vs institutional punishment, compulsory vs voluntary participation). For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment is likely to increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, and in some cases may reduce it due to the costs of punishment. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates or new empirical data, so its value is mainly in providing qualitative, mechanism-based expectations and summarizing prior findings.

However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (especially when reinforced by a metanorm, such as rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) can shift a population from an inefficient equilibrium (low efficiency, no cooperation) to an efficient one (high efficiency, full cooperation and sanctioning), provided the costs and benefits are favorable. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the cost/benefit structure and initial conditions.   outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper formalizes trust as a trade-off between vulnerability (potential loss) and positive expectations of a partner's behavior, using a computational model applied to the investment game. Simulations show that trustful investors (high trust attitude) invest more and are more likely to recover trust after breaches, but are also more vulnerable to exploitation. Loss aversion reduces investment and makes trust repair harder, while positive expectations and evidence of partner benevolence or competence increase investment. The model predicts that both trust attitude and learning about the partner's behavior jointly determine investment and, by extension, payoffs. The model is extended to consider both benevolence and competence of the trustee, showing that both traits affect investment and expected payoffs. However, the paper does not address punishment, sanctions, or public goods games, and does not report empirical efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It is a theoretical modeling paper focused on the investment game (trust game), which is adjacent but not equivalent to the public goods game. The model and its simulations may inform understanding of how psychological factors (trust, expectations, loss aversion) affect investment and payoffs in dyadic trust settings, but do not address punishment, sanctions, or group-level efficiency in multi-player public goods contexts. Therefore, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games.

It does not include punishment, sanctions, or payoff-based outcomes, and is focused on a two-player, one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Its main contribution is to show that social norm preferences can support cooperation in the absence of punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative, except as a reminder that cooperation can arise from norm compliance alone, and that the absence of punishment does not necessarily imply zero cooperation. It does not inform how punishment affects efficiency or how game design dimensions moderate that effect.
