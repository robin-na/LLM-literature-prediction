- source: Cressman, R., Song, J. W., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2012). Cooperation and evolutionary dynamics in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

- source: Gintis, H. (2000). Strong reciprocity and human sociality. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that strong reciprocity—cooperation combined with costly punishment of defectors—can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, even when repeated interaction is unlikely. Theoretical results show that the ability to inflict punishment at low cost (high punishment effectiveness) and the presence of a sufficient fraction of strong reciprocators can induce cooperation among self-interested agents, raising group fitness. The model predicts that punishment increases group efficiency, especially when the cost to punishers is low and the impact on defectors is high. Laboratory evidence is reviewed to support these claims, showing that allowing punishment in public goods experiments prevents the decay of cooperation and increases average contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially when punishment is effective (high impact per cost) and not too costly for punishers. The model identifies key moderators: group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the probability of future interaction. The reviewed experimental evidence (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000) shows that punishment prevents the decline of cooperation and can even increase it to near full cooperation, implying a positive effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment will have a more positive effect on efficiency when the punishment technology is strong and the cost is low, and that group size and the structure of repeated interaction also matter.

- source: Rand, D. G., Armao, J. J., Nakamaru, M., & Ohtsuki, H. (2010). Anti-social punishment can prevent the co-evolution of punishment and cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, allowing anti-social punishment (punishment of cooperators) prevents the evolution of cooperation in public goods games with costly punishment. In models where only pro-social punishment is possible, cooperation and punishment can co-evolve, leading to high group efficiency. However, when anti-social punishment is included, defecting strategies that punish others become dominant, and cooperation is not stable. This result holds across a wide range of parameter values and is robust to small behavioral errors. The findings highlight that the efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the absence of anti-social punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly contingent on the type of punishment allowed. If anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, as cooperation is not stable and defectors who punish others dominate. In contrast, if only pro-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of anti-social punishment as a design dimension is critical: if anti-social punishment is allowed, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment; if it is not allowed, efficiency gains are possible under the right parameter regimes.

- source: Henrich, J., & Boyd, R. (2001). Why people punish defectors - Weak conformist transmission can stabilize costly enforcement of norms in cooperative dilemmas. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods dilemmas with costly punishment, the combination of payoff-biased and conformist social learning can stabilize both punishment and cooperation, even when punishment is costly and higher-order free-riding is possible. The model demonstrates that, for a finite number of punishment stages, an arbitrarily small amount of conformist transmission is sufficient to stabilize cooperation and punishment, leading to higher group payoffs. The expected payoff of individuals in cooperative groups is strictly higher than in non-cooperative groups, and these high-payoff equilibria can spread through cultural group selection. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation and punishment are stable and when prosocial genetic traits may invade.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling costly punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency, provided that some mechanism (such as conformist transmission) supports the stability of punishment. The model clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the costs of cooperation and punishment, the error rate, and the strength of conformist transmission. It also highlights that, in the absence of punishment, cooperation is not stable and efficiency is low. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of social learning mechanisms and the cost structure are critical. The model is most informative for settings where group size is large, punishment is costly but effective, and social learning is plausible.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive

  findings: The paper finds that in both compulsory and voluntary public goods games, when all forms of punishment are allowed (including anti-social punishment), the introduction of punishment does not increase the average payoff or efficiency of the group under high mutation rates. Analytical results and agent-based simulations show that the average payoff of cooperators is not increased by punishment when anti-social punishment is possible; in fact, the outcome is equivalent to the no-punishment baseline. Only when punishment is restricted to pro-social forms (punishing defectors only) does punishment increase efficiency. These findings are robust to changes in mutation rate and selection intensity.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in PGG-like environments with high exploration/mutation and the possibility of anti-social punishment, enabling punishment will not increase group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative when anti-social punishment is possible, regardless of other game parameters such as player count, rounds, or selection intensity. If the game design allows anti-social punishment, do not expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

- source: Schoenmakers, S., Hilbe, C., Blasius, B., & Traulsen, A. (2014). Sanctions as honest signals - The evolution of pool punishment by public sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and public visibility of a punishment institution in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to control conditions without punishment. The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Janssen, M. A., & Bushman, C. (2008). Evolution of cooperation and altruistic punishment when retaliation is possible. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that when agents in a public goods game can retaliate against those who punish them, the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation is greatly reduced. In the absence of retaliation, punishment can support high levels of cooperation (and thus higher group payoffs/efficiency). When retaliation is possible, cooperation and punishment both decline, especially in larger groups or when the cost of retaliation is low relative to the cost of punishment. The visibility of the punisher (i.e., whether punished agents can identify who punished them) is also important: if punishers are less visible, retaliation is less likely and cooperation can be higher. The model also shows that spiteful punishment (defectors punishing others unconditionally) does not undermine the main result: retaliation is the key factor that reduces cooperation and, by implication, efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games are highly contingent on whether retaliation is possible and on the relative costs of punishment and retaliation. In environments where punished agents can easily identify and retaliate against punishers, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be much smaller or even negative, especially in larger groups. The model also highlights the importance of punisher anonymity: making it harder to identify punishers can help preserve the efficiency benefits of punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of retaliation and punisher identifiability are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2013). Effectiveness of conditional punishment for the evolution of public cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that conditional punishment, where the severity of punishment is proportional to the number of punishers in a group, is more effective than unconditional punishment in promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when punishment is costly. Conditional punishers can maintain cooperation at lower fines and costs, and are more efficient in terms of cooperation per unit cost. Unconditional punishers only outperform conditional punishers when punishment is very cheap. The results are robust across different values of the multiplication factor (mpcr), and the findings are explained through the mechanism of indirect territorial competition and pattern formation on the spatial grid.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the design of the punishment mechanism (conditional vs. unconditional) and the cost-to-fine ratio are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperation and cost-effectiveness. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the results suggest that conditional punishment is more likely to yield higher cooperation per unit cost, especially when punishment is not cheap. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency as a fraction of the maximum possible payoff, so predictions about absolute efficiency should be made with caution. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and no communication.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Unemi, T. (2011). Replicator dynamics in public goods games with reward funds. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  findings: The paper finds that in a compulsory public goods game with a pool-rewarding mechanism, cooperation and high efficiency can be maintained if the net benefit from the reward fund exceeds the cost of contributing to the public good. Specifically, if c2*(r2-1) > sigma (where sigma is the net cost of contributing to the public good), the population can converge to all rewarders (full efficiency) or exhibit stable cycles with high average payoff. If this condition is not met, defectors dominate and efficiency collapses. The model also shows that introducing second-order sanctions (reducing rewards to second-order free riders) can stabilize full cooperation. The findings are directly relevant to predicting efficiency in PGG-like games with reward mechanisms, but only indirectly inform about punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public goods games with reward funds, the efficiency outcome depends critically on the relative effectiveness and cost of the reward mechanism compared to the cost of cooperation. The explicit condition c2*(r2-1) > sigma can be used to predict when enabling a reward mechanism will increase efficiency relative to the control (no reward) case. However, the paper does not analyze punishment, so its direct value for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency is limited. The model structure and results may be informative by analogy for understanding how the cost and effectiveness of sanctions (punishment or reward) interact with game parameters (group size, MPCR, etc.) to determine efficiency outcomes.
