- source: Liu, X. S., & Guo, W. L. (2010). Persistent cooperators in nature. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with compulsory participation, if contributors are willing to pay a second cost to retrieve a portion of the public good (persistent cooperation), this can functionally act as punishment and promote cooperation. When the second cost is not too high relative to the benefit, persistent cooperators can dominate the population, leading to higher average payoffs and more stable cooperation. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of benefit-to-cost ratios and the size of the second cost) under which persistent cooperation is evolutionarily stable, risk-dominant, or advantageous. When the second cost is high, persistent cooperators act as traditional costly punishers and are less favored. The abundance of persistent cooperators is used as a proxy for group welfare and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (modeled as a second cost paid by persistent cooperators) can increase the prevalence of cooperation and, by extension, group efficiency, as long as the cost of punishment is not too high relative to the benefit. The model gives explicit parameter thresholds for when punishment is beneficial. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling punishment (with moderate cost and sufficient benefit) in a public goods game is likely to increase efficiency compared to a control without punishment, especially when the punishment cost is not excessive. The model also highlights the importance of the ratio between the benefit of cooperation, the cost of contributing, and the cost of punishment in determining the outcome. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions of efficiency, and its results are theoretical rather than empirical.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: McEvoy, D. M. (2012). Paying to punish on a free ride: member and nonmember punishments in voluntary coalitions. *Applied Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a public good game with voluntary coalition formation, allowing voluntary punishment (where any player can punish any other) does not increase public good provision relative to no punishment. However, when coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance (quasi-voluntary punishment), public good provision increases significantly. Both coalition members and nonmembers are equally likely to punish noncompliance, and the extent of nonmember punishment does not change when members can credibly enforce compliance. The main outcomes are contribution levels and punishment frequencies; efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that simply enabling voluntary punishment (without credible commitment) is not sufficient to increase efficiency or public good provision in coalition-based public good games. Only when punishment is structured so that coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance does cooperation (and likely efficiency) increase. For prediction, the presence of a credible, committed punishment mechanism is a key moderator; voluntary, non-committed punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes.

- source: Loukopoulos, P., Eek, D., Gärling, T., & Fujii, S. (2006). Palatable punishment in real-world social dilemmas? Punishing others to increase cooperation among the unpunished. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that intermittent punishment (where only some group members are punished for defection in a given round) increases cooperation rates among those not directly punished, compared to a no-punishment baseline. This 'spillover effect' is robust to changes in the framing of the decision (individual vs. group focus) and to information about prior cooperation by others. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

- source: Billard, E. A. (1996). Evolutionary strategies of stochastic learning automata in the prisoner's dilemma. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper argues that cooperation in resource dilemmas (including public goods and commons dilemmas) is shaped by individual value orientation (pro-social vs egoistic), situational constraints (such as group identification, communication, and social norms), and the degree and type of uncertainty (social and environmental). It proposes that pro-social individuals are more likely to cooperate regardless of uncertainty, while egoistic individuals are more likely to defect, especially under uncertainty. Situational factors (including the possibility of sanctions or rewards) can constrain egoism and promote cooperation. The paper reviews prior findings that rewards increase cooperation and sanctions decrease defection, but notes that the costs of such interventions may outweigh collective gains and that effects may not persist when interventions are removed. The analysis is theoretical and hypothesis-generating, not empirical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual background on the psychological mechanisms that may moderate the effect of punishment, reward, and uncertainty on cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that punishment and reward can increase cooperation, but may not always improve efficiency due to their costs, and that their effects may not persist. The paper does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of situational constraints (including punishment/reward), group identification, communication, and uncertainty, and in warning that interventions may have costs that offset their benefits. However, it does not provide direct evidence for the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.

- source: Corriveau, L. (2012). Game theory and the kula. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in a repeated, circular n-player game (the 'game of kula'), the unique Nash equilibrium of the one-shot game is inefficient (all defect). However, when the game is repeated indefinitely and players are sufficiently patient, efficient (fully cooperative) equilibria can be sustained by social norms that attach punishment to deviations. The model formalizes three types of punishment-based norms: direct reciprocity (punished by the harmed party), indirect reciprocity (punished by a third party), and mixed reciprocity (punished by both). The mixed norm is shown to be the most robust, existing under the widest range of parameters. The analysis is entirely theoretical, but it provides formal conditions under which punishment enables efficient cooperation, and shows that the presence of punishment strategies can transform an inefficient game into an efficient one.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in repeated, multi-player social dilemma games (with circular or networked structure) can increase efficiency, provided players are sufficiently patient and punishment is credible. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the results generalize the repeated prisoner's dilemma. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is possible and observable, and where players have high discount factors, the introduction of punishment mechanisms can shift the equilibrium from inefficient (defection) to efficient (cooperation), thus increasing group payoff. The paper also clarifies that the structure of punishment (direct, indirect, mixed) affects the robustness of cooperation, with mixed norms being most robust. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, networked social dilemmas, especially as the number of players increases and as the possibility of both direct and indirect punishment exists.

- source: Ziegler, R. (1997). The normative structure of solidarity and inequality. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both dyadic and collective solidarity games (including public-goods-like settings), strategies that combine defection with active sanctioning of others (Hypocritical Cooperation) can robustly enforce high levels of solidarity (cooperation), provided the costs of sanctioning are not too high. However, this can also lead to 'overcompliance' or excessive norm enforcement unless checked by counterstrategies (Full Opposition). The evolutionary outcome depends on the mix of strategies, the costs and effectiveness of sanctions, and the distribution of 'poor' and 'rich' in the population. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that informal punishment can sustain cooperation under certain conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that informal punishment mechanisms can sustain cooperation in public-goods-like environments, especially when the cost of sanctioning is not prohibitive and when the population includes actors motivated to enforce norms even while defecting themselves. However, the findings are based on simulation and focus on the prevalence of strategies and 'level of solidarity' rather than explicit group efficiency or payoff. The results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency will depend on the cost and effectiveness of sanctions, the strategic mix in the population, and the degree of inequality. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency, so its value for direct prediction is limited to qualitative insights about when punishment is likely to be effective.

- source: Castro, L., Serrano, J. M., & Toro, M. A. (1998). Conceptual capacity to categorize and the evolution of altruism. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the Assessor strategy, which incorporates a punishment-like mechanism (reprobation) based on observed or reported selfish behavior, achieves higher fitness (i.e., greater group efficiency or payoff) than standard reciprocal strategies like Tit-for-Tat or Observer Tit-for-Tat when competing against always-defect strategies. This advantage is robust across both unstructured and family-structured populations, provided certain thresholds of cooperation frequency are met. The Assessor strategy is especially effective when individuals can observe or be informed about others' reprobation of selfish behavior, allowing them to withhold cooperation from defectors more efficiently. The model predicts that such punishment-like mechanisms can support higher levels of cooperation and group payoff than reciprocity alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment or sanctioning mechanisms (here, reprobation) in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency/payoff relative to control conditions without such mechanisms. The findings are most directly relevant to iterated prisoner's dilemma settings, which are adjacent to public goods games, and suggest that the ability to observe and act on others' punishment signals is a key moderator. For prediction tasks, the results imply that, all else equal, introducing a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, especially when players can observe or learn about others' punishment actions. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide quantitative effect sizes or empirical validation.

- source: Ma, Y. P., Gonçalves, S., Mignot, S., Nadal, J. P., & Gordon, M. B. (2009). Cycles of cooperation and free-riding in social systems. *European Physical Journal B*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model predicts that, depending on parameters, communities can settle into fixed points (Nash equilibria) with stable mixtures of cooperators and free-riders, or exhibit persistent cycles in the fractions of cooperators and free-riders. The presence and width of the distribution of moral costs (idiosyncratic disutility for free-riding) are crucial for the emergence of cycles. Stronger social interactions (bandwagon effects) can stabilize high-cooperation equilibria and suppress cycles. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning actions, but models the deterrent to free-riding as an internalized moral cost. The efficiency or total surplus of the group is not directly compared across different institutional designs (e.g., with/without punishment), so the findings are not directly predictive for the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is of limited direct use for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not model explicit punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency outcomes in the standard sense. However, it provides indirect evidence that internalized costs for free-riding (moral disapproval) can sustain cooperation and lead to complex group dynamics, including cycles. The model's structure and results may be informative for understanding how heterogeneity in social preferences and costs can affect cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative predictions for the effect of punishment interventions or for the mapping from control to treatment efficiency in PGGs.

- source: Stiff, C. (2008). Are They Bothered? How the Opportunity to Damage a Partner's Reputation Influences Giving Behavior in a Trust Game. *Journal of Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The opportunity to leave feedback (damage a partner's reputation) increases the amount participants are willing to give in a one-shot trust game, but only when the partner is perceived to care about their reputation. This effect is strongest when the partner's future interaction group consists of strangers rather than friends. The effect of feedback opportunity and group familiarity on giving is mediated by the participant's perceived control over the partner's reputation. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare, and the design is a dyadic trust game rather than a public goods game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that reputation-based indirect sanctions (opportunity to leave feedback) can increase cooperative behavior (giving/trust) in dyadic trust games, especially when the target is reputation-sensitive. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of reputation-based sanctions depends on the perceived concern for reputation and the social context of future interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper is only adjacent: it supports the idea that indirect, reputation-based sanctions can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency effects or direct evidence for public goods games with explicit punishment mechanisms.

- source: Aruka, Y., & Akiyama, E. (2009). Non-self-averaging of a two-person game with only positive spillover: a new formulation of Avatamsaka's dilemma. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in the Avatamsaka game, cooperation dynamics can exhibit either self-averaging or non-self-averaging behavior depending on the stochastic process model used (e.g., one-parameter vs two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet). Positive spillover means that mutual cooperation increases expected gains, but the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff directly. Punishment or sanctions are not a focus, and the game structure is distinct from standard public goods games. The main contribution is a theoretical exploration of how cooperation rates fluctuate and may fail to converge in certain stochastic environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its theoretical results on cooperation dynamics in a two-person positive spillover game may be of limited, adjacent interest for understanding path dependence or stochasticity in cooperation, but are not informative for the downstream prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency.
