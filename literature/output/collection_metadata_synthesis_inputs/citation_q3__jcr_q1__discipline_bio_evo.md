- source: Dong, Y. L., Sasaki, T., & Zhang, B. Y. (2019). The competitive advantage of institutional reward. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

- source: Podder, S., Righi, S., & Pancotto, F. (2021). Reputation and punishment sustain cooperation in the optional public goods game. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the OPGG, punishment alone (including anti-social punishment) does not increase cooperation or group fitness, and can even reduce them by increasing the prevalence of loners. Reputation alone can increase cooperation and fitness, but only under specific social norms (e.g., when defectors are assigned a strictly worse reputation than loners). The combination of reputation and punishment leads to the highest levels of cooperation and population fitness, with most agents adopting conditional cooperation strategies and rarely using punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size, synergy factor, punishment cost and effectiveness, and other parameters. The findings suggest that reputation and punishment interact synergistically to sustain cooperation and improve group outcomes, but that reputation can substitute for costly punishment in many cases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in optional public goods games, the addition of punishment alone (especially when anti-social punishment is possible) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and may even reduce it. However, when reputation mechanisms are also present—especially those that allow agents to distinguish defectors from loners—punishment and reputation together can synergistically increase cooperation and group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the presence and nature of reputation mechanisms and social norms. In the absence of reputation, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency; with reputation, especially under certain norms, enabling punishment can have a more positive effect. The model's robustness checks indicate these findings are not highly sensitive to group size, rounds, or punishment cost within the tested ranges.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

  findings: The study finds that the opportunity for decentralized punishment in a repeated public goods game increases contributions to the public good compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect is especially pronounced for individuals high in extraversion, who tend to free-ride in the absence of punishment but increase their contributions when punishment is possible. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be quantified from this paper alone. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the punishment mechanism is standard (costly, decentralized, 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio). The findings support the general prediction that punishment increases cooperation, but do not allow direct prediction of efficiency outcomes for the downstream task.

Gossip is especially important when direct observation of others' behavior is limited, as it enables vicarious learning and helps participants adjust their own contributions based on information received from others. However, it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on punishment effects or efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that social information transmission (gossip) can substitute for formal sanctions in sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative estimates for efficiency outcomes or for games with punishment enabled. The design dimensions most directly addressed are player count, number of rounds, chat/communication, and information visibility. The absence of punishment and efficiency outcomes limits its direct applicability to the prediction task.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., El Mouden, C., & West, S. A. (2017). Social learning and the demise of costly cooperation in humans. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that providing social information about other groups' behaviors or payoffs does not increase cooperation in repeated public goods games; in most cases, it leads to a significant decline in cooperation. The decline is strongest when individuals can observe the behavior and payoffs of the most successful individuals, supporting the idea of payoff-biased or prestige-biased social learning. There is no evidence for conformity bias (copying common behaviors) or group-prestige bias (copying successful groups). The study does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the design. Its findings are relevant for understanding how social learning and information about others' behavior/payoff affect cooperation, but not for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper should be considered as not providing evidence about punishment effects, but may be informative about baseline cooperation dynamics in the absence of punishment.

- source: Okada, I., Yamamoto, H., Toriumi, F., & Sasaki, T. (2015). The Effect of Incentives and Meta-incentives on the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a theoretical evolutionary game framework, cooperation and high efficiency (group payoff) can only be stably achieved if the incentive system includes second-order incentives for first-order rewards. Systems with only first-order punishment or reward, or with second-order incentives for punishment but not for reward, cannot maintain cooperation or efficiency in the long run due to the second-order free-rider problem. The cost-effect ratio of incentives (how much impact per unit cost) is a critical parameter: higher ratios make it easier for cooperation to be sustained. The model predicts that systems with second-order rewards for first-order incentives are more robust and can achieve high efficiency with fewer incentive-providers than systems with second-order punishments. The analysis is entirely theoretical, using replicator dynamics, and does not provide empirical estimates of efficiency but gives qualitative and parametric conditions for when efficiency is high or low under different incentive structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment and reward systems (including meta-incentives) on efficiency in public-goods-like games. It suggests that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for high efficiency; the structure of incentives, especially the presence of second-order incentives for rewarding, is crucial. If a game design includes only first-order punishment, efficiency will not improve in the long run. If second-order incentives for first-order rewards are present and the cost-effect ratio is high, efficiency can be high. The model also clarifies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional on the broader incentive structure. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the presence or absence of second-order incentives, and the cost-effectiveness of punishment/reward, are key moderators of the efficiency effect of punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that negative indirect reciprocity (NIR)—whereby individuals are allowed to exploit those with bad reputations—can suppress exploitation and support the emergence and maintenance of cooperation and public goods provision. The model shows that cooperation is most stable when exploitation is inefficient (harm to victim > gain to exploiter), and that as the frequency of opportunities for reputation improvement increases, the minimum benefit required for stable cooperation also increases.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (here, tolerated exploitation of ill-reputed individuals) can increase group efficiency and public goods provision, especially when exploitation is inefficient and when opportunities for norm adherence are not too frequent. The model gives explicit payoff-based conditions for when cooperation is stable, which can inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The findings suggest that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when the cost to the victim of exploitation is high relative to the benefit to the exploiter, and when errors in reputation assignment are low. The model also highlights that as norm adherence becomes more mandatory and frequent, the requirements for stable cooperation become stricter, potentially reducing the positive effect of punishment on efficiency in some parameter regimes.

- source: Vollan, B., Prediger, S., & Frölich, M. (2013). Co-managing common-pool resources: Do formal rules have to be adapted to traditional ecological norms?. *Ecological Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This field-lab experiment with communal farmers in southern Africa shows that the efficiency of formal, democratically chosen rules for managing a common-pool resource depends on their alignment with local ecological norms. The quota rule (limiting extraction) increases group earnings and resource availability in both high and low sustainability groups, but is unpopular. The rotation rule only increases efficiency in low sustainability groups (who previously failed to coordinate), but is ineffective or even counterproductive in high sustainability groups (who already tacitly coordinated for sustainability), due to high rates of rule breaking. Thus, the effect of rule-based interventions on efficiency is highly context-dependent.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of introducing a formal rule (akin to a punishment or restriction mechanism) on efficiency in a common-pool resource game, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect depends on the compatibility of the rule with pre-existing group norms. Rules that align with local sustainability norms (e.g., quotas in groups that value sustainability) can increase efficiency, while rules that conflict with these norms (e.g., rotation in high-norm groups) may reduce efficiency due to non-compliance. The presence of a sanctioning mechanism (imperfect monitoring and penalty) is not sufficient to ensure compliance or efficiency gains. Thus, the effect of punishment-like interventions on efficiency is moderated by group-level norms and prior behavior, not just by the formal game design. This paper is highly informative for settings where the main intervention is a formal rule or restriction, but less so for classic peer-punishment PGGs.
