- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Pi, J. X., Yang, G. H., & Yang, H. (2022). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in N-person snowdrift games with peer punishment and individual disguise. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Punishment mechanisms and cooperation in public goods games: Experimental evidence. *Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with four players per group, the introduction of either individual or majority-voting punishment mechanisms increases average contributions relative to the no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether MPCRs are homogeneous or heterogeneous. However, group payoffs (efficiency) do not significantly differ between the no-punishment, individual punishment, and majority-voting punishment conditions. The cost of punishment offsets the gains from increased cooperation. The majority-voting punishment mechanism reduces the frequency of antisocial punishment compared to individual punishment, particularly in homogeneous groups, but antisocial punishment remains present in all conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds per condition, and either homogeneous (MPCR=0.5) or heterogeneous (MPCR=0.3/0.5/0.7) returns, enabling punishment (either individual or majority-voting) increases contributions but does not increase efficiency relative to the control. The cost of punishment absorbs the surplus from higher cooperation. Thus, when predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency unless the cost structure or punishment technology is more favorable. The majority-voting mechanism may reduce antisocial punishment but does not improve efficiency over individual punishment. The findings are most applicable to small, anonymous, stranger-matched groups with no communication and known round structure.

- source: Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., & Reddmann, L. (2022). Shadow of conflict: How past conflict influences group cooperation and the use of punishment. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a lab experiment with groups of four, participants either experienced an asymmetric intergroup conflict (attacker-defender game) or not, and then played a repeated public goods game with peer punishment. The main findings are: (1) Prior conflict does not significantly reduce average cooperation rates, but does reduce group earnings (efficiency) due to less effective punishment. (2) When previous conflict roles are identifiable, punishment is less likely to be targeted at free-riders and is less effective at increasing cooperation, especially when administered by previous attackers. (3) Hiding previous conflict roles (resetting group tags) increases both cooperation and group earnings over time, and punishment regains its effectiveness. (4) The negative effect of conflict on efficiency is not due to lower cooperation per se, but to maladaptive punishment patterns that reduce the welfare gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with peer punishment, the presence of a prior asymmetric conflict (with identifiable roles) reduces group efficiency (earnings) even if average cooperation rates are not significantly lower. The reduction in efficiency is due to less effective punishment: free-riders are punished less, and punishment by previous attackers is less effective. If prior conflict roles are hidden, efficiency increases and punishment regains its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is moderated by the social context—specifically, whether there is a 'shadow of conflict' and whether past roles are identifiable. In standard PGG designs without such conflict history, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, identifiability) are all well specified, and the control efficiency is directly comparable.

- source: Wang, C. Q., & Huang, C. C. (2022). Between local and global strategy updating in public goods game. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing the option for costly punishment in a repeated public goods game increases group contributions and group payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline. This effect holds both when sanctions are observable (feedback given immediately) and when sanctions are unobservable (feedback delayed until the end). The mechanism is that subjects expect free riders to be punished and adjust their behavior to avoid being punished, even when they do not observe sanctions in real time. Sanctioning is mainly non-strategic and norm-driven, targeting free riders. The presence of punishment stabilizes cooperation and prevents the unraveling of contributions, with some groups achieving high cooperation and others not. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency (as measured by group payoffs and contributions as a fraction of maximum) is positive.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to whether punishment is observable or not during play, suggesting that the mere possibility of punishment, and the associated social norm, is sufficient to increase cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment to a repeated linear PGG with 4 players, 10 rounds, and MPCR=0.4 will increase efficiency, even if punishment is not immediately observable. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

- source: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T. (2023). Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.

  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

- source: Eichenseer, M. (2023). Leading-by-example in public goods experiments: What do we know?. *Leadership Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The paper synthesizes 62 experimental studies (mainly linear public goods games) and conducts meta-analyses and weighted least squares regressions. It finds that sequential play (leadership) increases group efficiency compared to simultaneous play. Centralized punishment and reward (where only the leader can punish or reward) further increase efficiency, with punishment having a stronger effect than reward.
