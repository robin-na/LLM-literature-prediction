- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., & Reddmann, L. (2022). Shadow of conflict: How past conflict influences group cooperation and the use of punishment. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a lab experiment with groups of four, participants either experienced an asymmetric intergroup conflict (attacker-defender game) or not, and then played a repeated public goods game with peer punishment. The main findings are: (1) Prior conflict does not significantly reduce average cooperation rates, but does reduce group earnings (efficiency) due to less effective punishment. (2) When previous conflict roles are identifiable, punishment is less likely to be targeted at free-riders and is less effective at increasing cooperation, especially when administered by previous attackers. (3) Hiding previous conflict roles (resetting group tags) increases both cooperation and group earnings over time, and punishment regains its effectiveness. (4) The negative effect of conflict on efficiency is not due to lower cooperation per se, but to maladaptive punishment patterns that reduce the welfare gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with peer punishment, the presence of a prior asymmetric conflict (with identifiable roles) reduces group efficiency (earnings) even if average cooperation rates are not significantly lower. The reduction in efficiency is due to less effective punishment: free-riders are punished less, and punishment by previous attackers is less effective. If prior conflict roles are hidden, efficiency increases and punishment regains its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is moderated by the social context—specifically, whether there is a 'shadow of conflict' and whether past roles are identifiable. In standard PGG designs without such conflict history, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, identifiability) are all well specified, and the control efficiency is directly comparable.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Ambrus, A., & Greiner, B. (2019). Individual, Dictator, and Democratic punishment in public good games with perfect and imperfect observability. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper experimentally compares three punishment institutions (individual, democratic, dictator) in repeated 5-person public goods games, under both perfect and imperfect monitoring. Democratic punishment, where a majority vote is required to punish, results in significantly higher average net profits (efficiency) and higher contributions than individual or dictator punishment. This is true in both monitoring conditions, though the efficiency advantage is somewhat reduced under noise. The improvement is due to a sharp reduction in anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators), while pro-social punishment (punishing defectors) is maintained. Dictator punishment does not outperform individual punishment in terms of efficiency. The study provides direct, quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment institution and monitoring noise on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games. It shows that the institutional design of punishment (democratic vs. individual vs. dictator) and the observability of contributions (perfect vs. noisy) are key moderators. Democratic punishment substantially increases efficiency compared to individual punishment, especially by reducing anti-social punishment. However, the efficiency gain is somewhat sensitive to monitoring noise. The paper provides exact values for average net profits under each condition, allowing for quantitative calibration of prediction models. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, observability, etc.) are all clearly specified, making the results highly transferable to similar prediction tasks.

- source: Bühren, C., & Dannenberg, A. (2021). The demand for punishment to promote cooperation among like-minded people. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games is highly contingent on group type and information. Highly cooperative groups achieve near-optimal payoffs without punishment, and introducing punishment reduces efficiency due to its cost. Uncooperative groups only benefit from severe punishment if they are informed about their own composition and implement the institution early; otherwise, punishment is often misapplied or subverted, leading to low efficiency. Middle cooperators benefit from punishment only in the absence of information about group composition. Information about group cooperativeness allows groups to better match their institution choice to their needs, increasing overall efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment is not uniform and is moderated by group composition and information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games is not a simple function of the game design dimensions alone, but is critically moderated by group composition (cooperativeness) and information about that composition. For prediction tasks, this means that the same punishment institution can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency depending on whether the group is composed of high, middle, or low cooperators, and whether they know this. In the absence of such information, punishment is often over- or under-used, reducing efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction from this paper are the presence and cost of punishment, the severity/technology of punishment, the availability of information about group composition, and the baseline cooperativeness of the group. Control efficiency (no punishment) is a strong predictor: if control efficiency is high, adding punishment reduces efficiency; if control efficiency is low, adding severe punishment with information can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of comprehension and early institution choice for realizing efficiency gains from punishment.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Chapkovski, P. (2021). Strike one hundred to educate one: Measuring the efficacy of collective sanctions experimentally. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Collective sanctions are not effective at increasing cooperation or contributions in a public good game, and may even reduce them compared to individual sanctions, especially when peer punishment is available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions overall, but under collective sanctions, contributions are significantly lower than under individual sanctions with peer punishment. Average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions, but this difference is not statistically significant.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard PGG with 3 players, 15 rounds, continuous contributions, and a fixed threshold for external punishment, enabling collective sanctions (punishing the whole group for one member's under-contribution) does not improve efficiency or group payoff compared to individual sanctions, and may even reduce cooperation and contributions, especially when peer punishment is also available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions, but collective sanctions reduce this effect. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that switching from individual to collective sanctions is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, particularly in small groups with peer punishment. Gender composition may moderate effects.

- source: Paál, T., & Bereczkei, T. (2015). Punishment as a Means of Competition: Implications for Strong Reciprocity Theory. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

Punishment was not targeted at low contributors but at those with higher accumulated profits, indicating strategic rather than norm-enforcing use. There was no association between contributions and punishment received or imposed. The results suggest that in competitive environments, punishment can become a tool for rivalry rather than for sustaining cooperation, and does not reverse the decline in cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that in a PGG with a competitive, tournament-style payoff structure and full information about others' profits, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency or group payoff. Instead, punishment is used for strategic competition, and cooperation continues to decline. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent: in competitive, rank-based settings, punishment may not improve and may even undermine group efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so this inference is based on contribution behavior and the described use of punishment.

- source: Kubena, A. A., Houdek, P., Lindová, J., Príplatová, L., & Flegr, J. (2014). Justine Effect: Punishment of the Unduly Self-Sacrificing Cooperative Individuals. *Plos One*.
