- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Hetzer, M., & Sornette, D. (2013). An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, evolutionary dynamics lead to two regimes: (1) a defection regime with low cooperation and low group payoff when punishment is weak or absent, and (2) a coordination regime with higher cooperation and higher group payoff when punishment is sufficiently strong and driven by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model predicts the empirically observed propensity to punish in lab experiments. The presence of punishment transforms the social dilemma into a coordination problem, enabling higher efficiency. Disadvantageous inequity aversion is shown to be evolutionarily dominant, and populations with this trait invade and outcompete purely self-regarding populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency (group payoff) when punishment is sufficiently strong and is motivated by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model gives explicit formulas for the threshold propensity to punish and shows that, above this threshold, populations coordinate on higher contributions and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar design parameters (e.g., n=4, r=3, MPCR=0.4), enabling punishment will shift the group from low to high efficiency, provided the punishment regime is effective. The paper also predicts how efficiency and punishment propensity depend on group size and punishment effectiveness, which can inform predictions for other parameterizations. However, the paper does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency change, so predictions should be interpreted as strong theoretical expectations rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: Roberts, G. (2013). When Punishment Pays. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, punishment can be self-interested and increase group efficiency when the cost to punishers is outweighed by the benefits of increased cooperation. The key condition is that the cost to the punisher (p) is less than the product of the punishment impact (q), the benefit per cooperative act (b), divided by the net cost of cooperation (c): p < q*b/c. Simulations confirm that with typical lab parameters (e.g., group size 3, k=2, p=1, q=3), punishment leads to high stable cooperation and high group payoffs. The effect is robust to group size and number of rounds, provided the parameter condition holds. The paper also explains why punishment sometimes fails to increase payoffs (e.g., when p is too high or q is too low), and why punishment is more effective in repeated than one-shot games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for predicting that, in repeated public goods games with punishment enabled and with favorable punishment parameters (low p, high q), efficiency will be substantially higher than in control games without punishment. The key moderators are the punishment cost (p), punishment effectiveness (q), group size (g), and the number of rounds (m). The paper gives explicit formulas for when punishment will increase efficiency, and shows that the effect is robust across a range of typical lab parameters. If the control game has low efficiency and the punishment parameters satisfy p < q*b/c, enabling punishment should lead to a large increase in efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Bodnar, T., & Salathé, M. (2012). Governing the Global Commons with Local Institutions. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment to maintain high efficiency in a public goods game depends critically on the spatial reach of the sanctioning institution relative to the interaction range of the commons. Both peer and pool punishment can maintain the commons (i.e., prevent the tragedy of the commons and sustain high group payoff/efficiency) if the coverage coefficient (probability that two players can punish each other) is above a threshold. There are sharp phase transitions: small increases in the reach of punishment can lead to large increases in efficiency. Pool punishment can solve the second-order free rider problem and maintain the commons even when peer punishment cannot, but only if the cost of contributing to the punishment pool is low and the punishment imposed on defectors is high. The model also shows that in some parameter regimes, peer punishment can be more effective than pool punishment, especially when the reach of the sanctioning system is intermediate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, provided that the punishment system has sufficient reach (coverage) and the cost/impact parameters are favorable. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not linear but can exhibit threshold effects: if the punishment system is too local or weak, it may have little effect, but once a critical coverage is reached, efficiency can increase dramatically. The model also distinguishes between peer and pool punishment, showing that institutionalized (pool) punishment can maintain efficiency even in the face of second-order free riding, but only if the cost structure is appropriate. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the spatial reach and cost-effectiveness of punishment are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment, and that the absence of sufficient reach can nullify the effect of punishment.

- source: Ye, H., Chen, S., Luo, J., Tan, F., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2016). Increasing returns to scale: The solution to the second-order social dilemma. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a PGG with increasing returns to scale, the presence of punishment leads to a dramatic increase in group efficiency and average payoff, provided the IRS parameter (alpha) is above a threshold (about 1.2–1.3). In standard PGGs (alpha=1), punishment does not stably increase efficiency, as defectors can invade and dominate. With strong IRS (alpha > 1.3), punishers can resist invasion by both defectors and second-order free-riders, leading to stable high cooperation and high group payoff. The effect of punishment is thus highly contingent on the returns-to-scale structure of the public good. The results are supported by both analytical evolutionary models and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment in a PGG depends critically on the degree of increasing returns to scale in the production function. In standard linear PGGs, punishment may not improve efficiency, but in games with strong IRS, punishment can lead to near-maximal efficiency by stabilizing cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence and strength of IRS (or analogous nonlinear production functions) should be considered a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Other game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are also discussed, but the IRS effect is dominant.

- source: Hintze, A., Staudacher, J., Gelhar, K., Pothmann, A., Rasch, J., & Wildegger, D. (2020). Inclusive groups can avoid the tragedy of the commons. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment in public goods games lowers the critical synergy threshold required for cooperation to emerge, thereby increasing the likelihood of cooperation and improving group-level payoff (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. However, the cost of punishment reduces the total group payoff, so the efficiency gain from increased cooperation is partially offset by punishment costs. The effect is stronger when punishment is more efficient (higher fine per unit cost). The paper also shows that inclusiveness (redistribution of payoffs) is an even more effective way to promote cooperation and efficiency, often making punishment unnecessary if inclusiveness is high enough.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is efficient (high fine, low cost). The effect is moderated by the costliness of punishment: higher punishment costs reduce the efficiency benefit. The findings also highlight that the structure of payoff redistribution (inclusiveness) can be a more powerful moderator than punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that, all else equal, adding punishment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the degree of payoff redistribution.

  findings: Collective sanctions are not effective at increasing cooperation or contributions in a public good game, and may even reduce them compared to individual sanctions, especially when peer punishment is available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions overall, but under collective sanctions, contributions are significantly lower than under individual sanctions with peer punishment. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions, but collective sanctions reduce this effect. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that switching from individual to collective sanctions is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, particularly in small groups with peer punishment. Gender composition may moderate effects.

Instead, punishment is used for strategic competition, and cooperation continues to decline. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent: in competitive, rank-based settings, punishment may not improve and may even undermine group efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so this inference is based on contribution behavior and the described use of punishment.
