- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

- source: Jia, C., Zhang, R. X., & Wang, D. (2023). Evolutionary game of cooperative behavior among social capitals in PPP projects: A complex network perspective. *Ain Shams Engineering Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that increasing the default penalty (punishment) leads to higher rates of cooperation among social capitals in a networked prisoner's dilemma setting. Higher network density reduces cooperation, and initial cooperation probability can promote cooperation only when the temptation to defect (betrayal income) is not too high. The results are based on simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not empirical data. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that cooperation is important for the efficiency of PPP projects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that introducing or increasing punishment in a networked prisoner's dilemma can increase cooperation rates, which the authors argue is beneficial for project efficiency. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report results in terms of efficiency ratios. The findings are most relevant for understanding the behavioral mechanism (cooperation) rather than direct prediction of efficiency outcomes. The model's focus on network structure, punishment, and cooperation may inform qualitative expectations about the direction of efficiency effects when punishment is enabled, but does not provide direct predictive data for the downstream task.

- source: Wang, Q. E., Lai, W., Ding, M. M., & Qiu, Q. (2022). Research on Cooperative Behavior of Green Technology Innovation in Construction Enterprises Based on Evolutionary Game. *Buildings*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stronger punishment mechanisms (higher penalty and indirect punishment) increase the likelihood that both parties will adopt positive cooperation strategies, thus reducing opportunistic behavior. Similarly, higher willingness to cooperate, greater positive spillover (sharing), and more favorable income distribution also promote positive cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of the probability or stability of cooperation strategies, not in terms of group efficiency or total payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves cooperation with spillovers and punishment, but is tailored to the context of construction enterprise innovation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task: it shows that increasing punishment intensity (both direct and indirect) makes positive cooperation more likely in a repeated game with spillovers, which is structurally similar to public goods games. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so its value is in supporting the mechanism that punishment can deter defection and promote cooperation under certain parameter regimes. The findings are most useful for understanding how punishment, sharing, and income distribution interact to affect cooperation, but less directly useful for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Li, J. M., & Jiang, S. S. (2023). How can governance strategies be developed for marine ecological environment pollution caused by sea-using enterprises?-A study based on evolutionary game theory. *Ocean & Coastal Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in the absence of strong governance mechanisms (punishment, compensation, or high ecological performance appraisal), all parties tend to adopt minimal effort strategies, resulting in low efficiency and poor environmental outcomes (prisoner's dilemma). Introducing punishment (penalties for non-compliance, ecological damage compensation), increasing the weight of ecological indicators in local government performance appraisal, and combining these tools can shift the system to a more efficient equilibrium, where local governments and enterprises fully implement and comply with environmental policies, even if central government supervision is weak. The effect of any single tool is limited, but their combination is necessary to achieve high efficiency in governance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms (penalties, compensation for damage) and reward mechanisms (performance appraisal) are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from introducing punishment in public-goods-like governance settings. The results suggest that, in adjacent multi-agent settings, punishment alone may not be sufficient, but when combined with other governance tools, it can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence and magnitude of punishment, reward, and their combination should be considered as key moderators of treatment efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to weak incentives.
