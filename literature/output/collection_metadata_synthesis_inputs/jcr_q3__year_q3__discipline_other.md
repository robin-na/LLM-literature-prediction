- source: Gomez-Ruiz, L., & Sánchez-Expósito, M. J. (2020). The Impact of Team Identity and Gender on Free-Riding Responses to Fear and Cooperation Sustainability. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that strong team identity significantly reduces free-riding responses to fear and increases cooperation rates among men, but not among women. The effect of team identity on cooperation sustainability (maintenance of cooperation over time) is marginally significant for men and not significant for women. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. No formal punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on informal control (team identity) and gender differences. The findings suggest that team identity can be an effective informal mechanism to sustain cooperation among men in repeated social dilemmas with binary contribution choices and feedback, but not among women.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It studies a repeated binary-choice social dilemma (close to a public goods game) without any punishment or reward mechanism. The main manipulations are team identity and gender. The outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. Therefore, this paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it does provide contextual evidence about the role of informal mechanisms (team identity) and gender in sustaining cooperation. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation rates in binary-choice, feedback-rich, no-punishment settings, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Zhang, Q. H., Chen, Y. Y., Tao, Y., Farid, T., & Ma, J. H. (2019). How Consistent Contributors Inspire Individuals to Cooperate: The Role of Moral Elevation and Social Value Orientation. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: This study demonstrates that introducing a consistent contributor (CC) into a step-level public-goods-dilemma game increases both cooperation and group/individual earnings. The effect is robust across both pro-social and pro-self participants, though the underlying psychological mechanisms differ: pro-selfs are influenced via increased moral elevation, while pro-socials respond directly to the CC's behavior. The design does not include punishment or sanction mechanisms, but provides strong evidence that motivational (role model) interventions can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanction mechanisms, introducing a consistent contributor (role model) can increase group efficiency (payoff) in step-level public-goods games. The findings are most relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar motivational interventions, but do not inform the effect of punishment. The design and results can serve as a control or baseline for understanding efficiency in the absence of punishment, and highlight the potential for non-punitive mechanisms to improve outcomes.

- source: Ferguson, E. (2021). Inequality averse and compassionate blood donor: implication for interventions. *Vox Sanguinis*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Blood donors are more likely to choose compensation (directly helping the victim) over punishment (sanctioning the perpetrator) in a one-shot 3PPC game, while organ donors are more likely to choose punishment. The compensation and punishment options are both costly to the third party, and the game is designed so that each £1 spent on compensation or punishment has a 2x effect. The study does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, but describes how different choices affect the distribution of payoffs. A follow-up experiment finds that an intervention message based on advantageous inequality aversion increases non-donors' willingness to take information about blood donation, with some evidence that guilt-based appeals may have negative effects on current donors.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about individual preferences for compensation versus punishment in a third-party context, which is adjacent to public goods games with punishment. The design is a one-shot, three-player game with both punishment and compensation options, and the main outcomes are behavioral choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the presence of a compensation option may reduce the use of punishment, and that individuals' philanthropic identities (e.g., blood donor vs. organ donor) influence their preference for direct help versus norm enforcement. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in repeated public goods games, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited and indirect.

- source: Liu, W. W., & Yang, J. N. (2018). The Evolutionary Game Theoretic Analysis for Sustainable Cooperation Relationship of Collaborative Innovation Network in Strategic Emerging Industries. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that government intervention through incentives and punishments can significantly influence the frequency of positive cooperation between enterprises and URIs in collaborative innovation networks. When the sum of incentives and punishments exceeds the total additional cost (TAC) of cooperation, positive cooperation becomes stable. If incentives and punishments are too low, negative cooperation prevails. The model does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, but focuses on the conditions for stable cooperation strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that punishment (and incentives) can increase the frequency of cooperative behavior in a multi-agent innovation network, but it does not quantify effects on efficiency or group payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves free-riding and cooperation, but the outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies) rather than payoff-based. The findings suggest that, in similar environments, increasing punishment and incentives above a threshold can shift the system toward more cooperation, which may imply higher efficiency, but this is not directly modeled or measured.

- source: Berger, J. (2021). Social Tipping Interventions Can Promote the Diffusion or Decay of Sustainable Consumption Norms in the Field. Evidence from a Quasi-Experimental Intervention Study. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that providing regular normative feedback about the prevalence of sustainable behavior (use of mugs) can trigger rapid norm change in the field, either increasing or decreasing sustainable behavior depending on the initial prevalence. In one cafeteria with a high initial rate of sustainable choices, the intervention led to a sharp increase in sustainable behavior, consistent with a social tipping dynamic. In another with a low initial rate, the same intervention led to a sharp decline (a 'boomerang effect'). Control conditions (appeal only or no intervention) showed little or no persistent change. The study does not report on efficiency, payoffs, or group welfare, and the outcome is strictly behavioral (proportion of sustainable choices).
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The intervention is not a PGG or close variant, and there is no punishment or sanctioning mechanism. The outcome is behavioral (norm adoption), not payoff-based. However, the study is relevant for understanding how feedback and norm interventions can shift group behavior in social dilemma contexts, and it highlights the importance of initial conditions for intervention success. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers only indirect, contextual evidence about norm change dynamics, not about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment.
