- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Dannenberg, A., & Levin, S. A. (2022). Punishment institutions selected and sustained through voting and learning. *Nature Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

  findings: The paper finds that gossip can promote cooperation in public goods game environments, even under heterogeneous interpersonal relationships. Among the three mechanisms for dealing with gossip (punishment only, punishment with reputation compensation, and punishment with monetary compensation), punishment with monetary compensation is most effective at increasing cooperation rates and total contributions. The model also identifies optimal parameter ranges for tolerance, penalty time, and rebirth coefficient to maximize cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (cooperation speed, total contributions) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that monetary compensation for those wrongly affected by gossip, combined with punishment for false gossipers, can best promote cooperative behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing punishment (especially with monetary compensation for those wrongly affected) can increase cooperation rates and total contributions in public-goods-game-like environments. However, because the outcomes are not reported as efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting a more positive effect of punishment on cooperation and contribution behavior, rather than on efficiency per se. The model's design and parameter exploration may inform predictions about the likely direction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require caution. The paper is most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms and the relative effectiveness of different punishment/compensation schemes.

Communication (chat) treatments increased collective investment and made high-investment equilibria more likely, but did not guarantee them. Penalty (punishment) treatments, both low and high, reduced collective investment compared to baseline, contrary to theoretical expectations. Communication (chat) increases investment, but does not guarantee high efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in similar field settings may decrease efficiency, especially when intrinsic motivation is important. The main outcome is collective investment, not explicit group payoff, but the relationship is close. The findings are most relevant for field settings with real-world participants and repeated games with investment as the public good.

- source: Lim, I. S., & Capraro, V. (2022). A Synergy of Institutional Incentives and Networked Structures in Evolutionary Game Dynamics of Multiagent Systems. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment and network structure can interact synergistically to promote prosocial behavior and increase group efficiency (mean payoff) in a trust game. Specifically, the combination of even modest punishment and network structure can achieve full cooperation and maximal efficiency in parameter regions where neither mechanism alone would suffice. The required punishment level to achieve full cooperation is lower in structured populations than in well-mixed populations. The mean payoff is maximized at the threshold punishment level that induces full cooperation; increasing punishment beyond this point does not further increase efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of maintaining the incentive system. The paper provides explicit analytical boundaries for these effects as a function of the trust return (r), network degree (k), and punishment size (p).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a networked trust game (and by extension, in adjacent public-goods-like games) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is not too costly and the network structure is not too sparse. The results suggest that the efficiency gain from punishment is greater in structured populations than in well-mixed ones, and that the combination of network structure and punishment can achieve high efficiency at lower punishment levels than punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the network structure (degree), the effectiveness and cost of punishment, and the underlying return to cooperation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games but are likely to transfer to public goods games with similar strategic structure.

- source: Rubin, H. (2022). When it pays to punish in the evolution of honesty and cooperation. *Synthese*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper uses evolutionary game theory and simulations to analyze how different forms of costly punishment (punishing lying vs. punishing defection) affect the evolution of honesty and cooperation in a repeated signaling/donation game. It finds that: (1) Punishment of lying increases honesty but often reduces the prevalence of cooperation, because the costs of punishing and being punished can outweigh the benefits of increased honesty. (2) Punishment of defection (as in standard strong reciprocity models) increases cooperation and group success, consistent with prior findings in stag hunt and repeated PD games. (3) The effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency depends on game parameters (number of rounds, benefit of donation, lie detection probability, punishment cost and effectiveness). (4) When both types of punishment are available, the results are intermediate. The paper highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and can be negative when punishment targets lying rather than defection.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important cautionary evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in games with multi-stage cooperation (e.g., signaling plus donation). If punishment targets lying (dishonest signaling), it may increase honesty but can reduce overall cooperation and thus efficiency, especially when punishment is costly and lie detection is imperfect. If punishment targets defection (failure to cooperate), it is more likely to increase cooperation and efficiency, as in standard public goods or stag hunt models. For prediction tasks, the design dimension 'punishment target' (lying vs. defection) is critical. The paper also shows that the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the number of rounds, and the benefit of cooperation all moderate the effect. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not always a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment), especially when punishment targets lying.

  findings: The paper finds that under a static reward and punishment mechanism (SRPM), the system does not reach a stable equilibrium; strategies of all parties fluctuate periodically.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that dynamic, behavior-dependent punishment mechanisms (analogous to more flexible or responsive punishment rules in public goods games) can stabilize cooperative behavior (legal emissions) and reduce the need for costly monitoring and enforcement. However, the model is not a standard public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The main outcomes are strategy probabilities, not payoffs. The findings suggest that increasing punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech) and using dynamic punishment rules may improve stability and compliance, but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The results are most relevant for understanding how punishment design (cost, tech, dynamic vs static) may affect behavioral outcomes in multi-agent governance games.
