- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Sun, X. P., Liu, X. Z., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2025). The impact of reputation-driven locally variable synergy factors on public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing manipulators—who both punish defectors and reward cooperators—into a spatial public goods game can sustain cooperation and increase average group payoff, even when the synergy factor (r) is low and cooperation would otherwise fail. The model shows that for a range of parameter values (punishment/reward efficiency, manipulation cost), manipulators and cooperators can coexist, and the group achieves higher payoffs than in the absence of punishment/reward. When punishment is sufficiently efficient (beta/gamma high), the system can reach the all-cooperator state, maximizing group payoff. If the cost of manipulation is too high or punishment/reward is inefficient, cooperation collapses and payoffs drop. Reward withdrawal (manipulators reducing rewards when surrounded by cooperators) reduces the average payoff of cooperators and narrows the payoff gap between manipulators and cooperators. The model demonstrates that the combined effect of punishment and reward is robust but sensitive to parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-signal evidence that enabling punishment (and reward) in a spatial public goods game increases group efficiency and average payoff, especially when the control (no punishment/reward) condition would result in low cooperation and low payoff. The effect is moderated by the efficiency of punishment (punishment impact per cost), the cost to punishers/rewarders, and the synergy factor (mpcr equivalent). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment (especially when combined with reward) will generally increase efficiency, provided the punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective. The model also highlights that the presence of defectors and the structure of the population (spatial lattice) are important, but the main prediction is robust across a range of parameter values. This paper should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar spatial PGG designs.

- source: Yang, Z. H., & Yang, Y. L. (2024). Evolutionary dynamics of tax-based strong altruistic reward and punishment in a public goods game. *Chinese Physics B*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, both strong altruistic punishment and reward are unable to sustain cooperation when implemented individually and funded only by voluntary contributions, due to their high cost. However, when a tax is imposed on all participants and the proceeds are used to fund either punishment or reward institutions, cooperation and group efficiency are greatly enhanced. The model shows that with sufficient tax compensation, strong altruistic punishers or rewarders can coexist with cooperators, eliminating defectors and increasing group welfare. Furthermore, tax-based strong altruistic reward leads to higher levels of cooperation and efficiency than tax-based strong altruistic punishment under otherwise identical conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that institutional (tax-based) punishment and reward mechanisms can substantially increase group efficiency in public goods games, compared to traditional voluntary punishment/reward or no-sanction conditions. The results suggest that, for a given set of game design parameters (e.g., player count, MPCR, cost/effectiveness of punishment/reward), introducing a tax-funded punishment or reward institution will increase the average efficiency of the group, with reward institutions being even more effective than punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to settings where punishment/reward is implemented institutionally and funded by mandatory contributions (taxes), and less so to peer-to-peer or voluntary sanctioning. The results are based on evolutionary game theory and numerical simulations, not empirical experiments.

The results are most applicable to settings with continuous contribution, no communication, and fixed group size and structure. The study is simulation-based, so real-world generalizability may be limited, but the mechanism-level insight is strong for theoretical prediction.

- source: Cui, P. B., Wu, Z. X., & Zhou, T. (2022). Small world can alleviate the social dilemmas originating from self-regulation or community policing issues. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that the introduction of prosocial punishment in public goods games, especially when implemented on small-world networks, can substantially increase group efficiency (average payoff) by suppressing defectors and supporting the dominance of cooperative strategies. The effect is strongest when the network structure balances local clustering (which supports network reciprocity) and shortcuts (which allow punishers to reach defectors). Both the strength of punishment (fine) and the sensitivity of punishment implementation are important moderators. Regular lattices support cooperation but mainly favor cooperator-driven punishers, while random networks suppress punishment and cooperation. Small-world networks enable both high cooperation and high efficiency, with an optimal range of network randomness maximizing the effect. The model provides direct predictions for how efficiency changes as a function of punishment parameters and network structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when the game is played on small-world networks with appropriate parameter settings (moderate randomness, sufficient punishment fine, and intermediate feedback sensitivity). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing punishment will lead to a substantial increase in efficiency if the network structure allows for both local clustering and shortcuts. The results also indicate that the effect of punishment is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the network topology. The paper's detailed mapping of efficiency and strategy frequencies across parameter spaces can be used to inform quantitative predictions of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions.

- source: Gao, S. P., & Li, N. (2023). Preference reversal and the evolution of cooperation. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.

- source: Ishikawa, B. Y. S., & Fontanari, J. F. (2025). Revisiting institutional punishment in the N-person prisoner's dilemma. *Theory in Biosciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

The results suggest that moderate, well-funded punishment (and reward) mechanisms, especially when executors are compensated, can stabilize cooperation, but excessive reward or subsidy can backfire. The presence of corruption can reduce the effectiveness of punishment, but this effect is mitigated by higher subsidies. For prediction tasks, this paper informs the expected direction and possible moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency.

- source: Sun, X. P., Bi, Y. Z., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2024). Effect of distribution of fines on evolution of cooperation in spatial public goods game. *Chinese Physics B*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Redistributing fines from defectors to cooperators and punishers in a spatial public goods game generally increases the persistence of cooperation and raises the total system payoff, especially when punishment costs are high. When punishment costs are low, the standard pool punishment model (without redistribution) can achieve higher cooperation and higher total payoff, as punishers dominate and defectors are eliminated. The effect of fine redistribution is thus mixed: it is beneficial for efficiency and cooperation under high punishment cost, but can reduce efficiency and cooperation when punishment cost is low. The distribution parameter (alpha) also matters: allocating more fines to punishers (lower alpha) increases cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the cost of punishment and the mechanism for handling fines. If fines are redistributed to cooperators and punishers, efficiency losses from punishment can be mitigated, especially when punishment is costly. However, in settings where punishment is cheap, standard punishment (without redistribution) may yield higher efficiency by allowing punishers to eliminate defectors. For prediction, the presence or absence of fine redistribution, the punishment cost, and the fine distribution parameter (alpha) are important moderators. The results suggest that enabling fine redistribution is likely to increase efficiency when punishment is expensive, but may not always do so when punishment is cheap.

For prediction tasks, the results suggest that enabling punishment can increase efficiency (by supporting all-cooperation) when the control game is below the cooperation threshold, but may have little or even negative effect when the control game already achieves high cooperation due to network reciprocity.
