- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Hou, G. L., Wang, F., Shi, J. Y., Chen, W. J., & Yu, J. (2019). Which is the ideal sanction for cooperation? An experimental study on different types of third-party sanctions. *Psych Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a standard PGG increases group earnings and cooperation compared to a baseline with no sanctions. The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E. (2019). The effect of decision timing on the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish noncooperation: Sanctioning the past, the present, or the future. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The main finding is that people are less willing to assign costly rewards or punishments when they must decide before observing others' (non)cooperation, compared to after. This effect is robust across both dichotomous and continuous public goods game variants, and for both reward and punishment, though the effect is stronger for punishment. The effect is not explained by differences in emotional reactions. The results are interpreted as evidence for nonconsequential reasoning: uncertainty about future behavior reduces decisiveness and willingness to sanction. The study does not measure or report group efficiency or total payoffs, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to use costly punishment (and reward) in public goods games is lower when sanctions must be decided before observing others' behavior, compared to after. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding when punishment is likely to be used, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Lippert, S., & Tremewan, J. (2021). Pledge-and-review in the laboratory. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment compares a standard two-player public goods game (PG) to three pledge-and-review (P&R) variants: fixed disagreement payoffs (F), heterogeneous/certainty (C), and uncertainty (U). In all P&R treatments, after pledges are made, both players must vote to accept the pledges for them to be implemented; otherwise, both receive a disagreement payoff. Efficiency (average payoffs as a fraction of the full cooperation benchmark) is significantly higher in all P&R treatments than in the control PG game. The efficiency gain is persistent in the fixed disagreement payoff treatment, with no decline over 20 rounds, while some decline is observed in the other P&R treatments. There is no significant difference in efficiency between the three P&R variants. The mechanism allows conditional cooperators to avoid the typical decline in cooperation seen in repeated public goods games. The P&R institution functions as a form of second-party punishment (via voting down low pledges), but with endogenous costs and without explicit costly punishment tokens.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that adding a pledge-and-review (P&R) mechanism—where players can veto low contributions after seeing pledges—substantially increases efficiency in a two-player, 20-round public goods game with continuous contributions and no communication. The effect is robust to variations in disagreement payoffs (fixed, heterogeneous, uncertain). For prediction, if the control (PG) efficiency is known, enabling a P&R-style review stage is likely to increase efficiency by about 10% of the full cooperation benchmark, with the effect persisting over repeated rounds (at least in the fixed disagreement payoff case). The P&R mechanism is not identical to standard costly punishment (no explicit punishment tokens), but functions similarly by allowing players to block low contributions at a cost. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, continuous-contribution games without chat, with full feedback, and a review/veto stage after pledges.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2020). The impact of peer ratings on cooperation: The role of information and cost of rating. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, adding a mandatory, costless peer rating stage (where players rate each other's contributions on a 0-10 scale, with no monetary consequences) increases group contributions and efficiency relative to a baseline with no rating. This effect is robust to whether rating information is private (only the recipient sees their ratings) or public (all see everyone's ratings). When a small cost (1 token per peer rated) is introduced for assigning ratings, most players stop rating, and the effect on contributions and efficiency vanishes. The study provides direct evidence that non-monetary, payoff-irrelevant peer approval can foster cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, but only if it is costless to provide.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costless, non-monetary peer approval mechanism (rating) in a standard repeated public goods game increases efficiency, even when ratings have no direct payoff consequences and are anonymous. The effect is robust to whether rating information is private or public. However, even a small cost to rating eliminates the effect. For prediction, the presence of a costless, mandatory, non-monetary peer approval mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control. If rating is costly, no efficiency gain should be expected. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the only sanction/reward is social approval, not monetary punishment or reward.

- source: Falvey, R., Lane, T., & Luckraz, S. (2025). On a mechanism that improves efficiency and reduces inequality in voluntary contribution games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

- source: Oechssler, J., Reischmann, A., & Sofianos, A. (2022). The conditional contribution mechanism for repeated public goods-The general case. *Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The CCM leads to much higher efficiency (as measured by average group contributions and the share of Pareto optimal outcomes) than the standard VCM in repeated public goods games with complete information. The CCM achieves about 78% Pareto optimal outcomes, compared to 24% for the VCM. The SCCM (one-message version) and VCMC (VCM with non-binding communication) perform between the two, with SCCM catching up to CCM in later periods. Under incomplete information, both CCM and VCM achieve similar, moderate efficiency, and the difference is not significant. The risk of being a 'sucker' (earning less than the endowment) is much lower in CCM than in VCM. The study provides strong evidence that the CCM can sustain high efficiency in repeated public goods games without requiring knowledge of others' preferences, but does not test any punishment or sanctioning treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated public goods games with mechanisms that allow conditional contributions, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. The experimental design and outcome measures are directly relevant to efficiency prediction, but since no punishment is present in any treatment, the paper cannot inform the effect of enabling punishment. It does, however, provide a strong benchmark for efficiency in control games without punishment, especially for games with 5 players, 60 rounds, continuous contributions, and no communication or punishment. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper can only inform the baseline (control) efficiency, not the treatment effect of punishment.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.
