- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

- source: Engel, C., & Zhurakhovska, L. (2017). You Are in Charge: Experimentally Testing the Motivating Power of Holding a Judicial Office. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Authorities (randomly assigned, with no personal stake in group outcomes) are willing to incur costs to punish free riders in a repeated linear public goods game. This punishment leads to higher contributions and higher payoffs for active participants than the Nash equilibrium (no contribution, no punishment). The effect is robust to framing the authority as a public official or judge, which further reduces the influence of personal preferences on punishment. Announcing punishment policies, election of judges, and experience all modulate the targeting and sensitivity of punishment, but the basic effect—punishment increases cooperation and group payoffs—remains. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), but group profit is reported and is higher than in the absence of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized punishment (by a non-beneficiary authority) in a repeated linear public goods game increases group payoffs and efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across variations in authority framing, policy announcement, election, and experience. The design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, full information on others' actions and punishment) are all specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the social optimum, so the effect must be inferred from reported mean earnings and profit.

- source: Park, C. (2022). Network Characteristic Control of Social Dilemmas in a Public Good Game: Numerical Simulation of Agent-Based Nonlinear Dynamics. *Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary PGGs, the average payoff and the prevalence of cooperation depend strongly on the participation cost, the incentive (interest rate), mutation rate, and the structure of the social network (network density, eigenvector centrality). High participation costs or low incentives lead to nonparticipation and lower average payoffs. High network density can mediate the effects of mutation, sometimes stabilizing cooperation. The model does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, so it does not address the effects of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs or related environments. It is useful for understanding how network structure, mutation, and incentive parameters affect efficiency and cooperation in PGGs without punishment. It cannot be used to predict the effect of enabling punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is modeled or analyzed.

- source: Janssen, M. A. (2015). A behavioral perspective on the governance of common resources. *Current Opinion in Environmental Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The paper finds that, contrary to the predictions of standard economic theory, groups can achieve higher efficiency and avoid the tragedy of the commons when allowed to communicate or implement costly punishment. Both lab and field experiments show that these mechanisms increase group earnings and reduce overuse of resources. The review also notes that centralized sanctioning mechanisms may be more effective and sustainable than peer punishment. However, the effectiveness of punishment and other interventions is context-specific, depending on factors such as group size, communication, trust, and cultural background. The paper also discusses the phenomenon of 'crowding out,' where externally imposed rules can sometimes reduce performance compared to voluntary agreements.
  prediction_guidance: This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments generally increases group efficiency or earnings, especially when compared to baseline games without punishment or communication. It also highlights that the effect of punishment is moderated by contextual factors such as group size, communication, and the nature of the sanctioning institution (peer vs. centralized). While the review does not provide quantitative estimates, it offers strong qualitative evidence that punishment mechanisms, when available, tend to improve efficiency outcomes, but the magnitude and sustainability of these effects depend on the specific game design and social context.

- source: Baker, S., & Choi, A. H. (2018). Reputation and Litigation: Why Costly Legal Sanctions Can Work Better than Reputational Sanctions. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that legal sanctions (punishment) can be more efficient than reputational sanctions (boycotts) in deterring undesirable behavior, even when both are costly. This is because legal sanctions have an inframarginal effect: increasing damages not only deters more lawsuits at the margin but also increases the penalty for existing lawsuits, improving deterrence at lower cost. Reputational sanctions lack this inframarginal effect, so increasing their severity is less efficient. The efficiency advantage of legal sanctions is greatest when courts are accurate and when litigation outcomes are public. When court error is high or when consumers have independent, reliable information about quality, reputational sanctions become more important. The model predicts that the optimal regime combines both types of sanctions, but leans toward legal sanctions unless litigation is highly error-prone or costly. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide experimental or empirical estimates, but it offers clear comparative statics and policy implications for the efficiency of punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (legal sanctions) in repeated moral hazard environments can increase efficiency relative to relying solely on reputational sanctions, especially when punishment is not too costly or error-prone and when outcomes are public. The inframarginal effect of punishment means that its efficiency benefit can be greater than that of reputational sanctions, which only deter at the margin. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment should increase efficiency, particularly when the punishment mechanism is accurate and visible. However, if punishment is highly error-prone or costly, or if reputational information is highly reliable, the efficiency gain from punishment may be reduced or even reversed. The model is not a direct PGG or lab experiment, so quantitative predictions should be made with caution, but the qualitative comparative statics are clear and relevant.

- source: Lim, I. S., & Capraro, V. (2022). A Synergy of Institutional Incentives and Networked Structures in Evolutionary Game Dynamics of Multiagent Systems. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment and network structure can interact synergistically to promote prosocial behavior and increase group efficiency (mean payoff) in a trust game. Specifically, the combination of even modest punishment and network structure can achieve full cooperation and maximal efficiency in parameter regions where neither mechanism alone would suffice. The required punishment level to achieve full cooperation is lower in structured populations than in well-mixed populations. The mean payoff is maximized at the threshold punishment level that induces full cooperation; increasing punishment beyond this point does not further increase efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of maintaining the incentive system. The paper provides explicit analytical boundaries for these effects as a function of the trust return (r), network degree (k), and punishment size (p).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a networked trust game (and by extension, in adjacent public-goods-like games) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is not too costly and the network structure is not too sparse. The results suggest that the efficiency gain from punishment is greater in structured populations than in well-mixed ones, and that the combination of network structure and punishment can achieve high efficiency at lower punishment levels than punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the network structure (degree), the effectiveness and cost of punishment, and the underlying return to cooperation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games but are likely to transfer to public goods games with similar strategic structure.

- source: Wang, C. Y., & Cui, W. F. (2022). Supervision for the Public Health Services for Older Adults Under the Background of Government Purchasing: An Evolutionary Game Analysis Framework. *Frontiers in Public Health*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a principal-agent setting with information asymmetry, static punishment or reward mechanisms are insufficient to ensure full compliance (self-discipline) by service providers. Dynamic mechanisms, where the intensity of punishment or reward depends on the observed behavior, can stabilize higher levels of self-discipline. Specifically, higher punishment intensity increases the proportion of self-discipline, while higher reward intensity can decrease it due to moral hazard and adverse selection. The model is theoretical and does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the results suggest that dynamic sanctioning mechanisms are more effective at promoting compliance than static ones.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (or self-discipline) depends on the design of the sanctioning mechanism, particularly whether it is static or dynamic. While the model is not a public goods game per se, it is structurally similar in that it models a social dilemma with potential for free-riding and the use of sanctions. The findings suggest that, in analogous PGG-like environments, dynamic punishment mechanisms are likely to be more effective at increasing cooperation than static ones, and that increasing punishment intensity generally increases compliance. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, so its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. It is most useful for understanding the qualitative effects of punishment design and intensity on compliance behavior.

- source: Jiang, X. H., & Zheng, W. Q. (2024). Dynamic strategies for collaborative governance of rural environments: a simulation study on the most effective mechanisms. *Environment Development and Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that under a static reward and punishment mechanism (SRPM), the system does not reach a stable equilibrium; strategies of all parties fluctuate periodically. Under a dynamic reward and punishment mechanism (DRPM), the system converges to a stable mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE), interpreted as an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Increasing the upper limit of punishments for illegal emissions by village enterprises significantly increases the probability of legal emissions and reduces the need for strict government regulation and active supervision by villagers. Increasing the upper limit of rewards for villagers' active supervision increases their supervision probability but may reduce legal compliance by enterprises and government regulatory effort. The DRPM is recommended for more effective and stable governance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that dynamic, behavior-dependent punishment mechanisms (analogous to more flexible or responsive punishment rules in public goods games) can stabilize cooperative behavior (legal emissions) and reduce the need for costly monitoring and enforcement. However, the model is not a standard public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The main outcomes are strategy probabilities, not payoffs. The findings suggest that increasing punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech) and using dynamic punishment rules may improve stability and compliance, but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The results are most relevant for understanding how punishment design (cost, tech, dynamic vs static) may affect behavioral outcomes in multi-agent governance games.

- source: Li, J. Y., Wu, W. H., Li, Z. Z., Wang, W. X., & Zhang, B. Y. (2023). Data-driven evolutionary game models for the spread of fairness and cooperation in heterogeneous networks. *Frontiers in Psychiatry*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment, when amplified by network heterogeneity and increased inequality aversion (e.g., via oxytocin), can spread through a network and promote higher levels of fairness and cooperation. This effect is observed in both the ultimatum game and the two-stage prisoner's dilemma with punishment, but not in the trust game, where the absence of punishment means that increased trust does not diffuse. The models fit experimental data well for behavioral outcomes (offers, cooperation rates, punishment rates), but do not directly analyze or report group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms are more effective than reward/trust mechanisms for promoting prosocial behavior in networks.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling costly punishment in networked social dilemmas (such as public goods or prisoner's dilemma variants) increases cooperation and fairness, especially when network heterogeneity allows central nodes to amplify the effect. However, because the paper is theoretical and focuses on behavioral outcomes (not efficiency or group payoff), it does not provide direct quantitative evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The models suggest that punishment is more effective than reward in promoting prosocial outcomes, but efficiency implications must be inferred from increased cooperation and fairness, not directly measured. The paper is most useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators (e.g., network structure, inequality aversion), but less so for direct efficiency prediction.

  findings: The paper finds that severe government penalties for non-compliance (punishment), sufficient incentives, and reduced costs for producers to build traceability systems are key to achieving stable cooperation among stakeholders. Depending on the relative size of these parameters, the system can stabilize in different equilibria: (Not regulate, Not build, Satisfied), (Regulate, Not build, Satisfied), or (Regulate, Build, Satisfied). The presence and strength of punishment (penalties) are critical in shifting the equilibrium toward higher compliance by producers. However, the outcomes are reported as stable strategy profiles, not as explicit efficiency or group payoff measures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence and severity of punishment (penalties) can shift the equilibrium from non-compliance to compliance in a multi-actor regulatory game. However, because the model does not report group efficiency or explicit payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency changes due to punishment in public-goods-like games. The findings suggest that increasing punishment can increase compliance, but the translation to efficiency or group payoff is not quantified. The model structure and parameter dependencies may inform qualitative expectations about the effect of punishment in similar multi-actor regulatory environments.

- source: Qirko, H. (2020). Altruistic punishment in modern intentional communities. *Interaction Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that, in modern intentional communities, free-riding is often acknowledged as a problem and most communities have rules or norms to address it. However, only about half of the communities report actually enforcing these rules through third-party punishment, and even then, enforcement is context-dependent and often flexible. The data do not support the existence of a strong, inherited predisposition to engage in costly third-party punishment. Instead, punishment behavior appears to be shaped by contextual, self-maximizing considerations. No direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or payoff are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It suggests that, in real-world cooperative groups with explicit rules and opportunities for punishment, actual use of costly third-party punishment is infrequent and context-dependent. This implies that the presence of punishment mechanisms does not guarantee their use or an increase in efficiency, especially outside of laboratory or game-like settings. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings are most useful for understanding the limits of external validity for lab-based punishment effects and for highlighting the importance of contextual moderators.
