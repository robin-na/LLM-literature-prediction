- source: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C. (2014). Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In reputation-based helping games (without punishment), cognitive disturbance (superfluous words) reduces cooperation and lowers group payoffs, indicating that efficiency is sensitive to cognitive constraints. In reputation-based punishment games, disturbance increases antisocial punishment and reduces punishment of defectors, but does not significantly change overall cooperation or mean final payoffs. The link between reputation and payoff is weakened under disturbance. The study highlights that experimental outcomes in reputation and punishment games are highly sensitive to environmental and cognitive factors, and that the expected efficiency gains from punishment or reputation mechanisms may not materialize under cognitive load.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effects of punishment and reputation mechanisms in indirect reciprocity games are sensitive to cognitive context. For prediction tasks, it suggests that efficiency gains from punishment (or reputation) may be undermined or nullified if cognitive load is high, even when game design parameters are otherwise favorable. The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Larson, J. M. (2016). The evolutionary advantage of limited network knowledge. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with gossip-based punishment, the ability to sustain full cooperation (and thus maximize group efficiency/fitness) depends on both the structure of the social network and the precision of individuals' knowledge about that structure. When all individuals have precise knowledge of the network, it is harder to sustain full cooperation because some individuals can be identified as weakly connected and thus are tempting targets for defection. When individuals have only coarse knowledge (e.g., know only the average neighborhood size), it is easier to sustain full cooperation, as targeting is not possible. The minimum discount factor required to sustain cooperation is lower under coarse knowledge than under precise knowledge, making full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) more robust. The model thus predicts that limited network knowledge facilitates the enforcement of cooperation through punishment, leading to higher group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the information structure—specifically, the precision of network knowledge. In environments where individuals have only coarse knowledge of the network, punishment is more effective at sustaining full cooperation and thus maximizing efficiency. If a public goods game or similar environment allows only limited information about the network or about others' positions, the model predicts a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency. Conversely, if players have precise knowledge of the network, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency may be reduced. The paper does not provide direct empirical estimates but offers clear theoretical mechanisms and comparative statics relevant to prediction.

- source: Güney, S., & Newell, B. R. (2013). Fairness overrides reputation: the importance of fairness considerations in altruistic cooperation. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiments show that rejection of unfair offers (costly punishment) in the Mini Ultimatum Game is primarily driven by fairness considerations, particularly the perceived intentions of the proposer. When intentions are not assessable, outcome fairness still motivates some punishment, but to a lesser degree. The opportunity for reputation building and future interaction (i.e., repeated, non-anonymous play) does not significantly increase costly punishment when fairness considerations are strong, but can increase punishment when intentions are ambiguous. The study does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the psychological drivers of costly punishment in ultimatum-like bargaining games are primarily fairness-based, not reputation-based, and that the presence of repeated interaction or reputation opportunities does not necessarily increase punishment or change behavioral outcomes when fairness motives are strong. However, because the study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment occurs, not its aggregate payoff consequences.

- source: Syi (2014). Hidden patterns of reciprocity. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper formalizes and compares four second-order action rules for direct reciprocity (e.g., Tit-for-tat, Contrite Tit-for-tat, Pavlov, Grim Trigger) and four second-order assessment rules for indirect reciprocity (e.g., Image Scoring, Simple Standing, Stern Judging, Shunning). It shows that these rules can be paired and exhibit similar patterns, especially in their relationship to punishment. The analysis highlights that moderate punishment policies (e.g., Simple Standing, Stern Judging) are most successful at maintaining cooperation, while extreme policies (e.g., Shunning, Grim Trigger) can undermine cooperation. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical evidence about efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual and mechanistic insight into how different punishment and reciprocity rules may affect cooperation in repeated social dilemmas, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants. Its value for the prediction task is limited to theoretical framing: it suggests that moderate, justified punishment rules are more likely to sustain cooperation (and thus potentially higher efficiency), while overly harsh or overly lenient rules may be less effective. However, these insights are not tied to specific game design dimensions or empirical efficiency data.

- source: Furuzono, Y., Yamakawa, T., Ohnishi, S., & Kaneko, T. (2013). Combined effects of social pressure from the inside and outside of fishing communities promote cooperation for stock management. *Ecological Research*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops a coupled socioeconomic-bioeconomic model of a fishing community, where individuals choose to cooperate with management or not, and non-cooperators experience psychological punishment ('social pressure') that depends on both internal (community-based) and external (outside community) sources. The model shows that when both internal and external social pressures are sufficiently strong, the system can reach full cooperation, resulting in sustainable fish stocks and maximum sustainable yield (MSY)—a proxy for group efficiency. If only one type of social pressure is strong, or both are weak, the system may stabilize at lower cooperation and lower stock levels. The model highlights the importance of both community relationships and external societal pressure in promoting cooperation for resource management.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence that psychological punishment mechanisms (social pressure) can increase group efficiency in common-pool resource settings analogous to public goods games, especially when both internal and external sources are present. While the model is not a standard PGG and does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, it shows that the presence and strength of punishment-like mechanisms (social pressure) are critical moderators of group outcomes. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling both internal and external punishment/sanction mechanisms is likely to increase efficiency, while the absence or weakness of either reduces the effect. The model also suggests that the effect of punishment is moderated by group size (player count), as social pressure per individual decreases with larger groups.

- source: Inaba, M., Takahashi, N., & Ohtsuki, H. (2016). Robustness of linkage strategy that leads to large-scale cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that a linkage strategy—where defectors in a social dilemma are excluded from indirect reciprocity benefits—can be evolutionarily stable and maintain high group efficiency, but only when there are implementation errors in the social dilemma and no perception errors. In the absence of errors, linkage and non-linkage strategies are neutral, allowing non-linkage strategies to drift in and eventually enable defectors to invade, collapsing cooperation and efficiency. Perception errors (misjudging others' actions) undermine the stability and efficiency of linkage strategies, as they disrupt the ability to assortatively cooperate and exclude defectors. The results highlight that the effectiveness of exclusionary sanctions (linkage) for maintaining efficiency is highly sensitive to the type and prevalence of errors in the system.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-like exclusion mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that exclusionary sanctions (linkage) can increase efficiency, but only if players can reliably observe and agree on others' actions (i.e., low perception error) and if there is some noise in implementation (so that linkage strategies can distinguish themselves from non-linkage cooperators). If perception errors are common, the efficiency benefit of punishment/exclusion is undermined. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is implemented as exclusion from future benefits rather than direct costly punishment, and where the information environment is well-specified. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with explicit costly punishment, but the mechanisms and moderators (error types, information) are highly relevant for prediction.

- source: Ghachem, M. (2016). The institution as a blunt instrument: Cooperation through imperfect observability. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that institutional tagging, even when imperfect, can substantially increase the efficiency (payoff) of cooperation in social dilemmas like the Prisoner's Dilemma. The key mechanism is that tagged cooperators are more likely to be chosen for interaction, leading to non-uniform interaction rates that favor cooperators. The model provides analytical conditions under which cooperation is favored by selection, is risk dominant, or is more abundant in the mutation-selection equilibrium. The positive effect on efficiency is robust to moderate costs of tag production and search. The effect is highly sensitive to the institution's error rates, especially the rate at which defectors are mistakenly tagged as cooperators. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, but institutional tagging serves a similar function by enabling partner selection based on observed tags.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional mechanisms which increase the observability of cooperation (even imperfectly) can increase group efficiency in repeated social dilemmas. While not a public goods game per se, the findings are adjacent and likely transferable to PGG-like settings where partner choice or reputation is possible. The model suggests that, in environments where institutions can credibly tag cooperators and individuals can use these tags to select partners, enabling such mechanisms will increase efficiency relative to a control with no tagging. The effect is stronger when the institution is more accurate (especially in avoiding false positives for defectors), when more of the population is screened, and when individuals can search more for partners. The results are less directly applicable to settings where punishment is the main intervention, but the underlying logic (institutional information enabling discrimination) is relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment-like or reputation-based interventions.

- source: Garay, J., Csiszár, V., & Móri, T. F. (2014). Under multilevel selection: When shall you be neither spiteful nor envious?. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in structured populations with multilevel selection, the evolutionary stability of strategies depends on the relative strength of group-level survival effects versus within-group competition. When group survival (due to predation or intergroup conflict) is highly sensitive to group size (i.e., total fecundity), strategies that maximize group fecundity (neutral or donator) are favored, leading to higher group efficiency. When group-level effects are weak, spiteful or envious strategies can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The model shows that increasing the benefit of donation or the cost of spite/envious behavior further promotes efficient, cooperative strategies. The results are robust to parameter changes and apply to both predation and intergroup conflict scenarios.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in environments where group-level survival or payoff is highly sensitive to group size or total contribution, mechanisms that increase group efficiency (such as donation or cooperation) will be favored over spiteful or envious (punitive) strategies. While the model does not directly implement a public goods game with explicit punishment, it is adjacent: 'spiteful' and 'envious' strategies are analogous to costly punishment, and 'donator' is analogous to cooperation or reward. The findings suggest that increasing the importance of group-level outcomes (e.g., by increasing the marginal per-capita return or the impact of group payoff on survival) will make punishment less stable and cooperation more stable, thus increasing efficiency. However, the model is not a direct PGG and does not include explicit punishment technology or cost parameters as in standard PGG experiments.
