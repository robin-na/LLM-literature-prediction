- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Powers, S. T. (2018). The Institutional Approach for Modeling the Evolution of Human Societies. *Artificial Life*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Jiao, Y. H., Chen, T., & Chen, Q. (2020). Probabilistic punishment and reward under rule of trust-based decision-making in continuous public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing probabilistic punishment and reward (i.e., punish/reward with probability rho < 1) into a continuous PGG can maintain high levels of cooperation and mean payoff (efficiency), even when the cost of punishment/reward is high. Traditional (always executed) punishment/reward leads to collapse of cooperation and low efficiency when costs are high. There is an optimal execution probability (rho ≈ 0.6) and moderate fine/bonus size (f = r = 1) that maximize mean payoff and cooperation, saving about 40% of the cost compared to always executing punishment/reward. If either the execution probability or the fine/bonus is too low, cooperation and efficiency remain low. The model shows that probabilistic execution is robust to high costs and can avoid the collapse of cooperation seen in traditional models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in continuous PGGs on structured populations, enabling punishment (and reward) with a moderate probability (rather than always) can increase group efficiency compared to both no punishment and always-on punishment, especially when costs are high. The effect is robust across a range of synergy factors (mpcr), costs, and fine/bonus sizes. For prediction, the presence of probabilistic punishment (with moderate execution probability and moderate cost/fine) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to the control (no punishment) and to traditional always-on punishment, particularly when punishment/reward costs are nontrivial. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the presence and cost of punishment, the effectiveness of punishment, and whether execution is probabilistic or deterministic.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary advantage (interpreted as efficiency in promoting cooperation and group payoff) of peer punishment versus social exclusion in PGGs is not fixed, but depends on the intensity of selection (rationality), the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, and whether participation is compulsory or voluntary. Under weak selection, peer punishment can be favored if it is efficient or exclusion is costly. Under strong selection, social exclusion with low cost dominates. The presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not change the condition for which punishment is favored, but reduces the difference in their abundances. The rank between peer punishment and social exclusion can change as rationality changes, and this sensitivity is mapped for a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGGs, especially regarding how the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, group size, and rationality (selection intensity) interact. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive or negative depending on whether punishment is efficient and/or exclusion is costly, and on the rationality of agents. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, theoretical PGGs with the specified parameters. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is conditional on the cost/benefit structure and the rationality of players, and that the presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not fundamentally alter the ranking condition but can affect the magnitude of effects.

- source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in a population with probabilistic corrupt enforcers and violators. *Mathematical Models & Methods in Applied Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. If the expected loss equals the bribe, the system exhibits neutral cycles (limit cycles) with periodic oscillations in cooperation and efficiency. If the expected loss exceeds the bribe, the system can either converge to all-defection (low efficiency) or to a heteroclinic cycle with cyclic dominance, which may still allow for periods of cooperation. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings. The findings are directly informative for mapping game design parameters (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, bribe size, probability of corruption) to expected efficiency outcomes.
