- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Pi, J. X., Yang, G. H., & Yang, H. (2022). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in N-person snowdrift games with peer punishment and individual disguise. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Wang, C. Q., & Huang, C. C. (2022). Between local and global strategy updating in public goods game. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing the option for costly punishment in a repeated public goods game increases group contributions and group payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline. This effect holds both when sanctions are observable (feedback given immediately) and when sanctions are unobservable (feedback delayed until the end). The mechanism is that subjects expect free riders to be punished and adjust their behavior to avoid being punished, even when they do not observe sanctions in real time. Sanctioning is mainly non-strategic and norm-driven, targeting free riders. The presence of punishment stabilizes cooperation and prevents the unraveling of contributions, with some groups achieving high cooperation and others not. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency (as measured by group payoffs and contributions as a fraction of maximum) is positive.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to whether punishment is observable or not during play, suggesting that the mere possibility of punishment, and the associated social norm, is sufficient to increase cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment to a repeated linear PGG with 4 players, 10 rounds, and MPCR=0.4 will increase efficiency, even if punishment is not immediately observable. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

- source: Pancotto, F., Righi, S., & Takács, K. (2023). Voluntary play increases cooperation in the presence of punishment: a lab in the field experiment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiments show that punishment increases contributions in public goods games, both when participation is compulsory and when it is optional. The effect is strongest when participation is compulsory and punishment is available. Optional participation (opt-out) alone does not significantly increase contributions compared to compulsory participation. Loners (those who opt out) contribute less when forced to participate, but are not responsible for anti-social punishment. Instead, low contributors (free-riders) are the main source of anti-social punishment. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the contribution data suggest that punishment increases group welfare, especially in compulsory settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in discrete public goods games with 4 players, 1:3 punishment technology, and no communication, enabling punishment increases contributions, especially when participation is compulsory. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions strongly suggests that efficiency would also increase with punishment, particularly in compulsory games. The design dimensions are well specified, allowing for mapping to prediction tasks, but the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff data means that predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes. The results are most informative for settings with similar group size, discrete contributions, and punishment technology.

- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Effects of majority-vote reward mechanism on cooperation: a public good experimental study. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The majority-vote reward mechanism significantly increases group efficiency and cooperation compared to both peer reward and no reward, in both homogeneous and heterogeneous MPCR settings. Peer reward increases efficiency and cooperation only in homogeneous groups. In heterogeneous groups, only the majority-vote reward mechanism leads to a significant increase in efficiency. The efficiency gains are due to higher contributions and more willingness to pay reward costs under the majority-vote mechanism. The paper provides direct evidence on efficiency outcomes (Table 6), showing that the majority-vote reward mechanism achieves the highest efficiency among the tested mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players, 6 rounds per condition, continuous contribution, no chat, and full information, adding a majority-vote reward mechanism (with a 1:3 cost-to-reward ratio) increases efficiency relative to the control (no reward) and peer reward conditions. Peer reward also increases efficiency in homogeneous groups, but not in heterogeneous groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially regarding the presence and type of reward mechanism, MPCR homogeneity, and the absence of punishment. The results suggest that, in the absence of punishment, majority-vote reward is a robust way to increase efficiency, especially when group members have heterogeneous returns from the public good.

- source: Yang, Q. X., & Yang, Y. L. (2024). A social monitoring mechanism for third-party judges promotes cooperation in evolutionary games. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that in repeated PGGs without punishment or reward, cooperation and group payoff decline over time, consistent with previous literature. The authors reconstruct a micro-dynamic model based on proportional changes in expected payoff, showing that players' willingness to cooperate depends on their current strategy and local cooperation levels. The model predicts the observed decline in cooperation and group payoff. The paper also documents a 'restart effect' in two-stage games, where cooperation temporarily increases at the transition point. The findings provide a framework for predicting the evolution of cooperation and group payoff in repeated PGGs without external interventions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on baseline efficiency and group payoff dynamics in repeated PGGs without punishment or reward. It is directly relevant for predicting control (no-punishment) efficiency as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and information structure. However, it does not provide any evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment-related variables. Thus, it is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it is highly informative for modeling the baseline (control) case.

- source: Jiang, L. L., Chen, Z., Perc, M., Wang, Z., Kurths, J., & Moreno, Y. (2023). Deterrence through punishment can resolve collective risk dilemmas in carbon emission games. *Chaos*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment simulating a collective-risk public goods game (carbon emission game), costly punishment of free-riders (with fines imposed probabilistically and costs shared by all group members) increases the probability that groups reach the collective target, but only when the punishment risk is high (p > 0.8). At low punishment risk, deterrence is weak, and group failure rates remain high. Larger groups are less likely to succeed at any given punishment risk, requiring higher punishment risk for similar deterrence. The introduction of punishment shifts most players to fair-share contributions, reducing both free-riding and maximal altruism. The effect of punishment on group success (a proxy for efficiency) is robust to different loss probabilities. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the probability of reaching the target and average investment are closely related and show a positive effect of punishment when sufficiently strong.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in collective-risk public goods games with a threshold target and probabilistic costly punishment, enabling punishment increases group success rates (and thus efficiency) only when the punishment risk is high. The effect is moderated by group size (larger groups require higher punishment risk) and is robust to different loss probabilities. For prediction, the presence and strength of punishment (punishment risk, fine size, cost structure), group size, and the absence of communication are key moderators. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low group success rates, especially as group size increases. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is strong and credible, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or infrequent.

- source: Shuvo, M. F. R., & Kabir, K. M. A. (2024). Exploring cyclic dominance in rock-paper-scissor strategies within the Optional Prisoner's Dilemma: A quantitative analysis with environmental feedback. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment increases cooperation in one-shot, three-person PD games, both when imposed exogenously (costless) and endogenously (costly to the punisher). The effect of punishment is decomposed into a direct effect (altering incentives for the punished individual) and an indirect effect (raising expectations that others will cooperate, thus encouraging conditional cooperators). Both effects are of similar magnitude. In the endogenous punishment condition, the cost of punishment is small relative to the benefit from increased cooperation, resulting in a net positive benefit. However, the main outcome is contribution, not group efficiency or total payoff, though some calculations of net benefit are provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (even when costly to the punisher) increases cooperation in public-goods-like environments, and that the effect is due to both direct and indirect mechanisms. While efficiency (as defined by group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported, the paper's calculations of net benefit and cost-effectiveness suggest that punishment increases group welfare. The design is a one-shot, three-player, continuous-contribution PD with and without punishment, and with both exogenous and endogenous punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when both direct and indirect effects are present, and when punishment is not prohibitively costly. However, the lack of direct efficiency reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution and net benefit data.

- source: Li, Y. Z., Luo, J., Niu, H., & Ye, H. (2023). When punishers might be loved: fourth-party choices and third-party punishment in a delegation game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the paper shows: (1) Helpers are more trusted and preferred as partners in trust games, but punishers are more likely to be offered higher amounts in ultimatum games due to their deterrence signal, resulting in higher actual payoffs for punishers. (2) In a delegation game, when the proposer's knowledge of the delegate's punishment history is made explicit, fourth-party players prefer to select punishers as their delegates, and this choice leads to higher expected payoffs for both the delegate and the principal. Thus, punishment increases efficiency/payoff in environments where its deterrence is visible and relevant.

The effect is context-dependent: punishment is less effective in trust-based settings but more effective in settings where deterrence is valuable and observable. For prediction tasks, the presence of a visible punishment mechanism, the structure of the game (ultimatum/delegation vs. trust), and the information available to other players are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is lower than with punishment in deterrence-relevant settings.

- source: Herne, K., Lappalainen, O., Setälä, M., & Ylisalo, J. (2022). Accountability as a Warrant for Trust: An Experiment on Sanctions and Justifications in a Trust Game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment used a 2x2 design (punishment/no punishment x justification/no justification) in a three-player trust game (two senders, one responder, repeated stranger matching, 6 rounds). The opportunity for senders to punish responders (at a cost: 1 point spent reduces responder's payoff by 3) did not significantly increase the amounts sent or returned, nor did it improve group efficiency. In fact, net earnings for responders were lower in punishment treatments due to the cost of punishment, and group efficiency was not improved. Justification requirements (responders must explain their choices) increased contributions and returns, but this effect was not robust when controlling for the amount received. The combination of punishment and justification did not outperform justification alone in terms of efficiency. The main effect of punishment was to reduce responder payoffs via costly sanctions, not to increase group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a trust-game-like environment with costly punishment (punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment does not increase group efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of punishment. The design is adjacent to public goods games (not a standard PGG, but a trust game with two senders and one responder, continuous contributions, no communication, 6 rounds, known horizon, no rewards). The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be lower than or similar to the control (no punishment), unless the punishment is costless or much more effective. The paper also shows that justification requirements can increase contributions, but this does not translate into higher efficiency when punishment is present. Thus, for prediction, enabling costly punishment in similar trust or PGG-adjacent games is likely to reduce or not improve efficiency relative to control.

- source: Lec, F. L., Matthey, A., & Rydval, O. (2023). Punishing the weakest link - Voluntary sanctions and efficient coordination in the minimum effort game. *Theory and Decision*.

  findings: The experiment demonstrates that voluntary costly punishment opportunities in a repeated minimum effort game (with 8 players per group, 16 rounds, and a two-stage design) significantly increase both effort levels and group efficiency compared to a baseline with no punishment and a treatment with costless disapproval (communication). In the punishment treatment, after an initial drop, average group payoff (net of punishment costs) rises and eventually exceeds the control, with about a third of groups reaching or approaching the Pareto-optimal equilibrium. Punishment is mainly directed at minimum-effort players, and its use is frequent. Disapproval/communication alone produces only a temporary or weak improvement.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated weakest-link (minimum effort) games with large groups and a history of inefficient coordination, enabling voluntary costly punishment leads to a robust and lasting increase in group efficiency, even after accounting for the direct costs of punishment. The effect is stronger and more persistent than that of costless communication. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, holding control efficiency and other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment (with a 2:1 fine-to-cost ratio and anonymous assignment) will increase average efficiency, especially in settings with multiple rounds, large groups, and no prior communication. The initial rounds after introducing punishment may see a drop in net efficiency, but this reverses as groups coordinate on higher efforts. The results are most directly applicable to coordination games with similar structure, but provide adjacent evidence for public goods games.

For prediction tasks, the presence of observable, flexible, and potentially discriminatory punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to control conditions without such punishment.
