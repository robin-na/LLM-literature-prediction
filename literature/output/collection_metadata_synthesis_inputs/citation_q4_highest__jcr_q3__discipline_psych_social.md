- source: Gintis, H. (2003). Solving the puzzle of prosociality. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment by strong reciprocators can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise unravel due to self-interest. Theoretical results show that with sufficient oblique cultural transmission and a non-trivial fraction of punishers, full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) can be a stable equilibrium. The paper also reviews experimental evidence showing that when punishment is allowed, group payoffs and cooperation rates are significantly higher than when punishment is not allowed. The model further predicts that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining efficiency depends on the cost and impact of punishment, the fraction of punishers, and the strength of cultural transmission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control games without punishment, provided that punishment is not prohibitively costly and that a sufficient fraction of players are willing to punish defectors. The model and supporting evidence suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of game parameters, including group size and number of rounds, as long as punishment is effective and not too costly. The paper also highlights the importance of cultural and institutional context (e.g., norm internalization, oblique transmission) in sustaining prosocial behavior and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defection.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2014). Optimal distribution of incentives for public cooperation in heterogeneous interaction environments. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Seip, E. C., Van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2014). Anger motivates costly punishment of unfair behavior. *Motivation and Emotion*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across three laboratory experiments, the paper finds that (1) participants assign more costly punishment to less cooperative (unfair) players in a PGG, (2) anger mediates the relationship between perceived unfairness and punishment in both PGG and STG settings, and (3) experimentally induced anger increases costly punishment of unfair players in a subsequent STG. The studies do not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment is motivated by anger in PGG and STG settings, and that non-cooperation reliably triggers costly punishment. However, it does not provide any data or analysis on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, which are the primary outcomes for the downstream prediction task. Therefore, while the paper supports the behavioral mechanism by which punishment is enacted, it does not inform whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency under any game design dimension. The design details (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are reported and may be useful for mapping to other studies, but no efficiency outcomes are available.

- source: Benard, S. (2012). Cohesion from Conflict: Does Intergroup Conflict Motivate Intragroup Norm Enforcement and Support for Centralized Leadership?. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that intergroup conflict increases the likelihood that group members will punish noncontributors (enforce norms) and support centralized leadership, but only when the outgroup is actively competing (i.e., high outgroup contribution). Conflict increases individual contributions to the group regardless of outgroup competitiveness. The effect of punishment is measured as the frequency of norm enforcement, not as its impact on group efficiency or total payoff. The presence of punishment does not appear to be experimentally manipulated as an on/off treatment; rather, punishment is always available, and the focus is on when and how much it is used. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about when group members are more likely to use costly punishment in public-goods-like games, specifically under intergroup conflict and high outgroup competition. However, it does not provide data on the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it compare efficiency with and without punishment. Thus, it is not directly informative for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, given game design dimensions and control efficiency. It does suggest that punishment is more likely to be used in conflictual, competitive intergroup settings, but does not address whether this improves or reduces group efficiency.

- source: Martin, J. M., Gonzalez, C., Juvina, I., & Lebiere, C. (2014). A Description-Experience Gap in Social Interactions: Information about Interdependence and Its Effects on Cooperation. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that providing more information about interdependence in a two-player, 200-round IPD increases both mutual cooperation and joint payoffs (efficiency). Four information conditions were tested: No-Info (players unaware of the other), Min-Info (aware of other but cannot see their actions), Mid-Info (can see other's actions and outcomes), and Max-Info (can also see the full payoff matrix). Joint performance (sum of points) was lowest in No-Info and Min-Info, higher in Mid-Info, and highest in Max-Info. The effect of information on performance is fully mediated by mutual cooperation. Punishment (defection after defection) is high in all conditions and does not vary with information. The results suggest that awareness and transparency of interdependence are key to achieving higher efficiency in repeated social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated dyadic social dilemmas structurally similar to the IPD, increasing the amount of information about interdependence (especially showing others' actions and the payoff matrix) leads to higher efficiency (joint payoff). For prediction, if a public-goods-like game allows players to see others' actions and/or the payoff structure, one should expect higher efficiency than in games where such information is hidden. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, simultaneous-move games without explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, but the mechanisms (reciprocation, not punishment) may generalize to other settings. The paper does not manipulate punishment cost or technology, nor does it include explicit costly punishment or reward options, so its direct predictive value is limited for games with those features.

- source: Rilling, J. K., Goldsmith, D. R., Glenn, A. L., Jairam, M. R., Elfenbein, H. A., Dagenais, J. E., Murdock, C. D., & Pagnoni, G. (2008). The neural correlates of the affective response to unreciprocated cooperation. *Neuropsychologia*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that unreciprocated cooperation (being defected on after cooperating) in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma elicits strong negative emotions and increased neural activity in regions associated with aversive and social pain (anterior insula, hippocampus, amygdala). These neural responses are stronger when the unreciprocated cooperation follows a cooperative move by the subject, suggesting sensitivity to perceived partner intent. Functional connectivity between the anterior insula and lateral orbitofrontal cortex predicts whether the subject will defect in the next round. The paper does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods or Prisoner's Dilemma games. It does, however, provide evidence that unreciprocated cooperation (partner defection) triggers strong negative affect and neural responses that bias subjects toward defection in subsequent rounds. This suggests a psychological mechanism by which cooperation can unravel in the absence of punishment or sanctioning. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the idea that, without punishment, negative experiences with defectors can lead to reduced cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or payoff, nor does it test the effect of introducing punishment.

- source: Yamagishi, T., Terai, S., Kiyonari, T., Mifune, N., & Kanazawa, S. (2007). The social exchange heuristic - Managing errors in social exchange. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in one-shot PDGs is significantly higher when participants perceive themselves to be in an ongoing social exchange relationship (i.e., when the SEH is activated) compared to when they do not. In Experiment 1, participants who deliberated about the game and their partner before being assigned to a specific exchange relationship (delayed-play) cooperated less than those who made decisions immediately after being assigned a partner (immediate-play). In Experiment 2, participants who made their contribution decision after being told they were matched with a specific partner (partner-specified) contributed more than those who made their decision before being matched (partner-unspecified). The study attributes these effects to the activation of the SEH, which frames the game as an Assurance Game and increases conditional cooperation. No punishment, sanctioning, or efficiency outcomes are studied.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or group payoff/efficiency. However, it does provide informative evidence that cues of social exchange and perceived interdependence can substantially increase cooperation rates in dyadic social dilemmas. For prediction tasks, this suggests that psychological framing and the presence of cues indicating ongoing relationships may be important moderators of cooperation, but the paper does not address punishment or efficiency directly.

- source: MOLM, L. D. (1994). DEPENDENCE AND RISK - TRANSFORMING AND STRUCTURE OF SOCIAL-EXCHANGE. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that the risk inherent in social exchange relations (mutual dependence) can be reduced by structuring transactions as negotiated (joint decision, binding agreements) or as serially dependent (allowing for contingent action and trust development). Negotiated transactions most directly transform dependence into interdependence, reducing risk and making outcomes more predictable. Serially dependent transactions (such as repeated reciprocal exchanges) also reduce risk, but less so, and rely more on emergent trust and norms. Generalized exchange (indirect reciprocity) is riskier and less amenable to these risk-reducing mechanisms. The paper suggests that these structural features influence the likelihood of stable cooperation, trust, and solidarity, but does not provide empirical evidence or direct predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how structural features of social exchange (e.g., repeated interaction, negotiation, direct vs. generalized exchange) may moderate the risk of defection and the emergence of cooperation. While it does not address public goods games or punishment directly, it suggests that mechanisms which increase interdependence (such as repeated play or enforceable agreements) should improve group outcomes and stability. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that game designs with more interdependence, repeated interaction, or enforceable agreements are likely to see higher efficiency or more stable cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence. The relevance to punishment is indirect: punishment is mentioned as a possible influence tactic in negotiation, but not as a central mechanism.

- source: Berigan, N., & Irwin, K. (2011). Culture, Cooperation, and the General Welfare. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The main findings are that higher national individualism is associated with higher trust and greater active membership in charitable organizations, while lower individualism (i.e., higher collectivism) is associated with greater support for government welfare programs. Trust mediates these relationships. The study interprets these as different strategies for solving social dilemmas, but does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or any direct payoff-based outcome. The results are based on observational, cross-national survey data (World Values Survey) and multilevel modeling.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar laboratory environments. Its relevance is limited to the broader conceptualization of how cultures coordinate on different cooperation strategies (voluntary vs. enforced), and how trust mediates these preferences. There is no direct manipulation or measurement of punishment, sanctions, or efficiency, and no experimental or game-based data. Thus, it should not be weighted heavily for quantitative prediction of efficiency effects from punishment in PGG-like settings, but may be useful for contextual or theoretical framing.

- source: YAMAGISHI, T., & YAMAGISHI, M. (1994). TRUST AND COMMITMENT IN THE UNITED-STATES AND JAPAN. *Motivation and Emotion*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The main findings are that Americans have higher general trust and knowledge-based trust than Japanese, value reputation and honesty more, and are less likely to see utility in personal connections compared to Japanese respondents. The study supports a theoretical distinction between trust (as a cognitive bias in the face of social uncertainty) and assurance (as security based on committed relations). The results are based on self-report survey data and do not involve experimental games, punishment, or efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not report any experimental or payoff-based outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze game design dimensions such as punishment, player count, or MPCR. Its relevance is limited to theoretical discussion of trust, assurance, and reputation, which may inform background understanding of cooperation and social uncertainty, but it does not provide empirical evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Kuwabaaa, K., Willer, R., Macy, M. W., Mashima, R., Terri, S., & Yamagishi, T. (2007). Culture, identity, and structure in social exchange: A web-based trust experiment in the United States and Japan. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that Japanese participants are more likely to form durable, committed exchange relations and are more trustworthy within those relations, while Americans are more likely to trust and interact with strangers. However, there is no significant difference in the amount entrusted (trust) between Americans and Japanese. The observed higher trustworthiness among Japanese is explained by their greater tendency to form committed relationships, not by culture per se. When controlling for relational commitment (mutuality, risk, provocation), nationality effects on trustworthiness disappear. The study does not include punishment or sanctioning, and does not directly analyze efficiency or group welfare outcomes. Total earnings are reported but not the focus of analysis.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. The game is a repeated dyadic trust game, not a public goods game or close variant. The findings are relevant for understanding how relational structure and partner choice affect trust and trustworthiness, but not for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The only payoff-related outcomes are total earnings and commitment concentration, which are not analyzed in relation to any punishment treatment. Thus, this paper should not be used as primary evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like games.

- source: Corgnet, B., Espín, A. M., & Hernán-González, R. (2015). The cognitive basis of social behavior: cognitive reflection overrides antisocial but not always prosocial motives. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab studies (US and Spain), individuals with higher CRT scores are more likely to make choices consistent with 'mild' altruism (increasing others' payoffs at low/no cost), while those with lower CRT scores are more likely to act out of egalitarian or spiteful motives. The studies use non-strategic, anonymous allocation tasks (not public goods or social dilemma games) and do not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are directly measured or reported. The findings suggest that cognitive reflection is associated with a specific pattern of social preferences, but do not inform about the effects of punishment or efficiency in public goods contexts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or efficiency/welfare outcomes, nor does it use a public goods or social dilemma design. The findings are about individual cognitive style and social motives in non-strategic allocation games. Therefore, it should not be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency in public goods games with or without punishment.

- source: Diekmann, A., Przepiorka, W., & Rauhut, H. (2015). Lifting the veil of ignorance: An experiment on the contagiousness of norm violations. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when subjects are informed about the extent of others' norm violations (cheating in a dice game), their own rate of norm violation increases in subsequent rounds, compared to a control group with no such information. This effect is robust to whether the information is about an external group or the current session. The effect is not due to punishment, as the design eliminates any possibility of sanctions. The effect is moderated by subjects' prior beliefs: underestimators of norm violations increase cheating when informed, while overestimators may decrease it. The study provides evidence for the contagiousness of norm violations via conditional norm compliance, but does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes in a public goods context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as there is no punishment, no group payoff, and no public good. The findings are relevant for understanding how information about norm violations can erode compliance, but not for the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and outcomes are adjacent to the prediction task, offering insight into norm dynamics but not into payoff-based outcomes or the role of sanctions.

- source: Simpson, B., & Willer, R. (2008). Altruism and indirect reciprocity: The interaction of person and situation in prosocial behavior. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The experiments show that prosocial behavior in dictator games is strongly moderated by both individual social value orientation and the presence of reputational incentives. Egoists increase giving when their actions are public and reputational benefits are possible, but give little in private. Altruists give at similar (high) levels in both public and private conditions. In indirect reciprocity decisions, participants reward others' prosociality more when it occurred in private (without reputational incentives) than in public, with egoists showing a stronger discounting effect. Altruists both give and reciprocate more overall. The study does not address efficiency, group payoff, or the effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not include punishment, sanctioning, or group cooperation dilemmas, nor does it report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. However, it provides informative evidence about the role of reputational incentives and individual heterogeneity in prosocial behavior, which may be relevant for understanding mechanisms in environments where reputation or indirect reciprocity is salient. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative, as it does not manipulate or report on punishment, efficiency, or public goods game structures.
