- source: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C. (2014). Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.

- source: Bell, A., Zhang, W., & Nou, K. (2016). Pesticide use and cooperative management of natural enemy habitat in a framed field experiment. *Agricultural Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot public goods game with three treatments: (1) Base (no punishment), (2) UCPun (conditional decision maker can be punished by an unconditional decision maker), and (3) CPun (conditional decision maker can punish an unconditional decision maker). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), but does lead to more sensitive punishment behavior. Responsibility effects (increased cooperation when given punishment power) are stronger than substitution effects. The overall effect of punishment on cooperation is positive, but the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment mechanisms affect conditional cooperation in a one-shot public goods game with four players, MPCR 0.4, and costless punishment. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings suggest that enabling punishment increases the responsiveness of cooperators to others' contributions and may increase overall cooperation, but also increases free riding. For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is likely to increase cooperation-related behaviors, but the net effect on efficiency is ambiguous due to the rise in free riders. The absence of direct efficiency analysis limits the ability to quantitatively predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency, but the behavioral mechanisms identified are informative for modeling.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that local, group-level sanctioning institutions are much more effective than global, population-level institutions at promoting successful collective action in a threshold public goods game with risk of collective loss. Local institutions increase the fraction of groups that reach the public good threshold (group achievement), especially when the perceived risk of failure is low. Global institutions are unstable and provide only marginal improvements. The effect is stronger in smaller groups. The model does not report efficiency or total payoff, but the results strongly suggest that local punishment institutions increase the likelihood of group success, which is closely related to efficiency in this context.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling local, group-level punishment institutions in threshold public goods games (with risk of collective loss) will increase the rate of successful group outcomes compared to no punishment or global punishment. While the model does not report efficiency or total payoff, the 'group achievement' outcome is a close proxy for efficiency in threshold settings. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is local, groups are small, and risk perception is low. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so its value is mainly in qualitative prediction and mechanism understanding.

  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in collective-risk social dilemma games is strongly affected by perceived risk, inequality, uncertainty about thresholds, intergenerational discounting, and the possibility of reputation-based rewards or punishments. High perceived risk, communication (pledges), intermediate targets, and reputation mechanisms can increase cooperation (i.e., the proportion of groups that avert catastrophe). The paper discusses the need for credible enforcement mechanisms (punishment) in real-world climate agreements but does not review experiments that directly implement or vary punishment in the game. The main findings are about behavioral cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the importance of enforcement (punishment) mechanisms for sustaining cooperation in public-goods-like threshold games, but does not provide direct experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff. It is useful for understanding moderators of cooperation and the conceptual role of punishment, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that credible punishment mechanisms are likely to be important, especially under uncertainty, but does not provide data or effect sizes.

- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

- source: Rommel, J. (2015). What can economic experiments tell us about institutional change in social-ecological systems?. *Environmental Science & Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that allowing third-party punishment of traders (who cause negative externalities) leads to a significant reduction in market entry rates compared to the baseline, especially for sellers. About 86% of observers are willing to punish, spending on average more than half their endowment to do so. The conditional entry treatment also reduces market entry, as about a quarter of participants condition their entry on others' behavior, reflecting norm conformity. The main outcome is the proportion of participants who choose to trade (i.e., cause the externality), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings show that punishment can enforce social norms and reduce harmful market behavior, but the study does not measure efficiency as defined in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of costly punishment reduces the frequency of norm-violating behavior (trading that causes negative externalities) in a large market setting. However, because the outcome is market entry rate (not group efficiency or total payoff), the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games. The punishment mechanism is effective at deterring harmful behavior, especially among sellers, and most observers are willing to punish at a cost. For prediction tasks, the main signal is that enabling punishment reduces the rate of norm violation, but the mapping to efficiency or group payoff in standard PGGs is indirect. The market structure, one-shot design, and externality to a third party differ from standard PGGs, so caution is needed in transferring quantitative effects.

- source: Zhang, Y., & van der Schaar, M. (2013). Robust Reputation Protocol Design for Online Communities: A Stochastic Stability Analysis. *Ieee Journal of Selected Topics in Signal Processing*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that in a repeated random matching game (asymmetric gift-giving, not a standard PGG), a reputation protocol with probabilistic punishment (parameterized by punishment probability beta) can induce full cooperation and achieve maximal efficiency (social welfare = b-c) in the long run, provided the discount factor and punishment strength are within certain bounds. If punishment is too weak or too strong, efficiency is lower. Theoretical analysis is supported by numerical simulations showing that the optimal protocol achieves much higher average social welfare than direct reciprocity (tit-for-tat) or less robust reputation schemes. The protocol's design is robust to stochastic perturbations and ensures convergence to the efficient equilibrium.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated random matching environments with binary contribution (gift-giving), the introduction of a well-calibrated reputation-based punishment protocol can move the system from zero efficiency (no cooperation) to full efficiency (all cooperate, social welfare maximized). The effect is robust to noise and initial conditions, provided the punishment probability is neither too low nor too high. While the game is not a standard PGG, the adjacent structure and explicit efficiency analysis make the results highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar environments. The findings suggest that, when punishment is enabled via a reputation protocol and properly tuned, efficiency can increase dramatically compared to the no-punishment baseline.

- source: Xu, J., Song, Y. B., & van der Schaar, M. (2014). Sharing in Networks of Strategic Agents. *Ieee Journal of Selected Topics in Signal Processing*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that distributed rating-based sanctioning protocols can fully or nearly fully restore efficiency in repeated network sharing games with strategic agents, provided certain incentive compatibility conditions are met. When the network's connectivity is not too high and agents' discount factors are sufficient, the protocol achieves the social optimum (PoA=1). As network connectivity increases or agents become more impatient, efficiency declines but remains higher than with direct reciprocity mechanisms. The protocol is robust to imperfect monitoring and can be tuned for dynamic networks. Theoretical and simulation results show that the protocol outperforms Tit-for-Tat and similar direct punishment schemes, especially in heterogeneous or asymmetric networks.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that distributed, rating-based punishment mechanisms can substantially increase efficiency in repeated networked cooperation environments, often achieving the social optimum. The effect is most pronounced when the network is not too highly connected and agents are sufficiently patient. The protocol's performance is robust to imperfect monitoring and network dynamics. For prediction tasks, the presence of a distributed, indirect punishment mechanism (ratings affecting future access) is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially compared to direct, bilateral punishment. The results are most directly applicable to networked repeated games with costly sharing, which are adjacent to but not identical to standard public goods games.
