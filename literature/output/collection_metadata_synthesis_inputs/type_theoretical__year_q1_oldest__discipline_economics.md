- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schmidt, K. M. (1999). A theory of fairness, competition, and cooperation. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model predicts that in standard public goods games without punishment, the equilibrium is typically full defection and low efficiency, matching empirical data. When a punishment stage is added (costly to the punisher, but more costly to the punished), the threat of punishment by inequity-averse players can sustain full cooperation and thus high efficiency, even if most players are selfish. The model provides explicit conditions under which this occurs, and shows that the presence of even a single sufficiently inequity-averse 'enforcer' can be enough to sustain efficient cooperation. The theory also predicts that the effect of punishment on efficiency is stronger when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is higher, and when the cost of punishment is lower.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, often dramatically, provided there is at least a small fraction of inequity-averse players. The model gives explicit conditions relating group size, mpcr, and punishment cost to the likelihood of efficient cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, for typical lab PGG designs, adding punishment will shift efficiency from low (near zero) to high (near one), unless punishment is extremely costly or the group is very large and inequity aversion is rare. The model also suggests that the marginal effect of punishment is moderated by mpcr and punishment cost.

- source: Kranz, S. (2010). Moral norms in a partly compliant society. *Games and Economic Behavior*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper provides a formal model showing that in public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment technology increases both contributions and group welfare (efficiency), as long as the punishment is sufficiently effective and the share of compliers is not too low. The model gives explicit formulas for the increase in contributions and welfare as a function of the marginal per capita return, punishment cost, and the share of compliers. It also shows that the effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is less costly, the marginal per capita return is higher, and the share of compliers is higher. The model's predictions are consistent with experimental findings that punishment increases efficiency in public goods games under many conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games and close variants. It provides explicit theoretical predictions for how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, as a function of game design parameters (player count, marginal per capita return, punishment cost/tech, etc.) and the baseline (control) efficiency. The model can be used to generate quantitative or qualitative predictions for the downstream task, especially in settings where the control game is a standard public goods game and the treatment adds a punishment option. The paper also clarifies the conditions under which punishment will or will not increase efficiency, depending on the share of compliers and the effectiveness/cost of punishment.

- source: Darcet, D., & Sornette, D. (2008). Quantitative determination of the level of cooperation in the presence of punishment in three public good experiments. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper proposes that the observed level of punishment in public goods and related games can be quantitatively predicted by a cost-benefit analysis rooted in evolutionary feedback selection. The theory is applied to three canonical games: the third-party punishment game, the ultimatum game, and the public goods game with punishment (as in Fehr & Gächter 2002). The predicted punishment propensities (k) closely match experimental data from these games. Agent-based simulations show that the predicted punishment level is an emergent property of evolutionary dynamics. The theory suggests that punishment is tuned to maximize group efficiency, but the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes as dependent variables.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a theoretical rationale for why punishment increases cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency in public goods games. It offers quantitative predictions for the level of punishment as a function of game parameters (e.g., group size, punishment efficiency, marginal per-capita return). However, it does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report new experimental data on group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation and likely efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly, but does not provide direct efficiency data for model training or calibration.

- source: Carpenter, J., Matthews, P., & Ong'ong'a, O. (2004). Why Punish? Social reciprocity and the enforcement of prosocial norms. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games is best explained by social reciprocity: individuals are motivated to punish norm violators (free riders) even when doing so is costly and does not improve their own material payoff, and even when the violator is outside their immediate group. Experimental and survey evidence shows that punishment is not solely driven by the desire to reduce payoff differentials, and that people continue to punish even when the cost of punishment exceeds the harm inflicted. The demand for punishment is relatively inelastic to price, and motivations are primarily normative. While the models present payoff calculations and show that punishment can stabilize cooperation under certain initial conditions, the empirical focus is on behavioral motivations and patterns rather than direct measures of group efficiency or total earnings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment mechanisms in public goods games can sustain higher levels of cooperation, but it does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff outcomes. The theoretical models show that, under certain conditions, populations with punishers can achieve higher average payoffs than those dominated by free riders, but these are model-based results rather than empirical efficiency measurements. The experimental and survey data are primarily about who punishes, why, and under what conditions, rather than about the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that punishment can support cooperation even when it is costly, and that motivations are primarily normative, but it does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, ability to punish outgroup) are discussed in the context of behavioral patterns, not efficiency outcomes.

- source: Gerber, A., & Wichardt, P. C. (2009). Providing public goods in the absence of strong institutions. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a voluntary deposit-commitment mechanism in public goods games can fully implement efficient contribution profiles as equilibrium outcomes, even in the absence of strong external enforcement or punishment institutions. The mechanism works by making non-contribution costly (loss of deposit) only if all players commit, thus aligning individual incentives with group welfare. Theoretical results show that, under this mechanism, the group can achieve payoffs and efficiency levels that strictly Pareto-dominate the standard Nash equilibrium of zero contribution. The mechanism is robust to various extensions, including repeated games and alternative uses of forfeited deposits.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that self-commitment mechanisms (such as refundable deposits) can substitute for traditional punishment in public goods games, leading to higher efficiency and group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in environments where such a deposit-commitment mechanism is available and properly designed, the efficiency with 'punishment' (here, self-sanctioning via deposit) can approach the social optimum, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the deposit mechanism is feasible and all players can observe and verify commitments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or parameterized predictions, but the qualitative direction is clear: enabling credible self-sanctioning or commitment mechanisms can dramatically improve efficiency in public goods games.

The effect is strongest at intermediate tolerance thresholds and when expulsion costs are high. The mechanism allows expellers to balance the cost of expulsion with the benefit of removing defectors, leading to higher and more stable cooperation levels. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined from the reported results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that tolerance-based expulsion mechanisms can increase cooperation rates in spatial public goods games, especially when the cost of expulsion is high and the tolerance threshold is optimally set. However, because the reported outcomes are cooperation levels rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects. The results suggest that enabling punishment (in the form of expulsion) is likely to increase cooperation, but the impact on efficiency is not directly quantified and may depend on the costliness of expulsion and the structure of the population. The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.

- source: Orr, S. W. (2001). The economics of shame in work groups: How mutual monitoring can decrease cooperation in teams. *Kyklos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds, through theoretical argument and reference to motivation crowding theory, that mutual monitoring and social sanctions (including social disapproval) in public-goods-like group incentive schemes can decrease cooperation among normatively motivated individuals due to crowding out of intrinsic motivation. The effect is non-linear: some monitoring may initially increase cooperation, but excessive or controlling monitoring leads to suspicion and a shift from normative to gain frames, reducing contributions. For individualistic types, monitoring may crowd in normative motivation and increase cooperation. The overall effect of mutual monitoring or social sanctions on group performance is thus frequency-dependent on the motivational composition of the group. The paper does not provide direct predictions or data on efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that in groups with many normatively motivated members, mutual monitoring may reduce cooperation and thus likely reduce efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical caution that enabling mutual monitoring or social sanctions (punishment) in public-goods-like environments may not always increase efficiency or cooperation, especially in groups with many normatively motivated members. The effect of punishment or monitoring is predicted to be non-linear and frequency-dependent: it may increase cooperation among individualists but decrease it among cooperators if perceived as controlling. For the prediction task, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and may depend on group composition and the perceived nature of monitoring. However, the paper does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on efficiency, so its value is mainly in highlighting possible negative or mixed effects of punishment mechanisms, especially social (rather than material) sanctions.

- source: Bergstrom, T. C. (2002). Evolution of social behavior: Individual and group selection. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (multiplayer prisoners' dilemmas), cooperation cannot be sustained in equilibrium under nonassortative (random) matching and individual selection; only defectors survive, leading to low group efficiency. However, cooperation and higher group payoffs can be sustained if (a) matching is assortative (e.g., kin selection, spatial structure, partner choice), (b) the game is not a prisoners' dilemma (e.g., stag hunt), or (c) the game is repeated and social norms (including punishment) are enforced. The paper discusses the theoretical role of punishment in repeated games as a means to sustain cooperation and efficient outcomes, but does not provide direct analysis or predictions about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical context for the importance of game structure (e.g., matching, repetition, group size, payoff parameters) in determining whether cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained in public goods games. It highlights that punishment can, in theory, support efficient cooperation in repeated games, but does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence about the magnitude or direction of punishment's effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of repeated interaction and enforceable punishment norms are key moderators, but does not provide parameter-level guidance for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in specific game designs.

- source: Benchekroun, H., & Van Long, N. (2008). The build-up of cooperative behavior among non-cooperative selfish agents. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with no punishment or sanctions, but where agents can condition their behavior on a public 'stock of cooperation' (social history), a continuum of Markovian Nash equilibria exist. These equilibria can support steady-state contribution and welfare levels above the static Nash equilibrium but below the social optimum. The social optimum cannot be stably achieved without punishment. The model provides explicit analytical characterizations of these equilibria and shows that the more patient the agents (lower discount rate), the closer the best equilibrium can approach the social optimum. The paper emphasizes that, unlike models with punishment, the highest sustainable efficiency is strictly less than the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games without punishment or sanctions, especially when social history or trust mechanisms are present. It provides strong theoretical evidence that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency can be improved above the Nash baseline if agents coordinate on social-history-based strategies, but the social optimum is not achievable. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (which is absent here) would allow efficiency to reach or exceed the best equilibrium described in this model, and potentially reach the social optimum. The paper is not informative about the effect of punishment per se, but it provides a theoretical upper bound for efficiency in the absence of punishment, given social history mechanisms.

- source: Tarui, N., Mason, C. F., Polasky, S., & Ellis, G. (2008). Cooperation in the commons with unobservable actions. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The analysis shows that the threat of the harshest possible group punishment (worst perfect equilibrium) is sufficient to deter defection, even when individual actions are not observable. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

- source: ABREU, D., MILGROM, P., & PEARCE, D. (1991). INFORMATION AND TIMING IN REPEATED PARTNERSHIPS. *Econometrica*.

  findings: The paper finds that in repeated partnership games with imperfect monitoring, the ability to sustain efficient cooperation through punishment is fundamentally limited by the informativeness of the monitoring technology and the timing of information. When monitoring is imperfect, reducing the interest rate (making players more patient) increases the efficiency of equilibrium outcomes, potentially allowing near-first-best payoffs. However, shortening the period of fixed action or reducing the lag with which information is reported can actually decrease efficiency, sometimes eliminating the possibility of cooperation altogether. Delaying information release can, counterintuitively, increase efficiency by reducing the cost of deterring deviations. The paper provides generalizations to n-player symmetric games and shows that these results are robust to more complex action and signal spaces.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public-goods-like games with punishment under imperfect monitoring. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the informativeness of monitoring, the timing of information, and the patience of players. In particular, enabling punishment does not guarantee high efficiency unless monitoring is sufficiently informative and the timing of information and action periods is chosen carefully. For prediction, the paper suggests that increasing patience (lower interest rate) is reliably associated with higher efficiency, but increasing the frequency of action or information updates can reduce efficiency when monitoring is imperfect. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to estimate efficiency given the relevant game design dimensions.
