- source: Duong, M. H., & Han, T. A. (2021). Cost efficiency of institutional incentives for promoting cooperation in finite populations. *Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in finite-population PGGs, the expected total cost to an institution of achieving a minimum cooperation level via punishment or reward can be exactly calculated and optimized. The cost efficiency of punishment versus reward depends on the required cooperation threshold: for low cooperation targets, reward is more cost-efficient; for high targets, punishment is more cost-efficient, with the threshold typically around 50% cooperation. The cost functions exhibit phase transitions as the intensity of selection changes. The analysis provides explicit formulas and algorithms for determining the optimal incentive size and minimal institutional cost, given the game parameters, population size, and selection intensity. The results are validated by numerical simulations and apply to both the PGG and the donation game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, theoretically grounded formulas for predicting the efficiency (cost-effectiveness) of institutional punishment (and reward) in finite-population PGGs, as a function of player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, selection intensity, and the required cooperation level. It shows that the efficiency of punishment depends critically on the required cooperation threshold and the selection intensity. For prediction tasks, the paper enables calculation of the minimal institutional cost (and thus efficiency) for any specified game design, provided the relevant parameters are known. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the institution can set the punishment/reward level to achieve a target cooperation rate, and where the main outcome of interest is the total cost to the institution (i.e., efficiency in terms of budget spent per unit of cooperation achieved). The findings are less directly applicable to settings where individual payoffs or group welfare (rather than institutional cost) are the main outcome, but the cost formulas can be mapped to group efficiency if the institutional cost is subtracted from total group payoffs.

- source: Chen, X. J., Sasaki, T., Brännström, & Dieckmann, U. (2015). First carrot, then stick: how the adaptive hybridization of incentives promotes cooperation. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. This policy outperforms pure reward or pure punishment in both effectiveness (range of conditions for full cooperation) and efficiency (cumulative cost to reach/restore cooperation). The findings hold for both well-mixed and spatial populations, and are robust to changes in group size, incentive leverage, spatial structure, and other model details. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.

- source: Sasaki, T., Brännström, Dieckmann, U., & Sigmund, K. (2012). The take-it-or-leave-it option allows small penalties to overcome social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, allowing optional participation (the 'take-it-or-leave-it' option) enables even small penalties to establish a globally stable cooperative norm, maximizing group welfare. In contrast, compulsory participation requires much larger penalties to achieve the same effect, and can leave the group trapped in full defection if initial cooperation is low. Rewarding also promotes cooperation, but requires much higher costs to achieve full cooperation and does not eliminate the possibility of stable coexistence between cooperators and defectors. The efficiency gains from punishment with optional participation are robust across several model variants, including user-pays and capped punishment. The key outcome is that punishment, when combined with voluntary participation, is a highly efficient mechanism for overcoming social dilemmas in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling optional participation (voluntary entry) dramatically increases the efficiency of institutional punishment: much smaller penalties suffice to achieve full cooperation and maximize group welfare. The model is directly relevant to settings where the game design allows players to opt out, and where punishment is administered by an institution rather than peers. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing institutional punishment with optional participation will yield a much larger increase in efficiency than with compulsory participation, and that the cost required to achieve this is much lower than for reward-based incentives. The results are robust to variations in group size, cost structure, and punishment allocation, making them broadly applicable to prediction tasks involving these design dimensions.

- source: Sasaki, T., Uchida, S., & Chen, X. J. (2015). Voluntary rewards mediate the evolution of pool punishment for maintaining public goods in large populations. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary public goods games with large populations, pool punishment cannot emerge from rare initial conditions due to the second-order free rider problem. However, if a voluntary reward fund is available and especially if it rewards those who contribute to pool punishment, then pool punishment can emerge and become stable, leading to high group efficiency (i.e., the population converges to all-punisher state). The reward mechanism acts as a mediator, facilitating the transition from free riding to stable punishment-supported cooperation. Once established, pool punishment is self-sustaining and the reward fund is no longer needed. The findings are robust to variations in group size, benefit multipliers, and cost parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of a reward mechanism, especially one that rewards contributors to punishment, is a key moderator for the emergence and stability of efficient outcomes in public goods games with punishment. In the absence of such a reward, punishment alone cannot overcome the second-order free rider problem in large populations, and efficiency remains low. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the presence or absence of a reward mechanism (and its design) is critical. Other dimensions such as group size, MPCR, and cost parameters are explored and found not to qualitatively change the main result: without reward, punishment does not increase efficiency from a free-rider baseline; with reward, punishment can emerge and efficiency increases dramatically.

- source: Perc, M. (2012). Sustainable institutionalized punishment requires elimination of second-order free-riders. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. If second-order free-riders are not punished sufficiently, the punishment institution cannot be sustained, and efficiency remains low. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

- source: Rand, D. G., & Nowak, M. A. (2011). The evolution of antisocial punishment in optional public goods games. *Nature Communications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The main finding is that in optional public goods games, allowing all forms of punishment (including antisocial punishment) leads to the breakdown of the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group efficiency. Punishment does not increase average payoffs or cooperation rates; instead, it is used by all strategy types to protect against invasion, resulting in a 'tragedy of the commons' where costly punishment is widespread and destructive. Only when punishment is weak (low impact per cost) and the public good is highly efficient (high multiplier) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained. Theoretical results are supported by large-scale simulations and are robust to parameter variation. Preliminary behavioral experiments are consistent with the model's predictions, but the main results are theoretical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the set of available punishment strategies. If only pro-social punishment (cooperators punishing defectors) is allowed, punishment can greatly increase efficiency. However, if antisocial punishment is possible, punishment does not improve efficiency and can even reduce it. The effect is robust across player count, group size, and a wide range of parameters. The efficiency impact of punishment is more positive when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is high and punishment is weak (low punishment impact per cost). For prediction, the presence of antisocial punishment options and the effectiveness of punishment are key moderators.

The model shows that the cost of punishing a defector decreases as more punishers participate, making punishment more effective and less costly at higher frequencies. The equilibrium with coordinated punishment achieves higher average fitness than the nonpunishing equilibrium, and even modest levels of genetic assortment allow punishment to invade when rare. The model predicts that only some individuals will engage in punishment, but this is sufficient to achieve a large fraction of the maximum possible gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling coordinated punishment in a public goods game will increase group efficiency (payoff) relative to a control without punishment, provided that punishment is coordinated and costs are shared among punishers. The model highlights the importance of coordination, increasing returns to scale in punishment, and the presence of a signaling stage. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that if the game design allows for coordinated punishment (e.g., communication, quorum requirements, or cost-sharing), one should expect a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency. The model also suggests that the effect is robust to group size and persists under plausible levels of assortment, but is sensitive to the cost structure of punishment and the degree of coordination.

The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) and group size are also important moderators.
