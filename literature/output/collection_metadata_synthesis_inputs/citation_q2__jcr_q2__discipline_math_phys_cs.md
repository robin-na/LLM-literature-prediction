- source: Pi, J. X., Yang, G. H., & Yang, H. (2022). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in N-person snowdrift games with peer punishment and individual disguise. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Zhuang, Q., Wang, D., Fan, Y., & Di, Z. R. (2012). Evolution of cooperation in a heterogeneous population with influential individuals. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a spatial PGG with influential individuals, both punishment and reward mechanisms (administered by influential individuals) can promote cooperation and increase mean payoff compared to the baseline with no such interventions. Rewarding cooperators is more effective than punishing defectors in terms of both promoting cooperation and increasing mean payoff, especially at low multiplication factors (low MPCR). The cost of punishment or reward is paid by society, not by the influential individuals. The presence of influential individuals as mere exemplars (always cooperating, no sanctioning power) actually reduces cooperation and does not improve efficiency. The model demonstrates that, for a given intervention cost, reward leads to higher group efficiency than punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct simulation evidence that, in a spatial PGG with influential individuals, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (mean payoff) compared to the baseline with no punishment, but enabling reward increases efficiency even more. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, but smaller than that of reward, for a given cost. The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. The model is theoretical/simulation-based, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted with caution.

- source: Cong, R., Li, K., Wang, L., & Zhao, Q. C. (2016). Cooperation induced by wise incentive allocation in spontaneous institution. *Epl*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with optional institution membership, the optimal regime for cooperation and group efficiency is achieved when the institution balances punishment and reward. Pure punishment or pure reward is suboptimal: too much punishment collapses the institution, while too much reward allows institutional defectors to dominate. The efficiency of punishment and reward (i.e., their impact per unit cost) increases the range of conditions under which cooperation prevails. The model provides explicit conditions for when institutional cooperators outcompete defectors, and shows that the stationary distribution of strategies (and thus group efficiency) is maximized at intermediate values of the punishment/reward split.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the balance between punishment and reward, as well as the effectiveness of each. It suggests that enabling punishment alone may not always increase efficiency; the presence and strength of reward, and the cost-effectiveness of both, are crucial moderators. For prediction tasks, the model implies that intermediate punishment (with some reward) is optimal for efficiency, and that both the cost and impact of punishment and reward should be considered. The findings are directly relevant for settings where institutional incentives are endogenous and both punishment and reward are possible.

- source: Yao, Y., & Chen, S. S. (2014). Multiplicative noise enhances spatial reciprocity. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.

  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, both strong altruistic punishment and reward are unable to sustain cooperation when implemented in the traditional (non-tax-based) way, due to their high cost. When a tax-based mechanism is introduced—where all players pay a tax that is redistributed to punishers or rewarders—these strategies become viable, leading to stable coexistence of cooperators and punishers/rewarders and the elimination of defectors. This results in higher group efficiency and welfare. Tax-based strong altruistic reward leads to even higher cooperation and efficiency than tax-based strong altruistic punishment under equivalent tax conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the introduction of a tax-based institutional mechanism for funding punishment or reward can transform the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games from negative or neutral to strongly positive. The results suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling tax-based punishment or reward will substantially increase efficiency, with reward being even more effective than punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with strong institutional support and compulsory tax collection, and less so to voluntary or peer-based punishment. The model is deterministic and assumes infinite, well-mixed populations, so predictions should be adjusted for finite or noisy environments.

- source: Sasaki, T. (2014). The Evolution of Cooperation Through Institutional Incentives and Optional Participation. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with optional participation, institutional punishment (penalties) is highly effective at promoting full cooperation and maximizing group welfare, even when the same level of punishment would only produce bi-stability (coordination problem) under compulsory participation. Optional participation allows the population to escape from the inefficient defection equilibrium, making cooperation globally stable for a wide range of punishment strengths. In contrast, institutional rewards combined with optional participation do not robustly promote cooperation or welfare: they often lead to non-participation and only marginally improve group welfare over the baseline. The model provides explicit analytical thresholds for when each regime occurs, and shows that the minimal punishment needed to achieve full cooperation decreases as group size increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with optional participation and institutional punishment, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency (group welfare) compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, rounds, and incentive strengths, provided the punishment is above a threshold that decreases with group size. The model also predicts that institutional rewards are much less effective in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when optional participation and institutional punishment are present. However, the results are theoretical and assume infinite populations and well-mixed groups, so empirical generalizability may be limited.

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2018). Rationality alters the rank between peer punishment and social exclusion. *Epl*.

  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary advantage (interpreted as efficiency in promoting cooperation and group payoff) of peer punishment versus social exclusion in PGGs is not fixed, but depends on the intensity of selection (rationality), the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, and whether participation is compulsory or voluntary. Under weak selection, peer punishment can be favored if it is efficient or exclusion is costly. Under strong selection, social exclusion with low cost dominates. The presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not change the condition for which punishment is favored, but reduces the difference in their abundances. The rank between peer punishment and social exclusion can change as rationality changes, and this sensitivity is mapped for a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGGs, especially regarding how the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, group size, and rationality (selection intensity) interact. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive or negative depending on whether punishment is efficient and/or exclusion is costly, and on the rationality of agents. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, theoretical PGGs with the specified parameters. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is conditional on the cost/benefit structure and the rationality of players, and that the presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not fundamentally alter the ranking condition but can affect the magnitude of effects.

  findings: The paper provides a unified theoretical framework for public goods games with multiple tolerant punishment strategies. It shows that: (1) Less tolerant punishers (those who punish with fewer defectors present) are always more abundant and thus more evolutionarily successful than more tolerant ones. (2) The presence of punishment strategies increases group efficiency (measured by the abundance of cooperation and punishers) when the fine for defectors exceeds a threshold determined by group size, enhancement factor, and punishment cost. (3) Larger group sizes can facilitate the evolution of punishment and thus higher group efficiency, but only when the enhancement factor and punishment cost are small. (4) The paper provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment prevails, and these are validated by simulations. (5) The main outcome is the stationary abundance of strategies, which is directly linked to average payoffs and group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games. It provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment increases the abundance of cooperation and group efficiency, as a function of group size, enhancement factor (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency, especially in larger groups when costs are low, and that less tolerant (more responsive) punishment is more effective. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency of games with and without punishment, given the game design parameters. However, the results are derived under evolutionary dynamics with weak selection and large populations, so care should be taken when applying them to small groups or different update rules.

This effect holds both when sanctions are observable (feedback given immediately) and when sanctions are unobservable (feedback delayed until the end). The mechanism is that subjects expect free riders to be punished and adjust their behavior to avoid being punished, even when they do not observe sanctions in real time. The presence of punishment stabilizes cooperation and prevents the unraveling of contributions, with some groups achieving high cooperation and others not. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

While it does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it shows that synergistic punishment and certain network structures can make cooperation more stable, which would likely increase group efficiency in practice. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of synergistic punishment, lower punishment costs, and structured populations (as opposed to well-mixed) are likely to increase the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the lack of explicit efficiency or payoff data limits direct quantitative prediction.
