- source: Wolitzky, A. (2013). Cooperation with Network Monitoring. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. For pure public goods, efficiency is typically increasing in group size; for divisible public goods, it is typically decreasing. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schmidt, K. M. (1999). A theory of fairness, competition, and cooperation. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model predicts that in standard public goods games without punishment, the equilibrium is typically full defection and low efficiency, matching empirical data. When a punishment stage is added (costly to the punisher, but more costly to the punished), the threat of punishment by inequity-averse players can sustain full cooperation and thus high efficiency, even if most players are selfish. The model provides explicit conditions under which this occurs, and shows that the presence of even a single sufficiently inequity-averse 'enforcer' can be enough to sustain efficient cooperation. The theory also predicts that the effect of punishment on efficiency is stronger when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is higher, and when the cost of punishment is lower.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, often dramatically, provided there is at least a small fraction of inequity-averse players. The model gives explicit conditions relating group size, mpcr, and punishment cost to the likelihood of efficient cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, for typical lab PGG designs, adding punishment will shift efficiency from low (near zero) to high (near one), unless punishment is extremely costly or the group is very large and inequity aversion is rare. The model also suggests that the marginal effect of punishment is moderated by mpcr and punishment cost.

- source: Carpenter, J., Matthews, P., & Ong'ong'a, O. (2004). Why Punish? Social reciprocity and the enforcement of prosocial norms. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games is best explained by social reciprocity: individuals are motivated to punish norm violators (free riders) even when doing so is costly and does not improve their own material payoff, and even when the violator is outside their immediate group. Experimental and survey evidence shows that punishment is not solely driven by the desire to reduce payoff differentials, and that people continue to punish even when the cost of punishment exceeds the harm inflicted. The demand for punishment is relatively inelastic to price, and motivations are primarily normative. While the models present payoff calculations and show that punishment can stabilize cooperation under certain initial conditions, the empirical focus is on behavioral motivations and patterns rather than direct measures of group efficiency or total earnings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment mechanisms in public goods games can sustain higher levels of cooperation, but it does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff outcomes. The theoretical models show that, under certain conditions, populations with punishers can achieve higher average payoffs than those dominated by free riders, but these are model-based results rather than empirical efficiency measurements. The experimental and survey data are primarily about who punishes, why, and under what conditions, rather than about the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that punishment can support cooperation even when it is costly, and that motivations are primarily normative, but it does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, ability to punish outgroup) are discussed in the context of behavioral patterns, not efficiency outcomes.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: Gerber, A., & Wichardt, P. C. (2009). Providing public goods in the absence of strong institutions. *Journal of Public Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a voluntary deposit-commitment mechanism in public goods games can fully implement efficient contribution profiles as equilibrium outcomes, even in the absence of strong external enforcement or punishment institutions. The mechanism works by making non-contribution costly (loss of deposit) only if all players commit, thus aligning individual incentives with group welfare. Theoretical results show that, under this mechanism, the group can achieve payoffs and efficiency levels that strictly Pareto-dominate the standard Nash equilibrium of zero contribution. The mechanism is robust to various extensions, including repeated games and alternative uses of forfeited deposits.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that self-commitment mechanisms (such as refundable deposits) can substitute for traditional punishment in public goods games, leading to higher efficiency and group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in environments where such a deposit-commitment mechanism is available and properly designed, the efficiency with 'punishment' (here, self-sanctioning via deposit) can approach the social optimum, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the deposit mechanism is feasible and all players can observe and verify commitments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or parameterized predictions, but the qualitative direction is clear: enabling credible self-sanctioning or commitment mechanisms can dramatically improve efficiency in public goods games.

  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (multiplayer prisoners' dilemmas), cooperation cannot be sustained in equilibrium under nonassortative (random) matching and individual selection; only defectors survive, leading to low group efficiency. However, cooperation and higher group payoffs can be sustained if (a) matching is assortative (e.g., kin selection, spatial structure, partner choice), (b) the game is not a prisoners' dilemma (e.g., stag hunt), or (c) the game is repeated and social norms (including punishment) are enforced. The paper discusses the theoretical role of punishment in repeated games as a means to sustain cooperation and efficient outcomes, but does not provide direct analysis or predictions about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical context for the importance of game structure (e.g., matching, repetition, group size, payoff parameters) in determining whether cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained in public goods games. It highlights that punishment can, in theory, support efficient cooperation in repeated games, but does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence about the magnitude or direction of punishment's effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of repeated interaction and enforceable punishment norms are key moderators, but does not provide parameter-level guidance for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in specific game designs.

- source: ABREU, D., MILGROM, P., & PEARCE, D. (1991). INFORMATION AND TIMING IN REPEATED PARTNERSHIPS. *Econometrica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated partnership games with imperfect monitoring, the ability to sustain efficient cooperation through punishment is fundamentally limited by the informativeness of the monitoring technology and the timing of information. When monitoring is imperfect, reducing the interest rate (making players more patient) increases the efficiency of equilibrium outcomes, potentially allowing near-first-best payoffs. However, shortening the period of fixed action or reducing the lag with which information is reported can actually decrease efficiency, sometimes eliminating the possibility of cooperation altogether. Delaying information release can, counterintuitively, increase efficiency by reducing the cost of deterring deviations. The paper provides generalizations to n-player symmetric games and shows that these results are robust to more complex action and signal spaces.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public-goods-like games with punishment under imperfect monitoring. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the informativeness of monitoring, the timing of information, and the patience of players. In particular, enabling punishment does not guarantee high efficiency unless monitoring is sufficiently informative and the timing of information and action periods is chosen carefully. For prediction, the paper suggests that increasing patience (lower interest rate) is reliably associated with higher efficiency, but increasing the frequency of action or information updates can reduce efficiency when monitoring is imperfect. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to estimate efficiency given the relevant game design dimensions.

- source: Sethi, R., & Somanathan, E. (2003). Understanding reciprocity. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment and reciprocity can support efficient outcomes in both repeated and sporadic interactions, but only under certain conditions. In repeated games, strategies that support cooperation (and thus efficiency) can be stable if complexity costs or implementation errors are considered, but many inefficient strategies can also be stable. In one-shot or sporadic interactions, punishment can induce cooperation and improve efficiency, but this stability is fragile: populations of punishers can be invaded by non-punishing cooperators or defectors, and the cost of punishment can erode its effectiveness. In public goods and common pool resource games, the presence of reciprocators willing to punish can lead to higher group payoffs, but only if monitoring and punishment costs are not too high and if group composition allows reciprocators to coordinate. The evolution of mixed populations (reciprocators and defectors) is common, and the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the game structure and population dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games can increase efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines, but only under specific conditions: punishment must be sufficiently effective (high impact per cost), not too costly, and the population must contain enough reciprocators or the ability to assort. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive; in some models, punishment does not prevent the evolution of inefficient outcomes, and the stability of efficient cooperation is sensitive to the cost and observability of punishment, as well as to the possibility of second-order free-riding. For prediction, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the ability to observe or assort by type. Control efficiency (without punishment) is often low, but the introduction of punishment can raise efficiency if these moderating conditions are met.

  findings: The paper proves that in repeated partnership games with private monitoring and evidentiary communication, permanent ostracism (permanent exclusion of deviators) cannot achieve higher efficiency or group payoff than bilateral enforcement, because victims have incentives to conceal deviations rather than report them. This limits the effectiveness of the harshest forms of community punishment. However, if ostracism is temporary and includes forgiveness (eventual readmission of deviators), then higher levels of cooperation and group payoff can be sustained, as the prospect of future cooperation with forgiven players incentivizes truthful reporting.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the design of punishment—specifically, whether it is permanent or temporary (with forgiveness)—is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated partnership games with private monitoring. If punishment is permanent ostracism, efficiency does not improve over bilateral enforcement. If punishment is temporary (forgiveness is possible), efficiency can be strictly higher, especially as patience or group size increases. For prediction tasks, the presence of forgiveness/temporary ostracism should be coded as a key moderator, and the effect of punishment on efficiency should be expected to be positive only when forgiveness is possible. The results are derived in a variable-stakes partnership game, which is adjacent to but not exactly a standard public goods game.
