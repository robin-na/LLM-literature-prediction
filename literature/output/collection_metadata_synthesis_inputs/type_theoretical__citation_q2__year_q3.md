  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

- source: Dutta, R., Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2021). The whip and the Bible: Punishment versus internalization. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms leads to a large increase in group efficiency and welfare, as measured by group payoff and normalized utility. The model, calibrated to Fehr & Gächter (2000), matches the observed jump in average contributions and welfare when punishment is enabled. The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations. The model further predicts that the relationship between societal value of cooperation and punishment is non-monotonic in the lab, but always positive in the field.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The model is directly calibrated to the Fehr & Gächter (2000) design (4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, specific punishment cost/tech), and matches the observed efficiency increase. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2018). Rationality alters the rank between peer punishment and social exclusion. *Epl*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary advantage (interpreted as efficiency in promoting cooperation and group payoff) of peer punishment versus social exclusion in PGGs is not fixed, but depends on the intensity of selection (rationality), the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, and whether participation is compulsory or voluntary. Under weak selection, peer punishment can be favored if it is efficient or exclusion is costly. Under strong selection, social exclusion with low cost dominates. The presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not change the condition for which punishment is favored, but reduces the difference in their abundances. The rank between peer punishment and social exclusion can change as rationality changes, and this sensitivity is mapped for a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGGs, especially regarding how the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, group size, and rationality (selection intensity) interact. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive or negative depending on whether punishment is efficient and/or exclusion is costly, and on the rationality of agents. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, theoretical PGGs with the specified parameters. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is conditional on the cost/benefit structure and the rationality of players, and that the presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not fundamentally alter the ranking condition but can affect the magnitude of effects.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment mechanisms (cooperator-driven, defector-driven, or both) into public goods games increases the prevalence of cooperation and raises group efficiency/welfare compared to the baseline with no punishment. In infinite well-mixed populations, a mix of both punishment types is most effective, while in networked populations, cooperator-driven punishment is superior. The effect of punishment on efficiency is monotonic with respect to the synergy factor (mpcr), punishment fine (punishment_tech), and feedback sensitivity, though in networked populations, an intermediate feedback sensitivity is optimal. The study provides detailed phase diagrams and analytical boundaries for when nondefection (high efficiency) prevails. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and evolutionary scenarios.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in PGG-like environments increases group efficiency, especially when the punishment is cooperator-driven or when both types are available. The effect is sensitive to the synergy factor (mpcr), punishment fine (punishment_tech), and feedback sensitivity, with network structure further moderating the effect. For prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment, the value of mpcr, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the network structure should be considered as key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. The results suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude depending on these design dimensions.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment in public goods games lowers the critical synergy threshold required for cooperation to emerge, thereby increasing the likelihood of cooperation and improving group-level payoff (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. However, the cost of punishment reduces the total group payoff, so the efficiency gain from increased cooperation is partially offset by punishment costs. The effect is stronger when punishment is more efficient (higher fine per unit cost). The paper also shows that inclusiveness (redistribution of payoffs) is an even more effective way to promote cooperation and efficiency, often making punishment unnecessary if inclusiveness is high enough.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is efficient (high fine, low cost). The effect is moderated by the costliness of punishment: higher punishment costs reduce the efficiency benefit. The findings also highlight that the structure of payoff redistribution (inclusiveness) can be a more powerful moderator than punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that, all else equal, adding punishment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the degree of payoff redistribution.

In well-mixed populations, this can transform the game from one dominated by defection to one where full cooperation is stable. Structured populations (networked interactions) can allow cooperation to emerge at lower fine thresholds, but as the fine increases, well-mixed populations yield higher efficiency. The findings support the prediction that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective and not too costly. The model is theoretical, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted as qualitative guidance.

For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that enabling punishment and introducing targeted reward mechanisms can shift the system toward higher cooperation and punishment prevalence, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in empirical PGGs.
