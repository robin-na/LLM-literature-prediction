- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Bone, J., Silva, A. S., & Raihani, N. J. (2014). Defectors, not norm violators, are punished by third-parties. *Biology Letters*.

  findings: The study finds that third-party punishment in a one-shot public goods game is overwhelmingly directed at defectors, regardless of whether defection is the norm in the group. The manipulation of norm violation (defection being typical or atypical) does not affect the likelihood of defectors being punished. Cooperators are rarely punished, even when they are in the minority. The cost structure of punishment is fixed, so punishing multiple players is not more expensive than punishing one. Social rejection patterns mirror punishment: defectors are less preferred as future partners, regardless of norm status. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a one-shot PGG with third-party punishment and a fixed punishment cost, punishment is targeted at defectors rather than norm violators. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design details (one-shot, third-party punishment, fixed cost, no communication, binary contribution) are informative for mapping to other studies, but the lack of payoff outcomes limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction. The findings suggest that, under these design features, punishment is likely to be used against defectors, but the impact on group efficiency remains unmeasured.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, G. R., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Altered Voluntary Cooperative Norms Compliance Under Equal Decision-Making Power. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.

  findings: The study finds that modulating rDLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects voluntary cooperative norm compliance (measured as contribution) in a linear public goods game with four players, asymmetric endowments, and no punishment or sanction options. Anodal stimulation increases contributions, cathodal decreases them, and sham is intermediate. The effect is observed for both high and low endowment players. The study also finds that participants' own norm perceptions (norm.own) are more predictive of compliance than their perceptions of others' norms (norm.other), and that anodal stimulation changes the relative influence of these norm perceptions. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported, and the game does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its findings are limited to the effect of neural stimulation on voluntary cooperation (contribution) in a standard linear PGG. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are reported and may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform the downstream prediction task regarding the effect of punishment on efficiency. It may be useful as a control or baseline for voluntary cooperation in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Javaid, A., & Falk, T. (2015). Incorporating local institutions in irrigation experiments: evidence from rural communities in Pakistan. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implemented a sequential common-pool resource (CPR) game with five players per group, modeling irrigation dilemmas with asymmetric access. In the baseline (no institutional intervention), groups achieved high efficiency (average group earnings at 74% of the social optimum, group investment at 98% of the optimum). When external punishment (costly, probabilistic fines) was introduced, group earnings and efficiency significantly decreased (regression coefficient for group earnings: -9.03, p<0.1). The punishment mechanism reduced over-extraction by head-users but did not increase tail-user earnings; instead, it led to more water being wasted and lower group welfare. Communication and traditional authority treatments did not significantly affect group earnings or investment. The high baseline efficiency is attributed to strong local norms and the public revelation of individual extraction decisions. The main effect of punishment was to create additional coordination problems, not to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments with high baseline efficiency and strong social information (public feedback on individual actions), introducing an external punishment mechanism can reduce group efficiency and welfare. The punishment treatment in this design (costly, probabilistic, not directly linked to rule violations) led to more resource wastage and lower group earnings. For prediction tasks, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high and social information is available, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency. The effect is specific to the punishment design used here (costly, probabilistic, not directly rewarding the punisher). Other institutional interventions (communication, traditional authority) had no significant effect. The findings are most applicable to sequential CPR games with asymmetric access, public feedback, and close-knit communities.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

- source: Grimalda, G., Belianin, A., Hennig-Schmidt, H., Requate, T., & Ryzhkova, M. (2022). Sanctions and international interaction improve cooperation to avert climate change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed

  findings: The experiment tested the effect of sanctions (costly punishment) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma with German and Russian participants, both in national and international groups. Sanctions increased group cooperation (as measured by the probability of loss avoidance, PLA), especially for Russian participants and in international groups. However, the cost of sanctioning meant that final payoffs were actually higher in the no-sanction treatments, as shown in the supplementary material.   findings: This paper experimentally studies a dynamic public goods game with endogenous group formation, free migration, and treatments varying the presence of intergroup competition and ostracism (social exclusion by majority vote). Thus, for environments with similar group formation and migration, the presence of ostracism is a strong positive predictor for efficiency gains from sanctioning.
