- source: Jiao, Y. H., Chen, T., & Chen, Q. (2020). Probabilistic punishment and reward under rule of trust-based decision-making in continuous public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing probabilistic punishment and reward (i.e., punish/reward with probability rho < 1) into a continuous PGG can maintain high levels of cooperation and mean payoff (efficiency), even when the cost of punishment/reward is high. Traditional (always executed) punishment/reward leads to collapse of cooperation and low efficiency when costs are high. There is an optimal execution probability (rho ≈ 0.6) and moderate fine/bonus size (f = r = 1) that maximize mean payoff and cooperation, saving about 40% of the cost compared to always executing punishment/reward. If either the execution probability or the fine/bonus is too low, cooperation and efficiency remain low. The model shows that probabilistic execution is robust to high costs and can avoid the collapse of cooperation seen in traditional models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in continuous PGGs on structured populations, enabling punishment (and reward) with a moderate probability (rather than always) can increase group efficiency compared to both no punishment and always-on punishment, especially when costs are high. The effect is robust across a range of synergy factors (mpcr), costs, and fine/bonus sizes. For prediction, the presence of probabilistic punishment (with moderate execution probability and moderate cost/fine) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to the control (no punishment) and to traditional always-on punishment, particularly when punishment/reward costs are nontrivial. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the presence and cost of punishment, the effectiveness of punishment, and whether execution is probabilistic or deterministic.

- source: Eldakar, O. T., Farrell, D. L., & Wilson, D. S. (2007). Selfish punishment: Altruism can be maintained by competition among cheaters. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

- source: Oya, G., & Ohtsuki, H. (2017). Stable polymorphism of cooperators and punishers in a public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in well-mixed populations, punishment does not maintain cooperation or improve efficiency; free riders dominate unless initial conditions favor punishers, and even then, cooperation is lost. In spatially structured populations (Wright's island model and stepping stone model), if punishment is sufficiently efficient and the cost of cooperation is less than the cost of punishment, a stable coexistence of cooperators, punishers, and free riders can emerge, either as a limit cycle or a stable equilibrium (especially with mutation). In these cases, the presence of punishment can support higher average payoffs and group welfare than in the absence of punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: it can be positive in structured populations with the right parameters, but is neutral or negative in well-mixed populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on population structure, the relative costs of cooperation and punishment, and the efficiency of punishment. In well-mixed (random matching) environments, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency or maintain cooperation. In spatially structured environments, punishment can support higher efficiency and stable coexistence of strategies, but only if punishment is efficient and costly enough relative to cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive only in structured populations with high punishment efficiency and when cooperation is less costly than punishment. The model also highlights the importance of mutation and the inability to both cooperate and punish in the same round. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in theoretical or agent-based models, and provide mechanistic insight for interpreting empirical results.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Evolution of cooperation in a hierarchical society with corruption control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a hierarchical public goods game, the presence of corrupt punishment can maintain civilian cooperation but undermines altruistic punishment and reduces population mean payoff. Introducing corruption control, where altruistic punishers can also punish or exclude corrupt punishers, increases both cooperation and efficiency. Asymmetrical punishment (social exclusion of corrupt punishers) is more effective than symmetrical punishment (equal fines for corrupt punishers and defectors) in promoting both cooperation and population mean payoff. The model provides explicit formulas and phase diagrams showing how efficiency depends on group size, punishment cost, fine, bribe, cop proportion, and the probability of spiteful corruption. The results are robust across a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with hierarchical roles and corruption. It shows that the introduction of punishment (especially with corruption control) increases efficiency, and that the effectiveness depends on the design dimensions such as group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the structure of punishment (symmetrical vs. asymmetrical). The explicit formulas and phase diagrams can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game parameters. The results are most applicable to settings with hierarchical roles and the possibility of corrupt punishment, but the general findings about the positive effect of well-designed punishment on efficiency are likely to transfer to standard PGGs with punishment.

- source: Zhang, J. L., Chu, T. G., & Weissing, F. J. (2013). Does insurance against punishment undermine cooperation in the evolution of public goods games?. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with punishment and an additional option for defectors to buy insurance that covers their punishment costs. It shows that if insurance is cheaper than the expected punishment, cooperation can be destabilized, leading to lower group efficiency and welfare. However, if insurance is realistically priced (at or above the expected fine), cooperation remains stable and efficient. The results are presented as a set of scenarios, with the stability of cooperation, defection, or speculation depending on the relative costs of cooperation, punishment, and insurance. The main implication is that punishment remains an effective mechanism for sustaining cooperation and efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games are robust to the introduction of insurance against punishment, as long as the insurance is not priced below the expected punishment cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in standard public goods games with punishment, efficiency should remain high unless there are additional mechanisms (like cheap insurance) that allow defectors to avoid punishment at a lower cost. The model also clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment (and thus its impact on efficiency) depends on the relationship between the cost of cooperation, the expected punishment, and any available insurance. If the control game (without punishment) is inefficient, introducing punishment should increase efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.

- source: Powers, S. T., Taylor, D. J., & Bryson, J. J. (2012). Punishment can promote defection in group-structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games with group structure, the addition of anti-social punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) can prevent the evolution and maintenance of pro-social punishment and cooperation. This reduces the mean fitness and group payoff that would otherwise be achieved through cooperation and pro-social punishment. The effect is stronger in larger groups and when dispersal is less frequent. The presence of anti-social punishment raises the threshold for pro-social punishment to be stable, requiring greater between-group variance (e.g., smaller group sizes) for cooperation to persist. Thus, anti-social punishment makes it less likely that punishment will improve efficiency in group-structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the possibility of anti-social punishment and on group structure parameters such as group size and dispersal frequency. When anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it, especially in larger groups or when between-group variance is low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of anti-social punishment, larger group sizes, and less frequent dispersal all reduce the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency. The paper also clarifies that the effect-to-cost ratio of punishment and cooperation modulate these effects, but the negative impact of anti-social punishment is robust across a range of parameter values.

- source: Dercole, F., De Carli, M., Della Rossa, F., & Papadopoulos, A. V. (2013). Overpunishing is not necessary to fix cooperation in voluntary public goods games. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both peer-punishment (overpunishing) and shared-punishment (fixed sanction per defector, cost shared among punishers) in voluntary PGGs can lead to the fixation of cooperation and high group efficiency, provided antisocial punishment is absent. The main difference is quantitative: overpunishing increases the fraction of initial conditions leading to full cooperation, but shared-punishment is sufficient to achieve efficient outcomes in most cases, especially when stochasticity (finite populations) is considered. The likelihood of efficient cooperation increases with group size, sanction severity, and the public goods multiplier. The paper provides explicit payoff formulas and analyzes the basins of attraction for efficient (cooperative) equilibria under a wide range of parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in voluntary PGGs increases group efficiency, even when punishment is mild (shared-punishment). The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, round numbers, and parameter values. The paper gives explicit formulas for payoffs and the conditions under which cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved. For prediction tasks, the presence of voluntary participation, absence of antisocial punishment, and the ability to punish defectors (even with fixed, shared-cost sanctions) are sufficient to expect a large increase in efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is stronger with larger groups, higher punishment severity, and higher public goods multipliers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGG-like games with similar design features.

- source: Archetti, M., & Scheuring, I. (2013). Trading public goods stabilizes interspecific mutualism. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with non-linear (sigmoid or step) benefit functions, stable mutualism and high group efficiency can be achieved without punishment, even in large groups and with high costs. Punishment is only necessary to maintain mutualism and efficiency in the special case of linear benefits (the N-person prisoner's dilemma). When punishment is introduced in the linear case, it drives the system to full cooperation and maximal efficiency. In non-linear cases, punishment is not required for high efficiency, as the non-linearity itself stabilizes cooperation at high levels. The paper provides analytical and numerical results for the equilibrium frequency of cooperators and the conditions for stable mutualism.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the shape of the benefit function. If the game is linear (standard PGG), enabling punishment will have a strong positive effect on efficiency, driving the group to full cooperation. If the game is non-linear (e.g., threshold, sigmoid, or step), high efficiency can be achieved without punishment, and enabling punishment adds little or no additional benefit. Therefore, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to know whether the benefit function is linear or non-linear. Other game design dimensions (group size, cost/benefit ratio) interact with this, but the linearity of the benefit is the key moderator identified by this paper.

The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff directly, but the persistence of altruistic strategies is used as a proxy for cooperative success.

For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that punishment is most effective at low cost, can be detrimental at intermediate cost, and can again be effective at high cost if spatial symbiosis is possible. The results are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.
