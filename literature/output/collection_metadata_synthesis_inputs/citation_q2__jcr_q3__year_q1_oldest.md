- source: Nicklisch, A., & Wolff, I. (2011). COOPERATION NORMS IN MULTIPLE-STAGE PUNISHMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment implements a linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, and multiple endogenous punishment stages per round. Players can punish at a convex cost, and punishment reduces the target's payoff by 10% per point (up to 10 points). Two treatments are compared: BASIC (standard punishment) and OPINION (bystanders can approve/disapprove of punishment, but this has no direct payoff effect). The main findings are: (1) Average payoffs and efficiency remain above the Nash equilibrium and do not differ significantly between treatments or over time; (2) The opportunity for counter-punishment does not lead to a collapse in cooperation or efficiency; (3) Punishment is guided by an absolute full-contribution norm, and both punishers and bystanders apply similar standards; (4) Retaliation occurs but does not undermine overall efficiency; (5) The presence of bystander approval does not materially change efficiency or cooperation patterns. The results suggest that multi-stage punishment with changing groups can sustain cooperation and efficiency, even when counter-punishment is possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a four-player, ten-round linear PGG with continuous contributions, full information, and multi-stage punishment (with convex costs and 10% effectiveness per point), the introduction of punishment sustains or increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment Nash equilibrium. Efficiency remains stable and above the Nash level, and does not collapse even when counter-punishment is possible. The presence of bystander approval (OPINION treatment) does not significantly alter efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that in similar designs (with multi-stage punishment, convex costs, and changing groups), enabling punishment is likely to have a more positive effect on efficiency, and the risk of efficiency collapse due to counter-punishment is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in this class of games.

- source: Wolff, I. (2012). Retaliation and the role for punishment in the evolution of cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with punishment and the possibility of retaliation, punishment can help induce partially cooperative equilibria and temporarily increase cooperation and group payoff, especially when mutation is present. However, punishment cannot sustain full cooperation in the presence of even small mutation rates, as the costs of punishment and the threat of retaliation allow non-punishing cooperators and defectors to invade. The efficiency gains from punishment are thus limited and parameter-dependent: punishment is most effective at intermediate continuation probabilities and when retaliation is not too strong. At high continuation probabilities or when retaliation is easy, punishment is driven out and cannot maintain high efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in repeated public goods games is highly sensitive to the possibility of retaliation and the evolutionary dynamics of strategy mutation. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, one should consider whether retaliation is possible, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the continuation probability (analogous to number of rounds or game length), and the presence of mutation or experimentation. Punishment can increase efficiency in some cases, but its effect is undermined by retaliation and high continuation probability. The model suggests that punishment is most likely to improve efficiency when it is effective (high punishment impact per cost), retaliation is costly or rare, and the game is not too long. If retaliation is easy and the game is long, punishment may not improve and may even reduce efficiency.

- source: Kranz, S. (2010). Moral norms in a partly compliant society. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper provides a formal model showing that in public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment technology increases both contributions and group welfare (efficiency), as long as the punishment is sufficiently effective and the share of compliers is not too low. The model gives explicit formulas for the increase in contributions and welfare as a function of the marginal per capita return, punishment cost, and the share of compliers. It also shows that the effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is less costly, the marginal per capita return is higher, and the share of compliers is higher. The model's predictions are consistent with experimental findings that punishment increases efficiency in public goods games under many conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games and close variants. It provides explicit theoretical predictions for how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, as a function of game design parameters (player count, marginal per capita return, punishment cost/tech, etc.) and the baseline (control) efficiency. The model can be used to generate quantitative or qualitative predictions for the downstream task, especially in settings where the control game is a standard public goods game and the treatment adds a punishment option. The paper also clarifies the conditions under which punishment will or will not increase efficiency, depending on the share of compliers and the effectiveness/cost of punishment.

- source: Darcet, D., & Sornette, D. (2008). Quantitative determination of the level of cooperation in the presence of punishment in three public good experiments. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper proposes that the observed level of punishment in public goods and related games can be quantitatively predicted by a cost-benefit analysis rooted in evolutionary feedback selection. The theory is applied to three canonical games: the third-party punishment game, the ultimatum game, and the public goods game with punishment (as in Fehr & Gächter 2002). The predicted punishment propensities (k) closely match experimental data from these games. Agent-based simulations show that the predicted punishment level is an emergent property of evolutionary dynamics. The theory suggests that punishment is tuned to maximize group efficiency, but the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes as dependent variables.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a theoretical rationale for why punishment increases cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency in public goods games. It offers quantitative predictions for the level of punishment as a function of game parameters (e.g., group size, punishment efficiency, marginal per-capita return). However, it does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report new experimental data on group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation and likely efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly, but does not provide direct efficiency data for model training or calibration.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper reviews experimental evidence showing that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms typically increases contributions and cooperation. However, it also highlights that punishment is costly and not always optimal from a collective (efficiency) perspective, especially when antisocial punishment or counter-punishment occurs. The effectiveness of punishment is robust to parameter changes (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness), but can be undermined by incomplete information or network structure. Communication and group selection also enhance cooperation. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral regularities and mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong qualitative support that enabling punishment in public goods games generally increases cooperation and contributions, but warns that the net effect on efficiency depends on the costs of punishment and the prevalence of antisocial punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when punishment is targeted at free riders, costs are not excessive, and antisocial punishment is rare. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes, so its value is mainly in contextualizing and interpreting empirical findings from other sources.

However, it does provide informative evidence about the effects of communication (announcements) and observability (public attribution of actions) on contribution behavior. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly relevant, but it may be useful for understanding the baseline effects of non-punitive social mechanisms (communication, transparency) on cooperation. It also clarifies that, in the absence of punishment, such mechanisms can increase contributions, but do not always do so unless combined.

- source: Colombier, N., Masclet, D., Mirza, D., & Montmarquette, C. (2011). GLOBAL SECURITY POLICIES AGAINST TERRORISM AND THE FREE RIDING PROBLEM: AN EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a laboratory experiment simulating international counterterrorism cooperation as a public good with a stochastic loss, the authors find that, absent institutional incentives, most participants defect and invest in private protection rather than the collective good. Introducing punishment (sanction) or reward institutions significantly increases investment in the collective account. However, while both punishment and reward reduce the probability of a loss event and increase first-stage payoffs, only reward institutions lead to higher overall welfare. Punishment increases cooperation but the direct costs of punishing (borne by both punishers and targets) more than offset the efficiency gains from increased cooperation, resulting in lower final payoffs than the baseline. Reward institutions, by contrast, increase both cooperation and final payoffs. Thus, punishment is not efficiency-enhancing in this environment, while reward is.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public-goods-like environment with costly punishment, enabling punishment increases cooperation but reduces overall efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The costs of punishment (to both punisher and target) outweigh the benefits from increased cooperation. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, one should expect a less positive or even negative effect on group payoff/welfare, especially when punishment is costly and the structure is similar to this experiment (n=4, 40 rounds, costly punishment, no identification of punishers, no chat, continuous contributions). The paper also shows that reward institutions can increase both cooperation and efficiency, in contrast to punishment.

- source: Akpalu, W., & Martinsson, P. (2012). Ostracism and Common Pool Resource Management in a Developing Country: Young Fishers in the Laboratory. *Journal of African Economies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Allowing group members to ostracize (permanently exclude) over-extractors via majority vote, even at a cost to the remaining group, sharply reduced overfishing compared to a baseline with no ostracism. In the baseline, extraction effort increased over time toward the Nash equilibrium (over-exploitation), while with ostracism, effort dropped toward the social optimum. The effect was immediate and strong after ostracism was introduced. Voting to ostracize was targeted at over-extractors, and the number of votes received was the main determinant of reduced extraction. The costliness of ostracism did not prevent its use. The study does not report group payoffs or efficiency ratios, but the reduction in extraction effort implies improved efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a credible, group-administered punishment mechanism (ostracism) in a CPR environment can substantially reduce over-extraction and move behavior toward the social optimum, even when punishment is costly to the group. While efficiency is not directly measured, the reduction in extraction effort is a strong proxy for increased efficiency in this context. For prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, enforceable punishment mechanism (ostracism) is likely to increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, especially in settings with no communication, fixed group membership, and a known time horizon. The specific design (majority vote, permanent exclusion, cost to punishers) is important for generalization.

  findings: The experiment shows that the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation depends on both the trustworthiness of the leader who installs the sanction and the general trust participants have in authorities. When an alternative, more harmful defection option is available (i.e., sanctions can be evaded), participants with low general trust in authorities are less likely to cooperate if the leader is untrustworthy, and instead shift to the evasion option. High-trust participants are less sensitive to leader trustworthiness. The main outcomes are behavioral allocations; the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of an evadable punishment option (i.e., a trilemma with an alternative defection route) can undermine the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation, especially among participants with low trust in authorities and when the leader is perceived as untrustworthy. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in designs where punishment can be evaded, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and thus efficiency) may be reduced or even reversed for some subgroups. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of evasion options and trust, but not about the quantitative effect on efficiency.

For high group identifiers, procedural justice (accuracy) increases contributions only after group failure; for low identifiers, it increases contributions only after group success. The experiment uses a step-level public good game with a threshold and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The findings highlight the importance of procedural justice in sanctioning systems for promoting cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence about effects on efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions depends on the perceived procedural justice (accuracy) of the sanctioning system, and that this effect is moderated by group identification and feedback about group success or failure. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in step-level public good games with punishment, the design of the punishment system (especially its perceived fairness and accuracy) and the social context (identification, feedback) can strongly moderate behavioral responses, which may in turn affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment will be more effective at increasing cooperation (and possibly efficiency) when the system is accurate and procedurally just, but the effect is context-dependent.

- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Nakao, K. (2009). CREATION OF SOCIAL ORDER IN ETHNIC CONFLICT. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer monitoring, collective peer punishment, or ethnic conflict) can sustain higher group efficiency (expected payoffs) compared to no punishment, but only if in-group monitoring is sufficiently efficient and not too costly. When monitoring is inefficient or costly, or when noise (misinterpretation of actions) is high, punishment can reduce efficiency due to frequent erroneous punishment or high monitoring costs. The model provides comparative statics showing that efficiency is maximized with efficient peer monitoring and high tolerance, and that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the quality of in-group policing, the length of conflict, and the density of inter-group interactions. The model predicts that groups with high-quality in-group policing achieve higher efficiency and more stable cooperation, while groups with low-quality policing suffer more frequent and longer conflicts, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated social dilemma games depends critically on the efficiency and cost of monitoring and punishment. If the game design allows for efficient, low-cost peer monitoring (low punishment cost, high punishment tech), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. If monitoring is costly or ineffective, punishment can reduce efficiency due to high costs or frequent erroneous punishment. The model also shows that the effect of punishment is moderated by noise (misinterpretation), group size, and the structure of interactions. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency only when monitoring is efficient and not too costly, and that the marginal effect of punishment is lower or negative when monitoring is costly or noise is high. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, so findings should be transferred with caution.

- source: Stahl, D. O. (2011). Cooperation in the sporadically repeated prisoners' dilemma via reputation mechanisms. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in the SRPD, only a small subset of reputation mechanisms can sustain cooperation and achieve efficient outcomes. Specifically, mechanisms that distinguish between justified punishment and defection, and allow for penitence, can support equilibria where the long-run group payoff matches the fully cooperative outcome. The analysis shows that, with these mechanisms and high enough continuation probability, the population evolves toward full cooperation and efficiency. In contrast, most reputation mechanisms result in universal defection and zero efficiency. The paper also extends the analysis to clan-based (group) settings, showing that the efficiency result generalizes when within-group matching is sufficiently likely.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that the structure of the punishment/reputation mechanism is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated social dilemmas. If the punishment mechanism is designed to avoid penalizing justified punishers and allows for penitence, and if the continuation probability is high, then enabling punishment can move efficiency from zero (universal defection) to the full-cooperation benchmark. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the details of the punishment/reputation update rule and the effective 'delta' (continuation probability) are crucial for forecasting the efficiency impact of punishment. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, anonymous-matching games with public reputations, but the logic may transfer to PGG-like settings with similar reputation mechanisms.

- source: Chassang, S., & Takahashi, S. (2011). Robustness to incomplete information in repeated games. *Theoretical Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated prisoners' dilemma games, the set of robustly sustainable equilibrium payoffs (i.e., efficiency) is sharply affected by concerns about incomplete information. Under complete information, grim-trigger strategies can sustain cooperation if the discounted value of future cooperation exceeds the temptation to defect. However, when robustness to incomplete information is required, the cost of being the 'sucker' (cooperating while the other defects) becomes critical, and the set of sustainable payoffs can shrink dramatically. Selective-punishment strategies, which punish only the deviator, are more robust than grim-trigger strategies, and can sustain cooperation under a wider range of parameters. The paper provides explicit characterizations of when efficient cooperation is robustly sustainable and when only inefficient outcomes (permanent defection) are robust. The main outcomes are theoretical characterizations of efficiency/welfare as a function of game parameters and strategy types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated prisoners' dilemma–like games under incomplete information. It shows that the design of the punishment mechanism (grim-trigger vs. selective punishment), the cost of being punished, and the enforceability of asymmetric outcomes are critical moderators. If only symmetric cooperation (CC) is enforceable, robust efficiency collapses to permanent defection. If asymmetric outcomes (DC or CD) are enforceable, robust efficiency can be as high as in the complete information case. The results are directly about efficiency/welfare, not just contribution rates. For prediction, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment scheme and the game's payoff structure, especially under incomplete information. However, the results are for repeated prisoners' dilemma, not public goods games per se, so transfer to PGGs should be done with caution.
