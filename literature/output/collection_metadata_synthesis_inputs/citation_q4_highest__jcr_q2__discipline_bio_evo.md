- source: Archetti, M., & Scheuring, I. (2011). COEXISTENCE OF COOPERATION AND DEFECTION IN PUBLIC GOODS GAMES. *Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that the structure of the public goods function (linear vs. nonlinear/threshold) fundamentally changes the equilibrium outcome. In nonlinear/threshold (Volunteer's Dilemma-like) games, cooperation and defection can coexist at a stable mixed equilibrium, and the public good is produced with positive probability, even in one-shot, well-mixed populations without relatedness or repeated play. In linear (NPD) games, only defection is stable in the absence of assortment. The paper provides analytical and numerical results for the equilibrium frequency of cooperation and the probability of public good provision as a function of group size, cost/benefit ratio, and the threshold parameter. No punishment, reward, or external enforcement mechanisms are modeled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games as a function of the public good production function (linear vs. nonlinear/threshold), group size, and cost/benefit ratio, but it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions. It provides direct theoretical predictions for efficiency and group payoff in the absence of punishment, but cannot inform the effect of enabling punishment. For downstream prediction tasks focused on the effect of punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it is valuable for understanding baseline efficiency in control conditions without punishment, especially in games with nonlinear or threshold public goods functions.

- source: Gardner, A., & West, S. A. (2004). Cooperation and punishment, especially in humans. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment can promote and stabilize cooperation in public-goods-like environments, leading to higher group fitness (efficiency), provided that individuals who punish tend to receive more cooperation. The cost of punishment decreases as cooperation increases, making punishment more sustainable in cooperative populations. Relatedness between individuals is not required for punishment to be favored; instead, the key is a positive association between an individual's punishment and the cooperation she receives. The model predicts that punishment is more easily maintained than initially evolved, and that small group size and effective punishment (where the cost of being punished exceeds the cost of cooperation) facilitate the evolutionary stability of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games can increase efficiency, especially when punishment is effective (i.e., the penalty for being punished is greater than the cost of cooperation) and when there is a mechanism (such as behavioral adjustment) that links punishment to increased cooperation received. The findings suggest that, all else equal, introducing punishment should increase group efficiency, particularly in smaller groups and when punishment is not too costly to the punisher. However, the results are derived from theoretical models rather than empirical data, so quantitative predictions require caution. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is not dependent on relatedness or repeated interactions, but on the structure of behavioral associations.

- source: Lehmann, L., Rousset, F., Roze, D., & Keller, L. (2007). Strong reciprocity or strong ferocity? A population genetic view of the evolution of altruistic punishment. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that strong reciprocity (costly helping plus costly punishment) can evolve in spatially structured populations if helping and punishment are tightly linked (no recombination) and relatedness is sufficiently high. In this case, strong reciprocity can invade even when rare. When helping and punishment are unlinked (recombination is strong), neither costly helping nor costly punishment can invade from rarity unless punishment yields a direct fitness benefit to the actor. The model shows that kin selection (genetic or cultural) is necessary for the evolution of strong reciprocity. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide direct predictions about group efficiency or payoff, but rather about the evolutionary stability and spread of strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into the evolutionary preconditions for the emergence of punishment and cooperation in public-goods-like settings, especially the importance of linkage between helping and punishment, population structure (relatedness), and direct fitness effects. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions about group efficiency or payoff with and without punishment, nor does it report outcomes directly comparable to experimental public goods games. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting that punishment is unlikely to increase cooperation or efficiency in anonymous, unstructured, or one-shot settings unless there is kinship or direct benefit to punishers. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative.

- source: Henrich, J., & Henrich, N. (2006). Culture, evolution and the puzzle of human cooperation. *Cognitive Systems Research*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that punishment, especially when combined with cultural learning mechanisms (such as conformist and prestige-biased transmission), can stabilize costly cooperative norms in human groups. It reviews theoretical models showing that the effectiveness of punishment in maintaining cooperation depends on the cost of punishing relative to the cost of being punished, and that social norms enforced by punishment can persist even when the cooperative act is individually costly. The paper also discusses how group-level selection can favor groups with more effective cooperative norms, and how cultural evolution allows for rapid adaptation of cooperation strategies. However, the paper does not provide empirical or quantitative findings on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on the mechanisms and evolutionary logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that punishment can stabilize cooperation in public goods-like environments, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly relative to the cost of being punished. It highlights the importance of social norms, cultural learning, and group structure in moderating the effects of punishment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the magnitude of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. Its value for prediction lies in its mechanistic insights and identification of key moderators (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, norm strength), but not in providing direct outcome estimates.

Neuroimaging studies show that punishment threats activate cognitive control regions and increase cooperative behavior, particularly among self-regarding or Machiavellian individuals. The review also highlights that individual differences (e.g., social value orientation, Machiavellianism) moderate the impact of punishment and incentives on cooperation. However, the paper does not directly report or synthesize efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral and neural responses.
  prediction_guidance: This review provides strong theoretical and mechanistic support for the idea that punishment increases cooperation in public-goods-game-like environments, especially for self-regarding individuals. It suggests that the effect of punishment is likely to be more pronounced when extrinsic incentives are salient and among participants with self-regarding preferences. However, because the review does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its value for prediction is primarily in identifying moderators (e.g., individual differences, presence of incentives, trust signals) and in supporting the general mechanism by which punishment increases cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used to inform the likely direction and moderators of punishment effects, but not to calibrate the magnitude of efficiency changes.

- source: Gelcich, S., Guzman, R., Rodríguez-Sickert, C., Castilla, J. C., & Cárdenas, J. C. (2013). Exploring External Validity of Common Pool Resource Experiments: Insights from Artisanal Benthic Fisheries in Chile. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: This paper reports a field-lab experiment with Chilean artisanal fishers playing a 20-round common pool resource game. In the first 10 rounds (open access, no enforcement), overharvesting increased and efficiency declined, especially among low-performance unions and nonunionized fishers. In rounds 11-20, a weak enforcement regime was introduced: two players per round were randomly inspected, and if caught overharvesting, their entire round's harvest was confiscated. This sanction led to a sharp reduction in overharvest and a large increase in group earnings (efficiency) among unionized fishers, especially those from high-performance unions, who sustained cooperation and high efficiency through the end of the game. Low-performance unions showed a temporary improvement but cooperation eroded over time. Nonunionized fishers did not respond to enforcement and remained inefficient. The paper provides direct evidence that even weak sanctions can improve efficiency in CPR settings, but the effect is strongly moderated by group social capital and prior cooperative norms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment/sanction mechanism (even if weak and probabilistic) in a repeated CPR game with continuous contribution/extraction choices and no communication can substantially increase group efficiency, especially when baseline efficiency is not already at floor. The effect is strongest in groups with high social capital or prior cooperative norms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment in similar CPR/Pgg-like games will increase efficiency, with the magnitude depending on group composition and baseline cooperativeness. The design features—5 players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous extraction, no rewards, and a probabilistic, high-impact punishment—are all relevant for mapping to prediction. The control (no punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while the treatment (punishment enabled) yields much higher efficiency for unionized groups.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2014). Rewards and the evolution of cooperation in public good games. *Biology Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in threshold public good games, voluntary reward funds can evolve and lead to efficient cooperation, overcoming coordination problems that typically prevent the evolution of cooperation in large, anonymous groups. The model shows that, regardless of initial conditions, rewarders can invade a population of defectors if the reward fund's return is sufficiently high, leading to a cascade where cooperators eventually dominate. The resulting group payoff and average fitness are higher with rewards than without, and the system can reach full cooperation or stable mixed equilibria depending on the risk and threshold parameters. The paper contrasts this with institutional punishment, which is argued to be less effective or more difficult to initiate in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that introducing a reward mechanism in threshold public good games will increase group efficiency and average payoff, especially in settings where coordination is otherwise difficult. While it does not directly analyze punishment, it offers indirect evidence that reward mechanisms can be more effective than punishment in some coordination contexts. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a reward mechanism (with sufficient effectiveness and sustainability) should increase efficiency relative to control. The model's explicit treatment of group size, threshold, risk, and reward parameters can inform how these dimensions moderate the effect of rewards on efficiency.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that the ability to punish defectors, combined with the visibility of punishment reputation, leads to higher cooperation and higher group payoffs. Punishers are compensated for their costs through increased cooperation from others, and in some groups, punishers even achieve net benefits. When punishment or its reputation is removed, cooperation and payoffs decline. The effect is driven by the threat of punishment and the reputational benefits it brings, not by the mere display of past punishment behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment, especially when punishment reputation is visible, increases group payoffs and compensates punishers, thus improving efficiency-related outcomes. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment and the visibility of punishment reputation are key moderators. The game is not a standard public goods game but a helping game with punishment and reputation, so direct quantitative transfer to PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative effect of punishment on efficiency is robustly positive when reputation is present.

- source: Fletcher, J. A., & Zwick, M. (2006). Unifying the theories of inclusive fitness and reciprocal altruism. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper demonstrates that the evolution of cooperation (altruism) in both kin and non-kin contexts can be predicted by a generalized Hamilton's rule that incorporates the covariance between genotypes and the cooperative behaviors of others, as well as nonadditive (synergistic) effects. In iterated prisoner's dilemma models, conditional strategies like tit-for-tat can increase in frequency when the benefit received from others' cooperation exceeds the cost, as predicted by Queller's rule. The same logic applies to mutualistic interactions between species. The paper shows that synergy (nonadditivity) and conditional behavior can both facilitate the evolution of cooperation, even in the absence of kinship or repeated interactions. Punishment is discussed as a possible mechanism for positive assortment but is not modeled. The findings are theoretical and do not provide empirical estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical background for understanding how mechanisms like punishment, synergy, and conditional behavior can affect the evolution of cooperation and group efficiency, but it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It suggests that any mechanism (including punishment) that increases positive assortment or synergy can increase the likelihood that cooperation evolves, but does not specify the magnitude or direction of efficiency changes in experimental settings. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper is useful for mechanistic context but not for direct quantitative prediction.
