- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., & Villatoro, D. (2013). Punish and Voice: Punishment Enhances Cooperation when Combined with Norm-Signalling. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with 4-person groups over 30 rounds, the study compares three treatments: (1) punishment only, (2) norm-signaling (message) only, and (3) both punishment and norm-signaling (sanction). In the critical rounds where treatments differ (rounds 11-20), the sanction treatment yields significantly higher average net earnings (31% higher than punishment, 16% higher than message) and higher contributions, with less frequent and less intense punishment. The efficiency gains in the sanction treatment are attributed to the combination of explicit norm communication and the credible threat of punishment, which together increase norm salience and reduce the need for costly punishment. The findings are robust in both human experiments and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with costly punishment, the addition of norm-signaling (explicit communication of normative expectations) to punishment substantially increases group efficiency compared to punishment alone. The efficiency gains are due to both higher cooperation and lower punishment costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of norm-signaling or communication channels that make norms salient can be a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of punishment, the ability to send norm-signaling messages, and the structure of feedback about others' actions. The control efficiency (without punishment or norm-signaling) is lower than in the combined treatment. The results imply that, all else equal, enabling both punishment and norm-signaling will yield higher efficiency than enabling punishment alone.

- source: Wu, J. H., Balliet, D., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2016). Gossip Versus Punishment: The Efficiency of Reputation to Promote and Maintain Cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares the effects of punishment and gossip on cooperation and efficiency in a four-round public goods game. Punishment (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3) did not significantly increase overall cooperation and significantly reduced individual earnings (efficiency) compared to control. Gossip (costless) increased both cooperation and efficiency. When both mechanisms were present, gossip offset the negative efficiency impact of punishment. The study also finds that gossip, but not punishment, has positive spillover effects on trust and trustworthiness in a subsequent trust game. The results suggest that punishment, at moderate cost, is less efficient than gossip for promoting cooperation, and that gossip can mitigate the efficiency losses from punishment.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, four-round PGG with a punishment mechanism (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3, anonymous, no chat, continuous contributions), enabling punishment reduces efficiency (total/individual earnings) compared to control. The negative effect is robust, and the presence of a costless gossip mechanism can counteract this loss. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG designs, especially when punishment is moderately costly and anonymous. The results also suggest that the efficiency cost of punishment is not offset by increased cooperation, except possibly in the final rounds, and that gossip is a more efficient mechanism for promoting cooperation.

- source: Gross, J., Méder, Z. Z., Okamoto-Barth, S., & Riedl, A. (2016). Building the Leviathan - Voluntary centralisation of punishment power sustains cooperation in humans. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study introduces a 'power transfer game'—a repeated public goods game with punishment, where group members can voluntarily transfer their punishment power to others. In groups of five, three conditions were compared: (1) endogenous (voluntary) power transfer, (2) exogenous (mirrored, but not voluntary) power transfer, and (3) fixed (no transfer, standard peer punishment). Only the endogenous condition, where group members could choose whom to empower, led to sustained or increasing cooperation and higher group earnings, approaching the social optimum. In the fixed and exogenous conditions, cooperation and group earnings declined over time. The mechanism works because non-punishing cooperators delegate power to those willing to punish, and centralisation of power in the hands of pro-social punishers deters free riding. The study provides direct evidence that voluntary centralisation of punishment power can efficiently sustain cooperation and group welfare in public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with peer punishment, allowing voluntary centralisation of punishment power (i.e., enabling group members to transfer their punishment effectiveness to others) substantially increases group efficiency compared to standard decentralised peer punishment. The effect is not observed when centralisation is imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a voluntary power transfer mechanism is a key moderator: with it, efficiency is much higher than in standard peer punishment, even when the underlying MPCR, punishment cost, and other parameters are held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in games with and without voluntary centralisation of punishment power.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Drouvelis, M., & Grosskopf, B. (2016). The effects of induced emotions on pro-social behaviour. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study uses a one-shot voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) public goods game with and without punishment, manipulating both the announcement of punishment and participants' emotional state (anger vs. happiness). The main findings are: (1) Announcing the punishment stage increases contributions and average net earnings, confirming that punishment can improve efficiency in the short run. (2) Induced anger reduces contributions and increases the severity of punishment, leading to lower average net earnings (i.e., lower efficiency) compared to the happy condition. (3) The efficiency benefit of punishment is present but is diminished when subjects are angry, as harsher punishment increases costs and reduces net group payoffs. (4) The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured directly via average net earnings, and the design allows for clean causal inference due to the one-shot structure and between-subjects manipulation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a one-shot, three-player VCM with punishment, the introduction of a punishment stage (when announced) increases group efficiency (average net earnings) compared to the same game without punishment. The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends on the emotional state of participants: it is larger when subjects are happy and smaller (but still positive) when subjects are angry, due to increased punishment costs. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, controlling for baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the effect can be moderated by factors (such as mood) that affect the severity and frequency of punishment.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., & Masclet, D. (2015). Emotion venting and punishment in public good experiments. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard 4-player, 10-round public goods game with punishment (costly to punisher, more costly to target), the authors introduce several treatments allowing for different levels of emotion venting before punishment decisions: (1) Baseline (no venting), (2) Waiting (5-min cooling-off), (3) Waiting & Emotion (cooling-off plus self-reported emotions and unsent messages), and (4) Virtual Punishment (all previous plus non-binding virtual punishment). All treatments have punishment enabled. The main findings are: (a) All venting treatments reduce the amount and frequency of costly punishment compared to baseline; (b) The simple cooling-off period (Waiting) increases final payoffs (efficiency) relative to baseline, as reduced excessive punishment outweighs any loss in contributions; (c) More extensive venting (Virtual Punishment) reduces both punishment and contributions, and only increases efficiency in the early rounds, not in the long run; (d) The relationship between emotion venting and efficiency is non-linear (inverted-U): moderate venting is optimal for efficiency, while too little or too much venting is suboptimal. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so all results are relative to punishment-enabled baselines.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard lab PGG with punishment, the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the emotional context of punishment decisions. If punishment is enabled without any venting, excessive punishment can reduce efficiency. Introducing a simple cooling-off period (moderate venting) reduces excessive punishment and increases efficiency. However, if venting is too extensive (e.g., virtual punishment), punishment is reduced too much, contributions fall, and efficiency gains are not sustained. For prediction, this suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is maximized when the game design includes a moderate opportunity for emotional cooling-off before punishment, but not when venting is so extensive that it undermines the deterrence function of punishment. The results are most relevant for standard 4-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGGs with punishment enabled and no communication.

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2013). Enforcement of contribution norms in public good games with heterogeneous populations. *Games and Economic Behavior*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in the absence of punishment, public good groups (both homogeneous and heterogeneous) rapidly converge to low contributions and low efficiency, regardless of the normative appeal of efficiency or fairness rules. When punishment is enabled, contributions and efficiency increase sharply in all group types. About 40% of groups achieve full efficiency (maximal contributions by all) with punishment, while the rest enforce different relative contribution norms depending on group heterogeneity (e.g., equal contributions, proportional to endowment). The increase in efficiency from punishment is substantial and robust, though the exact efficiency level and norm enforced depend on the group structure. The paper provides direct evidence that punishment enables groups to overcome the collective action problem and coordinate on efficient or near-efficient outcomes, even in the presence of heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in linear public good games with three players and ten rounds leads to a large increase in group efficiency, regardless of whether the group is homogeneous or heterogeneous in endowments or marginal benefits. The effect is robust: without punishment, efficiency is low due to free-riding; with punishment, efficiency rises substantially, with many groups achieving full efficiency. The specific norm enforced (equal contributions, proportionality, etc.) depends on the group’s heterogeneity, but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar lab PGG designs, enabling punishment will significantly increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by group heterogeneity and the specific norm that emerges.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2014). Self-Organization for Collective Action: An Experimental Study of Voting on Sanction Regimes. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, groups could vote between no sanctions (NS), informal sanctions (IS: peer punishment), and formal sanctions (FS: centralized punishment with fixed and variable costs, deterrent or non-deterrent). Both IS and deterrent FS substantially increased group efficiency and earnings relative to NS. IS were highly cost-effective, yielding higher returns per point spent on sanctions than FS in most conditions. IS were especially effective when chosen by vote, with group earnings and efficiency about 30% higher than when IS were imposed exogenously. Non-deterrent FS also increased efficiency when chosen by vote, but less so than IS. High fixed costs for FS reduced both their efficiency and their popularity. Reducing the effectiveness of IS (increasing cost per unit punishment) decreased their efficiency somewhat, but IS remained popular and relatively effective. Allowing IS and FS to coexist did not substantially change these patterns. Overall, IS can be as or more effective than FS in promoting efficiency, especially when adopted democratically.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with 5 players, enabling informal peer punishment (IS) can increase group efficiency to levels comparable to or exceeding those achieved by formal sanctions, especially when IS are chosen by vote. The efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost-effectiveness of the sanction regime, the fixed and variable costs of punishment, and whether the regime is adopted endogenously. High fixed costs for FS reduce their efficiency advantage. IS are most effective when punishment is relatively cheap and well-targeted. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling IS is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, and that the effect is robust to moderate changes in punishment technology and institutional coexistence. The control (no-sanction) efficiency is typically well below full cooperation, while IS and deterrent FS can approach or reach high efficiency, with IS often being more cost-effective.

- source: Kölle, F. (2015). Heterogeneity and cooperation: The role of capability and valuation on public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: homogeneous groups, groups with one high-valuation member (valuation heterogeneity), and groups with one high-capability member (capability heterogeneity). Each group plays 20 rounds, with punishment enabled in rounds 11-20. Efficiency (group payoff as % of social optimum) is measured with and without punishment. In homogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly change efficiency. In valuation-heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase efficiency and increases inequality. In capability-heterogeneous groups, punishment significantly increases efficiency (from 86% to 93% of optimum) and reduces inequality. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus conditional on the type of heterogeneity, being most effective when group members differ in capability but not in valuation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform but depends on the type of group heterogeneity. If the game is homogeneous or has only capability heterogeneity (where some players' contributions are more productive but all value the public good equally), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially in the capability-heterogeneous case. If the group has valuation heterogeneity (where some players value the public good more), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency and increases inequality. The control efficiency (without punishment) is also higher in capability-heterogeneous groups than in valuation-heterogeneous groups. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, the type of heterogeneity (capability vs. valuation) is a critical moderator. Other design features (e.g., punishment cost, group size, feedback) are held constant in this study.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.
