- source: Thöni, C. (2014). Inequality aversion and antisocial punishment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that, while inequality aversion can theoretically motivate antisocial punishment under certain conditions, empirical data from several public goods game experiments indicate that most antisocial punishment acts do not fit this explanation. Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The findings suggest that other motives, such as revenge or a desire to harm others, are more likely explanations for antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

- source: Spitzer, M. L. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper does not present new empirical findings but critiques the interpretability of Kocher and Matzat's experimental results. It notes that subjects' preferences over punishment and reward mechanisms are difficult to interpret due to the multiplicity of possible utility functions and equilibria. The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. The author suggests that simpler experimental designs would make it easier to interpret the effects of institutional choices on cooperation and contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-good games. However, it offers important cautionary notes: (1) the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism (e.g., cost, impact, available choices); (2) subject beliefs and the complexity of institutional choice can obscure the relationship between mechanism and outcome; (3) the presence of multiple equilibria under different utility functions makes it difficult to infer efficiency effects from observed preferences or voting patterns. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

- source: Lee, J. H., & Iwasa, Y. (2014). Modeling socio-economic aspects of ecosystem management and biodiversity conservation. *Population Ecology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_tech
  findings: The paper's third model demonstrates that in social dilemma situations (akin to public goods or common-pool resource games), introducing punishment for selfish behavior can increase group welfare, especially when punishment is graduated (i.e., increases with the harm caused). The model shows that with evaluation errors and heterogeneity in player sensitivity to utility, graduated punishment is optimal for maximizing efficiency (group payoff). Severe, non-graduated punishment is only optimal under unrealistic assumptions. The findings support the use of graduated sanctions to improve efficiency in resource management games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public-goods-like environments with social dilemmas, enabling punishment (especially graduated punishment) increases group efficiency/welfare compared to no-punishment baselines, provided that punishment is not excessively severe and is sensitive to the harm caused. The model is abstract but maps closely to PGG-like games with two actions (cooperate/defect), and the efficiency outcome is directly analyzed. The findings are most applicable to settings where punishment can be graduated and where player heterogeneity and evaluation errors exist. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it gives qualitative guidance that enabling (graduated) punishment should increase efficiency relative to control.

- source: Voelkl, B. (2015). The evolution of generalized reciprocity in social interaction networks. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis demonstrates that introducing an effective penalty for unfaithfulness (selling outside the cooperative) leads to stable, efficient cooperative outcomes where all members sell to the cooperative. If the penalty is limited (not strong enough), full cooperation is unstable. When the penalty is strong (exceeds the extra profit from defection), the system converges to full cooperation, maximizing group payoff. Redistribution of penalties among faithful members further strengthens this effect, leading to a unique, stable Nash equilibrium with high efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions under which punishment stabilizes cooperation and maximizes group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public-goods-like settings with continuous contribution and the possibility of punishment, increasing the effectiveness and/or strength of punishment increases group efficiency and stabilizes cooperation. The results are directly relevant for predicting that enabling or strengthening punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is strong enough to outweigh the gains from defection. The model also highlights that redistributing penalties among cooperators can further enhance efficiency and stability. However, the results are derived from a stylized, theoretical model rather than empirical data, and the setting is a marketing cooperative with continuous contribution rather than a canonical PGG, though the structure is closely related.

- source: Blonski, M., & Spagnolo, G. (2015). Prisoners' other Dilemma. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, the riskiness of cooperation equilibria can be precisely characterized by comparing the Nash-products of cooperation and defection equilibria. The least risky cooperation equilibrium is always the grim trigger strategy. There exists a critical discount factor (delta*) above which cooperation can be supported without strategic risk, and below which all cooperation equilibria are risky. The more patient the players (higher discount factor), the more forgiving the punishment strategies can be while still supporting cooperation. The paper also introduces the concept of 'risk perfection', requiring that not only the equilibrium path but also all subgames (punishment phases) are not risky. The results generalize to asymmetric games and to repeated Cournot duopoly (collusion) games, which are structurally similar to public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on the relationship between punishment harshness, forgiveness, and the viability of cooperation in repeated games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it shows that the ability of punishment to support cooperation depends on the discount factor and the riskiness of the equilibrium. In environments structurally similar to public goods games, the results suggest that harsher punishments (like grim trigger) are less risky and more likely to support cooperation, especially when players are less patient. As patience increases, more forgiving punishments become viable. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the strategic riskiness of the environment and the patience of players, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

- source: Forges, F., Horst, U., & Salomon, A. (2016). Feasibility and individual rationality in two-person Bayesian games. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in two-person Bayesian games with a single informed player, the existence of a uniform punishment strategy (UPS) for the uninformed player guarantees the existence of feasible, individually rational (i.e., efficient/welfare-maximizing) solutions. These solutions can be implemented as Nash equilibria of appropriately designed noncooperative games. The set of such payoffs coincides with the set of joint plan equilibrium payoffs in the infinitely repeated game. When UPS does not hold, feasible, individually rational solutions may not exist. The results generalize to cases where both players have private information, under certain conditions. The paper provides theoretical foundations for how punishment strategies can support efficient outcomes in games with incomplete information, but does not provide empirical estimates or experimental results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that the ability to punish (specifically, the existence of a uniform punishment strategy) is crucial for achieving efficient, individually rational outcomes in two-person Bayesian games with incomplete information. For the downstream prediction task, the main implication is that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (punishment cost, punishment tech, and the ability to credibly threaten punishment) are key moderators of whether efficiency gains from punishment are possible. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical estimates, and its results are not specific to public goods games or experimental designs. It is most useful for understanding the theoretical boundary conditions under which punishment can support efficiency, rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes in experimental PGG-like environments.

- source: Golman, R. (2016). Good manners: signaling social preferences. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper develops a formal model of social preferences that incorporates both other-regarding preferences and social image concerns, showing that these together create incentives for individuals to credibly signal their social preferences through both actions (such as contributing to a public good, punishing, or rewarding) and communication (cheap talk). The model predicts that individuals will use both costly and costless signals to communicate their type (altruistic or spiteful), and that the availability of communication can affect the use of costly signals like punishment or reward. The paper discusses, but does not empirically test, the implications for public goods games, including the idea that punishment may serve as a signal of ill will and that communication may substitute for punishment. No direct findings on efficiency or payoff outcomes are provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions relevant to forecasting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its main contribution is a theoretical framework for understanding how social preferences and signaling motives may influence the use of punishment, reward, and communication. While it suggests that communication opportunities may reduce the use of costly punishment (and vice versa), and that punishment can serve as a signal of ill will, it does not provide data or direct analysis of efficiency outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative, mainly by highlighting possible mechanisms and moderators (such as the availability of communication or the signaling value of punishment/reward), but not by providing parameter estimates or outcome data.

- source: Beraldo, S., & Sugden, R. (2016). The emergence of reciprocally beneficial cooperation. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing
  findings: The model demonstrates that when participation is voluntary and the payoff to mutual cooperation is stochastic (sometimes exceeding the benefit from cheating), equilibria can exist in which both cooperation and cheating are present, but the expected payoff (efficiency) is strictly positive compared to non-participation. The frequency of cooperation and the value of the game (efficiency) increase as the distribution of cooperative benefits becomes more favorable. The model does not include punishment or sanctions, and thus does not address their effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It is a theoretical model of cooperation with voluntary participation and stochastic payoffs, but without any punishment or sanction mechanism. Therefore, it does not inform prediction of treatment efficiency from the game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment. However, it does provide theoretical support for the importance of voluntary participation and payoff structure in sustaining efficient cooperation.
