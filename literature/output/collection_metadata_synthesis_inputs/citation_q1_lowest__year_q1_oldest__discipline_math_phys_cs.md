- source: Liu, X. S., & Guo, W. L. (2010). Persistent cooperators in nature. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with compulsory participation, if contributors are willing to pay a second cost to retrieve a portion of the public good (persistent cooperation), this can functionally act as punishment and promote cooperation. When the second cost is not too high relative to the benefit, persistent cooperators can dominate the population, leading to higher average payoffs and more stable cooperation. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of benefit-to-cost ratios and the size of the second cost) under which persistent cooperation is evolutionarily stable, risk-dominant, or advantageous. When the second cost is high, persistent cooperators act as traditional costly punishers and are less favored. The abundance of persistent cooperators is used as a proxy for group welfare and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (modeled as a second cost paid by persistent cooperators) can increase the prevalence of cooperation and, by extension, group efficiency, as long as the cost of punishment is not too high relative to the benefit. The model gives explicit parameter thresholds for when punishment is beneficial. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling punishment (with moderate cost and sufficient benefit) in a public goods game is likely to increase efficiency compared to a control without punishment, especially when the punishment cost is not excessive. The model also highlights the importance of the ratio between the benefit of cooperation, the cost of contributing, and the cost of punishment in determining the outcome. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions of efficiency, and its results are theoretical rather than empirical.

- source: Zhao, L., Ye, X. J., Huang, Z. G., Sun, J. T., Yang, L., Do, Y., & Wang, Y. H. (2010). Promotion of cooperation in the form C0C1D classified by 'degree grads' in a scale-free network. *Journal of Statistical Mechanics-theory and Experiment*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in scale-free networks, cooperation can persist even when the PGG enhancement factor r < 1, due to a mechanism where high-degree ('hub') cooperators (C0) are imitated by their neighbors (C1), who may receive negative payoffs but still support the hub. The average payoff of agents is analyzed as a function of their network position (degree grads), showing that only the hubs (C0) receive positive payoffs, while most other cooperators (C1) have negative payoffs. The system-level average payoff increases with r, but the persistence of cooperation at low r is due to network structure and imitation, not payoff incentives. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present or analyzed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency or payoff in public goods games. It is useful for understanding how network structure and imitation can sustain cooperation and affect payoff distribution, but it does not inform predictions about the impact of enabling punishment. For the downstream prediction task (treatment efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is not directly relevant, as punishment is not present or discussed as a mechanism.

- source: Xiao, Y., & Hua, D. Y. (2012). Promotion of Cooperation in a Spatial Public Goods Game with Long Range Learning and Mobility. *Chinese Physics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that the evolution of cooperation in a spatial public goods game is strongly affected by the interplay of population density, learning range, and mobility range. When the learning range is larger than the mobility range, small local groups at low population density support high cooperation. At moderate densities, mobility allows cooperators to escape defector invasions, supporting stable cooperation. When the mobility range is larger than the learning range, high population density enables the formation of compact cooperator domains, which also promotes cooperation. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators; no efficiency or group payoff results are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism. It is relevant for understanding how spatial structure, mobility, and learning range affect cooperation rates, but not for payoff-based efficiency outcomes or the effect of punishment. Therefore, it should not be weighted heavily in predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment.

- source: Sigmund, K., & Hilbe, C. (2011). Darwin and the Evolution of Human Cooperation. *Principles of Evolution: from the Planck Epoch to Complex Multicellular Life*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The chapter synthesizes theoretical models showing that cooperation in public goods games is difficult to sustain without mechanisms for targeted punishment or reward. When such mechanisms are available, cooperation can be stabilized at high levels. The chapter also reviews the evolutionary logic behind kin selection, reciprocity, and group selection, and discusses the cognitive and informational demands of indirect reciprocity and moral systems. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical data on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in public goods games, suggesting that their presence is likely to increase cooperation rates. However, because the chapter does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform quantitative predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. Its value lies in contextualizing why and when punishment might be effective, rather than in providing parameter-specific guidance for efficiency prediction.

- source: Billard, E. A. (1996). Evolutionary strategies of stochastic learning automata in the prisoner's dilemma. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper argues that cooperation in resource dilemmas (including public goods and commons dilemmas) is shaped by individual value orientation (pro-social vs egoistic), situational constraints (such as group identification, communication, and social norms), and the degree and type of uncertainty (social and environmental). It proposes that pro-social individuals are more likely to cooperate regardless of uncertainty, while egoistic individuals are more likely to defect, especially under uncertainty. Situational factors (including the possibility of sanctions or rewards) can constrain egoism and promote cooperation. The paper reviews prior findings that rewards increase cooperation and sanctions decrease defection, but notes that the costs of such interventions may outweigh collective gains and that effects may not persist when interventions are removed. The analysis is theoretical and hypothesis-generating, not empirical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual background on the psychological mechanisms that may moderate the effect of punishment, reward, and uncertainty on cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It suggests that punishment and reward can increase cooperation, but may not always improve efficiency due to their costs, and that their effects may not persist. The paper does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of situational constraints (including punishment/reward), group identification, communication, and uncertainty, and in warning that interventions may have costs that offset their benefits. However, it does not provide direct evidence for the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.

- source: Tao, J., Zhong, X., & Lu, Y. F. (2011). Restrictive mechanism of flow control among non-cooperative Internet users. *Science China-information Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated flow control games, the use of punishment strategies (including trigger, tit-for-tat, and finite punishment) can deter selfish deviations and sustain cooperation among users, leading to optimal or near-optimal system utility. In the absence of such mechanisms, the equilibrium is inefficient. The FCAR algorithm, which operationalizes finite punishment, is shown via simulation to effectively regularize user behavior and maintain high system utility, with only temporary utility losses during punishment. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the number of users and the length of punishment. In finitely repeated games, cooperation is harder to sustain, especially in the final stage, but rational punishment strategies can still maintain cooperation for most of the game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in repeated resource allocation games (adjacent to public goods games) increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the number of users, provided the punishment is sufficiently strong and the game is sufficiently long or indefinite. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency, especially when users are patient (high discount factor) and punishment is coordinated. The results are most directly applicable to repeated, continuous-action, non-anonymous settings with full information about others' actions and payoffs.

- source: Evans, R., & Thomas, J. P. (2001). Cooperation and punishment. *Econometrica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper proves that in repeated common interest games (with no discounting), the mere presence of cooperative (nondraconian) strategies in the set of possible strategies does not guarantee efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcomes in equilibrium. However, if the set of possible strategies includes at least one 'draconian' strategy (i.e., a strategy that is willing to punish noncooperation to the maximum extent, such as min-maxing the opponent forever after a deviation), then all Nash equilibria are efficient. The paper also shows that this result is robust to certain computability restrictions and discusses the limits of these results under discounting and other technical assumptions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated games with a structure similar to public goods or common interest games, the availability of sufficiently severe punishment (i.e., the ability to impose maximal punishment at low or no cost) is both necessary and sufficient for punishment to induce efficient outcomes in equilibrium. If the game design allows for draconian punishment strategies, then enabling punishment should lead to high efficiency, regardless of the control efficiency. If only mild or 'cooperative' punishment is possible, efficiency gains are not guaranteed. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted games, but the logic is relevant for understanding the role of punishment severity and credibility in other settings.

- source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

- source: Jones, M. A. (1999). The effect of punishment duration of trigger strategies and quasifinite continuation probabilities for Prisoners' Dilemmas. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.

- source: Castro, L., Serrano, J. M., & Toro, M. A. (1998). Conceptual capacity to categorize and the evolution of altruism. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the Assessor strategy, which incorporates a punishment-like mechanism (reprobation) based on observed or reported selfish behavior, achieves higher fitness (i.e., greater group efficiency or payoff) than standard reciprocal strategies like Tit-for-Tat or Observer Tit-for-Tat when competing against always-defect strategies. This advantage is robust across both unstructured and family-structured populations, provided certain thresholds of cooperation frequency are met. The Assessor strategy is especially effective when individuals can observe or be informed about others' reprobation of selfish behavior, allowing them to withhold cooperation from defectors more efficiently. The model predicts that such punishment-like mechanisms can support higher levels of cooperation and group payoff than reciprocity alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment or sanctioning mechanisms (here, reprobation) in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency/payoff relative to control conditions without such mechanisms. The findings are most directly relevant to iterated prisoner's dilemma settings, which are adjacent to public goods games, and suggest that the ability to observe and act on others' punishment signals is a key moderator. For prediction tasks, the results imply that, all else equal, introducing a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, especially when players can observe or learn about others' punishment actions. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide quantitative effect sizes or empirical validation.

- source: Robert, J. M., Otrok, H., Quttoum, A. N., & Boukhris, R. (2012). A distributed resource management model for Virtual Private Networks: Tit-for-Tat strategies. *Computer Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: Simulation results show that classical Tit-for-Tat fails in this asynchronous, stochastic setting, leading to deadlocks and low total gain. Generous Tit-for-Tat avoids deadlocks and achieves high group gain, but is vulnerable to exploitation by selfish participants. Altruistic and reputation-based strategies can effectively punish selfish participants, forcing them to cooperate to receive benefits. The best strategies (reputation-based and altruistic-reputation) allow cooperative participants to achieve near-optimal total gain, while selfish participants are excluded unless they match cooperative behavior. The findings are robust across variations in the number of cooperative/selfish participants and the probability of offering resources.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment-like mechanisms (retaliation, exclusion, reputation-based refusal) can substantially increase group efficiency in repeated resource-sharing games with asynchronous moves and stochastic cooperation. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment or exclusion in public-goods-like environments, especially when cooperation is probabilistic and not always feasible. The specific game structure differs from standard PGGs (asynchronous, resource lending/borrowing, not simultaneous contribution), but the payoff logic and the effect of punishment on group efficiency are closely analogous. The paper supports the prediction that enabling effective punishment or exclusion will increase efficiency, especially when selfish behavior is otherwise possible.

- source: Ma, Y. P., Gonçalves, S., Mignot, S., Nadal, J. P., & Gordon, M. B. (2009). Cycles of cooperation and free-riding in social systems. *European Physical Journal B*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model predicts that, depending on parameters, communities can settle into fixed points (Nash equilibria) with stable mixtures of cooperators and free-riders, or exhibit persistent cycles in the fractions of cooperators and free-riders. The presence and width of the distribution of moral costs (idiosyncratic disutility for free-riding) are crucial for the emergence of cycles. Stronger social interactions (bandwagon effects) can stabilize high-cooperation equilibria and suppress cycles. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning actions, but models the deterrent to free-riding as an internalized moral cost. The efficiency or total surplus of the group is not directly compared across different institutional designs (e.g., with/without punishment), so the findings are not directly predictive for the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is of limited direct use for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not model explicit punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency outcomes in the standard sense. However, it provides indirect evidence that internalized costs for free-riding (moral disapproval) can sustain cooperation and lead to complex group dynamics, including cycles. The model's structure and results may be informative for understanding how heterogeneity in social preferences and costs can affect cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative predictions for the effect of punishment interventions or for the mapping from control to treatment efficiency in PGGs.

- source: Szilagyi, M. N., & Somogyi, I. (2010). Agent-Based Simulation of an N-Person Game with Parabolic Payoff Functions. *Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that the final proportion of cooperators depends strongly on the shape and relative position of the parabolic payoff functions, the agent update rule, and the definition of neighborhood (local vs. global). For some parameterizations, stable intermediate levels of cooperation emerge; in others, the system converges to all-cooperation or all-defection. The results are often unpredictable except in special cases (e.g., greedy or conformist agents with global neighborhoods). The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, nor does it include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms as in standard public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The game studied is adjacent to PGGs in that it is an N-person game with binary choices and payoff externalities, but it lacks explicit punishment/reward actions and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The findings may be useful for understanding how nonlinear payoff structures and local interactions affect cooperation rates, but they do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment interventions.
