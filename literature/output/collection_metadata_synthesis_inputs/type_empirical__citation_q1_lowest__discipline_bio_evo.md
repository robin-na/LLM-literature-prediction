- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Nhim, T., Schuch, E., & Richter, A. (2023). Water scarcity and support for costly institutions in public goods: Experimental evidence from Cambodia. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that costly institutions (minimum contribution/tax and strict enforcement with punishment) are broadly supported by participants, especially those with prior experience of water scarcity. Both types of costly institutions increase average contributions to the public good compared to costless regimes. However, only the minimum contribution (tax) regime leads to higher payoffs and welfare, while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but does not improve payoffs due to the cost of enforcement. The efficiency of the group (measured as payoff relative to the social optimum) is thus higher under the tax regime but lower under strict enforcement compared to their respective costless counterparts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing costly punishment or enforcement institutions in public goods games depends on the specific design. Minimum contribution (tax) regimes can increase both contributions and efficiency (payoff), while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but can reduce efficiency due to the cost of enforcement. For prediction, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism does not guarantee higher efficiency; the cost and structure of the institution are critical. Control efficiency (payoff) and the specific institutional design (tax/minimum vs. costly strict punishment) must be considered. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the cost and type of punishment or enforcement.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & D'Amico, V. (2021). A preference to learn from successful rather than common behaviours in human social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Participants in a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4, groups of 4, 6 rounds) were more motivated to learn from and copy successful (high-earning) behaviors than to conform to common behaviors. When shown both the average (common) contribution and the average contribution of the highest earners from a previous experiment, participants contributed significantly less than when shown only the common behavior. This decline in cooperation was not explained by simply knowing about free riders, but specifically by the desire to learn from success. Most participants preferred to see information about successful behaviors over common behaviors. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the lower contributions in the 'success' information condition imply lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games without punishment or reward, providing information about successful (high-earning) behaviors leads to lower cooperation and thus likely lower efficiency, compared to providing only information about common behaviors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of success-based social information (even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward) can undermine efficiency. However, the study does not manipulate or analyze punishment directly, and so its direct relevance to predicting the effect of enabling punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of social information and learning in public goods games, and suggest that efficiency may be lower when participants can observe and learn from successful free-riding.

- source: Liu, X. L., Li, J. B., Wang, G. R., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., & Fu, X. G. (2020). Transcranial direct current stimulation of the rLPFC shifts normative judgments in voluntary cooperation. *Neuroscience Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study demonstrates that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects both participants' normative judgments about appropriate contributions and their actual contributions in a one-shot, linear, asymmetric public goods game. Anodal stimulation (increasing rLPFC excitability) leads to higher normative judgments and higher contributions, while cathodal stimulation (decreasing rLPFC excitability) leads to lower judgments and contributions, relative to sham. The effect is robust across both 'rich' and 'poor' player types. No punishment, sanction, or reward mechanisms are present in the game, and no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Its findings are relevant to understanding the neural basis of normative judgment and voluntary cooperation, but not to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions reported (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds) may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this study should not be used as direct evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

- source: Del Ponte, A., Masiliunas, A., & Lim, N. (2025). Decentralized voluntary agreements do not reduce emissions in a climate change experiment. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment with a public bads game (3 players, 18 rounds), the introduction of voluntary pledge-and-review agreements, automatic financial penalties for exceeding pledges, and/or peer evaluation (reputation contest) did not reduce average production or disaster risk compared to baseline. Most participants joined agreements and complied with their pledges, but pledges were not ambitious enough to reduce emissions. Financial penalties increased compliance but did not improve group efficiency, as participants could avoid penalties by not joining or by making unambitious pledges. Peer evaluation did not reduce production and even reduced the ambition of pledges. The results suggest that, in decentralized and voluntary settings, punishment mechanisms do not improve efficiency unless participation and ambition can be enforced.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a voluntary, decentralized public bads game with 3 players and 18 rounds, adding automatic financial penalties for exceeding pledges does not improve group efficiency or reduce emissions compared to baseline, because participants can avoid penalties by not joining or by making unambitious pledges. Peer evaluation (reputation-based non-monetary punishment) also does not improve efficiency and may reduce ambition. For prediction, this suggests that in similar voluntary, opt-in, pledge-based games, the presence of punishment mechanisms (even if automatic and with high penalty rates) will not increase efficiency unless there are mechanisms to ensure ambitious pledges and broad participation. Control efficiency is a good predictor of treatment efficiency when punishment is voluntary and avoidable.

- source: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C. (2014). Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*.

The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

Social expectations and intrinsic respect for rules explain most rule-following. Adding externalities (harm to a charity) or punishment (with varying probabilities) increases rule-following rates, but a substantial baseline of rule-following exists without these.
