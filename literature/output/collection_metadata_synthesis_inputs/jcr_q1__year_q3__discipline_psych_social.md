- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

- source: van Aaken, A. (2019). Experimental Insights for International Legal Theory. *European Journal of International Law*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that experimental evidence from public goods games challenges the strong free-rider and sanctioners' dilemma assumptions of rationalist theory. It highlights that individuals often cooperate and punish defectors even at a cost to themselves, and that mechanisms such as communication, framing, trust, and legitimacy can enhance cooperation. Punishment (including decentralized, third-party, and symbolic forms) is shown to be effective in sustaining cooperation, especially when perceived as fair. Outcasting (exclusion) and endogenously chosen rules (consent) are also identified as effective mechanisms. However, the paper does not report new experimental results or provide direct measures of efficiency; rather, it draws on the literature to inform theoretical debates in international law.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and related mechanisms (communication, framing, trust, legitimacy) can increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in public goods games and similar environments. It is useful for understanding which design dimensions (e.g., presence of punishment, communication, framing, endogenous rule choice) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct empirical evidence on efficiency outcomes, so it should be used as a source of mechanism-based priors and qualitative guidance rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Harrell, A. (2018). Competition for Leadership Promotes Contributions to Collective Action. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that the opportunity to compete for an elected leadership position (with punishment power) increases contributions to the public good before the leader is chosen, but only when voters can observe and use prior contribution behavior in their decision. After the leader is installed, groups with elected leaders maintain higher contributions than those with appointed leaders. The leader's use of punishment does not differ significantly between elected and appointed conditions. The main outcome is contribution rate; no explicit group payoff or efficiency measures are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the process of leader selection (election vs. appointment) and the visibility of contribution history can significantly affect contribution rates in public goods games with punishment. However, because the paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. Its findings are most useful for predicting how leader selection mechanisms and information about contributions affect cooperation, which may indirectly inform efficiency predictions if contribution rates are assumed to map closely to efficiency in similar designs.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Kanitsar, G. (2019). Solidarity through punishment: An experiment on the merits of centralized enforcement in generalized exchange. *Social Science Research*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Centralized punishment substantially increases the frequency of cooperation in both public good and generalized exchange games. However, the main focus is on how punishment affects solidarity, not efficiency. Solidarity (measured affectively, behaviorally, and via beliefs) increases with punishment in generalized exchange but not in the public good setting. The positive effect of punishment on solidarity is largely mediated by increased cooperation frequency. The experiment does not report group efficiency or total payoff, and the only payoff-related outcomes are cooperation rates and within-group payoff variation. Thus, the findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that centralized punishment increases cooperation rates in both public good and generalized exchange games with four players and 16 rounds. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, binary choice, etc.) are well specified, and the manipulation of punishment is clear (centralized, probabilistic, fixed fine). The main value for efficiency prediction is the robust increase in cooperation frequency with punishment, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in standard PGGs, but the absence of direct efficiency or payoff analysis limits the strength of inference.

- source: Mischkowski, D., Glöckner, A., & Lewisch, P. (2018). From spontaneous cooperation to spontaneous punishment - Distinguishing the underlying motives driving spontaneous behavior in first and second order public good games. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in both one-shot and iterated public goods games with a punishment option, individuals who decide to punish quickly invest more in punishment, especially if they contributed more than their peers and feel highly upset. The spontaneous punishment effect is not moderated by dispositional pro-sociality (SVO), but by situational factors (above-average contribution and negative affect). The study does not analyze or report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs, so it does not provide evidence on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on the psychological and situational determinants of punishment behavior in public goods games, but does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. However, it does confirm that punishment is used more by above-average contributors and those experiencing negative affect, and that the cost-to-impact ratio of punishment is 1:4. The game design is a standard PGG with continuous contributions, four players, two rounds (Study 1) or one round (Study 2), no chat, and full information about others' contributions. The absence of efficiency or payoff analysis limits the paper's direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Pleasant, A., & Barclay, P. (2018). Why Hate the Good Guy? Antisocial Punishment of High Cooperators Is Greater When People Compete To Be Chosen. *Psychological Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that when participants in a public goods game with punishment are competing to be chosen for a subsequent trust game, antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is much higher than in a standard PGG with punishment. This effect is present from the first round and persists across rounds. In the control condition, contributions increase over rounds (as is typical when punishment is available), but in the markets condition, contributions do not increase, suggesting that antisocial punishment suppresses the escalation of cooperation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral pattern implies that the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment may be undermined or reversed in contexts with high antisocial punishment driven by partner competition.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the context of partner competition (biological markets) can substantially increase antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, which in turn suppresses the usual increase in cooperation seen with punishment. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings imply that in environments where partner choice or competitive altruism is salient, enabling punishment may not improve and may even harm group efficiency, as antisocial punishment undermines cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of partner competition or similar social comparison incentives is a critical moderator: in such settings, the efficiency benefit of punishment is likely reduced or reversed, even if control efficiency is known. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits quantitative prediction.

- source: Lindström, B., Jangard, S., Selbing, I., & Olsson, A. (2018). The Role of a Common Is Moral Heuristic in the Stability and Change of Moral Norms. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across nine experiments, participants observing one-shot PGGs judged common behaviors (whether altruistic or selfish) as more moral and less deserving of punishment than rare behaviors. This effect was robust across lab and online samples, and held for both preexisting moral norms (cooperation/selfishness) and arbitrary behaviors. In one experiment, participants could spend real money to punish, and commonness reduced costly punishment of selfishness. The effect of commonness was explained by a formal model of social influence (Social Impact Theory). Agent-based simulations showed that the CIM heuristic can generate both stability and sudden change in group-level moral norms. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived commonness of behaviors in PGGs shapes moral and punishment judgments, and that common selfishness is punished less than rare selfishness. It also shows that costly punishment is reduced when selfishness is common. However, because the experiments are one-shot and do not measure group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, the findings do not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled. The results are most useful for understanding psychological and behavioral responses to norm violation and punishment, rather than for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions in repeated or strategic PGGs.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schurtenberger, I. (2018). Normative foundations of human cooperation. *Nature Human Behaviour*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. - source: Stoop, J., van Soest, D., & Vyrastekova, J. (2018). Rewards and cooperation in social dilemma games. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that net-positive rewards (where the recipient gains more than the sender loses) dramatically increase cooperation and efficiency in standard public goods games (VCM), with most groups reaching high contribution levels when rewards are available.
