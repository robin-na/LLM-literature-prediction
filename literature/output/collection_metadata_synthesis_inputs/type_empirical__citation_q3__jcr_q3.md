- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2013). Legitimate punishment, feedback, and the enforcement of cooperation. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (unrestricted punishment, full feedback), (2) Full R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, full feedback), and (3) Partial R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, partial feedback). Restricting punishment to only allow higher contributors to punish lower contributors (legitimate punishment) increases both cooperation and efficiency compared to unrestricted punishment. The highest efficiency and earnings are observed in the Full R. treatment, where both legitimacy and full feedback are present. In the Baseline, a significant share of punishment is antisocial, which reduces efficiency. When feedback about high contributors is removed (Partial R.), the positive effect of legitimate punishment on efficiency and cooperation is greatly reduced. Thus, both legitimacy and feedback are necessary for punishment to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, restricting punishment to legitimate cases (i.e., only higher contributors can punish lower contributors) and providing full feedback about all group members' contributions both increase efficiency and group earnings compared to unrestricted punishment. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling unrestricted punishment may not increase efficiency due to antisocial punishment, but enabling legitimate punishment with full feedback is likely to yield a substantial efficiency gain. The effect is much weaker or absent if feedback about high contributors is not provided. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the structure of punishment and information feedback.

- source: Kamijo, Y., Nihonsugi, T., Takeuchi, A., & Funaki, Y. (2014). Sustaining cooperation in social dilemmas: Comparison of centralized punishment institutions. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study experimentally compares two centralized punishment institutions in a linear public goods game: absolute punishment (all violators punished) and relative punishment (only the worst violators punished). With deterrent sanctions (where the penalty is strong enough), both institutions achieve similar, high efficiency (group profit). With non-deterrent sanctions (penalty not strong enough to make compliance a dominant strategy), the relative punishment institution achieves significantly higher efficiency and contributions than the absolute institution. The theoretically optimal threshold for punishment does not always yield the highest observed profit, suggesting behavioral factors affect outcomes. Overall, relative punishment is at least as effective, and often more so, than absolute punishment for sustaining group efficiency, especially when sanctions are weak.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution matters: relative punishment (punishing only the worst violators) can yield higher efficiency than absolute punishment (punishing all violators) when sanctions are not strong enough to be fully deterrent. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and the punishment is non-deterrent, enabling relative punishment is likely to increase efficiency more than enabling absolute punishment. When sanctions are deterrent, both institutions perform similarly well. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 15 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, and fixed punishment parameters as in the experiment.

- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Irwin, K., Mulder, L., & Simpson, B. (2014). The Detrimental Effects of Sanctions on Intragroup Trust: Comparing Punishments and Rewards. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Both punishment and reward systems, when present, can increase short-term cooperation (especially when sanctions are moderate rather than extreme), but their removal leads to a significant decline in trust, and in some cases, a decline in cooperation. Strong sanctions do not increase cooperation above control, and their removal reduces cooperation. Weaker sanctions increase cooperation while present, but their removal does not immediately reduce cooperation, though trust is still undermined. The negative effects on trust are similar for both punishment and reward. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings must be interpreted through the lens of contribution/cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the presence and removal of punishment (and reward) systems can have complex effects on cooperation, which is a behavioral proxy for efficiency but not a direct measure. Strong punishment does not increase cooperation above control, and its removal reduces cooperation. Weaker punishment increases cooperation while present, but its removal does not immediately reduce cooperation. The undermining of trust by sanctions is robust, regardless of sanction strength. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on the strength of the sanction and the baseline level of cooperation, but direct efficiency outcomes are not reported. Use caution when extrapolating to efficiency, as only contribution rates are measured.

- source: Nelissen, R. M. A., & Mulder, L. B. (2013). What makes a sanction stick? The effects of financial and social sanctions on norm compliance. *Social Influence*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both financial and social sanctions increase cooperation (measured as average contribution) in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-sanction control. Financial sanctions produce higher contributions than social sanctions while the sanctioning opportunity is present. However, after the removal of sanctions, contributions drop much more in the financial sanction condition than in the social sanction or control conditions. Social sanctions thus create more persistent cooperation norms. The study does not report group payoff or efficiency, but the contribution results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency effects in similar game designs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that both financial and social punishment mechanisms increase cooperation in a standard PGG, and that the effect is larger for financial punishment while the mechanism is active. However, the persistence of cooperation after punishment is removed is greater for social sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment will be positive for both financial and social punishment, but the durability of the effect may depend on the type of punishment. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution rates, but the mapping is straightforward in standard PGGs. The paper's design and results are highly informative for settings with 4 players, continuous contributions, no communication, and known MPCR.

- source: Greiff, M., & Paetzel, F. (2016). Second-order beliefs in reputation systems with endogenous evaluations - an experimental study. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a repeated two-player public good game, providing information about both one's own and the partner's evaluation (reputation) increases contributions compared to control and to the treatment where only the partner's evaluation is shown. When only the partner's evaluation is available, contributions do not increase significantly relative to control. The effect is explained by the role of second-order beliefs in conditional cooperation. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but higher contributions in the BOTH-INFO treatment imply higher group payoffs. There is no punishment or costly sanctioning; evaluations are costless, non-binding signals.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is informative for predicting the effect of reputation-based information (not costly punishment) on contribution rates in repeated two-player public good games. It suggests that simply providing information about others' evaluations is insufficient to increase cooperation, but providing both own and partner's evaluation can increase contributions. However, because the study does not include a costly punishment mechanism, its direct relevance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for settings where social image or reputation feedback (not material punishment) is the main intervention. For prediction tasks involving costly punishment, this paper provides only adjacent evidence.

- source: Gianotti, L. R. R., Dahinden, F. M., Baumgartner, T., & Knoch, D. (2019). Understanding Individual Differences in Domain-General Prosociality: A Resting EEG Study. *Brain Topography*.

  findings: The paper finds that higher resting beta2 and beta3 current density in the right temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) is positively associated with higher domain-general prosociality, as measured by contributions in three one-shot PGGs (with multipliers 1.2, 2, and 3.2) and giving in a dictator game. The neural trait is stable and specific to the TPJ and beta bands. No relationship is found between prosociality and the honesty-humility personality scale. The study does not include any punishment, sanction, or reward mechanisms, nor does it report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or include punishment, nor does it report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. Its findings are limited to individual differences in prosociality (contribution/giving) and their neural correlates. Thus, it should not be used to inform predictions about the efficiency impact of punishment or related game design dimensions.

- source: Cardella, E., Kugler, T., Anderson, J., & Connolly, T. (2019). Is greed contagious? Four experimental studies. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The main finding is that in a two-person CPR dilemma, participants increase their own withdrawals in response to high withdrawals by their partner, both when interacting with the same partner and with a new partner. However, this behavioral contagion is not mediated by perceptions of greed: participants' ratings of their partner's greediness do not predict their own subsequent behavior after controlling for observed withdrawals. Information about an opponent's greedy reputation (as rated by a previous partner) does not affect new participants' behavior. Attempts to manage one's own reputation for greed (when warned it will be shared) are weak and not statistically significant. The mechanism for behavioral contagion appears to be norm learning or direct response to observed behavior, not to perceptions or reputational information. No punishment, sanction, or reward options are present, and no group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes are analyzed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, as no punishment or sanction mechanism is present in any condition. The design is a two-person CPR dilemma with continuous withdrawal choices, no communication, and no explicit efficiency or group payoff analysis. The findings are relevant for understanding behavioral contagion and norm learning in the absence of punishment, but do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results may be useful as a control or baseline for environments without punishment, showing that behavioral contagion (increased defection in response to observed defection) can occur, but do not address the effect of introducing punishment.

- source: Wilson, A. J., & Wu, H. (2017). At-will relationships: How an option to walk away affects cooperation and efficiency. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Introducing a termination (walk-away) option in repeated PD games increases cooperation and efficiency when the outside option is low, as termination is rarely used and serves as a credible threat. However, as the outside option rises above the in-relationship minmax, subjects begin to use termination more frequently as a punishment, leading to a sharp drop in efficiency. If the outside option increases further, efficiency rises again, as the cost of termination is less severe. Asymmetric division rules for termination payoffs have dramatic effects: if the party who terminates gets the higher payoff, almost all relationships dissolve immediately, resulting in very low efficiency. If the more cooperative party is rewarded, cooperation and efficiency are maximized. The paper provides direct evidence on how the structure and value of punishment options (termination) affect efficiency in repeated social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment (here, the option to terminate a relationship) on efficiency is highly sensitive to the value and distribution of the punishment's payoff. When punishment is costly (low outside option), its mere availability increases efficiency, but if it becomes too attractive (high outside option), it is overused and efficiency drops. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and value of the punishment option, the symmetry/asymmetry of its payoff, and the underlying game structure (here, repeated PD with imperfect monitoring). The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of enabling punishment depends non-monotonically on the cost/benefit of punishment, and that institutional details (who gets what on punishment/termination) can have very large effects. For prediction, the control efficiency (without punishment) and the specific design of the punishment option are critical moderators.

- source: Bohnet, I., & Baytelman, Y. (2007). Institutions and trust - Implications for preferences, beliefs and behavior. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional constraints, including a post-play punishment option, increase trustors' willingness to send money and trustees' willingness to return money in a trust game scenario, compared to an anonymous one-shot game. This suggests that efficiency (total group payoff) is higher with punishment. However, the presence of punishment and other institutional constraints reduces the role of intrinsic motivation (social orientation) in driving trust, supporting the 'crowding out' hypothesis. For trustworthiness, the effect of punishment is indirect: trustees return more because they expect to receive more, not because their intrinsic motivation changes. The study uses a within-subject design with senior executives and hypothetical payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that adding a punishment option to a trust game increases the amounts sent and returned, which is a proxy for increased efficiency. While the design is not a public goods game, the trust game with punishment is adjacent and the direction of effect is likely to generalize to PGG-like settings: punishment increases efficiency-related outcomes. However, the paper also highlights that the mechanism is not an increase in intrinsic motivation, but rather a shift in beliefs and expectations. For prediction, this suggests that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low. The effect is robust across several institutional manipulations, but the crowding out of intrinsic motivation may have long-term implications not captured by short-run efficiency.

- source: MOLM, L. D. (1994). IS PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVE - COERCIVE STRATEGIES IN SOCIAL-EXCHANGE. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that punishment is effective in increasing compliance (frequency of rewarding the punisher) when it is used frequently, contingently, and with high magnitude. The combination of high probability and high magnitude of punishment produces the highest compliance. Retaliation by the target does not increase with more frequent or stronger punishment, and negative affect toward the punisher is actually lower when punishment is strong and consistent, due to the resulting mutually rewarding exchanges. The effects are robust to the target's ability to retaliate and to variations in the probability and contingency of punishment. However, the main outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, but the frequency of compliance in a dyadic exchange. The results challenge classical exchange and bargaining theories that predict punishment provokes retaliation and reduces cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in dyadic reciprocal exchange settings with both reward and punishment options, frequent and contingent punishment increases compliance (rewarding behavior) by the target, without increasing retaliation or negative affect. However, because the primary outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, and the setting is not a public goods game or direct variant, the findings should be transferred to PGG-like environments with caution. The results suggest that, under conditions of high punishment frequency, contingency, and magnitude, punishment can be effective at increasing the target's prosocial behavior, but the implications for group efficiency in multi-person public goods games remain indirect. The paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment rather than direct efficiency outcomes.

- source: Rauhut, H. (2009). HIGHER PUNISHMENT, LESS CONTROL? EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE ON THE INSPECTION GAME. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that increasing the strength of punishment reduces both the rate of inspection (control) and the rate of theft (crime), contrary to the strict game-theoretic prediction that only inspection should decrease. The effect on inspection is strong and robust, while the effect on theft is also significant but not as strong as predicted by standard theory. The behavioral adaptation to changes in punishment is slower than predicted by rational learning models. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates; efficiency or group welfare is not directly measured, but the design ensures that theft is inefficient (gamma = 0.5). Inspectees lose money over time, while inspectors' earnings are roughly stable. The findings suggest that punishment has mixed effects on strategic behavior in inspection settings, and that human adaptation is slower and less complete than predicted by theory.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides informative evidence about the behavioral effects of punishment in an inspection game with inefficient theft, which is structurally adjacent to public goods games. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. However, since theft is inefficient (gamma < 1), reductions in theft due to higher punishment would increase group efficiency, while reductions in inspection (which is costly) could also increase efficiency if inspection is not needed. The paper shows that higher punishment reduces both theft and inspection, suggesting that the net effect on efficiency would depend on the relative magnitudes of these changes and the cost structure. The design and findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is formal, inspection is costly, and theft is inefficient. The lack of direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions, but the qualitative direction of effects (higher punishment reduces both crime and control) is informative for adjacent settings.

- source: Gummerum, M., López-Pérez, B., Van Dijk, E., & Van Dillen, L. F. (2022). Ire and punishment: Incidental anger and costly punishment in children, adolescents, and adults. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.

In third-party punishment games, incidental anger increases punishment severity for unfair offers in adults and adolescents, but not in children. The effect of anger is specific to unfair (unequal) offers and does not increase punishment of fair (equal) offers. Enforcement is more common in Switzerland than in New York City. The presence of disorder (litter) reduces the likelihood of both direct and indirect enforcement. However, because the study does not measure group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main value for prediction is as indirect, contextual evidence about the real-world prevalence and moderators of costly punishment, suggesting that laboratory findings may overestimate the frequency and possibly the efficiency impact of punishment in naturalistic settings.

- source: Lacomba, J. A., Lagos, F., Reuben, E., & van Winden, F. (2014). On the escalation and de-escalation of conflict. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot or randomly matched (Strangers) conflict games, conflict expenditures are high and escalate over time, leading to low efficiency (mean earnings well below the endowment). When losers can destroy resources (akin to punishment), conflict expenditures decrease, and in the 'Resistance' game (where destruction can be conditioned on the winner's appropriation), mean earnings are significantly higher than in the baseline.
