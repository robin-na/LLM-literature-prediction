- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in a population with probabilistic corrupt enforcers and violators. *Mathematical Models & Methods in Applied Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. If the expected loss equals the bribe, the system exhibits neutral cycles (limit cycles) with periodic oscillations in cooperation and efficiency. If the expected loss exceeds the bribe, the system can either converge to all-defection (low efficiency) or to a heteroclinic cycle with cyclic dominance, which may still allow for periods of cooperation. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings. The findings are directly informative for mapping game design parameters (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, bribe size, probability of corruption) to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Cui, P. B., Wu, Z. X., Zhou, T., & Chen, X. J. (2019). Cooperator-driven and defector-driven punishments: How do they influence cooperation?. *Physical Review E*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment mechanisms (cooperator-driven, defector-driven, or both) into public goods games increases the prevalence of cooperation and raises group efficiency/welfare compared to the baseline with no punishment. In infinite well-mixed populations, a mix of both punishment types is most effective, while in networked populations, cooperator-driven punishment is superior. The effect of punishment on efficiency is monotonic with respect to the synergy factor (mpcr), punishment fine (punishment_tech), and feedback sensitivity, though in networked populations, an intermediate feedback sensitivity is optimal. The study provides detailed phase diagrams and analytical boundaries for when nondefection (high efficiency) prevails. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and evolutionary scenarios.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in PGG-like environments increases group efficiency, especially when the punishment is cooperator-driven or when both types are available. The effect is sensitive to the synergy factor (mpcr), punishment fine (punishment_tech), and feedback sensitivity, with network structure further moderating the effect. For prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment, the value of mpcr, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the network structure should be considered as key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. The results suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude depending on these design dimensions.

- source: Liu, L. J., Wang, S. X., Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2018). Evolutionary dynamics in the public goods games with switching between punishment and exclusion. *Chaos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a well-mixed infinite population PGG, both peer-based and pool-based punishment and exclusion mechanisms can promote cooperation and increase average group payoff (efficiency) compared to defection. When individuals use a switching strategy—punishing defectors when they are few and excluding them when they are many—there is a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and sanctioning strategists, leading to higher average payoffs. The efficiency gains are maximized at intermediate switching thresholds. Pure punishment (high threshold) leads to dominance of defectors and low efficiency, while pure exclusion (low threshold) can lead to oscillatory coexistence but still supports higher efficiency than no sanctions. The model explicitly tracks average payoff as the key outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment (and/or exclusion) in a standard PGG increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to the control (no sanctions), especially when the sanctioning mechanism is flexible (switching between punishment and exclusion based on the number of defectors). The effect is sensitive to the threshold for switching, the costs and effectiveness of punishment/exclusion, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The model is fully specified for N=5, r=3, c=1, and various punishment/exclusion costs, and can be used to predict efficiency under different game design dimensions. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design parameters in PGG-like environments.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both autonomous and consensual punishment can promote cooperation and increase average payoffs in public goods games. Consensual punishment (where a group vote determines whether defectors are punished) can be more effective than autonomous punishment when the consensus threshold is low and the willingness to punish is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation dominates defection in terms of average payoffs. The effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the group size, the marginal per-capita return, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the consensus threshold for punishment. The paper also shows that increasing the cost of punishment or the group size can make autonomous punishment more effective relative to consensual punishment, while increasing the fine or the marginal return favors consensual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given game design parameters such as group size, number of rounds (implicitly infinite in the model), marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the decision rule for punishment (autonomous vs. consensual, and the consensus threshold). The explicit payoff formulas and comparative statics allow for quantitative or qualitative prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment (especially with low consensus thresholds and high willingness to punish) will increase efficiency, but the effect is moderated by punishment cost, group size, and the consensus rule.

- source: Wang, Q. L., Ren, X. B., Gao, B., & Wang, J. Q. (2020). Heterogeneity reproductive ability promotes cooperation in spatial prisoner's dilemma game. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a threshold-based resource allocation regime in a PGG with peer punishment can effectively solve the second-order free-rider problem and promote the dominance of punishment. The evolutionary dynamics depend on the threshold parameter (lambda), reward weights for cooperators and defectors, and punishment parameters (fine and cost). For a wide range of threshold and reward parameters, the system converges to full punishment or bistable states between punishment and defection. The basin of attraction for full punishment shrinks as more resources are allocated equally (higher lambda), but only a small portion of resources need to be used for reward to promote punishment. The model suggests that proper adjustment of threshold and reward weights is key to promoting punishment and resisting second-order free-riders. However, the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the structure of resource allocation and the presence of peer punishment can strongly affect the evolutionary stability of cooperation and punishment in PGG-like environments. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, it suggests that regimes which reward punishers (and to a lesser extent cooperators) based on contribution, especially when only a small portion of the public good is used for reward, can promote the dominance of punishment and suppress second-order free-riding. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that enabling punishment and introducing targeted reward mechanisms can shift the system toward higher cooperation and punishment prevalence, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in empirical PGGs. However, the lack of explicit efficiency or payoff reporting means the findings should be used as indirect, mechanistic support rather than direct quantitative evidence.

  findings: The paper finds that in the thermodynamic limit of the two-player PGG, the prevalence of cooperation (as measured by game magnetization) is strongly affected by the reward for providing, the cost of the public good, and the presence and magnitude of punishment for defection. Specifically, increasing punishment shifts the critical point for cooperation to lower reward values, making cooperation more prevalent. The analysis is entirely theoretical and does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the results imply that punishment increases the fraction of cooperators, which would generally be associated with higher efficiency in standard PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in the thermodynamic limit of a two-player PGG, the presence and magnitude of punishment increases the prevalence of cooperation (i.e., more players choose to provide), while higher cost suppresses cooperation. Although the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the increase in cooperation due to punishment suggests that, in standard PGGs, enabling punishment would increase efficiency, especially when the cost of the public good is not too high. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with two players, continuous contribution choices, and no communication, and may be less directly transferable to finite or empirical settings. The results are useful for predicting the qualitative effect of punishment on cooperation and, by extension, on efficiency, but do not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.
