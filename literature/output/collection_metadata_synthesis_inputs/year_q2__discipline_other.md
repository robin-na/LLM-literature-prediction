- source: Engel, C., & Zhurakhovska, L. (2017). You Are in Charge: Experimentally Testing the Motivating Power of Holding a Judicial Office. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Authorities (randomly assigned, with no personal stake in group outcomes) are willing to incur costs to punish free riders in a repeated linear public goods game. This punishment leads to higher contributions and higher payoffs for active participants than the Nash equilibrium (no contribution, no punishment). The effect is robust to framing the authority as a public official or judge, which further reduces the influence of personal preferences on punishment. Announcing punishment policies, election of judges, and experience all modulate the targeting and sensitivity of punishment, but the basic effect—punishment increases cooperation and group payoffs—remains. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), but group profit is reported and is higher than in the absence of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized punishment (by a non-beneficiary authority) in a repeated linear public goods game increases group payoffs and efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across variations in authority framing, policy announcement, election, and experience. The design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, full information on others' actions and punishment) are all specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the social optimum, so the effect must be inferred from reported mean earnings and profit.

- source: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C. (2014). Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.

- source: Bell, A., Zhang, W., & Nou, K. (2016). Pesticide use and cooperative management of natural enemy habitat in a framed field experiment. *Agricultural Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot public goods game with three treatments: (1) Base (no punishment), (2) UCPun (conditional decision maker can be punished by an unconditional decision maker), and (3) CPun (conditional decision maker can punish an unconditional decision maker). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), but does lead to more sensitive punishment behavior. Responsibility effects (increased cooperation when given punishment power) are stronger than substitution effects. The overall effect of punishment on cooperation is positive, but the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment mechanisms affect conditional cooperation in a one-shot public goods game with four players, MPCR 0.4, and costless punishment. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings suggest that enabling punishment increases the responsiveness of cooperators to others' contributions and may increase overall cooperation, but also increases free riding. For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is likely to increase cooperation-related behaviors, but the net effect on efficiency is ambiguous due to the rise in free riders. The absence of direct efficiency analysis limits the ability to quantitatively predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency, but the behavioral mechanisms identified are informative for modeling.

The models show that punishment changes the incentives so that cooperation becomes the dominant strategy, leading to higher group welfare. However, the paper does not provide empirical estimates or test specific parameter values, so its guidance is qualitative: punishment mechanisms, when credible and enforced, are predicted to improve efficiency in social dilemmas.

- source: Janssen, M. A. (2015). A behavioral perspective on the governance of common resources. *Current Opinion in Environmental Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The paper finds that, contrary to the predictions of standard economic theory, groups can achieve higher efficiency and avoid the tragedy of the commons when allowed to communicate or implement costly punishment. Both lab and field experiments show that these mechanisms increase group earnings and reduce overuse of resources. The review also notes that centralized sanctioning mechanisms may be more effective and sustainable than peer punishment. However, the effectiveness of punishment and other interventions is context-specific, depending on factors such as group size, communication, trust, and cultural background. The paper also discusses the phenomenon of 'crowding out,' where externally imposed rules can sometimes reduce performance compared to voluntary agreements.
  prediction_guidance: This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments generally increases group efficiency or earnings, especially when compared to baseline games without punishment or communication. It also highlights that the effect of punishment is moderated by contextual factors such as group size, communication, and the nature of the sanctioning institution (peer vs. centralized). While the review does not provide quantitative estimates, it offers strong qualitative evidence that punishment mechanisms, when available, tend to improve efficiency outcomes, but the magnitude and sustainability of these effects depend on the specific game design and social context.

  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling local, group-level punishment institutions in threshold public goods games (with risk of collective loss) will increase the rate of successful group outcomes compared to no punishment or global punishment. While the model does not report efficiency or total payoff, the 'group achievement' outcome is a close proxy for efficiency in threshold settings. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is local, groups are small, and risk perception is low. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so its value is mainly in qualitative prediction and mechanism understanding.

- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

- source: Bruner, J. P. (2013). POLICING EPISTEMIC COMMUNITIES. *Episteme-a Journal of Individual and Social Epistemology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that peer-policing can sustain high levels of honest reporting in a decentralized community if and only if punishment is opportunistic—i.e., if those who police and catch cheaters are rewarded. When the benefit of catching cheaters and the punishment for being caught are both high (with the benefit exceeding the punishment), the population stabilizes with a majority of honest reporters and a minority of policers and cheaters. If punishment is purely altruistic (costly to the punisher), honesty cannot be sustained and the population converges to all cheating. The model also shows that reducing the cost of policing (e.g., by rewarding replication) or increasing the punishment for cheating can further increase the prevalence of honesty. However, the model does not directly report on group efficiency or total welfare, focusing instead on the behavioral dynamics of honesty, policing, and cheating.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment can sustain prosocial behavior (honest reporting) in social dilemmas, but only if the incentive structure rewards punishers. While the model is not a direct public goods game, it is structurally similar and thus informative for predicting when punishment will be effective in PGG-like environments. The key implication is that punishment will only improve group outcomes (e.g., honesty, cooperation) if the cost-benefit structure makes policing individually rational—i.e., if the reward for punishing exceeds the cost and the punishment for being caught cheating exceeds the benefit of cheating. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency outcomes. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of the incentive structure for the effectiveness of punishment.
