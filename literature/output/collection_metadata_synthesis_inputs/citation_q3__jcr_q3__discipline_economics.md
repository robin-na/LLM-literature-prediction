- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2013). Legitimate punishment, feedback, and the enforcement of cooperation. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (unrestricted punishment, full feedback), (2) Full R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, full feedback), and (3) Partial R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, partial feedback). Restricting punishment to only allow higher contributors to punish lower contributors (legitimate punishment) increases both cooperation and efficiency compared to unrestricted punishment. The highest efficiency and earnings are observed in the Full R. treatment, where both legitimacy and full feedback are present. In the Baseline, a significant share of punishment is antisocial, which reduces efficiency. When feedback about high contributors is removed (Partial R.), the positive effect of legitimate punishment on efficiency and cooperation is greatly reduced. Thus, both legitimacy and feedback are necessary for punishment to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, restricting punishment to legitimate cases (i.e., only higher contributors can punish lower contributors) and providing full feedback about all group members' contributions both increase efficiency and group earnings compared to unrestricted punishment. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling unrestricted punishment may not increase efficiency due to antisocial punishment, but enabling legitimate punishment with full feedback is likely to yield a substantial efficiency gain. The effect is much weaker or absent if feedback about high contributors is not provided. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the structure of punishment and information feedback.

- source: Kamijo, Y., Nihonsugi, T., Takeuchi, A., & Funaki, Y. (2014). Sustaining cooperation in social dilemmas: Comparison of centralized punishment institutions. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study experimentally compares two centralized punishment institutions in a linear public goods game: absolute punishment (all violators punished) and relative punishment (only the worst violators punished). With deterrent sanctions (where the penalty is strong enough), both institutions achieve similar, high efficiency (group profit). With non-deterrent sanctions (penalty not strong enough to make compliance a dominant strategy), the relative punishment institution achieves significantly higher efficiency and contributions than the absolute institution. The theoretically optimal threshold for punishment does not always yield the highest observed profit, suggesting behavioral factors affect outcomes. Overall, relative punishment is at least as effective, and often more so, than absolute punishment for sustaining group efficiency, especially when sanctions are weak.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution matters: relative punishment (punishing only the worst violators) can yield higher efficiency than absolute punishment (punishing all violators) when sanctions are not strong enough to be fully deterrent. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and the punishment is non-deterrent, enabling relative punishment is likely to increase efficiency more than enabling absolute punishment. When sanctions are deterrent, both institutions perform similarly well. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 15 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, and fixed punishment parameters as in the experiment.

- source: Greiff, M., & Paetzel, F. (2016). Second-order beliefs in reputation systems with endogenous evaluations - an experimental study. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a repeated two-player public good game, providing information about both one's own and the partner's evaluation (reputation) increases contributions compared to control and to the treatment where only the partner's evaluation is shown. When only the partner's evaluation is available, contributions do not increase significantly relative to control. The effect is explained by the role of second-order beliefs in conditional cooperation. The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff, but higher contributions in the BOTH-INFO treatment imply higher group payoffs. There is no punishment or costly sanctioning; evaluations are costless, non-binding signals.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is informative for predicting the effect of reputation-based information (not costly punishment) on contribution rates in repeated two-player public good games. It suggests that simply providing information about others' evaluations is insufficient to increase cooperation, but providing both own and partner's evaluation can increase contributions. However, because the study does not include a costly punishment mechanism, its direct relevance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for settings where social image or reputation feedback (not material punishment) is the main intervention. For prediction tasks involving costly punishment, this paper provides only adjacent evidence.

- source: Figuières, C., Masclet, D., & Willinger, M. (2013). Weak Moral Motivation Leads to the Decline of Voluntary Contributions. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that when individuals' moral motivation is weak (i.e., sensitive to others' contributions), average contributions in repeated public goods games decline over time. The model predicts that this decay is robust under both myopic and forward-looking behavior, and can explain several empirical regularities observed in experiments, such as the restart effect and slower decay in longer games. The model does not include punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze efficiency or group payoff directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not model or analyze punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes. Its main relevance is in providing a theoretical account of contribution decay in standard linear public goods games without punishment. It may be useful for understanding baseline dynamics in the absence of punishment, but does not inform the prediction of treatment efficiency when punishment is enabled.

- source: De Silva, H., Hauert, C., Traulsen, A., & Sigmund, K. (2010). Freedom, enforcement, and the social dilemma of strong altruism. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Theoretical analysis and simulations demonstrate that costly punishment enables the emergence and maintenance of cooperation in joint effort games, but only when participation is voluntary. In the voluntary regime, the stationary distribution is dominated by punishers, leading to higher group payoffs and efficiency. In the compulsory regime, defectors dominate and group efficiency collapses. This result is robust for both 'weak' and 'strong' altruism (i.e., whether or not contributors benefit from their own contributions). The findings imply that the combination of voluntary participation and punishment is critical for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency is highly contingent on whether participation in the public goods game is voluntary or compulsory. If participation is voluntary, introducing punishment leads to a regime dominated by punishers and high group efficiency. If participation is compulsory, punishment does not prevent the dominance of defectors and group efficiency remains low. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of voluntary participation (i.e., whether players can opt out) is a key moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment. The model also suggests that these results are robust to variations in group size, population size, and the strength of selection, as long as the basic structure (voluntary participation, costly punishment) is maintained.

- source: Zhang, B. Y., & Hofbauer, J. (2016). Quantal response methods for equilibrium selection in 2 x 2 coordination games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a mini public goods game with punishment, the QRE method selects a cooperative equilibrium if the punishment is sufficiently strong (i.e., if the fine-to-fee ratio is high enough). The selection of cooperation depends on the effectiveness and cost of punishment. For intermediate punishment, cooperation can dominate for some parameter values even if the limiting QRE is defection. The results are derived analytically and illustrated with theoretical examples, but no empirical or simulated efficiency or payoff data are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical conditions for when punishment will lead to the selection of a cooperative equilibrium in a 2x2 mini public goods game. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it implies that strong punishment can shift equilibrium selection from defection to cooperation, which would be expected to increase efficiency in a public goods context. The results are most directly relevant for games with two players, binary choices, and explicit punishment options, and can inform predictions about the qualitative effect of punishment strength and cost on equilibrium selection, but not quantitative efficiency outcomes.

- source: Clavien, C., & Klein, R. A. (2010). EAGER FOR FAIRNESS OR FOR REVENGE? PSYCHOLOGICAL ALTRUISM IN ECONOMICS. *Economics and Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that while experimental economics and neuroeconomics have revealed robust patterns of altruistic punishment and cooperation in public goods and related games, these studies do not provide decisive evidence about the underlying psychological motives (altruism vs. egoism). Behavioral outcomes such as costly punishment of free-riders are well established, but the interpretation of these actions (e.g., as motivated by fairness, revenge, or self-interest) remains open. Neuroimaging studies (e.g., de Quervain et al. 2004) show reward-related brain activation during punishment, but this does not conclusively distinguish between altruistic and egoistic motives. The paper argues that more fine-grained experimental designs and controls are needed to advance the debate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games or variants. It is useful for understanding the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment behavior, but does not report or synthesize data on group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers conceptual context but no quantitative or qualitative evidence about efficiency outcomes under punishment. It highlights the need for careful experimental design and the limitations of current evidence, but does not inform the mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency.

- source: Ehmke, M. D., & Shogren, J. F. (2009). Experimental methods for environment and development economics. *Environment and Development Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that experimental economics is a valuable tool for understanding behavior in environmental and development contexts, especially regarding cooperation, public goods provision, and the effects of punishment and peer enforcement. It reviews evidence that peer punishment (monetary and non-monetary) can increase contributions in public goods games, and that communication can sometimes be more effective than regulation. The paper also notes that the effectiveness of punishment and enforcement depends on social context, group composition, and cultural factors. However, it does not provide new quantitative findings on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual support for the idea that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation in public goods games, referencing key experimental studies. However, it does not provide direct quantitative evidence or efficiency outcomes that can be used for prediction. Its main value is in summarizing the mechanisms and contextual factors that may moderate the effect of punishment, such as group composition, communication, and cultural context. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment is generally associated with higher cooperation, but does not quantify the efficiency impact or provide game design-specific results.

- source: Wilson, A. J., & Wu, H. (2017). At-will relationships: How an option to walk away affects cooperation and efficiency. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Introducing a termination (walk-away) option in repeated PD games increases cooperation and efficiency when the outside option is low, as termination is rarely used and serves as a credible threat. However, as the outside option rises above the in-relationship minmax, subjects begin to use termination more frequently as a punishment, leading to a sharp drop in efficiency. If the outside option increases further, efficiency rises again, as the cost of termination is less severe. Asymmetric division rules for termination payoffs have dramatic effects: if the party who terminates gets the higher payoff, almost all relationships dissolve immediately, resulting in very low efficiency. If the more cooperative party is rewarded, cooperation and efficiency are maximized. The paper provides direct evidence on how the structure and value of punishment options (termination) affect efficiency in repeated social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment (here, the option to terminate a relationship) on efficiency is highly sensitive to the value and distribution of the punishment's payoff. When punishment is costly (low outside option), its mere availability increases efficiency, but if it becomes too attractive (high outside option), it is overused and efficiency drops. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and value of the punishment option, the symmetry/asymmetry of its payoff, and the underlying game structure (here, repeated PD with imperfect monitoring). The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of enabling punishment depends non-monotonically on the cost/benefit of punishment, and that institutional details (who gets what on punishment/termination) can have very large effects. For prediction, the control efficiency (without punishment) and the specific design of the punishment option are critical moderators.

- source: Lacomba, J. A., Lagos, F., Reuben, E., & van Winden, F. (2014). On the escalation and de-escalation of conflict. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot or randomly matched (Strangers) conflict games, conflict expenditures are high and escalate over time, leading to low efficiency (mean earnings well below the endowment). When losers can destroy resources (akin to punishment), conflict expenditures decrease, and in the 'Resistance' game (where destruction can be conditioned on the winner's appropriation), mean earnings are significantly higher than in the baseline. However, destruction itself is wasteful and only increases efficiency if it deters appropriation and reduces conflict. In repeated interaction (Partners), a substantial minority of groups achieve long-lasting peace (zero conflict, high efficiency), but most do not. Peace is achieved through costly signaling (not fighting, low appropriation), not through destruction. Overall, the effect of punishment-like destruction on efficiency is mixed: it can increase efficiency by reducing conflict, but can also reduce it if destruction is frequent. The design dimensions (matching protocol, post-conflict options) strongly moderate these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, the ability of losers to destroy resources) on efficiency in conflict games is highly context-dependent. In one-shot or random-matching settings, allowing punishment (destruction) can reduce conflict expenditures and, if destruction is conditional on appropriation, can increase efficiency. However, if destruction is frequent, efficiency may not improve. In repeated games, the possibility of peace (high efficiency) depends more on costly signaling and restraint than on punishment. For prediction, the presence of a punishment mechanism (destruction) is likely to reduce conflict and increase efficiency only if it credibly deters appropriation and is not overused. The matching protocol (repeated vs. random) and the structure of post-conflict options are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low in these games; enabling punishment can increase efficiency in some variants (notably Resistance/RE), but not always.

- source: Fehr, D., & Sutter, M. (2019). Gossip and the efficiency of interactions. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that enabling third-party gossip in a repeated trust game increases both trustor investments and trustee return ratios, resulting in higher efficiency (total group earnings) compared to a baseline with no third party and to a treatment with only observation (no communication). Efficiency in the Gossip treatment is 13% higher than baseline and 5% higher than Observation. The effect is robust to the cost of gossip and the amount of information available to gossipers, but is reduced when gossip is noisy (messages may be misdelivered). The presence of a third party who can observe and potentially gossip is more effective at increasing efficiency than simply providing first-hand information to trustors. The results suggest that the threat or possibility of gossip acts as a cost-effective social sanction, increasing cooperation and efficiency without direct material punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in trust-game-like environments, enabling a third-party gossip mechanism (even when costly and unverifiable) increases efficiency relative to both a baseline and an observation-only condition. The efficiency gain is substantial and robust to variations in the cost and informativeness of gossip, but is reduced if gossip is unreliable. While the setting is not a standard public goods game, the findings are likely relevant for adjacent environments where indirect social sanctions (gossip, reputation) are possible. For prediction, the presence of a gossip/observation mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, with the largest gains when gossip is reliable and can be selectively transmitted. The effect is not due to direct punishment, but to reputational concerns and information transmission.

- source: Shibayama, S. (2015). Academic commercialization and changing nature of academic cooperation. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that increasing the prevalence of commercial actors (who do not cooperate gratis) in an academic community undermines the reputation mechanism that sustains indirect reciprocity. This leads to a decline in unconditional cooperation, a rise in demand for direct rewards (e.g., coauthorship, barter), and an increase in abstention from cooperation. The overall effect is a reduction in group efficiency and mean payoff, as the system moves away from the collectively optimal state. Centralized rewarding can partially restore cooperation and efficiency, but only if the reward is carefully calibrated; excessive rewards can backfire by incentivizing defectors. The model provides formal predictions and phase diagrams showing how the basin of attraction for efficient cooperation shrinks as commercialization increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and design of punishment, reward, and reputation mechanisms are critical moderators of efficiency in public-goods-like environments. While not a direct PGG or lab experiment, the model is closely related and analyzes efficiency outcomes as a function of game design parameters (e.g., cost/benefit of cooperation, reward size, reputation availability, prevalence of defectors). For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling effective punishment or reward (especially centralized, carefully sized rewards) can increase efficiency, but that the presence of many defectors or commercial actors will reduce the effectiveness of reputation-based cooperation and lower efficiency. The model also highlights the importance of the matching rate for rewards and the availability of reputation information. However, the absence of empirical or experimental data means that quantitative predictions should be made with caution.

- source: Bigoni, M., Potters, J., & Spagnolo, G. (2019). Frequency of interaction, communication and collusion: an experiment. *Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, without communication, cooperation (collusion) rates are low and not significantly affected by the frequency of interaction (flexibility). When pre-play communication is allowed, cooperation rates increase dramatically across all treatments, and the non-monotonic effect of flexibility predicted by theory (higher cooperation at intermediate flexibility) becomes more apparent, but is still secondary to the effect of communication. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, and there is no explicit punishment mechanism; instead, punishment is implemented via Nash reversion (endogenous). The findings suggest that communication is a much stronger facilitator of cooperation than flexibility in this setting with imperfect monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that communication (chat) has a very strong positive effect on cooperation rates in a repeated game with imperfect monitoring, even in the absence of explicit punishment. However, because the game is a Cournot duopoly (not a public goods game), there is no explicit punishment or reward mechanism, and efficiency or group payoff is not reported, the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task. The main implication is that enabling communication can greatly increase cooperation, but the effect of flexibility (frequency of interaction) is non-monotonic and only emerges when communication is present. The results do not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, but suggest that communication is a key moderator.
