- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Chaudhuri, A., & Paichayontvijit, T. (2017). On the long-run efficacy of punishments and recommendations in a laboratory public goods game. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with groups of four, both peer punishment and exhortative recommendations (public messages) increase efficiency compared to a control with no intervention. Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. Punishment starts with lower efficiency due to the cost of punishing but shows an upward trend over time, eventually catching up to or surpassing recommendations in some conditions, particularly with longer time horizons. However, in these experiments, punishment never outperforms the restart recommendation treatment. The effect of punishment is stronger with fixed matching than with random rematching. The cost-effectiveness of punishment depends on the punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) and the length of the game. The study provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, with detailed efficiency data for control, recommendation, and punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear public goods games. It reports efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the social optimum) for control and punishment treatments, with and without recommendations, across different matching protocols and time horizons. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2016). The dynamics of human behavior in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The group average payoff is strongly positively correlated with b1 (self-regarding preference) in punishment and combined conditions. The effect of varying the intensity of incentives (Up, Const, Down) is minimal. The presence of institutional punishment is the key driver of higher cooperation and payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with four players, 50 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, adding institutional punishment (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases group payoffs and average contributions compared to control. The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

- source: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2012). I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

- source: Otten, K., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N. (2020). Heterogeneous groups cooperate in public good problems despite normative disagreements about individual contribution levels. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a linear public goods game with peer punishment and heterogeneous returns, experimentally induced normative disagreement within groups does not reduce cooperation (measured by average contributions) or increase punishment. Instead, the average normative view within a group predicts the group's contribution level. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but notes that joint payoffs are maximized at full contribution, and that observed contribution levels are lower than this maximum, consistent with underprovision. The presence of punishment maintains cooperation at moderate levels, but does not eliminate the effect of group-mean norms. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of normative disagreement in a linear PGG with peer punishment, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, nor does it include a no-punishment control. For prediction tasks, it shows that, in a 3-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGG with punishment (cost 1, impact 3), with full information about others' contributions and payoffs but anonymous punishment, group-mean normative views are a strong predictor of contribution levels. The presence of punishment maintains moderate cooperation, but the paper does not allow direct estimation of the efficiency gain from enabling punishment versus disabling it. The results suggest that, under these parameters, punishment supports cooperation even in the presence of normative disagreement, but the efficiency effect relative to no-punishment is not measured.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Weber, T. O., Weisel, O., & Gächter, S. (2018). Dispositional free riders do not free ride on punishment. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that the availability of punishment leads dispositional free riders (DFR) to increase their contributions to the public good to levels similar to dispositional conditional cooperators (DCC). Both DFR and DCC are equally likely to punish non-cooperators, and their punishment expenditures and motives are similar, regardless of whether punishment reduces payoff inequality (3:1 ratio) or not (1:1 ratio). The presence of punishment thus increases cooperation among DFR, but the paper does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, focusing instead on behavioral outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a one-shot public goods game increases contributions among individuals who would otherwise free ride, and that both free riders and conditional cooperators are willing to punish non-cooperators. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation even among those predisposed to free ride, which is likely to translate into higher efficiency in similar game designs. The lack of direct efficiency data is a limitation for quantitative prediction.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Hendriks, A., Rand, D. G., & Nowak, M. A. (2016). Think global, act local: Preserving the global commons. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that when participants can observe their neighbors' contributions to the public good and can condition their pairwise cooperation (in a PD) on this information, contributions to the public good are sustained at high levels, even in very large groups (up to 1,000 participants). In the control condition, where such information is not available, contributions decline over time. The mechanism is not direct punishment but rather the withholding of cooperation in a separate pairwise game, which acts as a social sanction. The effect is robust to group size and does not require participants to know the overall level of public good provision. The main outcome is sustained contribution, not measured efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing for targeted social sanctions (withholding cooperation in a pairwise PD) based on observed public good contributions can sustain high contribution rates in large groups. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is adjacent to punishment (withholding cooperation as a sanction) but does not involve costly punishment or explicit payoff reduction. The findings suggest that enabling targeted, observable social sanctions—even if not costly—can have a strong positive effect on contribution rates, which may translate to higher efficiency in similar designs, but this is not directly measured.

- source: Shirado, H., & Christakis, N. A. (2020). Network Engineering Using Autonomous Agents Increases Cooperation in Human Groups. *Iscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that introducing bots that facilitate the breaking of ties with defectors ('disengaged' intervention) in dynamic network public goods games leads to a substantial increase in cooperation rates among human participants, compared to controls (no bots, bots that always cooperate, or tit-for-tat bots). The effect is robust even when only a single bot is present and when humans are aware of the bots' presence. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperative players per round. There is some supplementary evidence that, under the disengaged intervention, cooperators earn more than defectors, but group-level efficiency or total group payoff is not the main focus. The study does not involve punishment or sanctions in the standard sense, but the network exclusion of defectors is interpreted as a form of decentralized ostracism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include a punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Instead, it provides evidence about the effect of network engineering (ostracism via tie-breaking) on cooperation rates. The findings suggest that enabling players to break ties with defectors (or having bots facilitate this) can increase cooperation and, to some extent, the earnings of cooperators. However, because efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) is not directly measured or reported, and because punishment is not present, the paper's direct value for predicting efficiency under punishment is limited. It may be useful as adjacent evidence for the effects of exclusion or ostracism mechanisms.

However, it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency.
