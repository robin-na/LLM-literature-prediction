- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Dannenberg, A., & Levin, S. A. (2022). Punishment institutions selected and sustained through voting and learning. *Nature Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

- source: Goodman, J. R. (2023). The problem of opportunity. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based models of the Prisoner's Dilemma with punishment (strong reciprocity), cooperation can be maintained at high levels when only overt defectors are present. However, when agents can mimic cooperators or defect covertly (evading detection and punishment), the population can be dominated by individuals who are not true cooperators, even though the observed rate of cooperation remains high. The models highlight that the opportunity to defect undetected undermines the effectiveness of punishment-based strategies in ensuring genuine prosociality. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but raises concerns that high observed cooperation rates in experimental games may not indicate high group welfare or internalized prosocial preferences.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical caution for interpreting high cooperation rates in punishment-enabled games as evidence of increased efficiency or group welfare. It shows that punishment can maintain high cooperation in simple models, but that the introduction of undetectable defection strategies (mimicry, covert defection) can allow free-riding to persist without reducing the observed cooperation rate. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment may not always translate to higher efficiency, especially if the game design allows for undetected defection. However, the models are dyadic PDs, not public goods games, and do not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction is not possible from this paper.

- source: Jia, C., Zhang, R. X., & Wang, D. (2023). Evolutionary game of cooperative behavior among social capitals in PPP projects: A complex network perspective. *Ain Shams Engineering Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that increasing the default penalty (punishment) leads to higher rates of cooperation among social capitals in a networked prisoner's dilemma setting. Higher network density reduces cooperation, and initial cooperation probability can promote cooperation only when the temptation to defect (betrayal income) is not too high. The results are based on simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not empirical data. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that cooperation is important for the efficiency of PPP projects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that introducing or increasing punishment in a networked prisoner's dilemma can increase cooperation rates, which the authors argue is beneficial for project efficiency. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report results in terms of efficiency ratios. The findings are most relevant for understanding the behavioral mechanism (cooperation) rather than direct prediction of efficiency outcomes. The model's focus on network structure, punishment, and cooperation may inform qualitative expectations about the direction of efficiency effects when punishment is enabled, but does not provide direct predictive data for the downstream task.

- source: Fischborn, M. (2023). The Evolutionary Roots of Moral Responsibility. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that moral responsibility judgment evolved as an adaptation that enabled groups of cooperative individuals to hold free riders responsible more safely by acting in a coordinated way. It distinguishes between two senses of justification in responsibility judgment (demand and permission) and hypothesizes that the permission sense evolved to allow individuals to assess social support for punishment, making collective punishment episodes safer and more stable. The account is situated within Tomasello's two-step theory of the evolution of morality and Boehm's ethnographic work on hunter-gatherer punishment practices. The paper critiques Mameli's account of second-order punishment and meta-emotional dispositions, arguing that coordinated group punishment (rather than second-order punishment) is the key evolutionary innovation. The paper does not provide empirical evidence or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes, but offers a conceptual framework for understanding the robustness and negative bias of responsibility practices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or data for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is a theoretical account of the evolutionary function of punishment and responsibility judgment, emphasizing the importance of coordination and social support in making punishment episodes safer and more stable. While it discusses the role of punishment in stabilizing cooperation and addressing free rider problems, it does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes. Therefore, its value for the downstream prediction task is limited to providing conceptual context about why punishment may have evolved to support cooperation, but it does not inform quantitative or design-specific predictions about efficiency effects in experimental games.

- source: Li, J. M., & Jiang, S. S. (2023). How can governance strategies be developed for marine ecological environment pollution caused by sea-using enterprises?-A study based on evolutionary game theory. *Ocean & Coastal Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in the absence of strong governance mechanisms (punishment, compensation, or high ecological performance appraisal), all parties tend to adopt minimal effort strategies, resulting in low efficiency and poor environmental outcomes (prisoner's dilemma). Introducing punishment (penalties for non-compliance, ecological damage compensation), increasing the weight of ecological indicators in local government performance appraisal, and combining these tools can shift the system to a more efficient equilibrium, where local governments and enterprises fully implement and comply with environmental policies, even if central government supervision is weak. The effect of any single tool is limited, but their combination is necessary to achieve high efficiency in governance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms (penalties, compensation for damage) and reward mechanisms (performance appraisal) are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from introducing punishment in public-goods-like governance settings. The results suggest that, in adjacent multi-agent settings, punishment alone may not be sufficient, but when combined with other governance tools, it can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence and magnitude of punishment, reward, and their combination should be considered as key moderators of treatment efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to weak incentives.

- source: Wei, J., Zhong, Z. W., Chen, H. R., Arango-Aramburo, S., & Zhao, X. L. (2025). Exploring green electricity certificate purchasing behavior via a laboratory experiment. *Renewable & Sustainable Energy Reviews*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether job rotation can reduce the ratchet effect in a principal-agent setting. Three treatments are compared: no rotation (baseline), exogenous rotation (forced switch), and endogenous rotation (principal can pay to switch). In the baseline, workers often restrict effort to avoid revealing private information, resulting in lower efficiency. Exogenous rotation nearly eliminates the ratchet effect, with almost all workers providing high effort and firms earning significantly more. Endogenous rotation, where the principal can pay a small cost to rotate workers, also reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort, but the increase in firm earnings is not statistically significant. The findings support the idea that job rotation (especially exogenous) can improve efficiency by mitigating strategic output restriction, and that even a costly, non-credible threat of rotation (punishment) can have positive behavioral effects.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment option (here, job rotation) can increase efficiency by reducing strategic shirking, especially when the punishment is exogenously imposed. The effect is weaker but still present when the punishment is endogenous and costly. The experimental design is not a standard public goods game, but the principal-agent structure with costly punishment and efficiency outcomes is closely related. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling punishment (even at a cost) is likely to increase efficiency, with the effect size depending on whether the punishment is exogenous or endogenous and on the costliness of punishment.

- source: Lean, C. H., & Jones, C. J. (2023). The evolution of multispecies populations: a multilevel selection perspective. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical model showing that in multispecies mutualisms, group-structured dispersal (MLS1) can stabilize cooperation (mutualism) against cheater invasion, even when mutualism is individually costly. The model is applied to obligate and facultative mutualisms, arguing that metacommunity structure and weight-of-numbers dispersal can allow mutualists to outcompete cheaters at the community level. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or payoffs, but suggests that mutualistic communities are more productive and can proliferate under MLS1 dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is primarily theoretical and does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public-goods-game-like environments with or without punishment. However, it is relevant in that it discusses how group structure, dispersal, and the presence of cheaters affect the stability of cooperation (mutualism) in multispecies settings. The mechanisms discussed (e.g., metacommunity dispersal, weight-of-numbers advantage) are analogous to factors that can affect efficiency in PGGs, but the paper does not quantify these effects or relate them to specific game design dimensions. It does not analyze punishment as a discrete, costly action, but rather as community-level selection against cheaters. Thus, its value for the prediction task is mainly conceptual and mechanistic, not quantitative.

- source: Chen, W. M., Quan, J., Wang, X. J., & Liu, Y. (2023). Evolutionary dynamics from fluctuating environments with deterministic and stochastic noises. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical model of evolutionary dynamics in the hawk-dove game under fluctuating environments with both payoff and demographic noise. Through simulation, it finds that both deterministic and stochastic fluctuations can promote cooperation, sometimes even reversing selection so that cooperation dominates. Deterministic fluctuations are more effective than stochastic ones at promoting cooperation. The mechanism is that noise can create unfair time-averaged payoffs that favor cooperation. High variance in noise promotes cooperation but makes its maintenance less stable. The results are robust to the type of noise distribution. The paper briefly extends the analysis to the prisoner's dilemma, finding similar qualitative results. No public goods game, punishment, or explicit group efficiency analysis is present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not study public goods games, punishment, or group efficiency. Its findings about noise and cooperation in the hawk-dove and prisoner's dilemma games may be of limited indirect relevance for understanding how environmental uncertainty affects cooperation, but not for the specific prediction task involving punishment and efficiency.

  findings: The paper demonstrates, through theoretical analysis and simulation, that in continuous-action iterated dilemmas on double-layer networks, agents' strategies converge under a variety of conditions, including when agents have heterogeneous and limited learning abilities and when environmental effects are present. The convergence is robust to different network structures and layer weightings. However, the paper does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or the impact of punishment or reward mechanisms. The findings are relevant for understanding the stability of cooperation dynamics in complex networks but do not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as it does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency outcomes. Its relevance is limited to providing theoretical context for the convergence of strategies in networked social dilemmas with continuous actions and heterogeneous learning abilities. It may be useful for understanding how learning ability and network structure affect the stability of cooperation, but it does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Wang, L., Jia, D. Y., Zhang, L., Zhu, P. C., Perc, M., Shi, L., & Wang, Z. (2022). Levy noise promotes cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma game with reinforcement learning. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game with reinforcement learning, introducing Lévy noise into the payoff matrix increases the fraction of cooperators in the population. The effect is stronger for Lévy noise (lower stability index α) than for Gaussian noise (α=2) or no noise. The mechanism is that noise accumulates in the Q-value updates, making cooperation more attractive over time. The results are robust to initial conditions. However, the paper does not analyze group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, and does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment, reward, or efficiency outcomes, and the game is a spatial prisoner's dilemma with Q-learning and noise. The findings may be of limited indirect relevance for understanding how stochastic payoff fluctuations affect cooperation rates, but not for the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency.

- source: Li, J. Q., Dang, J. W., Zhang, J. L., Chen, Z. Q., & Dehmer, M. (2022). Degree of satisfaction-based adaptive interaction in spatial Prisoner's dilemma. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that allowing individuals to adapt the intensity of their interactions based on satisfaction with payoffs from neighbors can greatly increase the level of cooperation in a spatial Prisoner's Dilemma. There are optimal values of the satisfaction parameter (\alpha) that maximize cooperation, and both too low and too high values of \alpha reduce cooperation. The mechanism works by creating positive feedback between satisfaction and interaction among cooperators, leading to robust cooperative clusters. However, the study does not include punishment, reward, or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the prediction of efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanctioning, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. Its findings on adaptive interaction intensity and cooperation in spatial Prisoner's Dilemma are only weakly relevant for predicting the effects of punishment in PGGs. The main value is in illustrating that adaptive partner selection or interaction intensity can promote cooperation, but this is not directly transferable to the efficiency effects of punishment.
