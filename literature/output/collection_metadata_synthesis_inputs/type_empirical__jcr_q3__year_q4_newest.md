- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T. (2023). Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

- source: Falvey, R., Lane, T., & Luckraz, S. (2025). On a mechanism that improves efficiency and reduces inequality in voluntary contribution games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper can only inform the baseline (control) efficiency, not the treatment effect of punishment.

- source: van Klingeren, F., & Buskens, V. (2024). Graduated sanctioning, endogenous institutions and sustainable cooperation in common-pool resources: An experimental test. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a dynamic common-pool resource (CPR) game with four players and repeated rounds, the introduction of sanctioning (punishment) mechanisms—either strict or graduated—leads to higher cooperation and resource sustainability compared to no sanctioning. Strict sanctioning (fixed, high-cost punishment) initially results in higher resource size and profit, but graduated sanctioning (punishment severity increases with repeated defection) leads to greater cooperation and lower appropriation over time, eventually closing the gap in resource size and profit.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, dynamic CPR games with endogenous resource dynamics and mutual monitoring. The results suggest that both strict and graduated punishment mechanisms increase efficiency (resource sustainability and group profit) relative to no punishment. Strict punishment is more effective in the short term, but graduated punishment becomes equally or more effective over longer time horizons. Endogenously chosen punishment mechanisms (via group voting) further enhance cooperation and resource outcomes, though the effect on profit is small. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude and time course depending on the punishment regime and whether the institution is endogenously chosen. The findings are most applicable to settings with 4 players, 40 rounds, continuous contribution/appropriation, no chat, and mutual monitoring.
