- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Hou, G. L., Wang, F., Shi, J. Y., Chen, W. J., & Yu, J. (2019). Which is the ideal sanction for cooperation? An experimental study on different types of third-party sanctions. *Psych Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a standard PGG increases group earnings and cooperation compared to a baseline with no sanctions. The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Eldakar, O. T., Kammeyer, J. O., Nagabandi, N., & Gallup, A. C. (2018). Hypocrisy and Corruption: How Disparities in Power Shape the Evolution of Social Control. *Evolutionary Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that punishment, whether selfish or altruistic, can sustain altruism in public goods games, but the efficiency of this outcome is contingent on power dynamics. Selfish punishment by a powerful minority can prevent the collapse into pure selfishness when power is highly asymmetric, but this comes at the cost of reduced group productivity compared to scenarios where altruistic punishment prevails. When power is more evenly distributed, altruistic punishment dominates, leading to higher levels of altruism and group efficiency. The model also shows that if selfish punishment is not possible, populations are more vulnerable to a collapse into selfishness under power asymmetry. Thus, the presence of punishment generally increases group-level cooperation and productivity, but the efficiency gain is maximized when punishment is altruistic and power is symmetric.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the symmetry and distribution of power. In environments with high power asymmetry, punishment may maintain some cooperation but at a lower efficiency due to the persistence of selfish punishers. In more egalitarian settings, punishment is more likely to be altruistic, leading to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with symmetric power and distributed punishment opportunities will see greater efficiency gains from punishment, while those with concentrated or asymmetric power may see only partial gains or even efficiency losses if selfish punishment dominates. The model also highlights the importance of the severity of punishment consequences and the ability to exclude selfish punishers in determining efficiency outcomes.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E. (2019). The effect of decision timing on the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish noncooperation: Sanctioning the past, the present, or the future. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.

  findings: The main finding is that people are less willing to assign costly rewards or punishments when they must decide before observing others' (non)cooperation, compared to after. This effect is robust across both dichotomous and continuous public goods game variants, and for both reward and punishment, though the effect is stronger for punishment. The results are interpreted as evidence for nonconsequential reasoning: uncertainty about future behavior reduces decisiveness and willingness to sanction.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to use costly punishment (and reward) in public goods games is lower when sanctions must be decided before observing others' behavior, compared to after. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding when punishment is likely to be used, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Gërxhani, K., Volker, B., & Van Breemen, J. (2021). Who Will Do It? Volunteering to Change Cooperation Rules in a Heterogeneous Population. *European Sociological Review*.

  findings: Changing the minimum contribution rule from low to high in a public goods game increases both individual and collective earnings for all group members, regardless of their MPCR. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The likelihood of attempting a rule change depends on both social value orientation and benefit level: high-earning proselfs are most likely to initiate a change, while prosocials' behavior depends on whether they are high or low earners. The findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of institutional changes that increase minimum cooperation requirements, but less directly relevant for peer punishment interventions. The absence of peer punishment or reward means the results are most applicable to settings where institutional rules, rather than decentralized sanctions, are the main intervention. The efficiency gain from raising the minimum contribution is clear and robust, suggesting that, in similar designs, enabling a mechanism to increase minimum contributions can substantially improve efficiency.
