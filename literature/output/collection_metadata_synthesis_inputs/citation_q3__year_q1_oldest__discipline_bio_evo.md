- source: Eldakar, O. T., Farrell, D. L., & Wilson, D. S. (2007). Selfish punishment: Altruism can be maintained by competition among cheaters. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

- source: Deng, K. Y., Li, Z. Z., Kurokawa, S., & Chu, T. G. (2012). Rare but severe concerted punishment that favors cooperation. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that implementing rare but severe concerted punishment in a Public Goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable and increase group efficiency. The threshold probability for punishment to be effective decreases as the severity of punishment increases and as the population size increases. The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

- source: Powers, S. T., Taylor, D. J., & Bryson, J. J. (2012). Punishment can promote defection in group-structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games with group structure, the addition of anti-social punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) can prevent the evolution and maintenance of pro-social punishment and cooperation. This reduces the mean fitness and group payoff that would otherwise be achieved through cooperation and pro-social punishment. The effect is stronger in larger groups and when dispersal is less frequent. The presence of anti-social punishment raises the threshold for pro-social punishment to be stable, requiring greater between-group variance (e.g., smaller group sizes) for cooperation to persist. Thus, anti-social punishment makes it less likely that punishment will improve efficiency in group-structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the possibility of anti-social punishment and on group structure parameters such as group size and dispersal frequency. When anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it, especially in larger groups or when between-group variance is low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of anti-social punishment, larger group sizes, and less frequent dispersal all reduce the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency. The paper also clarifies that the effect-to-cost ratio of punishment and cooperation modulate these effects, but the negative impact of anti-social punishment is robust across a range of parameter values.

- source: Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2012). On the interaction of the stick and the carrot in social dilemmas. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that allowing punishment in public goods games reliably increases contributions and, over time, can increase group efficiency, especially when combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms. Punishment is more effective than reward alone in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. However, the costs of punishment can initially reduce efficiency, and only after repeated interactions does the net group payoff surpass the no-punishment baseline. When both punishment and reputation (indirect reciprocity) are available, efficiency is higher than with punishment alone, as the need for costly punishment is reduced. The review also notes that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its cost-effectiveness ratio and that the presence of reward does not always further increase efficiency unless it is combined with punishment in a way that targets norm violators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency relative to control, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly. The efficiency gains are most pronounced when punishment is combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms, which reduce the frequency and cost of punishment needed. The review highlights the importance of game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward or reputation mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency boost from punishment is moderated by these dimensions, and that combining punishment with reputation mechanisms yields the highest efficiency.

The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

However, because the main outcomes are the prevalence of altruistic strategies rather than direct measures of group efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, under certain heterogeneity conditions, punishment is more likely to support stable cooperation, which is typically associated with higher efficiency, but this link is not directly quantified in the paper. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides contextual and mechanistic support but not direct quantitative evidence.

  findings: The paper finds that cooperative strategies in public goods games and related social dilemmas are governed by a common conditional rule: cooperate with those of good reputation and enforce (by withholding benefits or costly punishment) against those of bad reputation. The choice between withholding benefits and costly punishment depends on the structure of the game—costly punishment is more relevant in public goods games where defectors cannot be targeted directly. The paper reviews experimental evidence showing that combining costly punishment with indirect reciprocity can increase cooperation at lower cost than punishment alone, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes. Theoretical and experimental evidence suggests that the same reputation rules (e.g., SCORING, SIMPLE STANDING, STERN JUDGING) operate across frameworks.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing cooperation (and potentially efficiency) in public goods games depends on whether withholding benefits is a viable enforcement mechanism. It suggests that costly punishment is most effective and necessary in public goods games where direct targeting of defectors is not possible. However, the paper does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. It is most useful for understanding the conditions under which punishment is likely to be effective as an enforcement mechanism, and for mapping the conceptual relationships between different enforcement strategies.

  findings: The paper finds that in finite populations, allowing individuals to reward contributors to the public good can initiate and maintain a significant minority of cooperators, even though the majority of the population is typically composed of non-cooperating shirkers. Reward is effective at jump-starting cooperation but is unable to stabilize it in the long run due to exploitation by second-order free-riders. The average group payoff (efficiency) is increased relative to pure defection, but full efficiency is not achieved. The effectiveness of reward is largely independent of group size when key parameters are scaled appropriately. The paper does not provide new results on punishment, but notes that punishment is more effective at stabilizing cooperation once established.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence on the effects of reward (not punishment) in public goods games. It shows that positive incentives can increase efficiency above the baseline of mutual defection, but do not achieve full efficiency due to the persistence of second-order free-riders. The findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, reward alone is insufficient to fully stabilize cooperation and maximize efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with reward enabled, but only indirectly inform predictions about punishment. The analysis of group size, MPCR, and reward parameters is informative for understanding how these dimensions affect efficiency with reward. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for cases where the treatment is reward (not punishment), and less directly informative for punishment-enabled games.

  findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player public goods games, reciprocators who are 'generous' (i.e., tolerate some defection before retaliating) can outperform less generous reciprocators and defectors in terms of average payoff and fixation probability, especially when group size is at least 4. The optimal generosity level depends on the benefit-to-cost ratio and group size. Generosity facilitates the emergence of cooperation (replacement of defectors by reciprocators) but does not help maintain cooperation once established. The model provides explicit conditions for when generosity is advantageous and quantifies the optimal generosity for maximizing the likelihood of cooperation's evolutionary success.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with n ≥ 4, the design of reciprocation strategies (specifically, the degree of generosity/tolerance for defection) can significantly affect the efficiency outcome when cooperation is replacing defection. While the model does not include explicit punishment mechanisms (such as costly punishment), it analyzes a form of contingent cooperation that functions as a sanctioning mechanism (withholding cooperation from defectors). The findings suggest that, for predicting efficiency in games with repeated interaction and contingent cooperation, allowing some tolerance for defection (generosity) can increase the likelihood of efficient cooperative outcomes, especially in the emergence phase. However, the model does not address explicit costly punishment or its direct efficiency effects, so its relevance to games with explicit punishment is adjacent rather than exact.

- source: Takezawa, M., & Price, M. E. (2010). Revisiting The Evolution of Reciprocity in Sizable Groups: Continuous reciprocity in the repeated n-person prisoner's dilemma. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper shows that in repeated n-person public goods games, continuous reciprocity (where players match the average contribution of others) is much more robust than discrete reciprocity (all-or-nothing) in supporting the evolution of cooperation, especially as the marginal per capita return (mpcr) increases. The basin of attraction for continuous reciprocity grows almost linearly with efficiency, allowing cooperation to invade from low initial frequencies when mpcr is high. Assortative interaction (preferential grouping of reciprocators) further enhances the viability of continuous reciprocity, even at lower efficiency. However, both forms of reciprocity struggle to invade when efficiency is low or group size is large, unless assortativity is present. The model does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency of cooperation (mpcr) is a critical moderator of the success of reciprocity-based cooperation in public goods games. In the absence of punishment, high mpcr (approaching 1) is necessary for cooperation to be stable or to invade. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the mpcr dimension should be weighted heavily when estimating efficiency outcomes in control (no-punishment) games. However, because the paper does not model punishment or sanctions, it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. It does, however, highlight that in high-mpcr environments, cooperation can be stable even without punishment, while in low-mpcr environments, cooperation is unlikely to persist without additional mechanisms.

- source: Okada, D., & Bingham, P. M. (2008). Human uniqueness-self-interest and social cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops a two-stage game-theoretic model in which players can cooperate or not, and, if there are non-cooperators, cooperators can choose to fight (coerce) or not. The key result is that the cost structure of coercion (punishment) is decisive: if coercion is cheap and effective (as in synchronous/remote attack, e.g., human projectile weapons), then robust equilibria with high cooperation and high efficiency are possible. If coercion is costly and ineffective (as in asynchronous attack, e.g., most non-human animals), only non-cooperation is robust, and group efficiency is low. The model thus predicts that enabling effective, low-cost punishment can transform a low-efficiency public goods game into a high-efficiency one, provided certain thresholds are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling effective, low-cost punishment (i.e., high punishment effectiveness per unit cost) in a public-goods-like environment can shift the equilibrium from low to high efficiency, especially as group size increases. The model is directly informative for the effect of punishment cost and punishment technology (effectiveness), and indirectly for player count and the structure of the contribution decision. It suggests that, all else equal, lowering punishment cost or increasing punishment effectiveness should make punishment more likely to increase efficiency. The findings are most relevant for one-shot or short games with simultaneous moves and no communication, and may be less directly applicable to repeated or highly structured lab experiments with additional features.

For prediction tasks, the paper implies that game design dimensions such as the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the structure of payoffs, and the degree of group interdependence (e.g., MPCR analogs) are critical moderators. The findings support the idea that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when group welfare is a significant component of individual fitness, and less likely (or even counterproductive) when individual competition dominates.

If the game is a single group or highly overlapping, punishment will not improve efficiency. The presence of a metanorm (either rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) is necessary for this outcome; without it, the only stable equilibrium is full defection (no norm adoption, no punishment).   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (especially when reinforced by a metanorm, such as rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) can shift a population from an inefficient equilibrium (low efficiency, no cooperation) to an efficient one (high efficiency, full cooperation and sanctioning), provided the costs and benefits are favorable. The results suggest that, in public goods game-like environments, the introduction of punishment (with or without additional metanorms) can substantially increase group efficiency, especially if the cost of punishing is not too high and the benefit to punishers is sufficient. The Reward Model (rewarding punishers) is particularly effective, suggesting that institutional or social mechanisms that reward sanctioning may be more robust in promoting efficiency than those that only punish non-punishers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the cost/benefit structure and initial conditions. However, the results are theoretical and assume well-mixed populations and specific forms of cultural transmission.
