- source: Kölle, F. (2015). Heterogeneity and cooperation: The role of capability and valuation on public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: homogeneous groups, groups with one high-valuation member (valuation heterogeneity), and groups with one high-capability member (capability heterogeneity). Each group plays 20 rounds, with punishment enabled in rounds 11-20. Efficiency (group payoff as % of social optimum) is measured with and without punishment. In homogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly change efficiency. In valuation-heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase efficiency and increases inequality. In capability-heterogeneous groups, punishment significantly increases efficiency (from 86% to 93% of optimum) and reduces inequality. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus conditional on the type of heterogeneity, being most effective when group members differ in capability but not in valuation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform but depends on the type of group heterogeneity. If the game is homogeneous or has only capability heterogeneity (where some players' contributions are more productive but all value the public good equally), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially in the capability-heterogeneous case. If the group has valuation heterogeneity (where some players value the public good more), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency and increases inequality. The control efficiency (without punishment) is also higher in capability-heterogeneous groups than in valuation-heterogeneous groups. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, the type of heterogeneity (capability vs. valuation) is a critical moderator. Other design features (e.g., punishment cost, group size, feedback) are held constant in this study.

- source: Perc, M. (2016). Phase transitions in models of human cooperation. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment into spatial public goods games can enable the survival and dominance of cooperative strategies under harsher conditions (lower multiplication factors) than in the absence of punishment. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the fine and cost parameters, with phase transitions (sometimes discontinuous) between different strategy compositions. The addition of reward and the interplay of positive and negative reciprocity further enrich the dynamics, leading to complex patterns such as cyclic dominance. However, the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes; instead, it focuses on the prevalence of cooperation and the conditions under which cooperation can persist.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment can promote cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when the punishment fine is sufficiently large and the cost is not prohibitive. The models specify how game design parameters (e.g., group size, multiplication factor, punishment fine and cost) affect the prevalence of cooperation. However, because the main outcomes are strategy frequencies and phase transitions rather than explicit efficiency or group payoff, the paper offers only indirect support for predicting efficiency effects of punishment. It is most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of punishment-related parameters and for mapping the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher efficiency) is likely to emerge, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data for direct prediction.

- source: Chen, X. J., Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2014). Probabilistic sharing solves the problem of costly punishment. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that probabilistic sharing of punishment duties in a public goods game can solve the problem of costly punishment and promote cooperation, even when punishment is expensive. In well-mixed populations, probabilistic sanctioning transforms the game into a coordination game with two stable equilibria (full cooperation and full defection). In structured populations, sharing punishment duties leads to high cooperation rates, but only if the severity and frequency of punishment are carefully balanced; both too little and too much punishment can reduce cooperation. The main reported outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. Theoretical payoff expressions are provided, and the analysis explains why intermediate punishment is optimal for cooperation, but efficiency is not directly measured or reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the way punishment is implemented (shared probabilistically or periodically) can have a major impact on cooperation rates in public goods games. While the main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, the derived payoff expressions and analysis of invasion dynamics suggest that intermediate levels of punishment severity and frequency can maximize group welfare by supporting high cooperation at lower cost. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the population and the sharing of punishment duties, are important moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, because efficiency is not directly reported, quantitative predictions about efficiency must be inferred indirectly from the cooperation rates and payoff formulas.

- source: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K. (2013). Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). In the stranger condition (random rematching), punishment sustains higher contributions (11.38) than reward (9.21). The effect of punishment on cooperation is robust to group reshuffling, while reward is much less effective when strategic reciprocity is not possible. The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.

- source: Joffily, M., Masclet, D., Noussair, C. N., & Villeval, M. C. (2014). Emotions, Sanctions, and Cooperation. *Southern Economic Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions, with groups converging to near-full cooperation. Emotional responses (measured by SCR and self-reported valence) are closely linked to both cooperation and punishment: positive emotions are associated with higher contributions, and negative emotions in response to free riding predict punishment behavior. Receiving punishment leads to negative emotions and increased subsequent contributions by free riders. The study provides strong evidence that punishment opportunities can sustain high cooperation, but the main outcomes are emotional and behavioral (contribution rates), not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and a punishment stage with convex costs and anonymous assignment, the introduction of punishment opportunities leads to a large increase in average contributions (from about 9.3 to 19.2 out of 20). While efficiency is not directly reported, the near-maximal contributions in the punishment condition imply that group efficiency approaches the social optimum. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no rewards, anonymous punishment, full feedback) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar lab PGG designs. However, the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff reporting is a limitation for precise quantitative prediction.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: Charness, G., & Yang, C. L. (2014). Starting small toward voluntary formation of efficient large groups in public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that endogenous group formation with exit, exclusion, and merger options produces very high contribution rates and group efficiency in public goods games. In the main treatment, where the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) increases with group size, groups tend to grow large and stable, and efficiency is maximized. When the efficiency gain from larger groups is capped, groups remain smaller and less stable, and efficiency is lower, but still substantially higher than in fixed-group baselines. Exclusion (a form of sanction) is used to remove low contributors, which protects cooperators and sustains high efficiency. Redemption (the ability for excluded individuals to rejoin) further boosts efficiency. The results suggest that voluntary group formation with exclusion and merger mechanisms can solve the free-rider problem and achieve high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games, mechanisms allowing voluntary group formation, exit, exclusion (as a sanction), and merger can dramatically increase efficiency compared to fixed groups. The effect is especially strong when economies of scale are present (i.e., when MPCR increases with group size). The exclusion mechanism functions as a sanction, but is not a direct costly punishment; rather, it is a group-level sanction that removes low contributors. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of exclusion/exit/merger mechanisms and increasing MPCR with group size are strong positive moderators of the efficiency effect of sanction-like mechanisms. However, the effect is not directly about costly punishment, but about exclusion as a sanction. The results are less informative for standard costly punishment, but highly informative for exclusion-based sanctions and endogenous group formation. Control efficiency (fixed group) is much lower than in the endogenous group treatments.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2016). Competition of tolerant strategies in the spatial public goods game. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that introducing diverse tolerant strategies into the spatial public goods game can significantly increase group efficiency, sometimes leading to defector-free, fully cooperative states. The effect depends on the synergy factor (r), the cost of monitoring (gamma), and the system size. In structured populations, coexistence of multiple tolerant strategies can create synergistic effects that outperform single-strategy or uniform-tolerance scenarios. The paper provides detailed phase diagrams showing which strategy combinations are stable under different parameter regimes. The main efficiency gains occur when tolerant strategies with different thresholds form alliances, crowding out defectors and maximizing group payoff. The results highlight the importance of diversity in tolerance and the structure of interactions for achieving high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the introduction of conditional strategies (tolerant players) that abstain from cooperation when defection is high can substantially increase efficiency, especially when multiple tolerance thresholds are present. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of such conditional strategies (even in the absence of explicit costly punishment) can be as or more effective than standard punishment in raising efficiency, particularly in structured populations. The results are most relevant for games with spatial structure, optional participation, and where monitoring costs are not prohibitive. The paper does not directly manipulate or analyze explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, so its direct applicability to punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled prediction is limited, but it provides a strong adjacent signal about the power of conditional sanctioning and abstention. For prediction, the key moderators are the synergy factor (mpcr), the cost of monitoring (analogous to punishment cost), and the structure of the population.

- source: Oprea, R., Charness, G., & Friedman, D. (2014). Continuous time and communication in a public-goods experiment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a challenging public-goods environment (MPCR = 0.3, 4 players), continuous time by itself does not significantly increase contributions compared to discrete time. However, when free-form chat is allowed, continuous time enables groups to coordinate and sustain full contributions, with median rates at 100%. Discrete time with chat also increases contributions, but less so and with more heterogeneity. Limited communication (pre-programmed messages) does not improve contributions over no communication. The findings suggest that the combination of continuous time and rich communication is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms means the results are specific to communication as a coordination device.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or reward, enabling free-form communication (chat) in a public-goods game with continuous time can dramatically increase contribution rates, which likely translates to higher efficiency. However, without communication, continuous time alone does not improve efficiency much over discrete time. The results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without chat, and with continuous versus discrete time, but do not inform about punishment effects. The findings are less relevant for settings where punishment or reward is available, as those mechanisms are not present in this study.

- source: Martinsson, P., Nam, P. K., & Villegas-Palacio, C. (2013). Conditional cooperation and disclosure in developing countries. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that public disclosure of individual contributions in a one-shot public goods game leads to a small, statistically insignificant increase in average unconditional contributions in both Colombia and Vietnam. The distribution of contributor types (free-riders, conditional cooperators, etc.) is largely stable across disclosure and no-disclosure treatments, with only minor shifts. The effect of disclosure is much smaller than the effects typically found for monetary punishment in the literature. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.

It does, however, provide evidence that public disclosure (a form of social image incentive) has only a small and statistically insignificant effect on average contributions in highly collectivist developing country contexts. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative about the efficiency effects of punishment, but it does suggest that non-monetary social incentives (like disclosure) may have limited impact on contributions and, by extension, on efficiency in similar settings.

Neuroimaging studies show that punishment threats activate cognitive control regions and increase cooperative behavior, particularly among self-regarding or Machiavellian individuals. However, because the review does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its value for prediction is primarily in identifying moderators (e.g., individual differences, presence of incentives, trust signals) and in supporting the general mechanism by which punishment increases cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used to inform the likely direction and moderators of punishment effects, but not to calibrate the magnitude of efficiency changes.

- source: Gelcich, S., Guzman, R., Rodríguez-Sickert, C., Castilla, J. C., & Cárdenas, J. C. (2013). Exploring External Validity of Common Pool Resource Experiments: Insights from Artisanal Benthic Fisheries in Chile. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: This paper reports a field-lab experiment with Chilean artisanal fishers playing a 20-round common pool resource game. In the first 10 rounds (open access, no enforcement), overharvesting increased and efficiency declined, especially among low-performance unions and nonunionized fishers. In rounds 11-20, a weak enforcement regime was introduced: two players per round were randomly inspected, and if caught overharvesting, their entire round's harvest was confiscated. This sanction led to a sharp reduction in overharvest and a large increase in group earnings (efficiency) among unionized fishers, especially those from high-performance unions, who sustained cooperation and high efficiency through the end of the game. Low-performance unions showed a temporary improvement but cooperation eroded over time. Nonunionized fishers did not respond to enforcement and remained inefficient. The paper provides direct evidence that even weak sanctions can improve efficiency in CPR settings, but the effect is strongly moderated by group social capital and prior cooperative norms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment/sanction mechanism (even if weak and probabilistic) in a repeated CPR game with continuous contribution/extraction choices and no communication can substantially increase group efficiency, especially when baseline efficiency is not already at floor. The effect is strongest in groups with high social capital or prior cooperative norms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment in similar CPR/Pgg-like games will increase efficiency, with the magnitude depending on group composition and baseline cooperativeness. The design features—5 players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous extraction, no rewards, and a probabilistic, high-impact punishment—are all relevant for mapping to prediction. The control (no punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while the treatment (punishment enabled) yields much higher efficiency for unionized groups.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2014). Rewards and the evolution of cooperation in public good games. *Biology Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in threshold public good games, voluntary reward funds can evolve and lead to efficient cooperation, overcoming coordination problems that typically prevent the evolution of cooperation in large, anonymous groups. The model shows that, regardless of initial conditions, rewarders can invade a population of defectors if the reward fund's return is sufficiently high, leading to a cascade where cooperators eventually dominate. The resulting group payoff and average fitness are higher with rewards than without, and the system can reach full cooperation or stable mixed equilibria depending on the risk and threshold parameters. The paper contrasts this with institutional punishment, which is argued to be less effective or more difficult to initiate in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that introducing a reward mechanism in threshold public good games will increase group efficiency and average payoff, especially in settings where coordination is otherwise difficult. While it does not directly analyze punishment, it offers indirect evidence that reward mechanisms can be more effective than punishment in some coordination contexts. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a reward mechanism (with sufficient effectiveness and sustainability) should increase efficiency relative to control. The model's explicit treatment of group size, threshold, risk, and reward parameters can inform how these dimensions moderate the effect of rewards on efficiency.

- source: Cooper, D. J., & Kühn, K. U. (2014). Communication, Renegotiation, and the Scope for Collusion. *American Economic Journal-microeconomics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment shows that communication, especially when it allows explicit threats of punishment, is critical for sustaining collusion in repeated games with incentives to cheat. Limited message spaces (even those allowing contingent punishment messages) do not sustain collusion, while open chat does. Renegotiation (chat between periods) further increases collusion, likely due to the role of verbal (non-monetary) punishment. Explicit threats of punishment significantly reduce cheating, and sending promises of trustworthy behavior also reduces cheating, though to a lesser extent. The effect of communication is not simply due to increased trust or appeals to mutual benefit, but specifically to the ability to coordinate on credible punishment strategies. The results are robust to various econometric specifications.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment depends critically on the communication environment. Open-ended chat that allows explicit threats of punishment leads to higher and more stable cooperation (collusion) than limited or structured message spaces. However, the game is not a standard public goods game, and efficiency is not directly measured as a share of maximum group payoff. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is possible, its effectiveness is greatly enhanced by the ability to communicate explicit threats, and that the structure of communication channels is a key moderator. The results also highlight that behavioral outcomes (collusion, cheating) can diverge from what would be predicted by monetary incentives alone, due to the role of verbal and social punishment.
