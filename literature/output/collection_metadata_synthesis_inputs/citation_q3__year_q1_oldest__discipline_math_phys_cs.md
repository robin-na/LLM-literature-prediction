- source: Eldakar, O. T., Farrell, D. L., & Wilson, D. S. (2007). Selfish punishment: Altruism can be maintained by competition among cheaters. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

- source: Deng, K. Y., Li, Z. Z., Kurokawa, S., & Chu, T. G. (2012). Rare but severe concerted punishment that favors cooperation. *Theoretical Population Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that implementing rare but severe concerted punishment in a Public Goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable and increase group efficiency. The threshold probability for punishment to be effective decreases as the severity of punishment increases and as the population size increases. The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

The effect is present for both perfectly rational and boundedly rational agents. The degree of rationality (K) also modulates the equilibrium, but punishment always leads to higher system payoffs and more cooperation than the control. The effect is robust to changes in the rationality parameter K. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to defection. The specific parameterization (N=6, r=3.6, c=1.25, punishment fine and cost) is informative for quantitative prediction.

- source: Powers, S. T., Taylor, D. J., & Bryson, J. J. (2012). Punishment can promote defection in group-structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games with group structure, the addition of anti-social punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) can prevent the evolution and maintenance of pro-social punishment and cooperation. This reduces the mean fitness and group payoff that would otherwise be achieved through cooperation and pro-social punishment. The effect is stronger in larger groups and when dispersal is less frequent. The presence of anti-social punishment raises the threshold for pro-social punishment to be stable, requiring greater between-group variance (e.g., smaller group sizes) for cooperation to persist. Thus, anti-social punishment makes it less likely that punishment will improve efficiency in group-structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the possibility of anti-social punishment and on group structure parameters such as group size and dispersal frequency. When anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it, especially in larger groups or when between-group variance is low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of anti-social punishment, larger group sizes, and less frequent dispersal all reduce the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency. The paper also clarifies that the effect-to-cost ratio of punishment and cooperation modulate these effects, but the negative impact of anti-social punishment is robust across a range of parameter values.

- source: Sigmund, K., Hauert, C., Traulsen, A., & De Silva, H. (2011). Social Control and the Social Contract: The Emergence of Sanctioning Systems for Collective Action. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Punishment (peer or pool) can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, especially when participation is voluntary. (2) Pool punishment with second-order punishment is particularly effective, leading to stable, high-efficiency cooperative regimes. (3) Peer punishment can also support cooperation, but is less robust and can be undermined by second-order free riders. (4) Compulsory participation (no option to abstain) undermines the effectiveness of punishment, resulting in dominance by defectors and low efficiency. (5) The costs of punishment (especially pool punishment) can reduce efficiency if not balanced by increased cooperation, but with sufficient rounds or strong imitation, efficiency gains are realized. (6) The structure of the punishment mechanism (peer vs. pool, presence of second-order punishment) and the option for voluntary participation are key moderators of the efficiency effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when participation is voluntary and when pool punishment with second-order punishment is available. The model shows that the design dimensions—such as player count, number of rounds, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and whether participation is voluntary—critically shape the efficiency outcome. In particular, pool punishment with second-order punishment and voluntary participation are predicted to yield the highest efficiency. Peer punishment can also increase efficiency, but is less stable and more vulnerable to free-riding. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency, enabling punishment (especially with the above features) is likely to substantially increase efficiency. However, if punishment is costly and not well-targeted, or if participation is compulsory, efficiency gains may be limited or negative.

- source: Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2012). On the interaction of the stick and the carrot in social dilemmas. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  findings: The paper finds that allowing punishment in public goods games reliably increases contributions and, over time, can increase group efficiency, especially when combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms. Punishment is more effective than reward alone in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. However, the costs of punishment can initially reduce efficiency, and only after repeated interactions does the net group payoff surpass the no-punishment baseline. When both punishment and reputation (indirect reciprocity) are available, efficiency is higher than with punishment alone, as the need for costly punishment is reduced. The review also notes that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its cost-effectiveness ratio and that the presence of reward does not always further increase efficiency unless it is combined with punishment in a way that targets norm violators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency relative to control, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly. The efficiency gains are most pronounced when punishment is combined with reputation or indirect reciprocity mechanisms, which reduce the frequency and cost of punishment needed. The review highlights the importance of game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward or reputation mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency boost from punishment is moderated by these dimensions, and that combining punishment with reputation mechanisms yields the highest efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games and similar social dilemmas, the introduction of punishment (even costly, altruistic punishment) reliably increases cooperation and group payoff, moving outcomes closer to the social optimum. Theoretical and computational models (e.g., Homo Egualis utility, learning automata) and empirical studies are reviewed, all supporting the claim that punishment transforms the equilibrium from inefficient defection to more efficient cooperation. The paper also notes that while punishment increases efficiency, it is not always evolutionarily stable due to the possibility of second-order free-riders. Additional mechanisms such as reputation, volunteering, and network structure can further support cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and review-based support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games and similar environments will increase group efficiency (payoff relative to the cooperative optimum), compared to control games without punishment. The effect is robust across a range of game structures, player counts, and repeated interactions, as long as punishment is possible and salient. The paper also highlights that the magnitude of the effect may depend on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of reputation mechanisms, and the network structure of interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when compared to the Nash equilibrium of defection in the absence of punishment.
