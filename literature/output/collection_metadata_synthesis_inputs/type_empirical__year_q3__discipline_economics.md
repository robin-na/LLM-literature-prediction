- source: Gürerk, Lauer, T., & Scheuermann, M. (2018). Leadership with individual rewards and punishments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the addition of individual punishment or reward mechanisms (either centralized via a leader or decentralized among peers) leads to significantly higher contributions and higher net payoffs compared to the baseline game without such mechanisms. The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment is more effective than reward at sustaining high contributions over time, especially in the peer punishment treatment, where contributions even increase in the second half of the game. Net payoffs are highest in reward treatments (due to the reward mechanism's structure), but also higher in punishment treatments than in the baseline. The presence of a leader (who contributes first and can reward or punish) does not further increase contributions or payoffs compared to peer-based reward or punishment. The effect of punishment on increasing contributions and payoffs is robust, and punishment from leaders has a stronger effect on subsequent contributions than peer punishment. Anti-social punishment is less frequent when punishment is centralized with a leader.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, enabling individual punishment (with a cost/impact ratio of 1:3) increases both contributions and net payoffs (i.e., efficiency) compared to the baseline without punishment. The effect is robust whether punishment is centralized (leader) or decentralized (peers), and is not further enhanced by adding a leader who leads by example. The control (no punishment) efficiency is lower than in the punishment-enabled treatments. The findings support the prediction that, holding game design dimensions constant, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency. The specific cost and impact structure (1 point cost reduces target by 3 points) is important for generalization. Reward mechanisms also increase efficiency, but the structure of the reward mechanism in this experiment is payoff-increasing by design. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar public goods game environments.

- source: Engl, F., Riedl, A., & Weber, R. (2021). Spillover Effects of Institutions on Cooperative Behavior, Preferences, and Beliefs. *American Economic Journal-microeconomics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Institutions that enforce cooperation in a public goods game (via a minimum contribution requirement, with full forfeiture for under-contribution) substantially increase group welfare in the regulated game and also increase welfare in an unregulated, otherwise identical game played by the same group. The direct effect on welfare is large, and the spillover effect is significant and economically meaningful (about 3-3.4 points on a 20-point endowment, or 30-40% of the direct effect). Both exogenous and endogenous institutions produce these effects, but the endogenous institution's spillover effect grows over time as the minimum requirement increases. The effect is mainly due to higher contributions among those already contributing, not by converting free riders. The positive effects on welfare and contributions persist after the institution is removed and extend to new partners. The mechanisms include increased beliefs about others' cooperativeness and stronger conditional cooperation preferences. No spillover is found to trust games. Heterogeneity exists: younger subjects and those with high positive reciprocity show smaller spillover effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a strong, central punishment institution (with full forfeiture for under-contribution) in a repeated linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and MPCR 0.5. The control (no institution) game shows declining contributions and welfare over time, while the treatment (with punishment) achieves near-full cooperation and much higher welfare. Critically, the paper quantifies both the direct and spillover effects on group welfare, allowing for prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design. The results are robust across exogenous and endogenous institution types, though the time dynamics differ. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with similar group size, round structure, and punishment technology (centralized, all-or-nothing, no cost to punisher). The absence of spillover to trust games suggests limits to generalizability across domains. The evidence is strongest for games with continuous contribution choices, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Lee, J., & Min, S. (2021). The effects of repeated induction of emotions on cooperation and punishment. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment used a repeated public goods game with costly punishment and stranger matching. In each round, groups of three played five rounds, with either happiness or anger induced via video clips after the contribution stage. The only difference between treatments was the induced emotion. The main findings are: (1) Contributions decay over rounds in the happiness treatment but remain stable in the anger treatment; (2) Final-round welfare and group earnings are higher in the anger treatment than in the happiness treatment; (3) The key mechanism is that antisocial-punished cooperators reduce their contributions in the happiness treatment but not in the anger treatment; (4) Prosocial and antisocial punishment levels do not differ significantly between treatments, but their trends and effects do. Thus, the emotional context moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency: punishment sustains efficiency only when anger is present, not when happiness is induced.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games depends on the emotional environment. With standard parameters (3 players, 5 rounds, MPCR 0.6, costly punishment with 1:3 cost:impact), punishment sustains or increases efficiency when anger is induced, but not when happiness is induced. In the absence of induced happiness, the efficiency effect of punishment is positive or neutral; with induced happiness, the effect is negative. For prediction, the emotional context (incidental happiness vs. anger) is a critical moderator. The control (no-punishment) baseline is not directly reported, but a baseline without emotion induction (see Appendix J) shows similar trends to the anger treatment, suggesting that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is typical unless happiness is induced. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, consider whether the environment is emotionally neutral/angry (expect positive or stable efficiency effect) or happy (expect negative effect).

- source: Gürdal, M. Y., Gürerk, & Yahsi, M. (2021). Culture and prevalence of sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Angelovski, A., Di Cagno, D., Güth, W., Marazzi, F., & Panaccione, L. (2018). Does heterogeneity spoil the basket? The role of productivity and feedback information on public good provision. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 4-player, 10-round public goods game with centralized punishment, the presence of a sanctioner (punishment enabled) increases both average contributions and total group payoffs (social welfare) compared to a control with no punishment. However, if the sanctioner's payoff is made contingent on group cooperation (pool punishment), both contributions and efficiency are lower than when the sanctioner receives a fixed endowment. The negative effect of the pool punishment scheme is not due to differences in punishment frequency or severity, but rather to contributors' aversion to the sanctioner's extrinsic incentives (perceived as indirect free-riding). The paper provides direct evidence that punishment increases efficiency, but the institutional details of the punishment mechanism (especially the sanctioner's payoff scheme) are crucial moderators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the institutional design—specifically, whether the sanctioner is paid a fixed or variable (pool) payoff. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger when the punishment institution uses fixed payoffs for sanctioners. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the sanctioner's payoff scheme, and the marginal per-capita return (MPCR). Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the treatment effect of punishment is moderated by the sanctioner's incentive structure.

- source: Mantilla, C., Zhou, L., Wang, C., Yang, D. H., Shen, S. P., & Seabright, P. (2021). Favoring your in-group can harm both them and you: Ethnicity and public goods provision in China. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in public goods games with punishment, ethnically mixed groups experience lower contributions and lower group earnings than homogeneous groups. This is primarily because participants are less likely to punish co-ethnic free-riders when out-group members are present, reducing the deterrent effect of punishment. The effect is strongest in unbalanced mixed groups (3+1 composition), where efficiency losses are largest. The study provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing institution is undermined by in-group favoritism in diverse groups, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or even reversed in ethnically mixed groups due to in-group favoritism in punishment assignment. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the group composition (homogeneous vs mixed) is a critical moderator: punishment is less effective (or even harmful) in mixed groups because in-group members are less likely to punish each other, reducing deterrence and lowering contributions and earnings. The game uses standard PGG parameters (4 players, 5 rounds, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=2, punishment impact=5, no chat, no rewards, anonymous punishment). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between group types with punishment enabled. The findings suggest that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment in mixed-ethnicity groups may not increase efficiency and can even decrease it relative to homogeneous groups.

- source: Bruhin, A., Janizzi, K., & Thöni, C. (2020). Uncovering the heterogeneity behind cross-cultural variation in antisocial punishment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper shows that the effectiveness of peer punishment in public goods games is highly heterogeneous and depends critically on the composition of punishment types within groups. Using a finite mixture model, the authors classify subjects into three types: non-punishers (Type N), antisocial/free rider punishers (Type AF), and free rider-only punishers (Type F). The presence of Type AF subjects, who punish both low and high contributors, is strongly associated with lower group earnings and reduced efficiency. In contrast, groups with only Type F and Type N subjects achieve much higher efficiency. The number of Type AF subjects in a group explains a large share of the variance in group earnings, more than baseline cooperativeness or period effects. Cross-cultural differences in the prevalence of Type AF subjects explain much of the observed variation in the effectiveness of punishment across societies.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform: it depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishers (Type AF) in the group or population. In environments where antisocial punishment is common, enabling punishment can substantially reduce efficiency. In environments where only free rider punishment occurs, enabling punishment can increase or sustain efficiency. The paper provides quantitative evidence (e.g., groups with four Type AF subjects earn 68.7% less than groups with only Type F/N) and regression models showing that the number of Type AF subjects is a strong predictor of group earnings. Thus, for prediction, the cultural or group composition regarding punishment types is a critical moderator, and control game efficiency alone is insufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering this heterogeneity.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., & Meraglia, S. (2019). Endogenous sanctioning institutions and migration patterns: Experimental evidence. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with two groups and endogenous migration, the presence of formal sanctioning institutions (a fixed fee plus a fine on private account allocations) leads to higher contributions and significantly higher average payoffs compared to sanction-free groups. When only one group has sanctions (No-Voting treatment), that group becomes overpopulated, and payoff inequality emerges. When groups can vote on whether to implement sanctions (Voting treatment), most groups eventually choose sanctions, group sizes become more balanced, migration decreases, and overall efficiency and average payoffs increase further. The ability to endogenously choose institutions (via voting) leads to more efficient and equitable outcomes than exogenously imposed sanctions. The effect of sanctions on efficiency is robust and positive, and the endogenous choice of sanctions amplifies this effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling formal punishment in a repeated PGG with migration increases efficiency and average payoffs, relative to a control without punishment. The effect is even stronger when the sanctioning institution is chosen endogenously by group vote. The design dimensions—such as player count (10, split into two groups of 5), number of rounds (30), absence of chat, continuous contributions, and a formal, automatic punishment mechanism—are all specified. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) varies with group size but is always less than 1, and the punishment is highly effective (80% fine on private account, plus a fixed fee). There is no reward mechanism. Players are shown summary information about both groups' contributions and rule-sets. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, and allowing endogenous institutional choice (voting) will further boost efficiency and reduce inequality. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is substantially lower than with punishment enabled. This paper is highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Waichman, I. (2020). LINKING WEALTH AND PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVENESS: PUNISHMENT AND COOPERATION UNDER CONGRUENT HETEROGENEITIES. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This lab experiment uses a linear public good game with peer punishment to study the effects of heterogeneity in endowment and punishment effectiveness, both separately and jointly. The main findings are: (1) Heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness alone does not affect efficiency or cooperation compared to symmetric groups. (2) Endowment heterogeneity (rich and poor members) reduces efficiency and cooperation, as rich members contribute a lower share of their endowment. (3) When both endowment and punishment effectiveness are heterogeneous (either the rich or the poor is the most effective punisher), efficiency and cooperation are not reduced compared to symmetry, and are higher than under endowment heterogeneity alone. (4) Among groups that achieve high cooperation, efficiency is higher in the 'Rich strong' treatment (richest is most effective punisher) than in the 'Poor strong' treatment, because less punishment is needed to sustain cooperation. (5) Overall, congruent heterogeneities (richest is most effective punisher) do not hinder and may even facilitate efficiency and cooperation compared to endowment heterogeneity alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public good games with peer punishment, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency compared to control conditions with endowment heterogeneity alone. However, the efficiency gain from punishment is moderated by the structure of heterogeneity: punishment is most effective (in terms of efficiency) when either all players are symmetric or when the richest player is also the most effective punisher. Endowment heterogeneity without matching punishment effectiveness reduces efficiency, but joint heterogeneities (especially congruent ones) restore efficiency to levels comparable to symmetry. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the alignment of endowment and punishment effectiveness. The control efficiency (without punishment) is lowest under endowment heterogeneity, and the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is largest in this case, especially if punishment effectiveness is also heterogeneous and congruent with endowment.

- source: Glöckner, A., Kube, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2018). The joint benefits of observed and unobserved social sanctions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.

- source: De Geest, L. R., & Kingsley, D. C. (2021). Norm enforcement with incomplete information *. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with two high-endowment and two low-endowment players per group, and costly peer punishment, the ability to observe others' endowments (complete information) leads to the emergence and enforcement of efficient 'contribute-your-endowment' norms. This results in higher contributions and group earnings, especially from high-endowment players, and higher payoffs for low-endowment players. When endowments are private (incomplete information), groups adapt by enforcing a 'contribute-the-Low-endowment' norm, primarily enforced by low-endowment players. This norm prevents total unraveling of cooperation but enables high-endowment players to mimic low-endowment behavior and contribute less, reducing group efficiency. The expected costs and payoffs of noncompliance are explicitly estimated, showing that punishment is less targeted and less effective at sustaining high efficiency under incomplete information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the information structure. When group members can observe both contributions and endowments, punishment supports high efficiency by enabling enforcement of optimal contribution norms.
