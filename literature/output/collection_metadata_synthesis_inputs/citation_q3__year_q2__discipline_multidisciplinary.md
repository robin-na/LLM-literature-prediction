- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Chaudhuri, A., & Paichayontvijit, T. (2017). On the long-run efficacy of punishments and recommendations in a laboratory public goods game. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with groups of four, both peer punishment and exhortative recommendations (public messages) increase efficiency compared to a control with no intervention. Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. Punishment starts with lower efficiency due to the cost of punishing but shows an upward trend over time, eventually catching up to or surpassing recommendations in some conditions, particularly with longer time horizons. However, in these experiments, punishment never outperforms the restart recommendation treatment. The effect of punishment is stronger with fixed matching than with random rematching. The cost-effectiveness of punishment depends on the punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) and the length of the game. The study provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, with detailed efficiency data for control, recommendation, and punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear public goods games. It reports efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the social optimum) for control and punishment treatments, with and without recommendations, across different matching protocols and time horizons. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2016). The dynamics of human behavior in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The group average payoff is strongly positively correlated with b1 (self-regarding preference) in punishment and combined conditions. The effect of varying the intensity of incentives (Up, Const, Down) is minimal. The presence of institutional punishment is the key driver of higher cooperation and payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with four players, 50 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, adding institutional punishment (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases group payoffs and average contributions compared to control. The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

- source: Hetzer, M., & Sornette, D. (2013). The Co-Evolution of Fairness Preferences and Costly Punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The simulation model, based on the structure of standard public goods games with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost: 1 MU spent removes 3 MUs from target), shows that when agents evolve under disadvantageous inequity aversion, they develop a stable, empirically-matched propensity to punish defectors (k≈0.25). This level of punishment is sufficient to sustain high cooperation and maximize group fitness/wealth. The model finds that disadvantageous inequity aversion is evolutionarily stable and dominates other preference types, leading to higher average wealth, lower volatility, and longer agent lifetimes. While the model does not report efficiency in the strict sense (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible), it provides strong evidence that punishment under disadvantageous inequity aversion increases group welfare and evolutionary success compared to self-regarding or non-punishing populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in a standard public goods game with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment leads to higher group welfare and evolutionary success, provided agents have disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model is calibrated to match empirical punishment levels and shows that these levels are sufficient to sustain high cooperation and maximize group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar game designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff/welfare) relative to the control. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative efficiency ratios, so the evidence is most useful for qualitative or directional prediction, especially in settings matching the model's design parameters.

- source: Abdallah, S., Sayed, R., Rahwan, I., LeVeck, B. L., Cebrian, M., Rutherford, A., & Fowler, J. H. (2014). Corruption drives the emergence of civil society. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with both peer and centralized punishment, the presence of corruption undermines the effectiveness of strong centralized punishment. When centralized punishment is severe and corruption is possible, corruptors exploit the system, leading to lower cooperation and more defectors. Weaker centralized punishment allows peer-punishers to maintain cooperation even in the presence of corruption. Introducing a hybrid strategy (paying both the pool and peer-punishing) can maintain high cooperation across all levels of centralized punishment. The model suggests that a mix of centralized and decentralized enforcement is necessary for robust cooperation, especially in the face of corruption.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency (or cooperation) in public goods games depends critically on the structure of punishment (peer, pool, hybrid), the possibility of corruption, and the severity of centralized punishment. In environments where only centralized punishment is available and corruption is possible, increasing punishment severity can reduce efficiency by enabling corruptors to exploit the system. Allowing peer or hybrid punishment can mitigate this effect and restore cooperation. For prediction, the presence of corruption, the type of punishment available, and the severity of punishment are key moderators. The findings suggest that simply enabling (strong) punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the institutional context and the possibility of corruption must be considered.

  findings: The simulations demonstrate that when punishment is possible and can recruit labor (i.e., induce free riders to cooperate in the future), even modest levels of punishment are sufficient to sustain high levels of cooperation in public goods games. The model relaxes several assumptions of prior models, allowing for probabilistic punishment, continuous cooperation probabilities, individual recognition, and group mixing. The results show that both punishment and cooperation can evolve by individual selection, even in large groups, and that second-order free riders do not prevent the evolution of cooperation. However, the outcomes reported are behavioral (cooperation and punishment rates), not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games can substantially increase cooperation rates, especially when punishment is probabilistic and can recruit labor. The findings suggest that game designs allowing for individual recognition and repeated interactions (mixing ecologies) will see higher cooperation supported by lower punishment rates. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. Its value is in supporting the mechanism by which punishment increases cooperation, which is likely to translate into higher efficiency in similar empirical designs, but this is not directly quantified here.

In spatially structured populations (lattice), strict punishment (high strictness parameter) leads to higher evolved cooperation levels than graduated punishment. In random-matching (well-mixed) populations, graduated punishment (low strictness parameter) is more effective at increasing cooperation. The results are robust across a range of parameters for public goods efficiency and punishment effectiveness. However, the paper does not report direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff; instead, it focuses on the average cooperation level as the main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by extension, efficiency) is moderated by the population structure: strict punishment is more effective in spatially structured settings, while graduated punishment is more effective in well-mixed settings. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the design dimension corresponding to population structure (e.g., fixed groups vs. random rematching) is a key moderator of punishment's effect. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as informative about contribution/cooperation rates, which are closely related but not identical to efficiency.

The model shows that mobility can help punishers survive, but only under certain cost and environmental conditions. The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but higher prevalence of punishers and cooperators is interpreted as beneficial for cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for how punishment, its cost, and the mobility of punishers affect the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the results suggest that punishment can promote cooperation under certain conditions, especially when the cost is not too high and punishers have a moderate migration tendency. For prediction tasks, the findings imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive when punishment is not too costly and when the game environment allows for spatial structure and mobility. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of quantitative predictions.

The strictest exclusion rule (requiring unanimous acceptance for membership) is most effective at promoting cooperation. While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the implication is that group efficiency would be higher when ostracism is enabled, since the share of cooperators is much higher. The findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is costless and takes the form of exclusion, rather than costly point-based punishment. The results are less directly applicable to predicting the quantitative effect of standard costly punishment on efficiency, but they support the qualitative prediction that effective exclusion or ostracism can greatly increase group efficiency if measured.

- source: Stewart, A. J., Parsons, T. L., & Plotkin, J. B. (2016). Evolutionary consequences of behavioral diversity. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in iterated public goods games, increasing the number of available contribution levels (behavioral diversity) can either increase or decrease group efficiency, depending on the ratio of public benefit to individual cost (mpcr). With low mpcr, more choices allow for more robust cooperative strategies and higher mean payoffs. With high mpcr, the abundance of suboptimal robust strategies causes populations to get stuck at lower efficiency. The ability to punish (by reducing one's own contribution in response to defection) is necessary for two-choice strategies to be robust, but explicit costly punishment is not modeled. The paper provides analytic conditions for when simple strategies can resist invasion by more complex ones, and uses evolutionary simulations to show the impact of choice set size on mean payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games as a function of the number of contribution options (all_or_nothing, player_count, mpcr, etc.), but it does not model explicit costly punishment as a separate mechanism. Instead, 'punishment' is operationalized as the ability to reduce one's own contribution in response to others' defection. The findings suggest that increasing the number of contribution options (behavioral diversity) can either help or hurt efficiency, depending on mpcr. For prediction, the paper supports the idea that more behavioral options do not always increase efficiency, and that the effect depends on the underlying game parameters. However, because explicit costly punishment is not present, the results are only adjacent to the core prediction task regarding punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled games.
