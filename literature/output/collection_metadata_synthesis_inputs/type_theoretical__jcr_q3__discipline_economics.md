- source: Dutta, R., Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2021). The whip and the Bible: Punishment versus internalization. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms leads to a large increase in group efficiency and welfare, as measured by group payoff and normalized utility. The model, calibrated to Fehr & Gächter (2000), matches the observed jump in average contributions and welfare when punishment is enabled. The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations. The model further predicts that the relationship between societal value of cooperation and punishment is non-monotonic in the lab, but always positive in the field.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The model is directly calibrated to the Fehr & Gächter (2000) design (4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, specific punishment cost/tech), and matches the observed efficiency increase. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

- source: Hwang, S. H. (2017). Conflict technology in cooperation: The group size paradox revisited. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that coordinated punishment in public goods games can lead to a concentration effect, making it easier to sustain cooperation and achieve high group efficiency as group size increases. When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run. This result is robust to several variations, including the presence of unconditional punishment costs and some decay in coordination as group size increases. The model provides a theoretical explanation for empirical findings that larger groups can sometimes achieve higher cooperation and efficiency, contrary to standard free-rider logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.

The model can be used to generate quantitative or qualitative predictions for the downstream task, especially in settings where the control game is a standard public goods game and the treatment adds a punishment option. The paper also clarifies the conditions under which punishment will or will not increase efficiency, depending on the share of compliers and the effectiveness/cost of punishment.

- source: Carpenter, J., Matthews, P., & Ong'ong'a, O. (2004). Why Punish? Social reciprocity and the enforcement of prosocial norms. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games is best explained by social reciprocity: individuals are motivated to punish norm violators (free riders) even when doing so is costly and does not improve their own material payoff, and even when the violator is outside their immediate group. Experimental and survey evidence shows that punishment is not solely driven by the desire to reduce payoff differentials, and that people continue to punish even when the cost of punishment exceeds the harm inflicted. The demand for punishment is relatively inelastic to price, and motivations are primarily normative. While the models present payoff calculations and show that punishment can stabilize cooperation under certain initial conditions, the empirical focus is on behavioral motivations and patterns rather than direct measures of group efficiency or total earnings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment mechanisms in public goods games can sustain higher levels of cooperation, but it does not directly report on group efficiency or total payoff outcomes. The theoretical models show that, under certain conditions, populations with punishers can achieve higher average payoffs than those dominated by free riders, but these are model-based results rather than empirical efficiency measurements. The experimental and survey data are primarily about who punishes, why, and under what conditions, rather than about the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that punishment can support cooperation even when it is costly, and that motivations are primarily normative, but it does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, ability to punish outgroup) are discussed in the context of behavioral patterns, not efficiency outcomes.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The model finds that punishment (authoritarian leadership) can increase organizational performance relative to no control when workers are low-skilled or when monitoring is efficient, but is always less efficient than motivation (authoritative leadership) when workers are high-skilled and supervisors are charismatic. Multiple equilibria are possible: (1) 'Discipline' (universal cooperation with punishment), (2) 'Cooperation' (universal cooperation with motivation), (3) 'Conflict' (universal defection with punishment), and (4) mixed or cyclical regimes. Punishment can prevent the spread of shirking (the 'bad company' effect) by increasing procedural justice, but also reduces morale and is less efficient. Inefficient equilibria with widespread punishment and/or shirking can persist due to path dependence. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime is stable and analyzes the welfare properties of each.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in a public-goods-like team production setting, conditional on game design dimensions such as monitoring efficiency (punishment tech), team size (player count), and the presence/absence of reward (motivation/charisma). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency relative to no control when monitoring is effective and workers are not highly skilled, but is always less efficient than a well-functioning reward/motivation regime. The model gives explicit parameter boundaries for when punishment is effective, and highlights the importance of initial conditions and path dependence. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to no-punishment in some parameter regimes (especially with low worker skill and high monitoring efficiency), but that the maximum efficiency is achieved with motivation/charisma rather than punishment. The model is highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings where reward/motivation is not available or not effective.

  findings: The paper finds that in large repeated public goods or similar games, the possibility of sustaining cooperation and high efficiency depends critically on the ability to punish defectors specifically.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods and related games. If only rewards or voluntary mechanisms are available, efficiency will be low in the presence of even a small fraction of defectors. The paper's results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is symmetric, the population is large, and there is a possibility of commitment types. For the prediction task, the presence or absence of targeted punishment (punishment_tech), the nature of the incentive (reward_exists, punishment_cost), and the ability to distinguish defectors are critical moderators.
