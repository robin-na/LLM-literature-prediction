- source: Rand, D. G., Dreber, A., Ellingsen, T., Fudenberg, D., & Nowak, M. A. (2009). Positive Interactions Promote Public Cooperation. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, both punishment and reward mechanisms can maintain high levels of cooperation and efficiency compared to a control with no targeted interactions. However, reward leads to higher total payoffs than punishment, as punishment is costly and does not increase efficiency beyond what is achieved by reward. When both reward and punishment are available, groups that use reward more achieve higher payoffs, while groups that use punishment more achieve lower payoffs. The results suggest that positive reciprocity (reward) is more effective than negative reciprocity (punishment) for promoting efficiency in repeated public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with persistent group identity and known group membership, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to a control, but enabling reward is equally or more effective and leads to higher group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment alone should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no-punishment controls, but not as much as reward or combined reward/punishment treatments. The effect of punishment is less positive for efficiency than reward, and when both are available, punishment may even reduce efficiency due to its cost. The findings are most applicable to repeated, fixed-group, non-anonymous settings with continuous contribution choices and no communication.

- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2006). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions. *Science*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with endogenous institution choice, participants could choose between a sanctioning institution (with both positive and negative sanctions) and a sanction-free institution. Initially, most participants preferred the sanction-free institution, but over time, nearly all migrated to the sanctioning institution. In the sanctioning institution, cooperation and efficiency increased steadily, reaching over 95% of the maximum possible group payoff in the final period, while the sanction-free institution's efficiency fell to zero. The presence of punishment (negative sanctions) was the key driver of sustained high cooperation and efficiency. The study demonstrates that sanctioning institutions have a strong competitive advantage and can resolve the social dilemma by attracting and converting even initially noncooperative individuals.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game dramatically increases efficiency compared to a control condition without punishment, even when participants can freely choose between institutions. The design closely matches canonical PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is explicitly measured. The findings support strong positive predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in repeated games with migration or institution choice. The specific punishment cost and effectiveness parameters, as well as the presence of both positive and negative sanctions, are informative for parameterizing prediction models.

  findings: The study finds that when participants can choose between groups with or without costly punishment, and with or without an indirect reciprocity (reputation) mechanism, most eventually prefer the group with both punishment and reputation. The combination (PUN&IR) leads to higher contributions, more efficient use of punishment (less frequent, more targeted), and significantly higher efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) than punishment alone (PUN). The destructive effect of punishment on efficiency is largely mitigated by the presence of reputation, as punishment is used less but more effectively. Full contributors in the combined treatment achieve payoffs close to the social optimum, and even the least cooperative subjects are more cooperative in the combined treatment. The results suggest that punishment and reputation are complementary, not substitutes, and their interaction provides a low-cost, high-efficiency solution to social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, adding a reputation/indirect reciprocity mechanism increases efficiency beyond what is achieved by punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the presence of both punishment and reputation should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, especially when punishment is costly and group members can observe each other's contributions and reputational history. The efficiency boost is due to reduced punishment frequency, more targeted punishment, and increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when both punishment and reputation mechanisms are present.

- source: Sasaki, T., Brännström, Dieckmann, U., & Sigmund, K. (2012). The take-it-or-leave-it option allows small penalties to overcome social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, allowing optional participation (the 'take-it-or-leave-it' option) enables even small penalties to establish a globally stable cooperative norm, maximizing group welfare. In contrast, compulsory participation requires much larger penalties to achieve the same effect, and can leave the group trapped in full defection if initial cooperation is low. Rewarding also promotes cooperation, but requires much higher costs to achieve full cooperation and does not eliminate the possibility of stable coexistence between cooperators and defectors. The efficiency gains from punishment with optional participation are robust across several model variants, including user-pays and capped punishment. The key outcome is that punishment, when combined with voluntary participation, is a highly efficient mechanism for overcoming social dilemmas in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling optional participation (voluntary entry) dramatically increases the efficiency of institutional punishment: much smaller penalties suffice to achieve full cooperation and maximize group welfare. The model is directly relevant to settings where the game design allows players to opt out, and where punishment is administered by an institution rather than peers. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing institutional punishment with optional participation will yield a much larger increase in efficiency than with compulsory participation, and that the cost required to achieve this is much lower than for reward-based incentives. The results are robust to variations in group size, cost structure, and punishment allocation, making them broadly applicable to prediction tasks involving these design dimensions.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2012). The Structure of Mutations and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

- source: Perc, M. (2012). Sustainable institutionalized punishment requires elimination of second-order free-riders. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. If second-order free-riders are not punished sufficiently, the punishment institution cannot be sustained, and efficiency remains low. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive

  findings: The main finding is that in optional public goods games, allowing all forms of punishment (including antisocial punishment) leads to the breakdown of the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group efficiency. Punishment does not increase average payoffs or cooperation rates; instead, it is used by all strategy types to protect against invasion, resulting in a 'tragedy of the commons' where costly punishment is widespread and destructive. Only when punishment is weak (low impact per cost) and the public good is highly efficient (high multiplier) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained. Theoretical results are supported by large-scale simulations and are robust to parameter variation. Preliminary behavioral experiments are consistent with the model's predictions, but the main results are theoretical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the set of available punishment strategies. If only pro-social punishment (cooperators punishing defectors) is allowed, punishment can greatly increase efficiency. However, if antisocial punishment is possible, punishment does not improve efficiency and can even reduce it. The effect is robust across player count, group size, and a wide range of parameters. The efficiency impact of punishment is more positive when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is high and punishment is weak (low punishment impact per cost). For prediction, the presence of antisocial punishment options and the effectiveness of punishment are key moderators.

The model also distinguishes between peer and pool punishment, showing that institutionalized (pool) punishment can maintain efficiency even in the face of second-order free riding, but only if the cost structure is appropriate. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the spatial reach and cost-effectiveness of punishment are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment, and that the absence of sufficient reach can nullify the effect of punishment.
