- source: Barclay, P. (2004). Trustworthiness and competitive altruism can also solve the tragedy of the commons. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment with Han Chinese and Mongolian Chinese students uses a standard public goods game with and without a costly punishment stage. Each group has 4 players, and each subject plays 10 rounds without punishment and 10 rounds with punishment. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4. Punishment costs 1 token to the punisher and reduces the punished's payoff by 3 tokens. The main findings are: (1) Punishment increases mean contribution in all treatments, but the efficiency of punishment (measured as the increase in contribution per unit of punishment) varies by group composition. (2) In all-Han and mixed-ethnicity groups, punishment is efficient (marginal effect > 1), while in all-Mongolian groups, punishment is inefficient (marginal effect < 1). (3) Ethnic diversity (mixed groups) boosts punishment efficiency for both Han and Mongolian Chinese. (4) Mongolian Chinese punish more harshly within their own group due to cultural norms ('Anda' tradition), leading to lower efficiency. (5) Han Chinese show strong ingroup bias, contributing more in all-Han groups, especially with punishment. (6) Cultural fusion (Mongolian Chinese with Han cultural background) increases contribution in mixed groups. (7) Overall, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive in all-Han and mixed groups, but not in all-Mongolian groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in public goods games with different group compositions. For games with 4 players, 10 rounds, MPCR 0.4, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3, punishment increases efficiency in all-Han and mixed-ethnicity groups (marginal effect > 1), but is inefficient in all-Mongolian groups (marginal effect < 1). If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, the expected efficiency with punishment will increase substantially in all-Han and mixed groups, but only modestly or not at all in all-Mongolian groups. The results are robust to a 'clear-cut' Han Chinese sample. The findings are most applicable to lab settings with similar design parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact, no chat, continuous contribution, no rewards, anonymous punishment, and known round count). The paper is highly informative for the prediction task, especially regarding the moderating role of group composition and cultural background.

- source: Gintis, H., Bowles, S., Boyd, R., & Fehr, E. (2003). Explaining altruistic behavior in humans. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Experimental public goods games consistently show that when participants can punish defectors at a personal cost, cooperation rates remain high or even increase over time, and group payoffs (efficiency) are substantially higher than when punishment is not allowed. In the Fehr & Gächter (2000) experiments, for example, allowing punishment led to a 40% increase in net payoffs to all subjects, even after accounting for the costs of punishment. The effect is observed even under strict anonymity and in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, indicating that the mechanism is not driven by expectations of future reciprocity. The paper also reviews cross-cultural evidence and evolutionary models, but the core experimental result is that punishment opportunities robustly improve group efficiency in public goods game environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control conditions without punishment. The effect is robust across standard lab PGG designs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR. The magnitude of the efficiency gain is substantial (e.g., 40% increase in net payoffs in Fehr & Gächter 2000). The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency given the control game design and efficiency, especially in standard PGG settings. The paper also discusses the importance of group structure (e.g., partner vs. stranger matching), but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent across these variations.

- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

- source: Fehr, E., & Gintis, H. (2007). Human motivation and social cooperation: Experimental and analytical foundations. *Annual Review of Sociology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, cooperation and group efficiency decline over time when punishment is not available, as self-regarding individuals free ride and conditional cooperators reduce their contributions in response. When a costly punishment mechanism is introduced, cooperation and group efficiency increase dramatically, approaching full cooperation and maximum group earnings. This effect is robust even when punishment is costly and when group composition changes to prevent repeated interactions. The review also highlights that a minority of strong reciprocators can sustain high efficiency when punishment is possible, but even a majority cannot do so without punishment. The findings are supported by experimental evidence from Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and others.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games substantially increases group efficiency, even when punishment is costly to the punisher and when group composition is randomized. The effect is robust across different group sizes and settings. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency from low or declining levels (in the control) to near-maximal levels (in the treatment), provided that punishment is sufficiently effective and visible. The review also suggests that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are important moderators, but the main qualitative result is that punishment enables high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: MACY, M. W. (1993). BACKWARD-LOOKING SOCIAL-CONTROL. *American Sociological Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The simulations show that the introduction of social control (punishment/sanctions) in a public goods environment can move a group from a low-efficiency, noncooperative equilibrium to a high-efficiency, cooperative equilibrium. Material sanctions (with moderate cost and linear production) are most effective at achieving and sustaining high efficiency, while moral sanctions are vulnerable to start-up problems unless the cost structure is highly favorable. Internalized (self-imposed) fairness norms can lead to even higher efficiency but risk overcooperation (wasteful excess contribution). The model demonstrates that sanctioning systems can be self-limiting: once high cooperation is achieved, the need for enforcement diminishes. The efficiency gains from punishment depend on the sanctioning regime's cost, production function, and the group's learning rate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game can increase group efficiency, especially when the punishment system is not too costly and is effective at inducing compliance. The effect is moderated by the type of sanction (material vs. moral vs. internalized), the cost structure of enforcement, and the learning dynamics of the group. For prediction, the findings suggest that, all else equal, adding a reasonably efficient punishment mechanism to a PGG-like environment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude and sustainability of the effect depend on the details of the punishment technology and cost. The model also highlights the risk of overcooperation with internalized norms and the importance of self-limiting enforcement. The results are most directly applicable to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games with group sizes around 100, moderate jointness of supply, and no communication.

- source: Guala, F. (2012). Reciprocity: Weak or strong? What punishment experiments do (and do not) demonstrate. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that laboratory experiments with public goods games and punishment show that while punishment can increase cooperation rates, it often reduces group efficiency due to the destruction of resources. Efficiency gains from punishment are only observed under specific conditions, such as very long repeated interactions with the same group, and even then, the need for punishment diminishes as cooperation stabilizes. In field and ethnographic studies, there is little evidence for the use of costly, uncoordinated punishment to sustain cooperation; instead, low- or no-cost sanctions (gossip, ostracism, coordinated punishment) are prevalent and effective. The paper argues that the external validity of laboratory punishment experiments is limited, and that real-world institutions that sustain cooperation do so by reducing the cost of punishment, extending the time horizon, and improving information flow.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that enabling costly, uncoordinated punishment in public goods games is unlikely to increase efficiency except under narrow conditions (e.g., long repeated games with stable groups). In most laboratory settings, punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to resource destruction. In real-world or field-like settings, cooperation is more effectively sustained by low-cost, coordinated, or symbolic sanctions, not by costly punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the presence of costly punishment (especially if uncoordinated and not paired with communication or institutional support) should not be expected to reliably increase efficiency over the control condition, and may even reduce it. Game design dimensions that reduce the cost of punishment, allow communication, or enable coordination are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Chen, X. P., Pillutla, M. M., & Yao, X. (2009). Unintended Consequences of Cooperation Inducing and Maintaining Mechanisms in Public Goods Dilemmas: Sanctions and Moral Appeals. *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Sanctions (punishment or reward) in public goods games increase cooperation while present, but when removed, cooperation drops sharply—sometimes to levels below those in groups that never had sanctions. Moral appeals also increase cooperation, but this effect is more persistent after the appeal is removed. The drop in cooperation after removing sanctions is mediated by reduced trust in others' cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings are based on contribution rates, not direct payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment (and reward) increases cooperation in the short term, but this effect is not sustained after removal, and may even backfire. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes for games with punishment enabled versus disabled. The findings are most useful for predicting contribution rates and for understanding the psychological mechanisms (trust) that may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. For efficiency prediction, the results suggest caution: increased cooperation under punishment may not translate to sustained efficiency gains, especially if punishment is later removed.

  findings: The main finding is that, in one-shot public goods games, participants are much more likely to support cooperation through reward (both first- and second-order) than through punishment. First-order punishment of defectors occurs, but second-order punishment of nonpunishers is rare or absent, and punishers are not socially endorsed.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, four-player public goods games with binary contribution and surprise sanctioning opportunities, punishment is not socially supported at the second order, and reward is more likely to be used and socially endorsed. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The behavioral results suggest that, in similar game designs, punishment may not be self-sustaining and may not improve efficiency unless supported by other mechanisms. The findings are most relevant for predicting the likelihood of punishment and reward behaviors, not their direct impact on efficiency.
