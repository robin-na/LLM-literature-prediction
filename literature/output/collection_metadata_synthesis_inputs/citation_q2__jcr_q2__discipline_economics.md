- source: Page, T., Putterman, L., & Garcia, B. (2013). Voluntary contributions with redistribution: The effect of costly sanctions when one person's punishment is another's reward. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the authors compare standard costly punishment (where fines are burned) to a redistributive punishment treatment (where fines are transferred to other group members as rewards). Both treatments use the same marginal per capita return (MPCR = 0.4), punishment cost (0.25 per unit), and group structure. The redistributive punishment treatment leads to significantly higher average contributions (9.03 vs. 7.09), higher average earnings (15.18 vs. 12.88), and higher efficiency, even after controlling for the resource-conserving effect of redistribution (earnings net of transfers: 14.20 vs. 12.88). The effect on efficiency is marginally statistically significant after netting out transfers. Redistributive punishment also reduces the share of punishment targeted at high contributors and increases the monetary return to contributing (from negative to positive). The study provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism can moderate the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no communication, and fixed partners, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency due to the costliness and mis-targeting of punishment. However, if the punishment mechanism is modified so that fines are redistributed as rewards to other group members (rather than burned), both contributions and efficiency increase, and the return to contributing becomes positive. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the structure of the punishment mechanism (specifically, whether punishment is redistributive or not) is a key moderator of the efficiency effect. The control (punishment-off) efficiency is not directly reported, but the paper references prior work showing that standard punishment does not increase efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, while redistributive punishment does. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the nature of the punishment and reward mechanisms.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that subjects are willing to pay to establish a norm enforcement mechanism that punishes or rewards deviations from the group average contribution. With the mechanism, average group contributions to the public good are substantially higher than in the baseline, and average earnings are also higher. The mechanism is almost always implemented, and its strength (enforcement parameter) is positively associated with higher public good contributions. Over time, groups learn to reduce over-investment in the mechanism. The mechanism treatment results in more stable and higher efficiency (measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling an endogenous, average-oriented punishment/reward mechanism in a repeated public good game with three players and ten rounds increases both group efficiency and average earnings compared to a control without such a mechanism. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency improvement is robust. The findings support predicting a more positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is endogenously funded and targets deviations from the group average. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed groups with repeated interaction and endogenous institution formation.

- source: Lee, J., & Min, S. (2021). The effects of repeated induction of emotions on cooperation and punishment. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment used a repeated public goods game with costly punishment and stranger matching. In each round, groups of three played five rounds, with either happiness or anger induced via video clips after the contribution stage. The only difference between treatments was the induced emotion. The main findings are: (1) Contributions decay over rounds in the happiness treatment but remain stable in the anger treatment; (2) Final-round welfare and group earnings are higher in the anger treatment than in the happiness treatment; (3) The key mechanism is that antisocial-punished cooperators reduce their contributions in the happiness treatment but not in the anger treatment; (4) Prosocial and antisocial punishment levels do not differ significantly between treatments, but their trends and effects do. Thus, the emotional context moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency: punishment sustains efficiency only when anger is present, not when happiness is induced.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games depends on the emotional environment. With standard parameters (3 players, 5 rounds, MPCR 0.6, costly punishment with 1:3 cost:impact), punishment sustains or increases efficiency when anger is induced, but not when happiness is induced. In the absence of induced happiness, the efficiency effect of punishment is positive or neutral; with induced happiness, the effect is negative. For prediction, the emotional context (incidental happiness vs. anger) is a critical moderator. The control (no-punishment) baseline is not directly reported, but a baseline without emotion induction (see Appendix J) shows similar trends to the anger treatment, suggesting that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is typical unless happiness is induced. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, consider whether the environment is emotionally neutral/angry (expect positive or stable efficiency effect) or happy (expect negative effect).

- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Angelovski, A., Di Cagno, D., Güth, W., Marazzi, F., & Panaccione, L. (2018). Does heterogeneity spoil the basket? The role of productivity and feedback information on public good provision. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 4-player, 10-round public goods game with centralized punishment, the presence of a sanctioner (punishment enabled) increases both average contributions and total group payoffs (social welfare) compared to a control with no punishment. However, if the sanctioner's payoff is made contingent on group cooperation (pool punishment), both contributions and efficiency are lower than when the sanctioner receives a fixed endowment. The negative effect of the pool punishment scheme is not due to differences in punishment frequency or severity, but rather to contributors' aversion to the sanctioner's extrinsic incentives (perceived as indirect free-riding). The paper provides direct evidence that punishment increases efficiency, but the institutional details of the punishment mechanism (especially the sanctioner's payoff scheme) are crucial moderators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the institutional design—specifically, whether the sanctioner is paid a fixed or variable (pool) payoff. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger when the punishment institution uses fixed payoffs for sanctioners. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the sanctioner's payoff scheme, and the marginal per-capita return (MPCR). Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the treatment effect of punishment is moderated by the sanctioner's incentive structure.

- source: Mantilla, C., Zhou, L., Wang, C., Yang, D. H., Shen, S. P., & Seabright, P. (2021). Favoring your in-group can harm both them and you: Ethnicity and public goods provision in China. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in public goods games with punishment, ethnically mixed groups experience lower contributions and lower group earnings than homogeneous groups. This is primarily because participants are less likely to punish co-ethnic free-riders when out-group members are present, reducing the deterrent effect of punishment. The effect is strongest in unbalanced mixed groups (3+1 composition), where efficiency losses are largest. The study provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing institution is undermined by in-group favoritism in diverse groups, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or even reversed in ethnically mixed groups due to in-group favoritism in punishment assignment. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the group composition (homogeneous vs mixed) is a critical moderator: punishment is less effective (or even harmful) in mixed groups because in-group members are less likely to punish each other, reducing deterrence and lowering contributions and earnings. The game uses standard PGG parameters (4 players, 5 rounds, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=2, punishment impact=5, no chat, no rewards, anonymous punishment). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between group types with punishment enabled. The findings suggest that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment in mixed-ethnicity groups may not increase efficiency and can even decrease it relative to homogeneous groups.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., & Meraglia, S. (2019). Endogenous sanctioning institutions and migration patterns: Experimental evidence. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with two groups and endogenous migration, the presence of formal sanctioning institutions (a fixed fee plus a fine on private account allocations) leads to higher contributions and significantly higher average payoffs compared to sanction-free groups. When only one group has sanctions (No-Voting treatment), that group becomes overpopulated, and payoff inequality emerges. When groups can vote on whether to implement sanctions (Voting treatment), most groups eventually choose sanctions, group sizes become more balanced, migration decreases, and overall efficiency and average payoffs increase further. The ability to endogenously choose institutions (via voting) leads to more efficient and equitable outcomes than exogenously imposed sanctions. The effect of sanctions on efficiency is robust and positive, and the endogenous choice of sanctions amplifies this effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling formal punishment in a repeated PGG with migration increases efficiency and average payoffs, relative to a control without punishment. The effect is even stronger when the sanctioning institution is chosen endogenously by group vote. The design dimensions—such as player count (10, split into two groups of 5), number of rounds (30), absence of chat, continuous contributions, and a formal, automatic punishment mechanism—are all specified. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) varies with group size but is always less than 1, and the punishment is highly effective (80% fine on private account, plus a fixed fee). There is no reward mechanism. Players are shown summary information about both groups' contributions and rule-sets. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, and allowing endogenous institutional choice (voting) will further boost efficiency and reduce inequality. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is substantially lower than with punishment enabled. This paper is highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Godoy, S., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2016). An individualistic approach to institution formation in public good games. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with four players and 20 rounds, the study compares four treatments: (1) standard VCM (no punishment), (2) exogenous universal punishment (StdPun), (3) endogenous punishment with zero cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP0), and (4) endogenous punishment with a small or large cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5). When the right to punish is costless (CTP0), participation is high enough that group contributions and efficiency are as high as in the exogenous punishment treatment (StdPun), and both are significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline (VCM). When there is any cost to acquiring the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5), participation drops, antisocial punishment increases, and group contributions and efficiency fall to levels not significantly different from the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the size of the cost: even a negligible cost eliminates the efficiency gains from punishment. The number of persistent punishers is a key moderator: at least two are needed for punishment to be effective. The study provides direct evidence that the institutional details of how punishment rights are provided (costless vs. costly, endogenous vs. exogenous) are critical for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend critically on whether the right to punish is costless and universally available, or must be acquired at a cost. When punishment is exogenously provided or costlessly available to all, efficiency increases substantially relative to the no-punishment baseline. When even a small cost is required to acquire the right to punish, participation drops, antisocial punishment rises, and efficiency gains disappear. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence and cost of acquiring punishment rights are key moderators: if the game design includes a cost to acquire punishment rights, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. If punishment is costless and universally available, expect large efficiency gains. The number of persistent punishers is also important: at least two are needed for effectiveness in this four-player design.

Control (VCM) and treatment (PUN) efficiency values are reported for each network, allowing for direct calibration.

- source: Waichman, I. (2020). LINKING WEALTH AND PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVENESS: PUNISHMENT AND COOPERATION UNDER CONGRUENT HETEROGENEITIES. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This lab experiment uses a linear public good game with peer punishment to study the effects of heterogeneity in endowment and punishment effectiveness, both separately and jointly. The main findings are: (1) Heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness alone does not affect efficiency or cooperation compared to symmetric groups. (2) Endowment heterogeneity (rich and poor members) reduces efficiency and cooperation, as rich members contribute a lower share of their endowment. (3) When both endowment and punishment effectiveness are heterogeneous (either the rich or the poor is the most effective punisher), efficiency and cooperation are not reduced compared to symmetry, and are higher than under endowment heterogeneity alone. (4) Among groups that achieve high cooperation, efficiency is higher in the 'Rich strong' treatment (richest is most effective punisher) than in the 'Poor strong' treatment, because less punishment is needed to sustain cooperation. (5) Overall, congruent heterogeneities (richest is most effective punisher) do not hinder and may even facilitate efficiency and cooperation compared to endowment heterogeneity alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public good games with peer punishment, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency compared to control conditions with endowment heterogeneity alone. However, the efficiency gain from punishment is moderated by the structure of heterogeneity: punishment is most effective (in terms of efficiency) when either all players are symmetric or when the richest player is also the most effective punisher. Endowment heterogeneity without matching punishment effectiveness reduces efficiency, but joint heterogeneities (especially congruent ones) restore efficiency to levels comparable to symmetry. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the alignment of endowment and punishment effectiveness. The control efficiency (without punishment) is lowest under endowment heterogeneity, and the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is largest in this case, especially if punishment effectiveness is also heterogeneous and congruent with endowment.

  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: De Geest, L. R., & Kingsley, D. C. (2021). Norm enforcement with incomplete information *. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with two high-endowment and two low-endowment players per group, and costly peer punishment, the ability to observe others' endowments (complete information) leads to the emergence and enforcement of efficient 'contribute-your-endowment' norms. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the punishment technology and payoff structure, which can be used to calibrate predictive models.

  findings: In a lab experiment with heterogeneous groups (different returns from the public good), the authors compare three treatments: (1) communication and punishment available together (ComEarly), (2) punishment first, then communication added later (ComLate), and (3) communication only (ComOnly). The timing of communication is a key moderator: early communication (before punishment) is much more effective at raising efficiency than late communication. For prediction, this suggests that the presence of both communication and punishment, especially with early communication, should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, but not full efficiency.
