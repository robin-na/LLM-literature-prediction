- source: Gürerk, Lauer, T., & Scheuermann, M. (2018). Leadership with individual rewards and punishments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the addition of individual punishment or reward mechanisms (either centralized via a leader or decentralized among peers) leads to significantly higher contributions and higher net payoffs compared to the baseline game without such mechanisms. The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment is more effective than reward at sustaining high contributions over time, especially in the peer punishment treatment, where contributions even increase in the second half of the game. Net payoffs are highest in reward treatments (due to the reward mechanism's structure), but also higher in punishment treatments than in the baseline. The presence of a leader (who contributes first and can reward or punish) does not further increase contributions or payoffs compared to peer-based reward or punishment. The effect of punishment on increasing contributions and payoffs is robust, and punishment from leaders has a stronger effect on subsequent contributions than peer punishment. Anti-social punishment is less frequent when punishment is centralized with a leader.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, enabling individual punishment (with a cost/impact ratio of 1:3) increases both contributions and net payoffs (i.e., efficiency) compared to the baseline without punishment. The effect is robust whether punishment is centralized (leader) or decentralized (peers), and is not further enhanced by adding a leader who leads by example. The control (no punishment) efficiency is lower than in the punishment-enabled treatments. The findings support the prediction that, holding game design dimensions constant, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency. The specific cost and impact structure (1 point cost reduces target by 3 points) is important for generalization. Reward mechanisms also increase efficiency, but the structure of the reward mechanism in this experiment is payoff-increasing by design. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar public goods game environments.

- source: Engl, F., Riedl, A., & Weber, R. (2021). Spillover Effects of Institutions on Cooperative Behavior, Preferences, and Beliefs. *American Economic Journal-microeconomics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Institutions that enforce cooperation in a public goods game (via a minimum contribution requirement, with full forfeiture for under-contribution) substantially increase group welfare in the regulated game and also increase welfare in an unregulated, otherwise identical game played by the same group. The direct effect on welfare is large, and the spillover effect is significant and economically meaningful (about 3-3.4 points on a 20-point endowment, or 30-40% of the direct effect). Both exogenous and endogenous institutions produce these effects, but the endogenous institution's spillover effect grows over time as the minimum requirement increases. The effect is mainly due to higher contributions among those already contributing, not by converting free riders. The positive effects on welfare and contributions persist after the institution is removed and extend to new partners. The mechanisms include increased beliefs about others' cooperativeness and stronger conditional cooperation preferences. No spillover is found to trust games. Heterogeneity exists: younger subjects and those with high positive reciprocity show smaller spillover effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a strong, central punishment institution (with full forfeiture for under-contribution) in a repeated linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and MPCR 0.5. The control (no institution) game shows declining contributions and welfare over time, while the treatment (with punishment) achieves near-full cooperation and much higher welfare. Critically, the paper quantifies both the direct and spillover effects on group welfare, allowing for prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design. The results are robust across exogenous and endogenous institution types, though the time dynamics differ. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with similar group size, round structure, and punishment technology (centralized, all-or-nothing, no cost to punisher). The absence of spillover to trust games suggests limits to generalizability across domains. The evidence is strongest for games with continuous contribution choices, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Bruhin, A., Janizzi, K., & Thöni, C. (2020). Uncovering the heterogeneity behind cross-cultural variation in antisocial punishment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper shows that the effectiveness of peer punishment in public goods games is highly heterogeneous and depends critically on the composition of punishment types within groups. Using a finite mixture model, the authors classify subjects into three types: non-punishers (Type N), antisocial/free rider punishers (Type AF), and free rider-only punishers (Type F). The presence of Type AF subjects, who punish both low and high contributors, is strongly associated with lower group earnings and reduced efficiency. In contrast, groups with only Type F and Type N subjects achieve much higher efficiency. The number of Type AF subjects in a group explains a large share of the variance in group earnings, more than baseline cooperativeness or period effects. Cross-cultural differences in the prevalence of Type AF subjects explain much of the observed variation in the effectiveness of punishment across societies.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform: it depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishers (Type AF) in the group or population. In environments where antisocial punishment is common, enabling punishment can substantially reduce efficiency. In environments where only free rider punishment occurs, enabling punishment can increase or sustain efficiency. The paper provides quantitative evidence (e.g., groups with four Type AF subjects earn 68.7% less than groups with only Type F/N) and regression models showing that the number of Type AF subjects is a strong predictor of group earnings. Thus, for prediction, the cultural or group composition regarding punishment types is a critical moderator, and control game efficiency alone is insufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering this heterogeneity.

- source: Marcin, I., Robalo, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Institutional endogeneity and third-party punishment in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public good game with binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, groups could vote to introduce a third-party punishment institution. When punishment was enabled, a B-type player could assign costly deduction points to defectors (non-contributors), with each deduction point costing the punisher 1 point and reducing the defector's income by 3 points. The main experimental manipulation was whether the punishment institution was implemented endogenously (by majority vote) or exogenously (by random assignment, sometimes overruling the vote). The study finds that: (1) Punishment is significantly milder when the institution is endogenously chosen, but is more effective in inducing cooperation per unit of punishment; (2) Exogenous punishment is harsher, leading to similar or slightly higher cooperation in the long run, but at higher punishment cost; (3) Overall, group efficiency (total payoffs) is not significantly different between endogenous and exogenous punishment conditions; (4) The presence of punishment (vs. no punishment) increases cooperation and efficiency, but the method of institution selection does not further increase efficiency. The study provides direct evidence on efficiency, group payoff, and the moderating role of institutional endogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a public good game increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the way the punishment institution is implemented (endogenous vs. exogenous) does not significantly affect overall efficiency, even though it affects punishment severity and effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is a key driver of efficiency gains, but whether the institution is chosen by vote or imposed does not further increase group efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, binary contribution, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for mapping design to efficiency outcomes. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is lower than with punishment enabled, and the efficiency gain from punishment is robust to the method of institution selection.

- source: Albrecht, F., Kube, S., & Traxler, C. (2018). Cooperation and norm enforcement - The individual-level perspective. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds substantial heterogeneity in both cooperation and punishment patterns. About half of subjects are pro-social punishers, and about 40% are non-punishers. There is only a moderate correlation between cooperation and punishment types: many conditional cooperators do not punish, and some free-riders do punish pro-socially. In repeated public goods games with punishment, groups with more pro-social punishers achieve higher and more stable contribution levels, even after controlling for the number of conditional cooperators. Regression analyses show that the number of pro-social punishers in a group is a stronger predictor of group contribution levels than the number of conditional cooperators. However, the paper does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public goods games with punishment, the presence of pro-social punishers in a group increases contribution levels. This suggests that enabling punishment can increase cooperation, but the paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoffs. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is informative about behavioral mechanisms (contributions and punishment) but not about the net payoff effects of punishment. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the experimental setup matches the canonical PGG with punishment, so the findings are highly relevant for understanding how group composition and punishment opportunities affect cooperation rates, but not directly efficiency.

- source: Engel, C., Kube, S., & Kurschilgen, M. (2021). Managing expectations: How selective information affects cooperation and punishment in social dilemma games. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Selective pre-play information about others' cooperativeness can strongly affect initial beliefs, contribution behavior, and the effectiveness of punishment in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment. Unfavorable information (showing low cooperation in other groups) leads to lower initial and sustained contributions, and reduces the ability of punishment to increase cooperation. Favorable information has little effect in already cooperative environments (ceiling effect), but in less cooperative environments it can prevent further decline in cooperation and enhance the positive effect of pro-social punishment. The effect of information does not depend on whether it is public or private, but is strongest when the information is surprising to participants. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the strong effects on contribution rates imply likely effects on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing cooperation (and thus likely efficiency) is moderated by participants' expectations, which can be manipulated by selective pre-play information. In environments where baseline cooperation is high, punishment is effective unless unfavorable information is provided, which undermines both cooperation and the effectiveness of punishment. In less cooperative environments, favorable information can help maintain cooperation and enhance the effect of pro-social punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the baseline expectations and informational context (not just structural game parameters) are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, predictions must be inferred from contribution data.

- source: Gallier, C. (2020). Democracy and compliance in public goods games. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that a non-deterrent contribution rule in a public goods game increases contributions, regardless of whether it is imposed exogenously or chosen democratically. The aggregate increase in contributions is primarily due to the presence of the rule itself, not the democratic process. Democratic participation (voting) does not have a statistically significant effect on contributions at the aggregate level after accounting for the direct effect of the rule, self-selection, and information effects. At the individual level, participants who are overruled in the group vote tend to reduce their contributions, but this negative effect is counteracted by a small democracy premium if the rule is democratically chosen. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with a mild, non-deterrent automatic sanction, the introduction of the rule (punishment) increases contributions, but the effect is not due to democratic participation per se. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of introducing a weak punishment mechanism on contribution rates, but not directly on efficiency or group payoff, as these are not reported. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contribution, etc.) are clearly specified and can inform parameterization of prediction models. However, since efficiency is not reported, the paper's quantitative results cannot be directly used for efficiency prediction, but the qualitative pattern (punishment increases cooperation even when non-deterrent) is robust.

- source: Dannenberg, A., Haita-Falah, C., & Zitzelsberger, S. (2020). Voting on the threat of exclusion in a public goods experiment. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with groups of 5, the option to exclude (ostracize) members is introduced either costlessly (B10) or with a 20% endowment cost to all (B8). Groups can vote to implement the exclusion institution (endogenous) or have it imposed (exogenous). The exclusion institution leads to significantly higher contributions in all treatments. When exclusion is costless (B10), average payoffs (efficiency) are higher with exclusion than without. When exclusion is costly (B8), average payoffs are lower with exclusion than without, despite higher contributions. The exclusion mechanism is used almost exclusively to remove the lowest contributors, and the threat of exclusion increases cooperation among those who remain. The effect of exclusion on efficiency is positive when costless, but negative or neutral when costly. Endogenous and exogenous implementation yield similar results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment (here, exclusion/ostracism) on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the costliness of the punishment institution. When exclusion is costless, enabling punishment increases both contributions and efficiency. When exclusion is costly (reducing endowments), enabling punishment increases contributions but does not improve efficiency and may reduce it. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), group size, and the presence/absence of communication are all specified, allowing for direct mapping to prediction tasks. The results are robust to endogenous vs exogenous institution choice. For prediction, the key moderator is whether the punishment institution imposes a cost on all group members; if so, efficiency gains are not realized even if cooperation rises.

Delegation leads to higher average contributions and efficiency than the Baseline, but both fall short of the social optimum. The design dimensions (player count, group size, rounds, MPCR, information, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is useful as a control or baseline for understanding efficiency in the absence of punishment.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2020). SUSTAINING COOPERATION: COMMUNITY ENFORCEMENT VERSUS SPECIALIZED ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of the European Economic Association*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the optimal way to sustain cooperation in repeated public-goods-like games depends on the effectiveness of the punishment technology. If specialized enforcers can punish at low cost and high impact, then pure enforcer punishment (with immediate return to cooperation) maximizes efficiency. If specialized enforcement is less effective, then combining enforcer punishment with community enforcement (e.g., temporary withdrawal of cooperation or ostracism) is optimal. The paper provides explicit characterizations of the maximum sustainable cooperation (efficiency) in each regime, and shows that the presence of effective specialized enforcement can increase group welfare compared to pure community enforcement. However, if the punishment technology is very weak, community enforcement alone is nearly optimal. The results generalize to both small and large groups, and to settings with imperfect monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of introducing punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on the effectiveness of the punishment technology and other game design parameters. It shows that, when punishment is highly effective (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the harm inflicted), enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency by supporting higher cooperation. However, if punishment is costly or ineffective, the efficiency gain is limited, and community enforcement may be nearly as good. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency as a function of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and discount factor. The results also highlight that the structure of punishment (one-time vs. repeated, enforcer vs. peer) and the possibility of ostracism or repentance are important moderators.

- source: Chávez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., & Stranlund, J. K. (2018). Managing and defending the commons: Experimental evidence from TURFs in Chile. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common-pool resource game with insiders (who can communicate) and outsiders (who cannot), the introduction of poaching without monitoring or sanctions dramatically reduces insiders' earnings and resource sustainability, nearly eliminating the efficiency gains from cooperation. Adding weak external enforcement (low-probability monitoring and fines) or allowing insiders to invest in monitoring (with the same fine) both significantly reduce poaching and increase insiders' earnings and harvests, but do not fully restore efficiency to baseline (no-poaching) levels. Combining external and local monitoring does not further reduce poaching or increase efficiency. The main constraint is insiders' inability to coordinate sufficient monitoring investment. The results are robust across field (TURF members) and student samples.

The effect is similar whether monitoring is provided externally or by insiders, and combining both does not yield additional efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment (even if weak) should be expected to increase efficiency above the poaching-only baseline, but not to the level of the no-poaching or full-cooperation baseline. The magnitude of the effect is limited by the monitoring probability and the coordination of monitoring investment.

- source: Deffains, B., Espinosa, R., & Fluet, C. (2019). Laws and norms: Experimental evidence with liability rules. *International Review of Law and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies a 'Liability Game' that is structurally similar to a public goods game but with a binary choice (harmful vs harmless action) and state-dependent payoffs, so that sometimes the harmful action is efficient and sometimes not. The main treatments compare no law (no liability), strict liability, and negligence rule, each with perfect (Severe) or imperfect (Mild, 50% probability) enforcement. The key findings are: (1) Both strict liability and negligence rules increase efficiency relative to no law, even when enforcement is imperfect and sanctions are not fully deterrent. (2) Strict liability is more effective than the negligence rule at deterring welfare-decreasing actions under imperfect enforcement, but also deters some welfare-increasing actions (over-deterrence). (3) The presence of liability rules increases the weight individuals place on group welfare in their decision-making, beyond the direct material incentives. (4) Social disapproval (punishment) is mainly targeted at those who cause harm, regardless of whether the action was efficient or inefficient. (5) Informal sanctions (disapproval points) further reduce harmful actions, but the main efficiency gains come from the liability rules themselves.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment-like liability rule in a public-goods-like environment increases efficiency, even when enforcement is imperfect and the expected sanction is not fully deterrent. The effect is robust across two types of liability rules (strict and negligence), but strict liability is more effective at deterring welfare-decreasing actions (at the cost of some over-deterrence). The game design is a close variant of a PGG: 4 players, 10 rounds per treatment, binary choice, no chat, and state-dependent payoffs.
