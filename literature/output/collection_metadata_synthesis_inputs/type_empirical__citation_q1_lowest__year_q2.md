- source: Hugh-Jones, D., & Perroni, C. (2017). The logic of costly punishment reversed: Expropriation of free-riders and outsiders. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the threat of group expropriation (a form of punishment) can increase public goods contributions, but only when the expropriation mechanism is sufficiently difficult to coordinate (i.e., requires a large group to act together). When expropriation is too easy (requires only one or two people), it is less targeted at free-riders, more arbitrary, and leads to lower contributions and more frequent expropriation, reducing efficiency. In the baseline (punishment enabled, M=3), contributions are significantly higher than in the control (no expropriation), and expropriation is mostly targeted at unique lowest contributors. When the minimum group size for expropriation is reduced (M=2 or M=1), expropriation increases and contributions fall. In heterogeneous groups, minorities are more likely to be targeted in some treatments, especially when ambiguity or a history of targeting exists, and may respond less to punishment. However, in the main treatments, overall group efficiency is not significantly different between homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (especially the ease of coordination required for punishment) is a critical moderator of its effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the punishment technology, specifically the minimum group size required for successful punishment (expropriation). When punishment is difficult to coordinate (higher M), it is more likely to be used as a credible threat against free-riders, increasing contributions and efficiency. When punishment is too easy (low M), it is used more arbitrarily, contributions fall, and efficiency is reduced. For prediction, the dimension 'punishment_tech' (minimum group size for punishment, or ease of expropriation) is a key moderator: higher coordination requirements for punishment (higher M) are associated with more positive effects of punishment on efficiency, while lower requirements (lower M) can make punishment detrimental. Control efficiency (without punishment) is much lower than with well-targeted, hard-to-coordinate punishment. Heterogeneity (minority status) can moderate targeting and behavioral response, but does not robustly reduce efficiency unless ambiguity or a history of targeting is present. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially punishment_tech, and provide indirect evidence for the importance of targeting and coordination in punishment mechanisms.

- source: Otto, P. E., & Bolle, F. (2016). The advantage of hierarchy: Inducing responsibility and selecting ability?. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment compares three regimes: standard PGG (voluntary contributions), AG (randomly assigned allocator determines all contributions), and PAG (principal selects allocator each round). Both AG and PAG significantly increase group production and average incomes relative to PGG. PAG (principal selection) yields the highest efficiency, with further increases in satisfaction and fairness ratings. The effect is not due to learning or order effects. The mechanism works by centralizing responsibility and leveraging selection, not by explicit punishment. In treatments with heterogeneous productivity, the most productive worker is more likely to be appointed allocator, but does not exploit the position to reduce own effort. The findings show that hierarchy can substitute for punishment in sustaining high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing hierarchical control (an allocator or principal-allocator structure) in a public goods game can substantially increase efficiency, even in the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a central authority who can determine or influence contributions should be expected to yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those typically attributed to punishment. The results are robust across group size (n=3), number of rounds (10 per regime), and both homogeneous and heterogeneous productivity. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is not available but hierarchy is, efficiency can be high. For prediction, if a game design includes a mechanism where one player can set others' contributions (with or without selection by a principal), expect efficiency to be much higher than in standard PGG, even if punishment is absent.

- source: Vermeer, A. B. L., Heerema, R. L., & Sanfey, A. G. (2016). Third-Party Cooperation: How Reducing Material Involvement Enhances Contributions to the Public Good. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that reducing personal material involvement in a public goods game increases cooperation rates: participants contribute more when making decisions for a third party than for themselves. This effect is further enhanced when the group consists of human players rather than computer players, suggesting that social motives drive third-party cooperation. The main outcome is the proportion of tokens contributed, not efficiency or group payoff. No punishment or sanctioning is present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. Its findings are relevant for understanding how personal involvement and social context affect cooperation rates, but not for the downstream prediction task focused on punishment effects. The design and results may be contextually informative for understanding baseline cooperation rates in the absence of punishment, but do not inform the efficiency impact of enabling punishment.

- source: Zhosan, D., & Gardner, R. (2013). Problems of the commons: group behavior, cooperation and sanctioning in a two-harbor experiment. *Environment and Development Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a laboratory experiment simulating a two-commons (two-harbor) resource dilemma, groups of 8 participants (students and professional fishermen) repeatedly chose how to allocate effort between two commons and a safe alternative. The experiment varied institutional features across blocks: baseline (no restrictions), enforcement (costly movement between commons), communication, and communication plus sanctioning. Efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) was the primary outcome. Communication led to large, significant increases in efficiency for all groups, with average net efficiency rising from ~0.8 to ~0.98. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings, then monetary penalties for repeated defection) further increased efficiency in most groups, sometimes reaching 1.0. The effect of sanctioning was generally positive but smaller than the effect of communication, and in one group, sanctioning led to a breakdown in cooperation. The results show that communication and sanctioning can both be effective in increasing efficiency in commons dilemmas, with communication having the largest and most consistent effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling communication among group members in a commons dilemma can dramatically increase efficiency, even before any formal or monetary sanctioning is introduced. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings and monetary penalties) yields further, though more modest, efficiency gains. For prediction tasks, the presence of communication and/or sanctioning should be expected to increase efficiency substantially relative to a control with neither. The effect of communication is robust and large; the effect of sanctioning is positive but may be context-dependent and sometimes less pronounced. The experiment's design and reporting allow for direct mapping to game design dimensions relevant for prediction.

- source: Hsu, L. C. (2013). TAX AUDITING AS A PUBLIC GOOD GAME: AN EXPERIMENTAL STUDY ON PUNISHMENT AND COMPLIANCE. *Pacific Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The endogenous-audit (collective punishment) treatment produces much higher compliance rates than both the no-audit and random-audit treatments. In the final 10 rounds, compliance rates are 0.297 (no audit), 0.758 (random audit), and 0.921 (endogenous audit). However, average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment (85.2 points) than in the random-audit treatment (92.1 points), due to the distribution of fines and discovered taxes. Audit productivity (e.g., reported tax per audit) is higher in the endogenous-audit treatment in the latter half of the game. The main effect of punishment is to increase compliance, but the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous due to the cost of monitoring and the way fines are pooled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a collective punishment mechanism (endogenous audit) in a public-goods-like tax game increases compliance rates compared to both no-punishment and exogenous punishment (random audit) conditions. However, the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous, as average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment than in the random-audit treatment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment can increase compliance but may not always increase efficiency, depending on the cost structure and how punishment/fines are redistributed. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit cost structure, etc.) are well specified and can be mapped to the prediction task, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Gaudeul, A., Crosetto, P., & Riener, G. (2017). Better stuck together or free to go? Of the stability of cooperation when individuals have outside options. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment varied the ease of exit from a public project (barriers to exit) in a repeated, two-player, stochastic public-good-like game. When exit was easier (lower barriers), participants exited more frequently, but this did not increase efficiency or average payoffs. In fact, average individual welfare was lower in treatments with lower barriers to exit. The main reason was excessive exit, driven by pessimistic beliefs about the peer's likelihood of leaving, rather than accurate cost-benefit analysis. The study finds that exit is used both as a response to perceived exploitation and as a punishment for failure, but these mechanisms do not improve group efficiency. The findings suggest that making exit easier undermines group stability and reduces welfare, contrary to some theoretical expectations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-good-like environments with imperfect monitoring and stochastic outcomes, making exit easier (i.e., lowering barriers to exit) reduces efficiency and average payoffs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of exit options (a form of sanction or punishment via exclusion/self-exclusion) on efficiency. The results suggest that, unless exit is tightly controlled or costly, its availability will likely decrease group welfare due to excessive and inefficient use. For prediction tasks, the presence and ease of exit should be treated as a negative moderator of efficiency, even when the control (no-exit) game already has moderate efficiency. The study does not test classic costly punishment (e.g., deduction points), but its findings on exit as a sanction are highly informative for settings where exit or exclusion is a possible response to non-cooperation.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C. (2014). Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In reputation-based helping games (without punishment), cognitive disturbance (superfluous words) reduces cooperation and lowers group payoffs, indicating that efficiency is sensitive to cognitive constraints. In reputation-based punishment games, disturbance increases antisocial punishment and reduces punishment of defectors, but does not significantly change overall cooperation or mean final payoffs. The link between reputation and payoff is weakened under disturbance. The study highlights that experimental outcomes in reputation and punishment games are highly sensitive to environmental and cognitive factors, and that the expected efficiency gains from punishment or reputation mechanisms may not materialize under cognitive load.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effects of punishment and reputation mechanisms in indirect reciprocity games are sensitive to cognitive context. For prediction tasks, it suggests that efficiency gains from punishment (or reputation) may be undermined or nullified if cognitive load is high, even when game design parameters are otherwise favorable. The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Liu, J., & Riyanto, Y. E. (2017). Information transparency and equilibrium selection in coordination games: an experimental study. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Revealing information about the existence and structure of centralized punishment or reward mechanisms in a stag hunt game increases the rate of mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant equilibrium) compared to a control where only final payoffs are shown. The effect is stronger for punishment than for reward. The mechanism works both by increasing beliefs in others' cooperation and, for punishment, by directly increasing cooperative choices. In contrast, when the underlying game remains a prisoner's dilemma (i.e., the punishment/reward is too small to change the equilibrium), revealing the mechanism does not increase cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in mutual cooperation implies higher group welfare in the context of the stag hunt game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that, in two-player, repeated stag hunt games with centralized punishment or reward, making the institutional mechanism transparent increases cooperation rates, especially when punishment is highlighted. While efficiency is not directly measured, the increase in mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant outcome) suggests that efficiency would also increase. The results are most relevant for coordination games adjacent to public goods games, rather than standard PGGs. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment and making its mechanism salient is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such information, especially when the punishment is strong enough to change the equilibrium structure.

  findings: The experiments show that rejection of unfair offers (costly punishment) in the Mini Ultimatum Game is primarily driven by fairness considerations, particularly the perceived intentions of the proposer. When intentions are not assessable, outcome fairness still motivates some punishment, but to a lesser degree. The opportunity for reputation building and future interaction (i.e., repeated, non-anonymous play) does not significantly increase costly punishment when fairness considerations are strong, but can increase punishment when intentions are ambiguous. However, because the study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment occurs, not its aggregate payoff consequences.

- source: Myers, C. D. (2016). Participation and punishment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that when punishment is possible, participation (turnout) increases dramatically (from 60% to 88%). Punishment is overwhelmingly directed at abstainers, and abstainers incur a net loss from being punished. Egoist subjects are more responsive to punishment than pro-social subjects, leading to a more egoist composition among participants when punishment is available. However, because participation is costly and does not increase the value of the prize, increased participation does not improve group efficiency or welfare. The study does not report treatment effects on efficiency or group payoff, and the design makes clear that increased participation is not efficiency-enhancing.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment increases participation in a binary-choice, team-based contest game where participation is individually costly and does not increase group welfare. However, it does not provide evidence that punishment increases efficiency or group payoff; in fact, the design ensures that increased participation is efficiency-reducing. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment depends critically on the structure of the game: in games where participation is not efficiency-enhancing, punishment may increase costly participation without improving (and possibly reducing) efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding behavioral responses (participation/cooperation) to punishment, not for predicting efficiency outcomes in standard public goods games.

- source: Koenig, B. L., & Riley, C. M. (2017). To what reference point do people calibrate cost-free, third-party punishment?. *Personality and Individual Differences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: Across three experiments, participants assigned preferred punishments (fines) to a perpetrator in a theft scenario, with victim loss and perpetrator gain manipulated independently. Both victim loss and perpetrator gain influenced punishment preferences, with participants tending to select the larger of the two as a reference point. On average, preferred punishments exceeded the sum of victim loss and perpetrator gain. A substantial minority of participants preferred punishments much larger than this outcome differential, while a small minority preferred no punishment. When asked about compensation for the victim (rather than punishment for the perpetrator), participants' preferred amounts were lower and rarely exceeded the outcome differential. The study demonstrates that third-party punishment preferences are not simply proportional to harm or gain, but often exceed both, and that there is considerable heterogeneity in punitive preferences.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. However, it is informative about the psychological calibration of punishment preferences in third-party contexts, showing that people often prefer punishments that more than offset both victim loss and perpetrator gain. This suggests that, in environments where third-party punishment is possible and costless, punishment levels may be set high, potentially deterring transgressions more strongly than would be predicted by models based solely on harm or gain. The findings are most relevant for understanding the psychological underpinnings of punishment, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency in PGG-like games.

- source: D'Exelle, B., & Riedl, A. (2013). Resource Allocations and Disapproval Voting in Unequal Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Disapproval voting as a punishment mechanism in unequal groups does not consistently improve efficiency or prosocial allocations. Instead, its effectiveness is highly sensitive to institutional details. When villagers have only private information about their share and voting is public, disapproval rates are low and representatives often use a minimal coalition (exclude-one) strategy, which avoids disapproval and maintains higher group earnings, though at the cost of excluding the poorest villager. When allocations are common knowledge or voting is secret, disapproval rates are higher, leading representatives to frequently switch to the exclude-all strategy, which is inefficient as it maximizes the representative's payoff at the expense of the group. The punishment mechanism can thus backfire, reducing efficiency, especially when disapproval is frequent. The study highlights the importance of information structure and voting rules in moderating the effects of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment mechanisms (here, disapproval voting) on efficiency in resource allocation games is not universally positive and depends critically on the design dimensions, especially information disclosure and the publicity of voting. In settings where punishment is possible but disapproval is rare (private information, public voting), efficiency is higher, but this is achieved by excluding the least powerful group member. In settings where punishment is more likely (common information, secret voting), efficiency is lower due to frequent costly disapproval and representatives' defensive strategies. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effect is moderated by how information and punishment are structured. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported, but the treatment effects indicate that punishment can reduce efficiency if it triggers defensive, exclusionary, or retaliatory behavior by the allocator.
