- source: Greenwood, G. W., Abbass, H. A., & Petraki, E. (2018). When is altruistic punishment useful in social dilemmas?. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment in a public goods game is only effective at promoting cooperation (and thus improving group outcomes) if the frequency of punishers exceeds a threshold determined by the cost of contribution and the punishment level. If this threshold is not met, defection remains the dominant strategy and group efficiency does not improve. The analysis provides explicit formulas for the returns to each strategy and the minimum frequency of punishers required for punishment to be effective. The findings imply that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to improve efficiency; the population composition and punishment parameters are critical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public goods games. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional: punishment only increases efficiency if the proportion of punishers is above a threshold that depends on the cost of contribution and the punishment level. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on the game parameters (contribution cost, punishment cost/impact) and the frequency of punishers. The explicit formulas can be used to estimate the required conditions for punishment to be effective, but the paper does not provide empirical efficiency values or group payoff data.

- source: Liu, X. S., & Guo, W. L. (2010). Persistent cooperators in nature. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with compulsory participation, if contributors are willing to pay a second cost to retrieve a portion of the public good (persistent cooperation), this can functionally act as punishment and promote cooperation. When the second cost is not too high relative to the benefit, persistent cooperators can dominate the population, leading to higher average payoffs and more stable cooperation. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of benefit-to-cost ratios and the size of the second cost) under which persistent cooperation is evolutionarily stable, risk-dominant, or advantageous. When the second cost is high, persistent cooperators act as traditional costly punishers and are less favored. The abundance of persistent cooperators is used as a proxy for group welfare and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (modeled as a second cost paid by persistent cooperators) can increase the prevalence of cooperation and, by extension, group efficiency, as long as the cost of punishment is not too high relative to the benefit. The model gives explicit parameter thresholds for when punishment is beneficial. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling punishment (with moderate cost and sufficient benefit) in a public goods game is likely to increase efficiency compared to a control without punishment, especially when the punishment cost is not excessive. The model also highlights the importance of the ratio between the benefit of cooperation, the cost of contributing, and the cost of punishment in determining the outcome. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions of efficiency, and its results are theoretical rather than empirical.

- source: Du, K., Fan, R. G., Xue, H., Wang, Y. T., & Bao, X. G. (2023). Evolution of cooperation in spatial public goods game based on heterogenous social preferences and dynamic punishment. *Journal of Intelligent & Fuzzy Systems*.

  findings: The paper finds that (1) heterogeneous social preferences (especially altruism) promote higher cooperation rates in spatial PGGs; (2) dynamic punishment mechanisms are more effective than static punishment in increasing cooperation and addressing second-order free-riding; (3) the enhancement factor (r_e, analogous to MPCR) is a key moderator—when it is low, cooperation is hard to sustain even with punishment, but when it is high, cooperation is high even without punishment; (4) inequality aversion slightly inhibits cooperation, and reciprocity has a mixed effect depending on r_e.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task: it suggests that enabling punishment (especially dynamic punishment) increases cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, particularly when the enhancement factor is moderate. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes. The findings are most useful for understanding how punishment and social preference heterogeneity affect cooperation rates, which may correlate with efficiency in some settings. The model's focus on spatial structure and dynamic punishment is relevant for games with similar features.

- source: Duong, M. H., Durbac, C. M., & Han, T. A. (2024). Cost Optimisation of Individual-Based Institutional Reward Incentives for Promoting Cooperation in Finite Populations. *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the expected total cost to the institution for achieving a target cooperation level in finite-population PGGs depends strongly on the threshold for incentive application (t), the strength of selection (β), mutation rate (μ), and the type of incentive (reward, punishment, hybrid). For small thresholds (t=1), the cost function is always non-decreasing in the per-capita incentive cost, and explicit formulas are derived. For larger thresholds, the cost function can exhibit non-monotonic behavior and phase transitions, especially at high selection strength. Numerical results show that both reward and punishment incentives can be optimized for cost, but the optimal cost and its behavior depend on the game parameters and incentive scheme. The paper provides a framework for predicting the institutional cost required to achieve a desired cooperation level, given the game design and population parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and numerical evidence on how the cost of institutional incentives (reward, punishment, hybrid) required to achieve a target cooperation level in PGG-like environments depends on game design dimensions such as player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, and the structure of the incentive scheme (threshold t, cost per unit, effectiveness). While the main outcome is the institutional cost rather than group efficiency, the results are directly relevant for predicting how changes in punishment or reward design (e.g., cost, threshold, effectiveness) will affect the efficiency of institutional intervention. The paper is most informative for settings where the goal is to optimize the cost of achieving cooperation, rather than to maximize group payoff per se. It provides explicit formulas and numerical results that can be used to inform predictions about the efficiency of punishment or reward interventions, conditional on game design and baseline cooperation levels.

- source: Kristensen, N. P., Chisholm, R. A., & Ohtsuki, H. (2025). Many-strategy games in groups with relatives and the evolution of coordinated cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that kin selection (homophily) can facilitate the initial invasion of coordinated cooperation in public goods games with nonlinear (sigmoidal) benefits, even when such cooperation cannot invade in a well-mixed population due to cognitive or coordination costs. Once established, coordinated cooperation can persist as homophily declines, provided that the benefit function is sufficiently nonlinear and the switching gain from non-contribution to contribution at the coordination point exceeds the cost. If this condition is not met, some level of homophily must be maintained to prevent the invasion of defectors or 'liars' (who commit to contribute but do not follow through). The framework provides analytical and numerical tools for predicting evolutionary outcomes (including efficiency and group payoff) as a function of group size, relatedness, benefit function nonlinearity, and strategy set. The results are directly relevant to predicting efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but do not address punishment or sanctions directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a general theoretical framework for predicting efficiency and group payoff in public goods games with arbitrary group size, number of strategies, and non-additive (e.g., sigmoidal) benefit functions, under varying levels of kin structure (homophily). It is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency of control games (without punishment) as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR (via the benefit function), and group structure. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The framework could, in principle, be extended to include punishment as a discrete strategy, but this is not done in the present paper. Therefore, the paper is highly informative for predicting baseline efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but provides only indirect or contextual support for predicting the effect of punishment.

- source: Wang, S., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2018). Non-Fixed Investment in Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Journal of the Korean Physical Society*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that allowing non-fixed investment in voluntary PGGs leads to complex rock-scissors-paper dynamics among cooperators, defectors, and loners. Increasing the maximum possible investment ratio can initially promote cooperation and increase average payoff, but further increases may reduce cooperation due to increased temptation to defect. Higher interest rates (MPCR) and lower loner payoffs promote both cooperation and higher average payoffs. The memory length (sensitivity to past payoffs) also moderates outcomes: moderate memory promotes higher average payoffs and cooperation, while very short or very long memory is detrimental. The system always converges to a stable equilibrium regardless of initial conditions. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present or discussed as interventions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in PGG-like environments, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the model or analysis. It is therefore not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. However, it does provide detailed evidence on how other design dimensions (investment flexibility, MPCR, loner payoff, memory) affect efficiency and cooperation in voluntary PGGs without punishment. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not relevant.

- source: Vinayak, R. K. (2025). FARMER'S GAME: A GAME THEORETIC APPROACH TOWARDS BURNING OF CROP RESIDUE. *Journal of Dynamics and Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that, without intervention, individual incentives lead to inefficient outcomes (universal burning of crop residue), analogous to the tragedy of the commons. Social pressure can sometimes induce cooperation, but is often not strong enough. The introduction of government punishment (penalty per burned plot) and/or reward (per non-burned plot) can shift the Nash equilibrium to full cooperation (no burning), maximizing group payoff and efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions for when this shift occurs: when the sum of penalty and reward per plot exceeds the net private benefit of burning minus the greener alternative and pollution cost. Simulations confirm these theoretical results, showing that moderate levels of punishment and reward are sufficient to achieve efficient, cooperative outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that introducing punishment (and/or reward) in a public-goods-like environment with negative externalities can shift the equilibrium from inefficient (all defect) to efficient (all cooperate), provided the policy parameters are set so that the penalty plus reward per unit exceeds the net private benefit of defection. The model is directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency: if the punishment is sufficiently strong (relative to the private incentive to defect), efficiency will increase dramatically, potentially reaching full efficiency. The findings are most applicable to settings where punishment is implemented as a per-action cost, is known to all, and is not so severe as to cause practical or political infeasibility. The model does not address empirical implementation issues, but provides clear guidance on the direction and mechanism of efficiency change.

- source: Cooney, D. B. (2025). Exploring the Evolution of Altruistic Punishment with a PDE Model of Cultural Multilevel Selection. *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed

  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment, when combined with group-level selection, can promote higher levels of cooperation and increase group efficiency compared to scenarios without punishment. However, the effect of punishment on efficiency is sensitive to the cost structure (per-interaction vs. fixed), the strength of punishment, and the form of group-level competition. In some models, increasing punishment strength or cost leads to higher cooperation but lower efficiency, due to the collective cost of punishment outweighing its benefits. The results highlight that the design of punishment mechanisms and the structure of group competition are critical moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment.

It shows that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the cost and strength of punishment, the type of cost (per-interaction or fixed), and the form of group-level competition. For prediction, the results suggest that: (1) punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when costs are per-interaction rather than fixed; (2) increasing punishment strength generally increases cooperation, but may decrease efficiency if costs are high; (3) the effect of punishment on efficiency can be non-monotonic, especially under group-level competition rules that normalize by maximum/minimum group payoffs; (4) the threshold group selection strength required for cooperation is lower with stronger or cheaper punishment. The paper's analytical formulas and numerical results can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design parameters, especially in settings with group-structured populations and multilevel selection.

- source: Kurokawa, S. (2023). Evolution of spite in an n-player game with an opting-out option. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player games with an opting-out option, spiteful (costly harming) strategies can sometimes persist, but only under restrictive conditions: low cost-to-harm ratio, small group size, and specific opting-out rules. As group size increases, it becomes harder for spiteful strategies to achieve higher payoffs than nonspiteful strategies, and the basin of attraction for all-nonspiteful populations grows. The opting-out rule (how easily groups disband) is a key moderator: when homogeneous groups (all spiteful or all nonspiteful) are forced to disband, spite can persist more easily, but this is a somewhat artificial rule. The model is mathematically explicit about when spiteful strategies can dominate, and these conditions almost always correspond to lower group efficiency compared to all-nonspiteful populations. The analysis is directly about payoff and efficiency, not just behavior.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public-goods-like n-player games with an opting-out mechanism, the introduction of costly punishment (here, spiteful harming) generally reduces group efficiency unless the cost-to-harm ratio is extremely low and the group size is small. As group size increases, the conditions for punishment/spite to persist become more restrictive, and group efficiency is more likely to be maximized when punishment/spite is absent. The details of the opting-out rule (e.g., whether homogeneous groups are forced to disband) can moderate this effect, but in most realistic settings, punishment/spite is not efficiency-enhancing. The model is not an exact PGG with standard punishment, but the payoff structure and evolutionary logic are closely related, and the results are directly about payoff/efficiency. Thus, the paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment/spite in large groups with standard cost structures will not increase efficiency, and may reduce it.

- source: Fontanari, J. F., & Santos, M. (2024). Solving the prisoner's dilemma trap in Hamilton's model of temporarily formed random groups. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in the 'others-only' n-person prisoner's dilemma (a strong altruism scenario), cooperation cannot evolve under asexual reproduction or sexual reproduction with selfing, but can evolve and become stable under biparental sexual reproduction if the group benefit multiplier (r) is sufficiently large relative to group size (n) and the cost of cooperation (c) is not too high. The model does not include punishment or reward, and the main mechanism enabling cooperation is the genetic structure imposed by biparental reproduction. The main outcome is the probability that the population reaches the all-cooperator state (fixation probability), which is a direct measure of group efficiency or welfare. The results are summarized in phase diagrams showing regions where cooperation is stable, bistable, or unstable, as a function of c, r, and n. The findings are robust to demographic noise and extend to dioecious populations. The paper does not provide evidence about the effects of punishment, communication, or other institutional design features.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments that lack punishment or reward mechanisms, especially when the game structure is the 'others-only' variant of the n-person prisoner's dilemma. It shows that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency (measured as fixation of cooperation and group payoff) is only achievable under specific genetic and demographic conditions—namely, biparental sexual reproduction, sufficiently high group benefit multiplier (r), and not-too-large group size (n) or cost (c). The results suggest that, without punishment, efficiency is generally low unless these conditions are met. However, the paper does not provide any evidence about the effect of enabling punishment or sanctions, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency difference between control and punishment-enabled conditions. Its main value is in establishing a baseline: in the absence of punishment, efficiency is generally low except under the specified genetic/demographic conditions. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not manipulate or analyze punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that the efficiency of costly punishment in sustaining cooperation depends critically on the detectability of defection. Execution errors and high punishment costs shrink the parameter space where punishment is efficient. The results challenge previous theoretical work that found only a narrow efficiency margin for punishment, showing that under asymmetric detection errors, punishment can be broadly efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, parameterized theoretical predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency in indirect reciprocity games with pairwise donation structure. While not a standard public goods game, the model is adjacent and the efficiency results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment in environments where defection is hard to detect. The explicit formulas and phase diagrams allow mapping from game design dimensions (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, error rates) and baseline efficiency to the expected efficiency with punishment enabled. The main moderator is the detectability of defection: when it is low, punishment can substantially increase efficiency; when high, punishment is unnecessary or even harmful. The results are most transferable to settings with pairwise interactions, reputation, and indirect reciprocity, but provide strong mechanistic insight for broader PGG-like environments.
