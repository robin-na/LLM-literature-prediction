- source: Cressman, R., Song, J. W., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2012). Cooperation and evolutionary dynamics in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

However, the effect is not universally positive: high punishment costs, the presence of antisocial punishment, or poorly tuned parameters can lead to lower efficiency or even the collapse of cooperation. Adaptive and probabilistic punishment mechanisms can improve efficiency by reducing unnecessary costs. The spatial structure of interactions (e.g., lattices, networks) is critical: it allows for clustering of cooperators and punishers, which can support higher efficiency at lower multiplication factors (mpcr) than in well-mixed populations. The paper provides detailed phase diagrams showing the regions where punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on efficiency, depending on the interplay of punishment cost, effectiveness, group size, and synergy factor.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, mpcr, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control only when punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective, and when the spatial structure allows for clustering of cooperators/punishers. If punishment is costly or antisocial punishment is possible, efficiency gains may be lost or reversed. The paper's phase diagrams and model descriptions can be used to map specific game designs to expected efficiency outcomes, given the control efficiency. However, the results are based on theoretical models and simulations, not direct empirical measurement, so predictions should be interpreted with caution and in the context of the model's assumptions.

The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with reasonable cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the marginal benefit of also enabling reward is likely negligible except under harsh conditions (low MPCR, low cost). The results are most directly applicable to spatial (networked) PGGs with group size 5, continuous contributions, and symmetric cost/impact for punishment and reward.

For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment will have a more positive effect on efficiency when the punishment technology is strong and the cost is low, and that group size and the structure of repeated interaction also matter.

  findings: The paper finds that in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, allowing anti-social punishment (punishment of cooperators) prevents the evolution of cooperation in public goods games with costly punishment. In models where only pro-social punishment is possible, cooperation and punishment can co-evolve, leading to high group efficiency. If anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, as cooperation is not stable and defectors who punish others dominate. In contrast, if only pro-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of anti-social punishment as a design dimension is critical: if anti-social punishment is allowed, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment; if it is not allowed, efficiency gains are possible under the right parameter regimes.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible. When the synergy factor is low, prosocial punishment can still restore cooperation and efficiency, because nonpunishing defectors (second-order free-riders) undermine antisocial punishers, allowing punishing cooperators to dominate. The result is that, across a wide range of parameters, the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency, even in the presence of antisocial punishment. The paper also identifies parameter regions with stable coexistence of strategies, but the main result is that prosocial punishment is robustly effective in structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.

Introducing a tax to support punishers expands the parameter space where defectors are eliminated and efficiency is high, but only if the punishment cost is significant. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with three strategies (C, D, P), and the results are sensitive to the exact parameter values of punishment cost, fine, and tax. - source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in a population with probabilistic corrupt enforcers and violators. *Mathematical Models & Methods in Applied Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. If the expected loss equals the bribe, the system exhibits neutral cycles (limit cycles) with periodic oscillations in cooperation and efficiency. If the expected loss exceeds the bribe, the system can either converge to all-defection (low efficiency) or to a heteroclinic cycle with cyclic dominance, which may still allow for periods of cooperation. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings. The findings are directly informative for mapping game design parameters (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, bribe size, probability of corruption) to expected efficiency outcomes.

  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.
