- source: Gintis, H. (2003). Solving the puzzle of prosociality. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment by strong reciprocators can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise unravel due to self-interest. Theoretical results show that with sufficient oblique cultural transmission and a non-trivial fraction of punishers, full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) can be a stable equilibrium. The paper also reviews experimental evidence showing that when punishment is allowed, group payoffs and cooperation rates are significantly higher than when punishment is not allowed. The model further predicts that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining efficiency depends on the cost and impact of punishment, the fraction of punishers, and the strength of cultural transmission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control games without punishment, provided that punishment is not prohibitively costly and that a sufficient fraction of players are willing to punish defectors. The model and supporting evidence suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of game parameters, including group size and number of rounds, as long as punishment is effective and not too costly. The paper also highlights the importance of cultural and institutional context (e.g., norm internalization, oblique transmission) in sustaining prosocial behavior and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defection.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

  findings: The paper reviews experimental evidence showing that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms typically increases contributions and cooperation. However, it also highlights that punishment is costly and not always optimal from a collective (efficiency) perspective, especially when antisocial punishment or counter-punishment occurs. The effectiveness of punishment is robust to parameter changes (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness), but can be undermined by incomplete information or network structure. Communication and group selection also enhance cooperation. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral regularities and mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong qualitative support that enabling punishment in public goods games generally increases cooperation and contributions, but warns that the net effect on efficiency depends on the costs of punishment and the prevalence of antisocial punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when punishment is targeted at free riders, costs are not excessive, and antisocial punishment is rare. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes, so its value is mainly in contextualizing and interpreting empirical findings from other sources.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: Benard, S. (2012). Cohesion from Conflict: Does Intergroup Conflict Motivate Intragroup Norm Enforcement and Support for Centralized Leadership?. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that intergroup conflict increases the likelihood that group members will punish noncontributors (enforce norms) and support centralized leadership, but only when the outgroup is actively competing (i.e., high outgroup contribution). Conflict increases individual contributions to the group regardless of outgroup competitiveness. The effect of punishment is measured as the frequency of norm enforcement, not as its impact on group efficiency or total payoff. The presence of punishment does not appear to be experimentally manipulated as an on/off treatment; rather, punishment is always available, and the focus is on when and how much it is used. No direct analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare is provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about when group members are more likely to use costly punishment in public-goods-like games, specifically under intergroup conflict and high outgroup competition. However, it does not provide data on the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it compare efficiency with and without punishment. Thus, it is not directly informative for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, given game design dimensions and control efficiency. It does suggest that punishment is more likely to be used in conflictual, competitive intergroup settings, but does not address whether this improves or reduces group efficiency.

  findings: The experiment shows that the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation depends on both the trustworthiness of the leader who installs the sanction and the general trust participants have in authorities. When an alternative, more harmful defection option is available (i.e., sanctions can be evaded), participants with low general trust in authorities are less likely to cooperate if the leader is untrustworthy, and instead shift to the evasion option. High-trust participants are less sensitive to leader trustworthiness. The main outcomes are behavioral allocations; the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of an evadable punishment option (i.e., a trilemma with an alternative defection route) can undermine the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation, especially among participants with low trust in authorities and when the leader is perceived as untrustworthy. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in designs where punishment can be evaded, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and thus efficiency) may be reduced or even reversed for some subgroups. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of evasion options and trust, but not about the quantitative effect on efficiency.

- source: De Cremer, D., Hoogervorst, N., & Desmet, P. (2012). Procedural Justice and Sanctions in Social Dilemmas: The Moderating Effects of Group Feedback and Identification. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that accurate sanctioning systems (those that monitor all contribution sessions) increase contributions relative to inaccurate systems, but this effect depends on group identification and feedback. For high group identifiers, procedural justice (accuracy) increases contributions only after group failure; for low identifiers, it increases contributions only after group success. The experiment uses a step-level public good game with a threshold and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The findings highlight the importance of procedural justice in sanctioning systems for promoting cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence about effects on efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions depends on the perceived procedural justice (accuracy) of the sanctioning system, and that this effect is moderated by group identification and feedback about group success or failure. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in step-level public good games with punishment, the design of the punishment system (especially its perceived fairness and accuracy) and the social context (identification, feedback) can strongly moderate behavioral responses, which may in turn affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment will be more effective at increasing cooperation (and possibly efficiency) when the system is accurate and procedurally just, but the effect is context-dependent.

- source: Clavien, C., & Klein, R. A. (2010). EAGER FOR FAIRNESS OR FOR REVENGE? PSYCHOLOGICAL ALTRUISM IN ECONOMICS. *Economics and Philosophy*.

  findings: The paper finds that while experimental economics and neuroeconomics have revealed robust patterns of altruistic punishment and cooperation in public goods and related games, these studies do not provide decisive evidence about the underlying psychological motives (altruism vs. egoism). Behavioral outcomes such as costly punishment of free-riders are well established, but the interpretation of these actions (e.g., as motivated by fairness, revenge, or self-interest) remains open. Neuroimaging studies (e.g., de Quervain et al. 2004) show reward-related brain activation during punishment, but this does not conclusively distinguish between altruistic and egoistic motives. The paper argues that more fine-grained experimental designs and controls are needed to advance the debate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games or variants. It is useful for understanding the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment behavior, but does not report or synthesize data on group efficiency, welfare, or total earnings. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers conceptual context but no quantitative or qualitative evidence about efficiency outcomes under punishment. It highlights the need for careful experimental design and the limitations of current evidence, but does not inform the mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency.

- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Loukopoulos, P., Eek, D., Gärling, T., & Fujii, S. (2006). Palatable punishment in real-world social dilemmas? Punishing others to increase cooperation among the unpunished. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that intermittent punishment (where only some group members are punished for defection in a given round) increases cooperation rates among those not directly punished, compared to a no-punishment baseline. This 'spillover effect' is robust to changes in the framing of the decision (individual vs. group focus) and to information about prior cooperation by others. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

- source: Nakao, K. (2009). CREATION OF SOCIAL ORDER IN ETHNIC CONFLICT. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer monitoring, collective peer punishment, or ethnic conflict) can sustain higher group efficiency (expected payoffs) compared to no punishment, but only if in-group monitoring is sufficiently efficient and not too costly. When monitoring is inefficient or costly, or when noise (misinterpretation of actions) is high, punishment can reduce efficiency due to frequent erroneous punishment or high monitoring costs. The model provides comparative statics showing that efficiency is maximized with efficient peer monitoring and high tolerance, and that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the quality of in-group policing, the length of conflict, and the density of inter-group interactions. The model predicts that groups with high-quality in-group policing achieve higher efficiency and more stable cooperation, while groups with low-quality policing suffer more frequent and longer conflicts, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated social dilemma games depends critically on the efficiency and cost of monitoring and punishment. If the game design allows for efficient, low-cost peer monitoring (low punishment cost, high punishment tech), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. If monitoring is costly or ineffective, punishment can reduce efficiency due to high costs or frequent erroneous punishment. The model also shows that the effect of punishment is moderated by noise (misinterpretation), group size, and the structure of interactions. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency only when monitoring is efficient and not too costly, and that the marginal effect of punishment is lower or negative when monitoring is costly or noise is high. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, so findings should be transferred with caution.

- source: Corriveau, L. (2012). Game theory and the kula. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in a repeated, circular n-player game (the 'game of kula'), the unique Nash equilibrium of the one-shot game is inefficient (all defect). However, when the game is repeated indefinitely and players are sufficiently patient, efficient (fully cooperative) equilibria can be sustained by social norms that attach punishment to deviations. The model formalizes three types of punishment-based norms: direct reciprocity (punished by the harmed party), indirect reciprocity (punished by a third party), and mixed reciprocity (punished by both). The mixed norm is shown to be the most robust, existing under the widest range of parameters. The analysis is entirely theoretical, but it provides formal conditions under which punishment enables efficient cooperation, and shows that the presence of punishment strategies can transform an inefficient game into an efficient one.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in repeated, multi-player social dilemma games (with circular or networked structure) can increase efficiency, provided players are sufficiently patient and punishment is credible. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the results generalize the repeated prisoner's dilemma. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is possible and observable, and where players have high discount factors, the introduction of punishment mechanisms can shift the equilibrium from inefficient (defection) to efficient (cooperation), thus increasing group payoff. The paper also clarifies that the structure of punishment (direct, indirect, mixed) affects the robustness of cooperation, with mixed norms being most robust. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, networked social dilemmas, especially as the number of players increases and as the possibility of both direct and indirect punishment exists.

- source: Bohnet, I., & Baytelman, Y. (2007). Institutions and trust - Implications for preferences, beliefs and behavior. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional constraints, including a post-play punishment option, increase trustors' willingness to send money and trustees' willingness to return money in a trust game scenario, compared to an anonymous one-shot game. This suggests that efficiency (total group payoff) is higher with punishment. However, the presence of punishment and other institutional constraints reduces the role of intrinsic motivation (social orientation) in driving trust, supporting the 'crowding out' hypothesis. For trustworthiness, the effect of punishment is indirect: trustees return more because they expect to receive more, not because their intrinsic motivation changes. The study uses a within-subject design with senior executives and hypothetical payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that adding a punishment option to a trust game increases the amounts sent and returned, which is a proxy for increased efficiency. While the design is not a public goods game, the trust game with punishment is adjacent and the direction of effect is likely to generalize to PGG-like settings: punishment increases efficiency-related outcomes. However, the paper also highlights that the mechanism is not an increase in intrinsic motivation, but rather a shift in beliefs and expectations. For prediction, this suggests that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low. The effect is robust across several institutional manipulations, but the crowding out of intrinsic motivation may have long-term implications not captured by short-run efficiency.

- source: MOLM, L. D. (1994). IS PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVE - COERCIVE STRATEGIES IN SOCIAL-EXCHANGE. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.

  findings: The study finds that punishment is effective in increasing compliance (frequency of rewarding the punisher) when it is used frequently, contingently, and with high magnitude. The combination of high probability and high magnitude of punishment produces the highest compliance. Retaliation by the target does not increase with more frequent or stronger punishment, and negative affect toward the punisher is actually lower when punishment is strong and consistent, due to the resulting mutually rewarding exchanges. The effects are robust to the target's ability to retaliate and to variations in the probability and contingency of punishment. However, the main outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, but the frequency of compliance in a dyadic exchange. The results challenge classical exchange and bargaining theories that predict punishment provokes retaliation and reduces cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in dyadic reciprocal exchange settings with both reward and punishment options, frequent and contingent punishment increases compliance (rewarding behavior) by the target, without increasing retaliation or negative affect. However, because the primary outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, and the setting is not a public goods game or direct variant, the findings should be transferred to PGG-like environments with caution. The results suggest that, under conditions of high punishment frequency, contingency, and magnitude, punishment can be effective at increasing the target's prosocial behavior, but the implications for group efficiency in multi-person public goods games remain indirect. The paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment rather than direct efficiency outcomes.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that increasing the strength of punishment reduces both the rate of inspection (control) and the rate of theft (crime), contrary to the strict game-theoretic prediction that only inspection should decrease. The effect on inspection is strong and robust, while the effect on theft is also significant but not as strong as predicted by standard theory. The behavioral adaptation to changes in punishment is slower than predicted by rational learning models. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates; efficiency or group welfare is not directly measured, but the design ensures that theft is inefficient (gamma = 0.5). Inspectees lose money over time, while inspectors' earnings are roughly stable. The findings suggest that punishment has mixed effects on strategic behavior in inspection settings, and that human adaptation is slower and less complete than predicted by theory.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides informative evidence about the behavioral effects of punishment in an inspection game with inefficient theft, which is structurally adjacent to public goods games. The main outcomes are theft and inspection rates, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. However, since theft is inefficient (gamma < 1), reductions in theft due to higher punishment would increase group efficiency, while reductions in inspection (which is costly) could also increase efficiency if inspection is not needed. The paper shows that higher punishment reduces both theft and inspection, suggesting that the net effect on efficiency would depend on the relative magnitudes of these changes and the cost structure. The design and findings are most relevant for settings where punishment is formal, inspection is costly, and theft is inefficient. The lack of direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions, but the qualitative direction of effects (higher punishment reduces both crime and control) is informative for adjacent settings.
