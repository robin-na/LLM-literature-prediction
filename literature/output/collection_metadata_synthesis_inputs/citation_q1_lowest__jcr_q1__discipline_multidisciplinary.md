- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., Gross, J., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., van Dijk, E., & de Dreu, C. K. W. (2023). Discriminatory punishment undermines the enforcement of group cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

- source: Bühren, C., Haarde, J., Hirschmann, C., & Kesten-Kühne, J. (2023). Social preferences in the public goods game-An Agent-Based simulation with EconSim. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The agent-based model, using reinforcement learning and a social preference framework, closely replicates the experimental findings of [20] in a public goods game with endogenous punishment. For low cooperators, severe punishment increases contributions and can raise payoffs toward the social optimum, but mild punishment is ineffective and reduces payoffs due to its cost. For high cooperators, punishment (both mild and severe) increases contributions only slightly but reduces payoffs because the cost of punishment outweighs the marginal gain in cooperation. Middle cooperators benefit from mild punishment, which increases both contributions and payoffs. The model demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the underlying social preference structure of the group. Endogenous institution choice in the simulation leads agents to select the institution that maximizes their own payoffs, mirroring the learning observed in the experimental Sorted-Info treatment but not in the Sorted treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with known design parameters. The simulation results, validated against experimental data, show that the impact of punishment on efficiency depends on the group’s baseline cooperativeness: severe punishment can increase efficiency for low cooperators but is costly and can reduce efficiency for high cooperators. The model’s detailed reporting of payoffs under different punishment regimes, player types, and institution selection mechanisms makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings highlight the importance of considering both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the social preference composition of the group.

- source: Zefferman, M. R. (2023). Constraints on cooperation shape hierarchical versus distributed structure in human groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the efficiency of punishment and monitoring technologies fundamentally shapes the optimal structure of cooperation in public goods games. When punishment is efficient (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the impact on free-riders), hierarchical (tree-like) organizations are optimal and can support universal cooperation (full efficiency) even in large groups. When punishment is inefficient (costly for punishers), distributed (collective) monitoring and punishment are optimal, but these structures are limited in scale by monitoring costs. The model provides explicit analytic conditions for when universal contribution (full cooperation) is a Nash equilibrium and socially efficient, and when universal free-riding is the only equilibrium. The model also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment networks (hierarchical vs. distributed) is an endogenous outcome of the costs and effectiveness of these institutions. Thus, the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency depends critically on the cost-effectiveness of punishment and monitoring, as well as group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that enabling punishment can increase group efficiency (by supporting universal cooperation) if punishment is sufficiently efficient (low cost, high impact), and if monitoring is not prohibitively costly. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds at which universal cooperation is efficient and stable, depending on group size, public goods multiplier, monitoring efficiency, and punishment efficiency. It also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment (hierarchical vs. distributed) is determined by these parameters, which in turn affects the scalability of cooperation. For prediction, the model implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is cheap and effective, monitoring is efficient, and group size is not too large for the given monitoring technology. If punishment is costly or monitoring is inefficient, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency, especially in larger groups.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2024). Discipline and punishment in panoptical public goods games. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a compulsory, fully monitored PGG, it is possible to achieve full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) by punishing only a fraction of defectors, provided the punishment is sufficiently severe. The model formalizes the trade-off between the fraction of defectors punished (scope) and the severity of punishment (magnitude), showing that these can be tuned to reach stable full cooperation. The results indicate that the threat of punishment, even if not universally applied, can discipline the population and sustain cooperation, thus maximizing group payoff. The findings are entirely theoretical, based on replicator dynamics and equilibrium analysis.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in a compulsory and fully observable PGG, enabling punishment (even if not universal) can substantially increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline. The model gives explicit conditions (in terms of player count, MPCR, punishment severity, and punishment scope) under which full cooperation is stable. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that introducing a sufficiently strong and well-targeted punishment mechanism will likely move efficiency close to 1, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. However, the model assumes perfect monitoring, compulsory participation, and centralized punishment, so predictions should be adjusted if these assumptions do not hold in the target environment.

Monetary punishment is most effective in raising contributions, but this effect is not persistent: contributions drop sharply after the punishment phase ends. Non-monetary reward and recommendation also increase contributions, with some evidence of longer-lasting effects, especially for recommendation among certain types (e.g., free-riders). However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The study also finds that the effect of incentives varies by social preference type: monetary punishment increases contributions across all types, while non-monetary reward and recommendation mainly affect strong conditional cooperators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling monetary punishment in a repeated linear PGG increases contribution rates during the intervention, but the effect is not persistent after removal. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency outcomes is not possible. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified and match standard PGG lab experiments. The findings support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) during the intervention, but the lack of payoff/efficiency data means the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred. The results are most useful for predicting contribution rate changes, not efficiency per se.

For the downstream prediction task, the paper confirms that, in a 3-player, 10-round VCM with standard punishment (linear cost, known identities), the introduction of punishment increases contributions, especially for sleep-restricted subjects. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed, the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, and the behavioral results are likely to correlate with efficiency, but the lack of direct payoff analysis is a limitation.

While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, the close link between contribution rates and efficiency in standard PGGs suggests that vertical trust communication could maintain efficiency at levels similar to those with punishment, at least in the short term. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the main outcome is contribution, not efficiency.
