- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Grieco, D., Faillo, M., & Zarri, L. (2017). Enforcing cooperation in public goods games: Is one punisher enough?. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: Dorrough, A., Glöckner, A., & Lee, B. (2017). Race for Power in Public Good Games with Unequal, Unstable Punishment Power. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: Kumakawa, T. (2013). Social Rewards and Sanctions: A Voluntary Contribution Mechanism Experiment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a two-player public goods game, the opportunity to send social (free-form) or monetary rewards/sanctions does not increase first-round contributions compared to a baseline with no such opportunity. However, when participants actually experience social sanctions (i.e., receive a negative message from their partner), their contributions in the next round increase significantly. Social rewards (positive messages) do not have a significant effect. Monetary sanctions and rewards are rarely used and do not significantly affect subsequent contributions. The study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player public goods game with a game-theoretic payoff table and no prior knowledge of a second round, social sanctions (blame via free-form messages) can increase subsequent contributions, but only after they are actually experienced. The mere opportunity for sanctions or rewards does not affect cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (contribution) responses to social sanctions, not efficiency outcomes. The design (two players, two rounds, no chat except for the sanction/reward stage, full information about partner's contribution, and a linear MPCR of 0.7) should be considered when generalizing to other settings.

- source: Romaniuc, R., Farrow, K., Ibanez, L., & Marciano, A. (2016). The perils of government enforcement. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer disapproval (private enforcement) increases contributions to the public good compared to baseline. Adding a mild, centralized monetary punishment (government enforcement) to peer disapproval stabilizes contributions but does not significantly increase average contributions compared to peer disapproval alone. Critically, if government enforcement is removed after being in place, the effectiveness of peer disapproval is undermined: low contributors become unresponsive to disapproval, and average contributions drop significantly below the level observed when only private enforcement was ever present. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the results on contributions imply that punishment can have both stabilizing and crowding-out effects depending on the sequence of enforcement mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the sequence and combination of enforcement mechanisms (private and government) can have important effects on cooperation (as measured by contributions) in public goods games. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings suggest that enabling punishment can stabilize or increase contributions, but that removing punishment after it has been in place can lead to a sharp decline in cooperation, even below the level observed with private enforcement alone. For prediction tasks, this implies that the history of enforcement mechanisms and their removal are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency outcomes.

The results also warn that adding rewards without second-order incentives can reduce cooperation, which may imply lower efficiency, but this is not directly measured.

- source: Sell, J., & Reese, B. (2014). Social Dilemma Experiments in Sociology, Psychology, Political Science, and Economics. *Laboratory Experiments in the Social Sciences, 2nd Edition*.

  findings: The chapter synthesizes findings from experimental studies on social dilemmas, emphasizing that cooperation is more common than predicted by strong free-riding hypotheses, and that structural features such as group size, information, communication, and institutional rules (including punishment and reward) shape cooperation. Punishment is discussed as a mechanism that can increase cooperation, but its effectiveness depends on design features such as who can punish, the cost, and the identification of punishers and recipients.   prediction_guidance: This chapter provides useful theoretical and qualitative evidence about the mechanisms by which punishment, communication, and information affect cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It does not provide quantitative or directly predictive evidence about efficiency or group payoff outcomes, nor does it report experimental results with efficiency as a dependent variable. For prediction tasks, the chapter supports the idea that punishment can increase cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency), but also warns that unconstrained punishment may reduce cooperation. The review is most useful for understanding which design dimensions are likely to moderate the effect of punishment, rather than for direct quantitative prediction.

- source: Lierl, M. (2016). Social sanctions and informal accountability: Evidence from a laboratory experiment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment studies a public goods game with two beneficiaries and one provider, where the provider can embezzle contributions. The main manipulation is the structure of costly, mutual sanctioning (punishment and reward) relationships: (1) no sanctioning, (2) only among beneficiaries, (3) between provider and one or both beneficiaries, and (4) combinations thereof. The key finding is that mutual sanctioning among beneficiaries increases public goods provision and efficiency compared to no-sanctioning control, but this effect disappears or reverses when the provider is also part of the sanctioning network. The highest efficiency is observed when only beneficiaries can sanction each other. When both beneficiaries and the provider can mutually sanction, efficiency is not improved and can be lower than control. Actual punishment/reward is rare; the effect is mainly anticipatory. The results challenge the idea that denser or more encompassing sanctioning networks always improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends critically on the network structure of sanctioning relationships. If only beneficiaries can punish each other, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to control. If the provider can also punish or be punished, the effect is reduced or negative. For prediction, the presence and direction of punishment effects should be conditioned on who can punish whom. The game is a close variant of a standard PGG, with the addition of a third-party provider who can embezzle, so results are most directly applicable to co-production or agency settings. Control efficiency (no-sanction) is reported, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) varies by network structure. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment is not always efficiency-improving; the details of the punishment network matter.

- source: Cason, T. N., & Gangadharan, L. (2016). Swords without covenants do not lead to self-governance. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.

  findings: The experiment tested four treatments (baseline, punishment only, communication only, both) in a repeated nonlinear common pool resource game with groups of six.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in nonlinear common pool resource environments (with concave private benefits and endogenous group costs), enabling peer punishment does not improve efficiency over baseline. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar nonlinear games, one should expect little or no efficiency gain, even with a punishment technology that is effective in linear games. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the payoff structure (linear vs nonlinear), and that control efficiency alone is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering the game's nonlinearity and the availability of communication.
