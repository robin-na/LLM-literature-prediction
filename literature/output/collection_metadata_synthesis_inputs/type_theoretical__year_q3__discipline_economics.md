- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

- source: Dutta, R., Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2021). The whip and the Bible: Punishment versus internalization. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms leads to a large increase in group efficiency and welfare, as measured by group payoff and normalized utility. The model, calibrated to Fehr & Gächter (2000), matches the observed jump in average contributions and welfare when punishment is enabled. The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations. The model further predicts that the relationship between societal value of cooperation and punishment is non-monotonic in the lab, but always positive in the field.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The model is directly calibrated to the Fehr & Gächter (2000) design (4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, specific punishment cost/tech), and matches the observed efficiency increase. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2020). SUSTAINING COOPERATION: COMMUNITY ENFORCEMENT VERSUS SPECIALIZED ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of the European Economic Association*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed

  findings: The paper finds that the optimal way to sustain cooperation in repeated public-goods-like games depends on the effectiveness of the punishment technology. If specialized enforcers can punish at low cost and high impact, then pure enforcer punishment (with immediate return to cooperation) maximizes efficiency. If specialized enforcement is less effective, then combining enforcer punishment with community enforcement (e.g., temporary withdrawal of cooperation or ostracism) is optimal. The paper provides explicit characterizations of the maximum sustainable cooperation (efficiency) in each regime, and shows that the presence of effective specialized enforcement can increase group welfare compared to pure community enforcement. However, if the punishment technology is very weak, community enforcement alone is nearly optimal. The results generalize to both small and large groups, and to settings with imperfect monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of introducing punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on the effectiveness of the punishment technology and other game design parameters. It shows that, when punishment is highly effective (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the harm inflicted), enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency by supporting higher cooperation. However, if punishment is costly or ineffective, the efficiency gain is limited, and community enforcement may be nearly as good. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency as a function of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and discount factor. The results also highlight that the structure of punishment (one-time vs. repeated, enforcer vs. peer) and the possibility of ostracism or repentance are important moderators.

If monitoring is strictly local, only highly clustered (triadically closed) networks can sustain cooperation, and efficiency gains from punishment are limited. The paper also shows that the cost and effectiveness of punishment (punishment_cost, punishment_tech) interact with network structure to determine whether efficient outcomes are possible. The results are most directly applicable to networked repeated games with bilateral ties, but the logic extends to public goods games with networked or local interaction structures.
