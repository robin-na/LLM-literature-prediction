- source: Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2016). Peer discipline and incentives within groups. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment, when feasible and not prohibitively costly, enables groups to sustain full contribution to public goods and achieve high efficiency, even in large groups. The model provides conditions under which peer discipline is feasible and optimal, and shows that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the cost and effectiveness of the punishment and audit technology. The model also shows that, in the absence of peer punishment, only small groups can achieve efficient outcomes, while large groups fail to contribute. The results are robust to various extensions, including renegotiation, spillover costs, and generalized auditing procedures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling peer punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that punishment and audit costs are not too high. The model gives explicit formulas for group utility as a function of game parameters, including player count, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in larger groups and when the punishment technology is effective and not too costly. The model also highlights the importance of the cost structure of punishment and auditing, and the quality of monitoring signals, for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment.

- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: Huang, S. T., Dong, Y. L., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). THE OPTIMAL INCENTIVE IN PROMOTING COOPERATION: PUNISH THE WORST AND DO NOT ONLY REWARD THE BEST. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: Orr, S. W. (2001). The economics of shame in work groups: How mutual monitoring can decrease cooperation in teams. *Kyklos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds, through theoretical argument and reference to motivation crowding theory, that mutual monitoring and social sanctions (including social disapproval) in public-goods-like group incentive schemes can decrease cooperation among normatively motivated individuals due to crowding out of intrinsic motivation. The effect is non-linear: some monitoring may initially increase cooperation, but excessive or controlling monitoring leads to suspicion and a shift from normative to gain frames, reducing contributions. For individualistic types, monitoring may crowd in normative motivation and increase cooperation. The overall effect of mutual monitoring or social sanctions on group performance is thus frequency-dependent on the motivational composition of the group. The paper does not provide direct predictions or data on efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that in groups with many normatively motivated members, mutual monitoring may reduce cooperation and thus likely reduce efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical caution that enabling mutual monitoring or social sanctions (punishment) in public-goods-like environments may not always increase efficiency or cooperation, especially in groups with many normatively motivated members. The effect of punishment or monitoring is predicted to be non-linear and frequency-dependent: it may increase cooperation among individualists but decrease it among cooperators if perceived as controlling. For the prediction task, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and may depend on group composition and the perceived nature of monitoring. However, the paper does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on efficiency, so its value is mainly in highlighting possible negative or mixed effects of punishment mechanisms, especially social (rather than material) sanctions.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Gavrilets, S., Qin, C. Z., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). Kinship can hinder cooperation in heterogeneous populations. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods and multi-player PD games with heterogeneous cost-benefit ratios, increasing genetic relatedness does not always increase cooperation or efficiency. For low relatedness, direct reciprocity (punishment via GRIM) can sustain cooperation and high efficiency. For high relatedness, cooperation is a dominant strategy and efficiency is high. However, for intermediate relatedness, some individuals become unconditional cooperators (AllC), which undermines the effectiveness of punishment and allows others to defect (AllD), making full cooperation and maximal efficiency unsustainable. This results in a non-monotonic, U-shaped relationship between relatedness and efficiency. The findings are supported by both subgame perfect equilibrium analysis and agent-based evolutionary simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public goods games with heterogeneity in cost-benefit ratios, the effect of mechanisms analogous to punishment (i.e., direct reciprocity via GRIM) on efficiency depends critically on the population structure and relatedness. When relatedness is low, punishment can sustain high efficiency; when relatedness is high, cooperation is self-sustaining. However, at intermediate relatedness, the threat of punishment is undermined, and efficiency drops. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity and the degree of relatedness (or analogous parameters) can cause non-monotonic effects of punishment on efficiency, and that punishment is most effective at low relatedness. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated PGG-like games with heterogeneity, but less so for one-shot games or games with explicit costly punishment mechanisms.

- source: Benchekroun, H., & Van Long, N. (2008). The build-up of cooperative behavior among non-cooperative selfish agents. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with no punishment or sanctions, but where agents can condition their behavior on a public 'stock of cooperation' (social history), a continuum of Markovian Nash equilibria exist. These equilibria can support steady-state contribution and welfare levels above the static Nash equilibrium but below the social optimum. The social optimum cannot be stably achieved without punishment. The model provides explicit analytical characterizations of these equilibria and shows that the more patient the agents (lower discount rate), the closer the best equilibrium can approach the social optimum. The paper emphasizes that, unlike models with punishment, the highest sustainable efficiency is strictly less than the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games without punishment or sanctions, especially when social history or trust mechanisms are present. It provides strong theoretical evidence that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency can be improved above the Nash baseline if agents coordinate on social-history-based strategies, but the social optimum is not achievable. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (which is absent here) would allow efficiency to reach or exceed the best equilibrium described in this model, and potentially reach the social optimum. The paper is not informative about the effect of punishment per se, but it provides a theoretical upper bound for efficiency in the absence of punishment, given social history mechanisms.

- source: Chessa, M., & Loiseau, P. (2024). Enhancing voluntary contributions in a public goods economy via a minimum individual contribution level. *Public Choice*.

  findings: The paper demonstrates that introducing a minimum individual contribution level (while still allowing for full free riding) in a voluntary public goods game can strictly increase the total contribution and social welfare at equilibrium, provided the minimum is not set too high. The analysis covers both heterogeneous and homogeneous cost cases, and characterizes the equilibrium structure as a function of the minimum. The results show that there is an optimal range for the minimum contribution: if set just above the lowest equilibrium contribution in the baseline game, it increases total contributions without causing contributors to drop out. If set too high, some contributors become free riders and total contributions can decrease. The paper provides operational guidance for setting the minimum but does not address punishment, reward, or other sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not study punishment or sanctions. However, it is highly informative about the effects of restricting the contribution set by imposing a minimum, which is a different intervention. The findings may be useful for understanding how changes to the action space (such as minimums or defaults) affect efficiency, but cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper should not be used as evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency.

The efficiency gains are achieved even when the provision rate is not maximized, due to the low cost of punishment and the stabilization of cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in TPGG-like environments, especially when punishment is endogenous, low-cost, and shared. The results are most applicable to games with group size 4, MPCR 0.5, and threshold 60, but the model explores a range of parameters. The paper's agent-based approach and focus on payoff outcomes make it highly informative for the prediction task.
