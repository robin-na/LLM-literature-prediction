- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Pi, J. X., Yang, G. H., & Yang, H. (2022). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in N-person snowdrift games with peer punishment and individual disguise. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Wang, C. Q., & Huang, C. C. (2022). Between local and global strategy updating in public goods game. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing the option for costly punishment in a repeated public goods game increases group contributions and group payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline. This effect holds both when sanctions are observable (feedback given immediately) and when sanctions are unobservable (feedback delayed until the end). The mechanism is that subjects expect free riders to be punished and adjust their behavior to avoid being punished, even when they do not observe sanctions in real time. Sanctioning is mainly non-strategic and norm-driven, targeting free riders. The presence of punishment stabilizes cooperation and prevents the unraveling of contributions, with some groups achieving high cooperation and others not. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency (as measured by group payoffs and contributions as a fraction of maximum) is positive.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to whether punishment is observable or not during play, suggesting that the mere possibility of punishment, and the associated social norm, is sufficient to increase cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment to a repeated linear PGG with 4 players, 10 rounds, and MPCR=0.4 will increase efficiency, even if punishment is not immediately observable. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

- source: Sun, X. P., Li, Y. B., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., Peng, J., Wang, H. Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2020). Co-Evolution of Complex Network Public Goods Game under the Edges Rules. *Entropy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that allowing players to break unsatisfactory connections and form new ones (edge rules) in a public goods game on a network leads to higher and more stable cooperation, especially at low gain factors (r=2). Both simulation and a 30-person lab experiment show that the fraction of cooperators increases and stabilizes under these rules, compared to a static network. The mechanism is interpreted as self-organized punishment (breaking ties with defectors) and reward (forming ties with cooperators), but there is no explicit costly punishment. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators; efficiency or group payoff is not reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that endogenous network adaptation (breaking and forming ties based on satisfaction) can promote cooperation in public goods games, even at low MPCR. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of explicit punishment on efficiency. The 'punishment' here is not a costly sanction but the breaking of ties, which is structurally different from standard punishment mechanisms. The findings may be useful for understanding the effects of network fluidity or endogenous partner choice, but not for predicting efficiency changes from adding explicit punishment to a fixed-group PGG.

- source: Jiang, L. L., Chen, Z., Perc, M., Wang, Z., Kurths, J., & Moreno, Y. (2023). Deterrence through punishment can resolve collective risk dilemmas in carbon emission games. *Chaos*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment simulating a collective-risk public goods game (carbon emission game), costly punishment of free-riders (with fines imposed probabilistically and costs shared by all group members) increases the probability that groups reach the collective target, but only when the punishment risk is high (p > 0.8). At low punishment risk, deterrence is weak, and group failure rates remain high. Larger groups are less likely to succeed at any given punishment risk, requiring higher punishment risk for similar deterrence. The introduction of punishment shifts most players to fair-share contributions, reducing both free-riding and maximal altruism. The effect of punishment on group success (a proxy for efficiency) is robust to different loss probabilities. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the probability of reaching the target and average investment are closely related and show a positive effect of punishment when sufficiently strong.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in collective-risk public goods games with a threshold target and probabilistic costly punishment, enabling punishment increases group success rates (and thus efficiency) only when the punishment risk is high. The effect is moderated by group size (larger groups require higher punishment risk) and is robust to different loss probabilities. For prediction, the presence and strength of punishment (punishment risk, fine size, cost structure), group size, and the absence of communication are key moderators. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low group success rates, especially as group size increases. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is strong and credible, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or infrequent.

- source: Noussair, C. N., van Soest, D., & Stoop, J. (2015). Punishment, reward, and cooperation in a framed field experiment. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing punishment or reward opportunities does not increase cooperation compared to baseline: average catch and effort are statistically indistinguishable across treatments. Punishment is directed at non-cooperators, but does not induce greater cooperation in subsequent periods. Reward is used more frequently than punishment, but is not targeted based on recipient cooperation. The results suggest that, in this field setting, peer punishment and reward are ineffective at increasing cooperation, in contrast to many laboratory PGG studies. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported, but the behavioral proxies (catch and effort) do not improve with punishment or reward.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a field setting with a common-pool resource framed as a social dilemma, enabling peer punishment (and reward) does not increase cooperation or its behavioral proxies, compared to a baseline with no sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency observed in many lab PGGs may not generalize to field environments with similar incentive structures but different context, framing, or real-world constraints. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited; it is most informative as a caution about generalizability and as evidence that punishment may not always improve group outcomes, even when the game structure is similar to a PGG.

- source: Becchetti, L., Pelligra, V., & Salustri, F. (2018). The impact of redistribution mechanisms in the vote with the wallet game: experimental results. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The introduction of an ex post redistribution mechanism (taxing defectors and subsidizing cooperators) in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game significantly increases the share of cooperative choices compared to the baseline. In the baseline, cooperation decays over time, but with redistribution, cooperation is stabilized at a higher level. Framing the responsible product as socially beneficial also increases cooperation, but the effect of redistribution is robust across frames. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the effect on efficiency cannot be directly determined, but the increase in cooperation rates suggests likely higher group payoffs under redistribution.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that a centralized, ex post redistribution mechanism (akin to a tax/subsidy rather than peer punishment) can substantially increase cooperation rates in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game with 10 players and no communication. While efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the structure of the payoffs and the increase in cooperation rates imply that group efficiency would also increase under the redistribution mechanism. The findings are most directly relevant for predicting the effect of centralized, automatic sanction/reward mechanisms (not peer punishment) in all-or-nothing, no-communication, 10-player repeated games with known rounds and MPCR of 0.3. The results are less directly applicable to standard peer punishment settings, but suggest that institutionalized redistribution can be highly effective at sustaining cooperation.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

The determinants of monetary punishment/reward are primarily payoff-based (inequity aversion), while non-monetary approval/disapproval is more strongly driven by reciprocity and norm-based considerations. - source: Fabbri, M., Barbieri, P. N., & Bigoni, M. (2019). Ride Your Luck! A Field Experiment on Lottery-Based Incentives for Compliance. *Management Science*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: A field experiment on city buses in Italy tested a lottery-based reward for buying tickets, layered on top of existing probabilistic sanctions for fare-dodging.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides field evidence that adding a lottery-based reward to an environment with probabilistic sanctions increases compliance and revenue (a proxy for efficiency), but the net efficiency gain depends on the cost of the reward and whether all prizes are claimed. For prediction, the presence of both probabilistic sanctions and a reward mechanism (lottery) increases efficiency relative to sanctions alone, but the magnitude of the gain may be modest and sensitive to the cost structure and salience of the reward. The design is not a standard PGG, but the compliance/public good context and outcome measures are closely related. The findings are most informative for settings where compliance is individually observable and can be incentivized with both sanctions and probabilistic rewards.
