- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

  findings: The paper finds that tax-based punishment can sustain cooperation and increase group efficiency in a public goods game, provided the tax is sufficient to offset the cost of punishment. There is a critical threshold for the synergy factor (mpcr) and punishment fine, above which contributing strategies dominate and group efficiency is maximized. The model also shows that, while tax-based punishment and reward can achieve the same maximum cooperation level, the average payoff (efficiency) can be higher under tax-based punishment when punishment/reward parameters are modest relative to the returns from cooperation. The results are most applicable to infinite, well-mixed populations with compulsory tax and continuous contribution choices. The explicit conditions and payoff formulas can be used to predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions, especially for settings with tax-based punishment. The findings also clarify that, while cooperation rate and efficiency can be aligned, the average payoff (efficiency) is the key outcome, and it can be higher for tax-based punishment than for tax-based reward under certain parameter regimes.

- source: Sun, Z. B., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2023). State-Dependent Optimal Incentive Allocation Protocols for Cooperation in Public Goods Games on Regular Networks. *Ieee Transactions on Network Science and Engineering*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in structured populations (regular networks), the optimal allocation of a fixed institutional incentive budget between reward and punishment depends critically on the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment. When punishment is more efficient, pure punishment is optimal for maximizing cooperation and minimizing the payoff advantage of defectors. When reward is more efficient, pure reward is optimal. For intermediate efficiency ratios, an adaptive hybrid protocol—rewarding when cooperation is low, punishing when it is high—is optimal. These results are derived analytically and verified numerically. The findings differ from well-mixed populations, where the adaptive hybrid protocol is always optimal regardless of efficiency ratio. The main outcome is the expected payoff difference between defectors and cooperators, which is minimized under the optimal protocol, leading to higher group efficiency and cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games with institutional incentives on regular networks. The key moderator is the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment: if punishment is more efficient, enabling punishment (with a fixed incentive budget) increases efficiency and cooperation more than reward; if reward is more efficient, the reverse holds. For intermediate ratios, a state-dependent switch is optimal. The results are most applicable to games with institutional (not peer) incentives, fixed budgets, and structured (not well-mixed) populations. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (vs. reward or hybrid) given knowledge of the incentive budget, the efficiency of each incentive, and the network structure.

  findings: The paper finds that mercenary punishment (punishers who do not contribute but punish defectors) can promote cooperation and increase group efficiency in structured populations, even without an additional tax, provided the punishment cost is not too high and the fine is sufficiently large. Introducing a tax to support punishers expands the parameter space where defectors are eliminated and efficiency is high, but only if the punishment cost is significant. If the fine is too low, the tax can actually harm cooperators and allow defectors to coexist with punishers, reducing efficiency. There is an optimal punishment cost that maximizes average payoff. The model also reveals regions of cyclic dominance among strategies, leading to complex spatial dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment cost, punishment effectiveness (fine), and the presence of a tax to support punishment interact to determine group efficiency in a spatial public goods game. For prediction, it suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment) if the punishment cost is moderate and the fine is high, especially if a tax is used to support punishers when costs are high. However, if punishment is too costly or the fine is too low, efficiency may not improve or may even decrease. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with three strategies (C, D, P), and the results are sensitive to the exact parameter values of punishment cost, fine, and tax. The paper does not provide empirical data but offers detailed phase diagrams and payoff analyses that can inform predictions about the efficiency impact of punishment under varying game design dimensions.

  findings: The paper shows that in public goods games with peer punishment, introducing state-based feedback (where the multiplication factor increases with the number of contributors) significantly increases the efficiency of the system by making full-cooperation equilibria more likely and more stable. Local state-feedback (where the multiplication factor depends on the fraction of contributors in the group) is especially effective, sometimes enabling the system to reach and maintain full cooperation even when the baseline multiplication factor is not high. The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to changes in punishment cost and fine, and state-based feedback can partially mitigate the second-order free-rider problem. The main outcome is the proportion of the state space (attraction basin) leading to efficient, full-contribution equilibria, which increases with both punishment and state-based feedback.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public goods games with peer punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (group payoff) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is enhanced by state-based feedback. The effect is robust to variations in punishment cost and fine, and is stronger when the feedback is local rather than global. For prediction, the presence of punishment, lower punishment cost, higher punishment effectiveness, and the presence of local state-feedback all predict higher efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

The transition between these regimes is governed by key parameters such as the synergy factor (r), the unit cost of punishment (delta), and the feedback mechanism parameters. High synergy and low punishment cost favor efficient, cooperative equilibria, while low synergy and high punishment cost favor inefficient, defective equilibria. The model provides explicit payoff equations and stability analysis for each regime.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the synergy factor (mpcr), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the feedback structure linking cooperation and punishment probability. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient (low cooperation), enabling punishment can, under favorable parameter settings (high mpcr, low punishment cost), shift the system to high efficiency. However, if punishment is too costly or the synergy is too low, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The presence of bistability means that initial conditions (e.g., initial cooperation rate) can also determine the outcome. Thus, for prediction, the model suggests that the effect of punishment is not universally positive and depends on the interplay of these design dimensions.

- source: Gao, S. P., & Li, N. (2023). Preference reversal and the evolution of cooperation. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.
