- source: Kocher, M. G., & Matzat, D. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that, although punishment increases contributions more than reward or the standard VCM, it reduces group efficiency because the costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. The reward institution, despite a lower maximum leverage, is both the most preferred and the most efficient, yielding the highest average profits. The standard VCM yields intermediate efficiency. The effectiveness (leverage) of both punishment and reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency. There is no significant difference in efficiency or institutional choice between partner and stranger matching. Gender differences exist in preferences for institutions, but not in efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency (average group profit) compared to both the control (standard VCM) and the reward institution. The efficiency loss is due to the cost of punishment actions. The reward institution, even with lower maximum leverage, is more efficient than both punishment and the standard VCM. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency, unless the punishment technology is extremely effective and/or punishment is used sparingly. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across partner and stranger matching, and is not moderated by self-selection or communication (which is absent). The paper also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment or reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency.

- source: Page, T., Putterman, L., & Garcia, B. (2013). Voluntary contributions with redistribution: The effect of costly sanctions when one person's punishment is another's reward. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the authors compare standard costly punishment (where fines are burned) to a redistributive punishment treatment (where fines are transferred to other group members as rewards). Both treatments use the same marginal per capita return (MPCR = 0.4), punishment cost (0.25 per unit), and group structure. The redistributive punishment treatment leads to significantly higher average contributions (9.03 vs. 7.09), higher average earnings (15.18 vs. 12.88), and higher efficiency, even after controlling for the resource-conserving effect of redistribution (earnings net of transfers: 14.20 vs. 12.88). The effect on efficiency is marginally statistically significant after netting out transfers. Redistributive punishment also reduces the share of punishment targeted at high contributors and increases the monetary return to contributing (from negative to positive). The study provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism can moderate the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no communication, and fixed partners, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency due to the costliness and mis-targeting of punishment. However, if the punishment mechanism is modified so that fines are redistributed as rewards to other group members (rather than burned), both contributions and efficiency increase, and the return to contributing becomes positive. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the structure of the punishment mechanism (specifically, whether punishment is redistributive or not) is a key moderator of the efficiency effect. The control (punishment-off) efficiency is not directly reported, but the paper references prior work showing that standard punishment does not increase efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, while redistributive punishment does. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the nature of the punishment and reward mechanisms.

- source: Reif, C., Rübbelke, D., & Löschel, A. (2017). Improving Voluntary Public Good Provision Through a Non-governmental, Endogenous Matching Mechanism: Experimental Evidence. *Environmental & Resource Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that subjects are willing to pay to establish a norm enforcement mechanism that punishes or rewards deviations from the group average contribution. With the mechanism, average group contributions to the public good are substantially higher than in the baseline, and average earnings are also higher. The mechanism is almost always implemented, and its strength (enforcement parameter) is positively associated with higher public good contributions. Over time, groups learn to reduce over-investment in the mechanism. The mechanism treatment results in more stable and higher efficiency (measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling an endogenous, average-oriented punishment/reward mechanism in a repeated public good game with three players and ten rounds increases both group efficiency and average earnings compared to a control without such a mechanism. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency improvement is robust. The findings support predicting a more positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is endogenously funded and targets deviations from the group average. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed groups with repeated interaction and endogenous institution formation.

- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Godoy, S., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2016). An individualistic approach to institution formation in public good games. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with four players and 20 rounds, the study compares four treatments: (1) standard VCM (no punishment), (2) exogenous universal punishment (StdPun), (3) endogenous punishment with zero cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP0), and (4) endogenous punishment with a small or large cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5). When the right to punish is costless (CTP0), participation is high enough that group contributions and efficiency are as high as in the exogenous punishment treatment (StdPun), and both are significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline (VCM). When there is any cost to acquiring the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5), participation drops, antisocial punishment increases, and group contributions and efficiency fall to levels not significantly different from the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the size of the cost: even a negligible cost eliminates the efficiency gains from punishment. The number of persistent punishers is a key moderator: at least two are needed for punishment to be effective. The study provides direct evidence that the institutional details of how punishment rights are provided (costless vs. costly, endogenous vs. exogenous) are critical for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend critically on whether the right to punish is costless and universally available, or must be acquired at a cost. When punishment is exogenously provided or costlessly available to all, efficiency increases substantially relative to the no-punishment baseline. When even a small cost is required to acquire the right to punish, participation drops, antisocial punishment rises, and efficiency gains disappear. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence and cost of acquiring punishment rights are key moderators: if the game design includes a cost to acquire punishment rights, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. If punishment is costless and universally available, expect large efficiency gains. The number of persistent punishers is also important: at least two are needed for effectiveness in this four-player design.

- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y. (2013). Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy. *Economic Record*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (b) In larger groups (n=8, 12), PPID yields the highest efficiency (80.60% and 78.05%), followed by AP (76.10% and 61.35%), with VCM lowest (68.60% and 63.30%). (c) In large groups, AP can reduce efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on group size and punishment institution. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A. (2016). Non-binding agreements in public goods experiments. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Punishment is used less frequently and more effectively in these settings, with little anti-social punishment. Punishment alone (without agreement) or with second-best agreements increases contributions but not payoffs, as punishment costs offset the gains. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

- source: Grieco, D., Faillo, M., & Zarri, L. (2017). Enforcing cooperation in public goods games: Is one punisher enough?. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: Dorrough, A., Glöckner, A., & Lee, B. (2017). Race for Power in Public Good Games with Unequal, Unstable Punishment Power. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Kingsley, D. C., & Brown, T. C. (2016). Endogenous and costly institutional deterrence in a public good experiment. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a linear public goods game with a central authority that can monitor and punish free-riders, increasing the probability of monitoring (deterrence) increases contributions up to a point, but the benefit to group earnings depends on the cost of monitoring. When monitoring is cheap or moderate, higher deterrence increases both contributions and efficiency (group earnings as a share of the social optimum). When monitoring is expensive, the cost can outweigh the benefit, and efficiency does not improve or may decline. Allowing groups to endogenously select the level of monitoring does not increase contributions or efficiency at non-deterrent (low monitoring) levels, contrary to some previous literature. In fact, endogenous selection of non-deterrent institutions sometimes reduces contributions. The relationship between monitoring, punishment, and efficiency is thus strongly moderated by the costliness of institutional enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment-enabled institutions on efficiency in public goods games, especially when the punishment is implemented via a central authority with costly monitoring. The results show that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on both the probability of monitoring (deterrence) and the cost of monitoring. At low and moderate costs, enabling punishment increases efficiency, but at high costs, the efficiency gain is negated or reversed. The endogenous selection of non-deterrent institutions does not provide an efficiency boost. For prediction, the key moderators are the cost of monitoring (punishment_cost), the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment_tech), and whether the institution is deterrent (i.e., the expected cost of free-riding exceeds the benefit). Control efficiency (without punishment) is much lower than with effective, affordable punishment, but high punishment costs can erase the efficiency gain.
