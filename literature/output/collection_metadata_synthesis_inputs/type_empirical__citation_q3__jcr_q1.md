- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Grechenig, K., & Thöni, C. (2016). Information-sensitive Leviathans. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend strongly on the information environment and the type of punishment institution. Under perfect information, decentralized punishment leads to the highest contributions and profits (efficiency), outperforming centralized punishment and no punishment. As information becomes less accurate (signals about others' contributions are noisy), both contributions and profits decline in all institutions, but especially in DecPun. Under moderate noise, CenPun can attract more citizens and sometimes matches or slightly exceeds DecPun in efficiency, provided the authority avoids punishing cooperators. Under high noise, neither punishment institution yields substantial efficiency gains over no punishment. The key moderator is the extent to which punishment is misapplied to cooperators, which is more likely in DecPun under noise. The paper provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes with punishment enabled versus disabled, across different institutional and informational settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the information environment and the punishment institution's design. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially decentralized) can substantially increase efficiency under perfect information. However, as information becomes noisy, the efficiency gain from punishment is reduced, and the difference between decentralized and centralized punishment narrows. The presence of a central authority who avoids punishing cooperators can maintain or improve efficiency under moderate noise, but not under high noise. For prediction, the paper suggests that the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost/tech, and especially the information structure (accuracy of contribution signals) are critical moderators. The results also imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on these dimensions.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2016). Judicial error and cooperation. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that error-free formal sanctions increase both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public goods game. When judicial errors are introduced (either type I or type II), both cooperation and efficiency decline. The effect on efficiency is more negative for type I errors than for type II errors, due to the wasteful punishment of cooperators. The negative effect of errors is stronger when the error probability is higher and when errors convert a deterrent sanction regime into a non-deterrent one. The effects of type I and type II errors on contributions are symmetric, but type I errors reduce group earnings more. Subjects show a clear preference for avoiding type I errors, but this preference is fully explained by income maximization rather than additional psychological aversion.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the introduction of punishment with judicial error reduces efficiency in public goods games, and that the magnitude of the efficiency loss depends on the type and probability of error, as well as the deterrence strength of the sanction regime. For prediction, the findings imply that enabling punishment increases efficiency only if sanctions are deterrent and error-free; otherwise, efficiency may decrease, especially with type I errors or high error probability. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the probability and type of judicial error, and whether sanctions are deterrent. Control efficiency (without punishment) is also reported, allowing for direct comparison.

Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

- source: Kube, S., Schaube, S., Schildberg-Hörisch, H., & Khachatryan, E. (2015). Institution formation and cooperation with heterogeneous agents. *European Economic Review*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with three players, the introduction of an institution formation stage (via unanimity voting) that, if implemented, imposes binding contribution obligations enforced by deterrent sanctions, leads to large increases in efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the maximum possible). For homogeneous groups, a symmetric institution (equal obligations) is almost always implemented and achieves efficiency above 90%. For heterogeneous groups, a symmetric institution is often rejected by low-benefit players due to inequality aversion, but an asymmetric institution (with obligations tailored to equalize payoffs) is much more likely to be implemented, though at a cost to total efficiency. In all cases, efficiency is higher when the institution is implemented than when it is not. The main moderators are the distribution of marginal per-capita returns (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) and the symmetry of institutional obligations. The study does not manipulate punishment cost or technology directly, as the institution, if implemented, is enforced with sufficiently strong sanctions to ensure compliance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with three players and 20 rounds, the introduction of a binding, deterrent-sanction institution (enabled by unanimity voting) increases efficiency relative to the baseline game without such an institution. The effect is strongest when the institution is both efficient and equitable (i.e., symmetric for homogeneous groups, asymmetric for heterogeneous groups). When the institution is rejected (often due to inequality aversion), efficiency is lower, but still generally higher than in the baseline. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, binding punishment institution (with high compliance) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on group heterogeneity and the design of institutional obligations. The study does not provide evidence on the effect of varying punishment cost or technology, as these are not manipulated; the punishment regime is always fully deterrent. Control efficiency (without institution) is reported and can be used as a baseline for prediction.

  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with centralized sanctions triggered by audits, the study manipulates the frequency and regularity of audits (continuous vs. intermittent) and the severity of sanctions. Under ambiguity (unknown audit probability), intermittent audits with high fines (but lower frequency) produce similar or higher contributions and significantly higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audits with low fines. High frequency-high severity (continuous audits with high fines) also sustains cooperation and efficiency, but at higher cost. Reducing audit frequency or sanction severity too much undermines these effects. The results suggest that, for a given expected cost of sanction, intermittent audits with high severity are more efficient than continuous audits with low severity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 3-player, 50-round linear PGG with centralized punishment, intermittent (irregular) audit schemes with high severity (punishment effectiveness) and sufficient frequency lead to higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audit schemes with lower severity, especially after audits are withdrawn. The effect is robust as long as audit frequency and severity are not too low. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency and other game dimensions constant, enabling punishment via intermittent, ambiguous audits with high severity will increase efficiency more than continuous, predictable audits with low severity. The paper also shows that both audit frequency and punishment effectiveness (severity) are key moderators: too few audits or too low severity reduces the efficiency benefit. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with centralized punishment.
