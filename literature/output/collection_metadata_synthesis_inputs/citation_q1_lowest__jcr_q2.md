- source: Zhang, Z. Y., Elvis, N. T., Wang, J. W., & Hou, G. L. (2024). Can Leading by Example Alone Improve Cooperation?. *Behavioral Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a 4-player, 32-round linear PGG with a 0.4 MPCR and no communication, enabling leader-administered punishment (with a 1:3 cost/impact ratio) increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to both a control (no leader, no punishment) and a leader-only (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

- source: JARUNGRATTANAPONG, R. (2022). PROMOTING COOPERATION IN A PUBLIC GOOD EXPERIMENT THROUGH OSTRACISM AS A PUNISHMENT MECHANISM. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that allowing group members to ostracize (temporarily exclude) others via majority vote in a repeated public goods game increases average contributions by about 45 percentage points and increases average earnings (welfare) by about 23% compared to the control (no punishment) game. The effect is strongest for free-riders but is present for all contributor types. Ostracism is implemented with a small direct cost (1% of endowment per vote), but the main cost is the reduction in group size and thus potential public good returns. Anti-social punishment is observed: non-cooperators who are punished tend to retaliate by voting against others, but this is not seen among high contributors. The positive effect on efficiency is robust, but cooperation and efficiency decline when ostracism is removed (end-game effect).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling ostracism (a form of social punishment) in a repeated public goods game with 4 players, 6 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.625 increases both contribution rates and average group earnings (efficiency) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is large and robust across contributor types. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar lab PGGs. The main caveat is the presence of anti-social punishment and the collapse of cooperation when punishment is removed, suggesting that the positive effect is contingent on the continued availability of the punishment mechanism.

- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Peng, H. C., & Fan, Y. H. (2023). Incomplete punishment networks, heterogeneity, and cooperation in public good experiments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the structure of the punishment network strongly moderates the effect of peer punishment on both cooperation and efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group payoffs (efficiency) and higher contributions than the complete punishment network. The complete network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower efficiency, lower contributions, and lower punishment severity, likely due to diffusion of responsibility (bystander effect). The effect of punishment is thus not monotonic in network density: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency. The type of MPCR (high or low) also affects individual contributions, with high-MPCR individuals contributing more. The punishment mechanism is costly and binary (1 token cost, 3 token impact), and the experiment uses stranger matching over 15 rounds. No communication or chat is allowed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment network is crucial for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, especially with heterogeneous returns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. When predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, it is not sufficient to know only the presence of punishment; the network structure (complete vs. incomplete, and the type of incomplete network) must be considered. In this design, incomplete networks (circle and homo-pairwise) outperform the complete network in both contributions and efficiency. The findings also show that punishment severity is lower in the complete network, likely due to a bystander effect. The experiment uses a four-player, 15-round, linear PGG with binary punishment, no chat, and random reassignment of MPCRs and groupings each round. These details should be matched when using this evidence for prediction. The results are most relevant for settings with heterogeneous returns and similar punishment mechanics.

- source: Gürdal, M. Y., Gürerk, & Yahsi, M. (2021). Culture and prevalence of sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.

  findings: In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with five players and MPCR 0.4, the introduction of punishment mechanisms (either formal, with a fixed administrative cost and group-voted sanction rate, or informal, with peer-to-peer costly punishment) leads to large and significant increases in group efficiency and payoffs. Without punishment, free riding is prevalent and efficiency is low. With punishment enabled, average group payoffs rise significantly, and a much higher proportion of groups achieve high payoff thresholds. Informal punishment also increases efficiency, though not as much as formal deterrent punishment.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard repeated public goods game. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward, etc.) are all clearly specified and match the canonical PGG setup. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while enabling punishment (either formal or informal) produces large, robust increases in efficiency and group payoff. The results are robust to manipulations of self-regulatory resources and to whether the punishment institution is chosen endogenously or imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, the findings support a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, with formal deterrent punishment achieving near-full efficiency and informal punishment producing substantial, though somewhat lower, efficiency gains.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

  findings: The experiment compared three public good game variants: (1) standard, (2) threshold (group must reach a minimum contribution or all is lost), and (3) monitor (one randomly chosen, anonymous group member can punish others at a cost). Both the threshold and monitor games increased individual contributions compared to the standard game, with the threshold game increasing contributions the most. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to order effects and individual characteristics.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a five-player, one-shot public good game with continuous contributions, no communication, and a known MPCR of 0.4. The monitor (punishment) treatment increased group efficiency/payoff compared to the control. The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

- source: Glöckner, A., Kube, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2018). The joint benefits of observed and unobserved social sanctions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players over ten rounds, the authors compare four conditions: no punishment, observed punishment only, unobserved (delayed) punishment only, and both punishment channels available. Both observed and unobserved punishment alone increase contributions but do not improve efficiency (average payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline, due to the costliness of punishment. However, when both punishment channels are available, group efficiency is significantly higher than in all other conditions. This is because the threat of unobserved punishment increases the effectiveness of observed punishment, leading to higher cooperation with less punishment expenditure. The combination of punishment channels thus overcomes the typical efficiency losses seen in standard lab PGG punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the punishment institution. Standard punishment (observed or unobserved alone) does not improve efficiency over the control, but the combination of both channels leads to a large positive effect. For prediction, this means that the presence of multiple punishment channels (observed and unobserved) is a key moderator: if only one is present, efficiency is not improved; if both are present, efficiency is substantially increased. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the punishment technology (timing/observability), cost, and whether multiple channels are available. Control efficiency (no punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless the institutional details of punishment are considered.

- source: Botelho, A., Harrison, G. W., Pinto, L. M. C., Ross, D., & Rutström, E. E. (2022). Endogenous choice of institutional punishment mechanisms to promote social cooperation. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study replicates and extends classic public goods experiments by allowing subjects to experience both a punishment-enabled and a no-punishment institution, then vote on which to use for a high-stakes final round. Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. Only one session (out of 13) saw a majority vote for the punishment institution, and this was in a small cohort with high returns and high initial cooperation. Statistical analysis shows that subjects' votes are overwhelmingly determined by which institution gave them higher profits, not by which supported higher cooperation. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in matching protocol, order of experience, and marginal per-capita return. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard public goods games with costly voluntary punishment, enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, even though it increases contributions. The effect is robust across a range of game parameters (player count, MPCR, matching protocol, order, and returns). Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.

  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.
