- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Yang, X. Q., Zhang, F., Wang, W. X., Zhang, D., Shi, Z. H., & Zhou, S. W. (2020). Effect of habitat destruction on cooperation in public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a weak, automatic punishment institution (where the lowest contributor in a group loses 1 yuan per round) does not significantly increase contributions compared to a control PGG or to a reward institution. There is no significant effect of matching or mismatching subjects' institutional preferences (punishment vs reward) with the institution they are assigned to. Subjects who prefer punishment tend to be lower contributors and have social preferences associated with anti-social or efficiency-reducing profiles. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff as a main outcome, and the punishment institution is designed to be strictly efficiency-reducing at the group level.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated linear PGG with weak, automatic, centralized punishment (punishment cost = 1, punishment tech = 1, imposed on lowest contributor), enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency compared to a control. The punishment is non-deterrent and strictly efficiency-reducing by design. Therefore, in similar game designs (weak, automatic, centralized punishment), one should not expect punishment to improve efficiency over the control. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is indirect and based on contribution rates and the institutional design. The findings are most relevant for settings with weak, automatic, non-peer punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment or stronger punishment regimes.

- source: Grund, C., Harbring, C., Thommes, K., & Tilkes, K. R. (2020). Decisions on Extending Group Membership-Evidence from a Public Good Experiment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that temporary group members contribute marginally more when the possibility of group membership extension is decided endogenously by a group member, compared to an exogenous random draw. However, this effect is weak and does not translate into significantly higher overall group contributions. Permanent members' contributions are not significantly affected by the extension mechanism. When a temporary member is replaced (especially exogenously), the newcomer initially contributes more than other group members, but this effect diminishes quickly. The study discusses monetary incentives and payoffs, but the main focus is on contribution behavior rather than efficiency or group payoff. There is no direct analysis of how the extension or replacement mechanisms affect group efficiency relative to the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that mechanisms resembling social sanctions (replacement or exclusion) can have incentive effects on contribution behavior, but these effects are modest and short-lived. The design does not include explicit costly punishment or reward mechanisms, and efficiency is not directly measured. For prediction, the results suggest that changing the group membership mechanism (endogenous vs. exogenous extension) has limited impact on overall group efficiency, and that replacement (as a form of exclusion) may temporarily boost newcomer contributions. However, these effects are primarily on contribution rates, not on efficiency or total group payoff. The study is most informative for understanding how exclusion or replacement (as a weak form of sanction) affects cooperation, rather than for predicting the efficiency impact of explicit punishment mechanisms.

- source: Sun, X. P., Li, Y. B., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., Peng, J., Wang, H. Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2020). Co-Evolution of Complex Network Public Goods Game under the Edges Rules. *Entropy*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that allowing players to break unsatisfactory connections and form new ones (edge rules) in a public goods game on a network leads to higher and more stable cooperation, especially at low gain factors (r=2). Both simulation and a 30-person lab experiment show that the fraction of cooperators increases and stabilizes under these rules, compared to a static network. The mechanism is interpreted as self-organized punishment (breaking ties with defectors) and reward (forming ties with cooperators), but there is no explicit costly punishment. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators; efficiency or group payoff is not reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that endogenous network adaptation (breaking and forming ties based on satisfaction) can promote cooperation in public goods games, even at low MPCR. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of explicit punishment on efficiency. The 'punishment' here is not a costly sanction but the breaking of ties, which is structurally different from standard punishment mechanisms. The findings may be useful for understanding the effects of network fluidity or endogenous partner choice, but not for predicting efficiency changes from adding explicit punishment to a fixed-group PGG.

- source: Ren, T. Y., & Zheng, J. J. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics in the spatial public goods game with tolerance-based expulsion and cooperation. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that in a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with four players and 20 rounds per part, groups that start with unequal endowments (two rich, two poor) and then experience redistribution to equality do not increase their group contributions or efficiency after redistribution. Group contributions in the post-redistribution phase are indistinguishable from those in groups that remain unequal, and both are significantly lower than in always-equal groups. The experience of initial inequality creates a persistent negative spillover, locking groups into low cooperation and efficiency even after equality is restored. The mechanism is traced to the former poor mimicking the low-contribution behavior of the former rich after redistribution. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms, simply reducing inequality via redistribution does not increase efficiency in public goods games if groups have previously experienced inequality. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, for games with similar design (no punishment, no reward, linear PGG, 4 players, 20+20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, no identification, no summary of individual contributions), the efficiency in the treatment with redistribution will not exceed that of the control with persistent inequality, and both will be lower than in always-equal groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGGs without punishment or reward, but do not inform about the effect of punishment.

- source: Nax, H. H., Balietti, S., Murphy, R. O., & Helbing, D. (2018). Adding noise to the institution: an experimental welfare investigation of the contribution-based grouping mechanism. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that, in a repeated public goods game with 16 players (4 groups of 4), introducing a contribution-based grouping mechanism (meritocratic matching) with low or moderate noise leads to much higher efficiency (average total payoff) than random matching (no meritocracy). The efficiency gains are robust across different levels of noise, with the highest efficiency observed under perfect meritocracy (no noise), but even substantial gains at moderate noise levels. The control (no meritocracy, i.e., random matching) produces the lowest efficiency, consistent with standard PGG decay. The study also finds that increased meritocracy reduces payoff inequality, contrary to theoretical expectations. The results are robust to different measures of inequality and are observed both in lab and online settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with grouping based on contributions, increasing the precision of the grouping mechanism (i.e., reducing noise in meritocratic matching) leads to large increases in efficiency relative to random matching. For prediction tasks, the presence of a contribution-based grouping mechanism (even if noisy) should be expected to substantially increase efficiency compared to standard PGG controls. The effect is robust across a range of noise levels, and the efficiency gains are not offset by increased inequality. However, the study does not involve punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so its direct applicability to punishment-enabled games is limited. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of sorting/grouping mechanisms, not punishment.

- source: Kanitsar, G. (2021). Self-Governance in Generalized Exchange. A Laboratory Experiment on the Structural Embeddedness of Peer Punishment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Peer punishment increases cooperation and group earnings (efficiency) in public good structures only when punishment is costless; costly punishment does not improve efficiency. In generalized exchange, peer punishment (both costly and costless) does not increase cooperation or group earnings. The effectiveness of punishment is thus highly contingent on the network structure: dense sanctioning networks (public good) allow punishment to deter defection and improve efficiency, while sparse networks (generalized exchange) do not. The cost-impact ratio of punishment is important, but its effect is moderated by the structural environment. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from peer punishment in public goods do not transfer to generalized exchange settings.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the network structure. In standard public good games, costless punishment can substantially increase efficiency, but costly punishment may not. In generalized exchange (circular, sparse punishment network), enabling punishment—even if costless—does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, it is crucial to account for the density of the punishment network and whether the game is a standard public good or a generalized exchange. Control efficiency is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency when moving from public good to generalized exchange structures.

- source: Liao, Y. L., Zhang, L., Lei, S. Y., Song, M. Z., Deng, W. K., & Hu, D. F. (2021). RETRACTED: Third-Party Punishment Mechanism and Corporate Cooperation in Environmental Investment: Experiments on Public Goods Game (Retracted Article). *Discrete Dynamics in Nature and Society*.

  findings: This paper experimentally compares a threshold public goods game with and without third-party punishment (TPP), using groups of three participants (framed as enterprises) making resource investment decisions toward a group threshold. The TPP mechanism automatically punishes group members whose investment rate is below the group mean. The presence of TPP leads to higher mean resource investment amounts, higher individual and group investment rates, and a substantially higher success rate of cooperation (from 55.57% to 76.85%). Regression analysis confirms that TPP has a significant positive effect on investment behavior, even after controlling for individual characteristics and measured preferences for interaction and equity (IEP).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a third-party punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods game increases group investment and the probability of achieving the public good. For prediction tasks, the presence of TPP (punishment enabled) should be expected to yield higher efficiency or group payoff than the control (punishment disabled), especially in small groups (n=3) with a threshold structure and automatic, impersonal punishment. The results are most directly applicable to threshold PGGs with automatic third-party punishment and no communication. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency-related outcomes under these design conditions.

While efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the structure of the payoffs and the increase in cooperation rates imply that group efficiency would also increase under the redistribution mechanism. The findings are most directly relevant for predicting the effect of centralized, automatic sanction/reward mechanisms (not peer punishment) in all-or-nothing, no-communication, 10-player repeated games with known rounds and MPCR of 0.3. - source: Zhao, Y. K., Zhao, X. B., & Shen, Z. J. M. (2018). The hot-versus-cold effect in a punishment game: a multi-round experimental study. *Annals of Operations Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment compares two elicitation methods (strategy vs. direct-response) in a multi-round, two-player punishment game. The strategy method leads to significantly higher punishment rates (84% vs. 62%) and more frequent cooperative sender actions (S1: 68% vs. 44%). Combined payoffs (group welfare) are higher for S1 actions, and the higher frequency of S1 under the strategy method results in higher average group payoffs. Regression and learning model analyses show that the difference in punishment rates is due to mental accounting: the strategy method allows receivers to integrate the cost and expected benefit of punishment, increasing its perceived utility. Over repeated rounds, senders learn to avoid punishment by choosing S1 more often, further increasing group welfare. Reward rates are low and do not differ between methods. The findings suggest that, in repeated games, the strategic value of punishment (deterrence) is more important than the immediate emotional satisfaction of punishing, and that the design of the punishment mechanism (including decision timing) can significantly affect cooperation and group outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated two-player punishment games, higher punishment rates (enabled by the strategy method) lead to more cooperative sender behavior and higher group payoffs. While the game is not a standard public goods game, the structure is adjacent: one player can take a selfish or cooperative action, and the other can punish. The results suggest that, in repeated settings, enabling effective punishment (with high perceived strategic value) increases efficiency by deterring selfish behavior. For prediction tasks, this implies that, all else equal, punishment mechanisms that increase the perceived strategic value of punishment (e.g., via decision timing or repeated interaction) will lead to higher efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low due to frequent selfish actions. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, punishment-enabled games, but the mechanism may generalize to other repeated social dilemmas with punishment.

- source: Di Guida, S., Han, T. A., Kirchsteiger, G., Lenaerts, T., & Zisis, I. (2021). Repeated Interaction and Its Impact on Cooperation and Surplus Allocation-An Experimental Analysis. *Games*.

  findings: The experiment compares three treatments: (1) Baseline (forced cooperation, exogenous re-matching), (2) Re-match (voluntary cooperation, exogenous re-matching), and (3) Endogenous-match (voluntary cooperation, endogenous group formation). Allowing refusal to cooperate (punishment) increases average contributions and receiver earnings, and decreases distributor earnings, relative to forced cooperation. Overall, the possibility of punishment (refusal to cooperate) increases efficiency, but repeated interaction does not further increase average efficiency.
