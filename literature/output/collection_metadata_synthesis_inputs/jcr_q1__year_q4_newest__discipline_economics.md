- source: Jiang, X., & Wang, X. H. (2024). Gender difference in leaders framed with responsibility or authority: An experimental study. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a leader with the power to punish increases group contributions and efficiency compared to the no-leader baseline. When the leader's role is framed as responsibility (rather than authority), and especially when the leader is selected by voting, female leaders are more effective at increasing group income and efficiency than male leaders. The responsibility framing increases both leader contributions and the effectiveness of punishment, but the positive effect on efficiency is robustly observed only in the voting stage for female-led groups. In other conditions (authority framing, male leaders, or self-selected leaders), the efficiency gains from punishment are smaller or not significant. The study also shows that leader contributions are a more important driver of efficiency than punishment per se, as punishment can offset its own benefits through costs. Overall, the presence of punishment increases efficiency, but the magnitude depends on leader gender, framing, and selection method.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a public good game increases efficiency, especially when the leader is female, the leadership role is framed as responsibility, and the leader is selected by voting. The game design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency gains from punishment are: presence of a leader with punishment power, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (4), number of rounds (5), and information structure (no chat, summary feedback). The control (no-leader) efficiency is reported, and the treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency is higher, particularly in the responsibility/voting/female leader condition. For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when the leader is prosocial (as selected or induced by responsibility framing), and that leader contributions are a key mechanism. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with punishment, especially with comparable group size, round number, and punishment parameters.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

The findings are useful for predicting contribution behavior under different cultural backgrounds, but not for direct efficiency prediction as required by the downstream task.

When punishment is enforced with high probability (p=1 or p=0.8), contributions are higher and sustained over time.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the credibility of punishment enforcement is a key moderator of punishment's effect on cooperation in public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the contribution results imply that efficiency gains from punishment are only realized when enforcement is highly credible. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is contingent on the probability of enforcement. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of quantitative predictions.

  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a symbolic scepter for the authority significantly increases citizens' support for the authority's punishment decisions, regardless of the payment scheme (appropriation vs. fixed salary) or information transparency. However, the symbol does not affect the frequency or style of punishment by authorities, nor does it affect citizens' contribution rates or group/individual payoffs. For the downstream prediction task, the presence or absence of a symbolic authority is unlikely to affect the efficiency outcome when punishment is enabled, holding other game design dimensions and control efficiency constant. The main moderators (payment scheme, transparency) do not interact with the symbol to affect efficiency. Thus, symbolic authority should not be expected to improve efficiency in similar lab PGG environments.

The game design dimensions are well specified (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), and the effect of punishment is robustly positive on contributions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect but informative evidence that punishment is likely to increase efficiency in similar settings, especially when baseline cooperation is low due to social factors.

- source: Riehm, T., Fugger, N., Gillen, P., Gretschko, V., & Werner, P. (2022). Social norms, sanctions, and conditional entry in markets with externalities: Evidence from an artefactual field experiment. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that all-can-win intergroup competition (where multiple groups can be ranked first and avoid penalties) leads to large increases in cooperation and group output across all treatments. The effect is strongest when penalties for not being ranked first are sharp (T1, T2), supporting near-perfect cooperation and benefiting a majority of individuals, though not all. In these cases, average earnings are about the same with and without competition, as severe penalties for a few groups offset gains for the majority. In the weaker-penalty treatment (T3), cooperation increases less, and the effect on earnings is neutral. The results suggest that the design of the penalty function (i.e., the sharpness of incentives) is crucial for both cooperation and efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a rank-based, all-can-win intergroup competition mechanism in a standard linear public goods game can substantially increase cooperation and group output, especially when penalties for low-ranked groups are sharp. However, the effect on efficiency (average earnings) is not always positive: while most individuals benefit, severe penalties for a minority can offset group-level gains. For prediction, the sharpness of the penalty function (punishment tech) and the degree of coordination among groups are key moderators. If the control game (no competition) has low efficiency, introducing sharp all-can-win competition is likely to increase group output and may increase average earnings, but the risk of severe losses for some groups must be considered. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments with intergroup competition as a sanctioning mechanism.

For prediction, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism does not guarantee higher efficiency; the cost and structure of the institution are critical. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the cost and type of punishment or enforcement.

  findings: The study finds that communication spillovers between villages (i.e., when participants in one village can talk to those in a neighboring village who have already played) lead to substantial increases in contributions to the public good. The effect is robust to different matching strategies and distance thresholds. In the second round, when some villages played alternative versions of the public goods game (including punishment, reward, or risk), the positive effect of these treatments on contributions was only observed in villages not exposed to communication spillovers. The main implication is that communication between sessions can significantly increase cooperation, potentially biasing estimates of treatment effects in field experiments.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that communication (even unstructured, informal communication between sessions) can increase cooperation in public goods games. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a main treatment, so it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, highlight the importance of controlling for communication and spillovers when interpreting the effects of any treatment (including punishment) in field experiments.

- source: Koessler, A. K., Müller, J., & Zitzelsberger, S. (2023). Asymmetric heterogeneities and the role of transfers in a public goods experiment. *European Economic Review*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with three players (one high-endowment/low-productivity, two low-endowment/high-productivity), enabling voluntary, unconditional transfers before contribution decisions leads to higher group efficiency and average payoffs compared to a baseline with no transfers and a condition with exogenous (non-voluntary) transfers. Most high-endowment players transfer all or half of their endowment, and recipients reciprocate with high contributions, resulting in larger public good provision and higher group profits. The effect is not observed when transfers are exogenously imposed, highlighting the importance of intention-based reciprocity. If the transfer option is available but not used, cooperation and efficiency are lower than in the baseline. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in any treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctions, introducing a voluntary transfer mechanism in a PGG with asymmetric heterogeneity can substantially increase efficiency and group payoffs, provided that transfers are used and reciprocated. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar heterogeneity and transfer options, but do not inform about the effects of punishment or sanctions, as these are not present in the design. The results suggest that mechanisms enabling trust and reciprocity (such as voluntary transfers) can substitute for punishment in boosting efficiency, but the effect is contingent on the use and perception of the transfer. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it is highly informative for understanding the efficiency effects of alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms in heterogeneous PGGs.
