- source: Levy, D. A. (2022). Optimizing the social utility of judicial punishment: An evolutionary biology and neuroscience perspective. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper argues that the human tendency to punish non-cooperators, even at a personal cost, is rooted in evolutionary and neural mechanisms. It reviews evidence from public goods games and other economic experiments showing that punishment can increase cooperation and, in some cases, average group profit. The author discusses the adaptive value of punishment, the neural reward mechanisms involved, and the importance of institutionalizing punishment to maintain social order and cooperation. The paper also highlights complexities and limitations, such as the costliness of punishment, the potential for retaliation, and the need for centralized enforcement in large societies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation in public goods games and, under some conditions, improve group outcomes. It references empirical studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, Gächter et al., 2008) that find punishment can increase average profit, but also notes limitations and boundary conditions (e.g., costliness, retaliation, institutional context). For the prediction task, this paper supports the general mechanism by which punishment can improve efficiency, but does not provide direct quantitative evidence or dimension-specific effect sizes. It is most useful for contextualizing why and when punishment might be effective, rather than for precise prediction.

- source: Miraghaie, A. M., Pouretemad, H., Villa, A. E. P., Mazaheri, M. A., Khosrowabadi, R., & Lintas, A. (2022). Electrophysiological Markers of Fairness and Selfishness Revealed by a Combination of Dictator and Ultimatum Games. *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that distinct neural markers (N1, P2, MFN, LPC) are associated with behavioral profiles of fairness and selfishness in economic games. Selfish participants show neural signatures consistent with spiteful punishment (e.g., rejecting low offers in UG, resulting in zero payoffs for both), while fair participants show markers consistent with costly punishment (e.g., rejecting selfish allocations in DG). However, the paper does not report or analyze group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and does not manipulate or compare the presence/absence of punishment as a treatment. The findings are relevant for understanding the neural basis of punishment and fairness, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The games studied (DG and UG) are adjacent to PGGs but lack the group structure, repeated interaction, and explicit public goods provision. Punishment in UG is endogenous (rejection of unfair offers) and always results in zero payoff for both, but the study does not compare efficiency with and without punishment, nor does it report group-level payoff outcomes. The main value for prediction is in the mechanistic and neural understanding of individual differences in punishment behavior, not in quantitative efficiency effects.

- source: Smith, K. M. M., Mabulla, I. A. A., & Apicella, C. L. L. (2022). Hadza hunter-gatherers with greater exposure to other cultures share more with generous campmates. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that Hadza participants are more likely to share with campmates they rank higher on generosity and, to a lesser extent, foraging ability. Importantly, the preference for generosity is significantly stronger among Hadza with greater exposure to other cultures. The effect of exposure is specific to generosity and does not extend to foraging ability. The results are interpreted as evidence for the flexibility of partner choice psychology, with socioecological context (exposure to other cultures) shaping the traits valued in social partners. The study does not involve punishment, sanctions, or analyze efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanction mechanisms, nor does it report efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes. Its design (anonymous sharing game with individual allocation decisions) is adjacent to public goods games but lacks the core features of contribution to a group project, punishment, or efficiency analysis. Therefore, it should not be used to inform predictions about punishment effects on efficiency in PGG-like settings.
