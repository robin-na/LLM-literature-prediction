- source: Jiang, X., & Wang, X. H. (2024). Gender difference in leaders framed with responsibility or authority: An experimental study. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a leader with the power to punish increases group contributions and efficiency compared to the no-leader baseline. When the leader's role is framed as responsibility (rather than authority), and especially when the leader is selected by voting, female leaders are more effective at increasing group income and efficiency than male leaders. The responsibility framing increases both leader contributions and the effectiveness of punishment, but the positive effect on efficiency is robustly observed only in the voting stage for female-led groups. In other conditions (authority framing, male leaders, or self-selected leaders), the efficiency gains from punishment are smaller or not significant. The study also shows that leader contributions are a more important driver of efficiency than punishment per se, as punishment can offset its own benefits through costs. Overall, the presence of punishment increases efficiency, but the magnitude depends on leader gender, framing, and selection method.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a public good game increases efficiency, especially when the leader is female, the leadership role is framed as responsibility, and the leader is selected by voting. The game design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency gains from punishment are: presence of a leader with punishment power, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (4), number of rounds (5), and information structure (no chat, summary feedback). The control (no-leader) efficiency is reported, and the treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency is higher, particularly in the responsibility/voting/female leader condition. For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when the leader is prosocial (as selected or induced by responsibility framing), and that leader contributions are a key mechanism. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with punishment, especially with comparable group size, round number, and punishment parameters.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

- source: Langenbach, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Inherited Institutions: Cooperation in the Light of Democratic Legitimacy. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot, three-player linear public goods game with or without a centralized, non-deterrent punishment (automatic fine for less-than-full contribution). The institutional setting (punishment or not) is determined either by majority vote (Endo) or random draw (Exo) in the first generation, and this setting is inherited by a matched group in the second generation. In the first generation, contributions are higher when punishment is democratically adopted and lower when it is democratically rejected, compared to random assignment. In the second generation, the positive effect of democratic adoption disappears, but the negative effect of democratic rejection persists (marginally). The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or total earnings as a share of the social optimum, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized, non-deterrent punishment mechanism can increase contributions if it is democratically adopted, but only in the generation that participates in the decision. The effect does not persist to subsequent generations who inherit the institution. The negative effect of democratic rejection may persist. However, since the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, only contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is informative for mapping design to expected contribution behavior, but not for direct efficiency prediction. If contribution is used as a proxy for efficiency (assuming linearity and no punishment cost to the group), the results suggest that democratic adoption of punishment increases efficiency in the first generation, but not in the second.

- source: Nhim, T., Schuch, E., & Richter, A. (2023). Water scarcity and support for costly institutions in public goods: Experimental evidence from Cambodia. *Ecological Economics*.

  findings: The study finds that costly institutions (minimum contribution/tax and strict enforcement with punishment) are broadly supported by participants, especially those with prior experience of water scarcity. Both types of costly institutions increase average contributions to the public good compared to costless regimes. However, only the minimum contribution (tax) regime leads to higher payoffs and welfare, while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but does not improve payoffs due to the cost of enforcement. The efficiency of the group (measured as payoff relative to the social optimum) is thus higher under the tax regime but lower under strict enforcement compared to their respective costless counterparts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing costly punishment or enforcement institutions in public goods games depends on the specific design. Minimum contribution (tax) regimes can increase both contributions and efficiency (payoff), while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but can reduce efficiency due to the cost of enforcement. For prediction, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism does not guarantee higher efficiency; the cost and structure of the institution are critical. Control efficiency (payoff) and the specific institutional design (tax/minimum vs. costly strict punishment) must be considered. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the cost and type of punishment or enforcement.

- source: Coutts, A. (2022). Identifying communication spillovers in lab-in-the-field experiments&x2729;. *Journal of Development Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that communication spillovers between villages (i.e., when participants in one village can talk to those in a neighboring village who have already played) lead to substantial increases in contributions to the public good. The effect is robust to different matching strategies and distance thresholds. In the second round, when some villages played alternative versions of the public goods game (including punishment, reward, or risk), the positive effect of these treatments on contributions was only observed in villages not exposed to communication spillovers. The main implication is that communication between sessions can significantly increase cooperation, potentially biasing estimates of treatment effects in field experiments. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so its direct relevance to predicting efficiency is limited.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that communication (even unstructured, informal communication between sessions) can increase cooperation in public goods games. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that allowing or enabling communication (or failing to prevent spillovers) may increase contributions, which could translate into higher efficiency if all other game parameters are held constant. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, and only analyzes contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The study does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a main treatment, so it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, highlight the importance of controlling for communication and spillovers when interpreting the effects of any treatment (including punishment) in field experiments.

- source: Koessler, A. K., Müller, J., & Zitzelsberger, S. (2023). Asymmetric heterogeneities and the role of transfers in a public goods experiment. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with three players (one high-endowment/low-productivity, two low-endowment/high-productivity), enabling voluntary, unconditional transfers before contribution decisions leads to higher group efficiency and average payoffs compared to a baseline with no transfers and a condition with exogenous (non-voluntary) transfers. Most high-endowment players transfer all or half of their endowment, and recipients reciprocate with high contributions, resulting in larger public good provision and higher group profits. The effect is not observed when transfers are exogenously imposed, highlighting the importance of intention-based reciprocity. If the transfer option is available but not used, cooperation and efficiency are lower than in the baseline. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in any treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctions, introducing a voluntary transfer mechanism in a PGG with asymmetric heterogeneity can substantially increase efficiency and group payoffs, provided that transfers are used and reciprocated. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar heterogeneity and transfer options, but do not inform about the effects of punishment or sanctions, as these are not present in the design. The results suggest that mechanisms enabling trust and reciprocity (such as voluntary transfers) can substitute for punishment in boosting efficiency, but the effect is contingent on the use and perception of the transfer. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it is highly informative for understanding the efficiency effects of alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms in heterogeneous PGGs.
