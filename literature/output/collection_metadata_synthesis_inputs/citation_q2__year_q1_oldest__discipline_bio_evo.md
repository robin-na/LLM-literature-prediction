- source: Wolff, I. (2012). Retaliation and the role for punishment in the evolution of cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with punishment and the possibility of retaliation, punishment can help induce partially cooperative equilibria and temporarily increase cooperation and group payoff, especially when mutation is present. However, punishment cannot sustain full cooperation in the presence of even small mutation rates, as the costs of punishment and the threat of retaliation allow non-punishing cooperators and defectors to invade. The efficiency gains from punishment are thus limited and parameter-dependent: punishment is most effective at intermediate continuation probabilities and when retaliation is not too strong. At high continuation probabilities or when retaliation is easy, punishment is driven out and cannot maintain high efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in repeated public goods games is highly sensitive to the possibility of retaliation and the evolutionary dynamics of strategy mutation. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, one should consider whether retaliation is possible, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the continuation probability (analogous to number of rounds or game length), and the presence of mutation or experimentation. Punishment can increase efficiency in some cases, but its effect is undermined by retaliation and high continuation probability. The model suggests that punishment is most likely to improve efficiency when it is effective (high punishment impact per cost), retaliation is costly or rare, and the game is not too long. If retaliation is easy and the game is long, punishment may not improve and may even reduce efficiency.

- source: Castro, L., & Toro, M. A. (2008). Iterated prisoner's dilemma in an asocial world dominated by loners, not by defectors. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated N-person Prisoner's Dilemma games, the presence of a 'loner' strategy (voluntary opt-out for a fixed payoff) changes the evolutionary dynamics: loners can displace defectors, and conditional cooperators (TFT0) can invade a population of loners if the benefit of cooperation is high enough and the number of repeated interactions is sufficient. The model demonstrates that cooperation can be established and maintained in sizable groups without the need for explicit punishment, provided that individuals can opt out. The paper does not model or test punishment directly, but discusses its potential role as an additional mechanism for sustaining cooperation in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that voluntary participation (the loner strategy) can enable high-efficiency outcomes in repeated public-goods-like games, even without punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency, as punishment is not modeled. The findings suggest that in environments where voluntary participation is possible, the need for punishment to sustain cooperation may be reduced. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the baseline efficiency in control games with voluntary participation, but not about the incremental effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Salomonsson, M. (2010). Group selection: The quest for social preferences. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that group selection can, under certain conditions, support the evolution of cooperation and altruism in public-goods-like and Prisoner's Dilemma games. Three main approaches are discussed: fixation (requiring isolated pure groups), assortative group formation (e.g., via conformity or signalling), and reproductive externalities (where group-level payoffs affect evolutionary success). The paper notes that group selection is more likely to support cooperation when migration is low, groups are small, or positive externalities can be internalized. It also reviews how cultural mechanisms (like conformity) and group competition can foster social preferences. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, and only discusses punishment as a possible mechanism in the context of conformity and social preferences.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how group structure, migration, and mechanisms like conformity or signalling can affect the evolution of cooperation in public-goods-like games. It is useful for understanding the broader conditions under which cooperation and social preferences may arise, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that group structure and the ability to internalize externalities are important moderators, but it does not inform the specific effect of punishment interventions in experimental public goods games.

- source: Vincent, J. R. (2007). Spatial dynamics, social norms, and the opportunity of the commons. *Ecological Research*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that economic incentives—specifically, the use of sanctions (punishments for non-cooperation and rewards for cooperation)—are critical for achieving efficient and sustainable management of common pool resources. Theoretical models (e.g., repeated games, evolutionary game theory) and empirical case studies (e.g., Ostrom 1990) show that monitoring and enforcement mechanisms enable groups to avoid the tragedy of the commons, leading to higher group profits and resource stocks. The author is skeptical that social norms alone, without sanctions, can achieve similar efficiency, especially when rapid or large-scale behavioral change is needed. The paper also notes that public goods (as opposed to common pool resources) face similar free-rider problems and require external enforcement (e.g., taxes, quotas) to achieve efficient provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment or sanctions in public-goods-game-like environments will increase efficiency, group payoff, and resource sustainability, especially when compared to environments without such mechanisms. The presence of monitoring and enforcement is highlighted as a key moderator: systems with sanctions are much more likely to achieve efficient outcomes than those relying on norms alone. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates but offers strong theoretical and qualitative support for the positive effect of punishment on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment/sanctions should be coded as a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially in settings analogous to common pool resources.

- source: Baum, W. M., Paciotti, B., Richerson, P., Lubell, M., & McElreath, R. (2012). Cooperation due to cultural norms, not individual reputation. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a trust game with a conditional fine, trustees return more to investors from their own ethnic group when no fine is available. When a fine is available and imposed, trustworthiness increases in cross-ethnic pairs, eliminating the in-group advantage. In same-ethnic pairs, the fine has no effect or may crowd out trustworthiness. The effect is similar when the fine is available but not used. The study does not report group efficiency or total earnings as a main outcome, but the behavioral results suggest that punishment is more effective at increasing trustworthiness across ethnic lines than within them.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (measured as trustworthiness) depends on group identity: punishment increases trustworthiness in cross-ethnic pairs but not in same-ethnic pairs. However, because the outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The design is a modified trust game, not a public goods game, so transferability to PGGs is limited but informative for settings where group identity and punishment interact. The punishment is costless to the punisher and has a fixed impact, which should be considered when mapping to other designs.

- source: Nakamaru, M. (2006). Lattice models in ecology and social sciences. *Ecological Research*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper reviews and synthesizes theoretical and simulation results showing that lattice-structured populations (as opposed to well-mixed populations) can promote the evolution of cooperation and punishment (Altruist-Punisher), as well as spiteful behavior, depending on the model's updating rules. In particular, the Altruist-Punisher strategy can invade selfish populations more easily in lattice models, and punishment is more effective in promoting cooperation in these settings. However, the paper does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on the evolutionary success of strategies and the spread of norms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that spatial or network structure (such as lattice models) can moderate the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or direct evidence about efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper's findings are only adjacent: they suggest that punishment may be more effective in structured populations, but do not quantify the effect on efficiency or provide data on payoff outcomes. The paper is more useful for understanding mechanisms and contextual moderators than for direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R. (2010). Interactions Between Sabre-Tooth Blennies and Their Reef Fish Victims: Effects Of Enforced Repeated Game Structure and Local Abundance on Victim Aggression. *Ethology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that resident reef fish species, which cannot avoid repeated interactions with parasitic blennies, are significantly more likely to show aggressive responses (interpreted as punishment) than visitor species, even after controlling for size and territoriality. Aggression is negatively correlated with local abundance (group size), both across species and within the most abundant species, suggesting that the benefits of punishment are diluted in larger groups. The authors interpret these patterns as consistent with theoretical predictions from repeated game and public goods models, but do not directly measure efficiency, group payoff, or the downstream effects of punishment on blenny behavior or group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, ecological evidence relevant to the prediction of punishment effects in public-goods-like settings. It supports the idea that repeated interactions (enforced by residency) increase the likelihood of punishment, and that larger group size dilutes the incentive to punish. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its main value is in supporting the importance of repeated interactions and group size as moderators of punishment behavior, which may inform priors or mechanistic expectations in related experimental PGG studies.

- source: Fishman, M. A. (2006). Involuntary defection and the evolutionary origins of empathy. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated dyadic cooperation games with involuntary defection, unconditional punishment (retaliation for any defection) undermines cooperation and reduces efficiency, as it leads to cycles of mutual defection. In contrast, an empathic retaliator strategy—where punishment is withheld if the opponent's defection follows one's own involuntary defection—can be evolutionarily stable and support high efficiency, especially when the expected number of repeated interactions is large. The stability and efficiency of cooperation depend on the cost of helping, the probability of being able to help, and the expected number of interaction cycles. The paper provides explicit conditions and payoff matrices for these outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the design of punishment mechanisms critically affects efficiency in repeated cooperation games. Specifically, unconditional punishment (retaliation) in the presence of involuntary defection leads to lower efficiency, while empathic (conditional) punishment can sustain high efficiency if repeated interactions are frequent. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on whether punishment is unconditional or conditional/empathic, the frequency of repeated interactions, and the possibility of involuntary defection. However, the model is not a public goods game, and punishment is not costly in the sense of standard PGG experiments; rather, it is refusal to help. Thus, direct quantitative predictions for PGGs should be made with caution, but the qualitative insight is that punishment can either harm or help efficiency depending on its design and the game context.

- source: Johnstone, R. A., & Bshary, R. (2007). Indirect reciprocity in asymmetric interactions: when apparent altruism facilitates profitable exploitation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in asymmetric games where only one party (the actor) can help or exploit, and the other (the recipient) can only choose whether to interact, a form of indirect reciprocity can evolve. Actors help when the gains from exploitation are low to maintain a positive image, which encourages recipients to approach them in the future, allowing profitable exploitation when the gains are high. This strategy is stable and increases the long-term average payoff for both actors and recipients, provided certain conditions on observability, state-switching, and payoff parameters are met. The model shows that efficiency (average group payoff) is higher under these conditions than if actors always exploit or if recipients always avoid interaction.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in asymmetric games with image scoring and conditional approach, mechanisms analogous to punishment (here, avoidance of exploiters) can increase efficiency by supporting conditional cooperation. The findings suggest that when harmful behavior is observable and actors' states vary, efficiency is maximized for intermediate values of key parameters (cost of harm, switching probability, observability). While not a PGG or direct punishment setting, the model is adjacent and supports the idea that social mechanisms that deter exploitation (even without direct punishment) can improve group efficiency. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling mechanisms for reputation-based avoidance or indirect sanctions can increase efficiency, especially when exploitation is not always profitable and can be detected.

- source: Rankin, D. J., & Eggimann, F. (2009). The evolution of judgement bias in indirect reciprocity. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that judgement bias—weighting bad acts more heavily than good acts in reputation updating—can evolve in indirect reciprocity models, especially when the benefit-to-cost ratio is low. Under 'standing' reputation rules, negative judgement bias is favored over a wider range of parameters than under 'scoring.' Allowing judgement bias to evolve increases the level of cooperation in the population. The model does not include explicit costly punishment, but negative judgement bias acts as a form of social sanctioning by reducing the likelihood of future help to defectors. The model does not report group efficiency or total payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it suggests that mechanisms that increase the reputational cost of defection (such as negative judgement bias) can promote cooperation, especially when the cost of cooperation is high. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation-based indirect reciprocity is the main mechanism, rather than explicit costly punishment. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGGs with or without punishment.

- source: Koike, S., Nakamaru, M., & Tsujimoto, M. (2010). Evolution of cooperation in rotating indivisible goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in a rotating indivisible goods game (a model of ROSCAs), cooperation collapses unless both a peer selection rule (based on reputation) and a forfeiture rule (excluding non-contributors from receiving goods) are present. Peer selection alone or forfeiture alone is insufficient. The model tracks the evolution of cooperation probabilities before and after receiving goods, showing that only the combination of both rules leads to sustained high cooperation. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its findings are limited to behavioral outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it shows that in a public-goods-adjacent environment with indivisible goods, the presence of both peer selection (reputation-based group formation) and a forfeiture rule (exclusion of non-contributors) is necessary to sustain high cooperation rates. For prediction, this suggests that punishment-like mechanisms (here, exclusion and peer selection) may only be effective in combination, and that the structure of the game (indivisible goods, rotation, exclusion) is critical. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits its direct applicability to efficiency prediction.

- source: Tucker, W. T., & Ferson, S. (2008). Evolved altruism, strong reciprocity, and perception of risk. *Strategies for Risk Communication: Evolution, Evidence, Experience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that human cognitive architecture is shaped by evolutionary pressures to maintain cooperation and punish free riders in social contract networks, such as those modeled by public goods games. It reviews evidence that people are willing to incur personal costs to punish defectors, supporting the concept of 'strong reciprocity.' The authors suggest that these evolved mechanisms are central to risk perception and cooperation, and that they help explain why people often resist narrowly self-interested, rational-maximizing behavior in favor of fairness and contract maintenance. However, the paper does not provide new empirical data or direct analysis of efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and contextual support for the idea that punishment mechanisms are evolutionarily grounded and play a key role in sustaining cooperation in public-goods-like environments. It supports the expectation that punishment can increase cooperation and deter free riding, but does not provide quantitative or direct empirical evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of punishment is likely to increase cooperation and potentially efficiency, but does not provide parameter-specific or design-dimension-specific guidance. Its value is mainly in supporting the plausibility and generality of punishment effects, not in calibrating their magnitude or moderators.

- source: Tobeña, A. (2009). Lethal Altruists Itineraries along the Dark Outskirts of Moralistic Prosociality. *Values, Empathy, and Fairness Across Social Barriers*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that strong altruism (including costly punishment) and parochialism are important temperamental traits that can drive both prosocial and antisocial behaviors, such as martyrdom and suicide terrorism. It reviews evidence that some individuals are predisposed to punish norm violators at personal cost, and that these tendencies are influenced by genetic, neurobiological, and temperamental factors. The paper references behavioral economic games (ultimatum, dictator, third-party punishment) as tools for measuring these traits, but does not report new experimental results or analyze efficiency or group payoff. The main contribution is a conceptual framework for understanding the interplay of individual traits and group dynamics in extreme forms of moralistic prosociality.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it discusses the existence of costly punishment and its association with certain temperamental and genetic traits, it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. The references to behavioral games are used to illustrate individual differences in punishment and altruism, not to quantify their effects on group efficiency. Therefore, this paper offers only indirect, contextual support for the idea that costly punishment is a stable human trait, but does not inform the quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in experimental game settings.

  findings: The paper argues that cooperation and helping are underpinned by distinct psychological mechanisms, with parental care mechanisms playing a key role in both asymmetrical helping and in supporting stable cooperation among autonomous agents (such as humans). It suggests that mechanisms for punishment, aggression, and power interact with care-based mechanisms to regulate cooperation and prevent exploitation. The paper critiques the abstraction of game-theoretical models and calls for more mechanistic, multi-agent modeling approaches. While it references public-goods-game-like environments (e.g., the prisoner's dilemma), it does not report empirical findings or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.
