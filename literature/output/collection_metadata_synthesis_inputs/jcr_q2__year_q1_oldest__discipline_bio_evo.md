- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Forsyth, P. A. I., & Hauert, C. (2011). Public goods games with reward in finite populations. *Journal of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in finite populations, allowing individuals to reward contributors to the public good can initiate and maintain a significant minority of cooperators, even though the majority of the population is typically composed of non-cooperating shirkers. Reward is effective at jump-starting cooperation but is unable to stabilize it in the long run due to exploitation by second-order free-riders. The average group payoff (efficiency) is increased relative to pure defection, but full efficiency is not achieved. The effectiveness of reward is largely independent of group size when key parameters are scaled appropriately. The paper does not provide new results on punishment, but notes that punishment is more effective at stabilizing cooperation once established.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence on the effects of reward (not punishment) in public goods games. It shows that positive incentives can increase efficiency above the baseline of mutual defection, but do not achieve full efficiency due to the persistence of second-order free-riders. The findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, reward alone is insufficient to fully stabilize cooperation and maximize efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with reward enabled, but only indirectly inform predictions about punishment. The analysis of group size, MPCR, and reward parameters is informative for understanding how these dimensions affect efficiency with reward. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for cases where the treatment is reward (not punishment), and less directly informative for punishment-enabled games.

- source: Archetti, M., & Scheuring, I. (2011). COEXISTENCE OF COOPERATION AND DEFECTION IN PUBLIC GOODS GAMES. *Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that the structure of the public goods function (linear vs. nonlinear/threshold) fundamentally changes the equilibrium outcome. In nonlinear/threshold (Volunteer's Dilemma-like) games, cooperation and defection can coexist at a stable mixed equilibrium, and the public good is produced with positive probability, even in one-shot, well-mixed populations without relatedness or repeated play. In linear (NPD) games, only defection is stable in the absence of assortment. The paper provides analytical and numerical results for the equilibrium frequency of cooperation and the probability of public good provision as a function of group size, cost/benefit ratio, and the threshold parameter. No punishment, reward, or external enforcement mechanisms are modeled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games as a function of the public good production function (linear vs. nonlinear/threshold), group size, and cost/benefit ratio, but it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions. It provides direct theoretical predictions for efficiency and group payoff in the absence of punishment, but cannot inform the effect of enabling punishment. For downstream prediction tasks focused on the effect of punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it is valuable for understanding baseline efficiency in control conditions without punishment, especially in games with nonlinear or threshold public goods functions.

- source: POLLOCK, G. B. (1988). POPULATION-STRUCTURE, SPITE, AND THE ITERATED PRISONERS-DILEMMA. *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that punishment-based strategies (TFT/NTFT) can maintain high group efficiency and suppress defection when the population is structured into multiple groups or dyads, allowing intergroup competition. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation and high payoffs is reduced as the scope of spite (the number of groups a defector can affect) increases or as population structure becomes less partitioned. In the limit of a single group (no structure), punishment cannot prevent defection and efficiency collapses. The analysis generalizes to n-person games, showing that the same logic applies: partitioned populations with group-level competition allow punishment to sustain cooperation and high efficiency, while unstructured populations do not.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments will increase efficiency (group payoff) relative to control, but only when the game design includes sufficient population structure (multiple groups, limited overlap in group membership, or mechanisms for intergroup competition). If the game is a single group or highly overlapping, punishment will not improve efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the marginal per-capita return, group size, and population structure can be specified, and they highlight the importance of these dimensions as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

- source: Gardner, A., & West, S. A. (2004). Cooperation and punishment, especially in humans. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment can promote and stabilize cooperation in public-goods-like environments, leading to higher group fitness (efficiency), provided that individuals who punish tend to receive more cooperation. The cost of punishment decreases as cooperation increases, making punishment more sustainable in cooperative populations. Relatedness between individuals is not required for punishment to be favored; instead, the key is a positive association between an individual's punishment and the cooperation she receives. The model predicts that punishment is more easily maintained than initially evolved, and that small group size and effective punishment (where the cost of being punished exceeds the cost of cooperation) facilitate the evolutionary stability of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games can increase efficiency, especially when punishment is effective (i.e., the penalty for being punished is greater than the cost of cooperation) and when there is a mechanism (such as behavioral adjustment) that links punishment to increased cooperation received. The findings suggest that, all else equal, introducing punishment should increase group efficiency, particularly in smaller groups and when punishment is not too costly to the punisher. However, the results are derived from theoretical models rather than empirical data, so quantitative predictions require caution. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is not dependent on relatedness or repeated interactions, but on the structure of behavioral associations.

- source: Lehmann, L., Rousset, F., Roze, D., & Keller, L. (2007). Strong reciprocity or strong ferocity? A population genetic view of the evolution of altruistic punishment. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that strong reciprocity (costly helping plus costly punishment) can evolve in spatially structured populations if helping and punishment are tightly linked (no recombination) and relatedness is sufficiently high. In this case, strong reciprocity can invade even when rare. When helping and punishment are unlinked (recombination is strong), neither costly helping nor costly punishment can invade from rarity unless punishment yields a direct fitness benefit to the actor. The model shows that kin selection (genetic or cultural) is necessary for the evolution of strong reciprocity. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide direct predictions about group efficiency or payoff, but rather about the evolutionary stability and spread of strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into the evolutionary preconditions for the emergence of punishment and cooperation in public-goods-like settings, especially the importance of linkage between helping and punishment, population structure (relatedness), and direct fitness effects. However, it does not provide quantitative predictions about group efficiency or payoff with and without punishment, nor does it report outcomes directly comparable to experimental public goods games. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting that punishment is unlikely to increase cooperation or efficiency in anonymous, unstructured, or one-shot settings unless there is kinship or direct benefit to punishers. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative.

- source: Henrich, J., & Henrich, N. (2006). Culture, evolution and the puzzle of human cooperation. *Cognitive Systems Research*.

  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that punishment, especially when combined with cultural learning mechanisms (such as conformist and prestige-biased transmission), can stabilize costly cooperative norms in human groups. It reviews theoretical models showing that the effectiveness of punishment in maintaining cooperation depends on the cost of punishing relative to the cost of being punished, and that social norms enforced by punishment can persist even when the cooperative act is individually costly. The paper also discusses how group-level selection can favor groups with more effective cooperative norms, and how cultural evolution allows for rapid adaptation of cooperation strategies. However, the paper does not provide empirical or quantitative findings on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on the mechanisms and evolutionary logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that punishment can stabilize cooperation in public goods-like environments, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly relative to the cost of being punished. It highlights the importance of social norms, cultural learning, and group structure in moderating the effects of punishment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the magnitude of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. Its value for prediction lies in its mechanistic insights and identification of key moderators (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, norm strength), but not in providing direct outcome estimates.

- source: Ubeda, F., & Duéñez-Guzmán, E. A. (2011). POWER AND CORRUPTION. *Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a Prisoner's Dilemma with costly punishment, allowing for corrupt punishers and power asymmetries enables the evolution of stable states with both cooperation and corruption. When the social cost of punishing a punisher (q+d) is less than the cost of punishing a civilian (p), and within a certain range, a mixed equilibrium emerges with both cooperating civilians and corrupt policers. In this equilibrium, the population mean payoff (efficiency) is higher than in the all-defect equilibrium, and can approach the fully cooperative payoff as power asymmetries increase. The model demonstrates that costly punishment can increase both cooperation and efficiency, especially when punishers are protected by power asymmetries. The likelihood of reaching the efficient equilibrium depends on the relative costs and effectiveness of punishment. The results challenge the view that costly punishment is always inefficient, showing that under the right conditions, it can benefit the group even when punishers are themselves corrupt.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the structure of punishment costs and power asymmetries. If punishment is costly but power asymmetries exist (punishers are less vulnerable to punishment or punishment is more effective against non-punishers), then enabling punishment can increase efficiency above the control (no-punishment) baseline. The model suggests that the efficiency gain is larger when the punishment of defectors is strong and the punishment of punishers is weak. The findings are most directly applicable to Prisoner's Dilemma settings, but the logic is relevant for public goods games with similar punishment structures. The paper does not address all game design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, communication), but provides direct, mechanistic insight into how punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and power asymmetries moderate the efficiency impact of punishment.

- source: Baum, W. M., Paciotti, B., Richerson, P., Lubell, M., & McElreath, R. (2012). Cooperation due to cultural norms, not individual reputation. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a trust game with a conditional fine, trustees return more to investors from their own ethnic group when no fine is available. When a fine is available and imposed, trustworthiness increases in cross-ethnic pairs, eliminating the in-group advantage. In same-ethnic pairs, the fine has no effect or may crowd out trustworthiness. The effect is similar when the fine is available but not used. The study does not report group efficiency or total earnings as a main outcome, but the behavioral results suggest that punishment is more effective at increasing trustworthiness across ethnic lines than within them.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (measured as trustworthiness) depends on group identity: punishment increases trustworthiness in cross-ethnic pairs but not in same-ethnic pairs. However, because the outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The design is a modified trust game, not a public goods game, so transferability to PGGs is limited but informative for settings where group identity and punishment interact. The punishment is costless to the punisher and has a fixed impact, which should be considered when mapping to other designs.

  findings: The study finds that cleaner fish, when exposed to simulated punishment (chasing) or partner switching (fleeing) after eating their preferred food, learn to avoid the preferred food and instead eat more of the less preferred food. This behavioral shift is interpreted as evidence that punishment and partner switching can promote cooperative behavior in mutualistic interactions. The findings support the idea that both punishment and the threat of partner switching can be effective partner control mechanisms, but the outcomes are measured in terms of behavioral change rather than direct payoffs or efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that punishment and partner switching can alter behavior in a way that is consistent with increased cooperation, but it does not provide direct evidence about efficiency or group payoff as defined in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent to PGGs, focusing on dyadic mutualism rather than group contributions and payoffs. For prediction tasks about efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper suggests that punishment and partner switching can change behavior toward more cooperative outcomes, but does not quantify the effect on efficiency or provide data on group-level payoffs. However, it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it use a public goods game or a repeated game structure. Thus, it offers only indirect, mechanistic insight for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games. It suggests that punishment may be more likely to occur (and thus potentially affect efficiency) when cheating creates disadvantageous inequity, but does not provide quantitative or direct evidence for efficiency outcomes.
