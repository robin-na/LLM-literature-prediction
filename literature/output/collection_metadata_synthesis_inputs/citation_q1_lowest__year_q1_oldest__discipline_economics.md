- source: Fehr, E., Gächter, S., Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2010). Institutions Fostering Public Goods Provision. *Selten School of Behavioral Economics: a Collection of Essays in Honor of Reinhard Selten*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment opportunities in public goods games increase cooperation and contributions, but typically reduce efficiency and group payoffs in standard (short) experimental settings due to the destruction of resources through costly punishment. In most experiments with 10 or fewer rounds, average payoffs are lower with punishment than without. However, in longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when punishment is combined with effective reputation mechanisms, efficiency can be higher with punishment. Endogenous institution choice leads most participants to eventually select punishment institutions, which then sustain high cooperation, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed unless the time horizon is long or reputation is leveraged. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed and highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as number of rounds and the presence of reputation mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive or negative, but depends critically on the number of rounds (time horizon) and the presence of reputation or endogenous institution choice. For standard short games (10 rounds or fewer), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to control. For longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when combined with reputation mechanisms, punishment can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of endogenous institution choice, which can foster high cooperation and, under the right conditions, higher efficiency. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the number of rounds and the presence of reputation or endogenous choice mechanisms are key moderators.

  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly informative for predicting efficiency in public good games with voting mechanisms but does not provide evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that mechanisms that safeguard against exploitation (like unanimity voting) can increase contributions but may reduce efficiency due to coordination failures or excessive vetoes. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

- source: McEvoy, D. M. (2012). Paying to punish on a free ride: member and nonmember punishments in voluntary coalitions. *Applied Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a public good game with voluntary coalition formation, allowing voluntary punishment (where any player can punish any other) does not increase public good provision relative to no punishment. However, when coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance (quasi-voluntary punishment), public good provision increases significantly. Both coalition members and nonmembers are equally likely to punish noncompliance, and the extent of nonmember punishment does not change when members can credibly enforce compliance. The main outcomes are contribution levels and punishment frequencies; efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that simply enabling voluntary punishment (without credible commitment) is not sufficient to increase efficiency or public good provision in coalition-based public good games. Only when punishment is structured so that coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance does cooperation (and likely efficiency) increase. For prediction, the presence of a credible, committed punishment mechanism is a key moderator; voluntary, non-committed punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes.

- source: Janus, T., & Lim, J. J. (2009). Sticks and carrots: Two incentive mechanisms supporting intra-group cooperation. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper proves that in a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma, the introduction of either collective punishment (Nash reversion by all after any defection) or reward mechanisms (transfers contingent on cooperation) can sustain full cooperation as a subgame perfect equilibrium, even when bilateral incentive compatibility constraints are violated. This means that group efficiency (welfare) can be maintained at the cooperative level under broader conditions than in the absence of such mechanisms. The results are general and apply to settings where players interact repeatedly and can observe each other's actions, and where the game is either infinite or has an unknown end point.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency by sustaining cooperation, even when cooperation would not be sustainable without such mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially collective punishment with Nash reversion) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment, provided players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor) and can observe actions. The model is adjacent to public goods games (it is a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma), so the results are most directly applicable to small-group, repeated, fully observable settings. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it does establish that the direction of effect is positive for efficiency when punishment is enabled.

- source: Matsushima, H. (2012). FINITELY REPEATED PRISONERS' DILEMMA WITH SMALL FINES: THE PENANCE CONTRACT. *Japanese Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper proves that with a suitable contract that imposes a fine for deviation in the final period, full cooperation (i.e., maximal efficiency) can be uniquely sustained in finitely repeated prisoners' dilemma games. The required fine can be small relative to total payoffs if the game is long enough. The penance contract is robust to renegotiation, unlike trigger contracts. The result generalizes to other two-player games with similar payoff structure. The findings are theoretical and show that the design of the punishment mechanism (timing, size, and contingency) is crucial for achieving efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in two-player finitely repeated social dilemma games, the introduction of a well-designed punishment mechanism (specifically, a contract that imposes a fine for final-period deviation) can move the group from inefficient (all-defect) equilibria to fully efficient (all-cooperate) equilibria. The effect is robust to renegotiation if the punishment is contingent on final-period behavior and the fine is large enough to outweigh the gain from deviation. The result is directly relevant for predicting that enabling such a punishment mechanism will increase efficiency to the maximum possible, provided the contract is enforceable and the fine is sufficiently large. However, the result is limited to two-player, finitely repeated, contractible games and does not address larger groups or public goods games directly.

- source: Evans, R., & Thomas, J. P. (2001). Cooperation and punishment. *Econometrica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper proves that in repeated common interest games (with no discounting), the mere presence of cooperative (nondraconian) strategies in the set of possible strategies does not guarantee efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcomes in equilibrium. However, if the set of possible strategies includes at least one 'draconian' strategy (i.e., a strategy that is willing to punish noncooperation to the maximum extent, such as min-maxing the opponent forever after a deviation), then all Nash equilibria are efficient. The paper also shows that this result is robust to certain computability restrictions and discusses the limits of these results under discounting and other technical assumptions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated games with a structure similar to public goods or common interest games, the availability of sufficiently severe punishment (i.e., the ability to impose maximal punishment at low or no cost) is both necessary and sufficient for punishment to induce efficient outcomes in equilibrium. If the game design allows for draconian punishment strategies, then enabling punishment should lead to high efficiency, regardless of the control efficiency. If only mild or 'cooperative' punishment is possible, efficiency gains are not guaranteed. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted games, but the logic is relevant for understanding the role of punishment severity and credibility in other settings.

- source: Aramendia, M. (2006). Asymmetric finite punishments in repeated games. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in repeated games, including social dilemmas and Cournot oligopoly, AFP strategies—where punishment is finite and asymmetric—can sustain any feasible and individually rational payoff profile when players are sufficiently patient. The punishment phase is designed so that nondeviators are always better off than the deviator, and after a finite number of periods, cooperation resumes. This approach is less restrictive than some standard equilibrium refinements and can support efficient outcomes (e.g., cartel profits) even in cases where other concepts (like WRP) cannot. The results are illustrated with examples including the prisoner's dilemma and Cournot oligopoly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that finite, asymmetric punishment strategies can sustain efficient, cooperative outcomes in repeated games, even when standard trigger or minimax punishments are not credible or too harsh. For the prediction task, it suggests that the design of the punishment phase (finite duration, asymmetry favoring nondeviators) can be critical for supporting high efficiency, especially as the number of players increases or when renegotiation-proofness is problematic. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide direct quantitative predictions for specific experimental public goods games, but they do indicate that efficiency can be maintained with appropriately designed punishment even in large groups or complex environments.

- source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

- source: Heller, W. B., & Sieberg, K. K. (2008). Functional unpleasantness: the evolutionary logic of righteous resentment. *Public Choice*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation can be sustained in populations where some individuals are willing to punish cheaters, even if those punishers are themselves not cooperative. The evolutionary stability of cooperation depends on the relative costs of punishing, being punished, and being cheated. When the cost of punishing is less than the cost of being cheated, punishment can support a stable mix of cooperators and punishers, increasing overall efficiency compared to a world with only cheaters. If punishment is too costly, cheaters dominate and cooperation collapses. The model demonstrates that the existence of punishment mechanisms—regardless of the punishers' motives—can improve group welfare and efficiency in collective action settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in collective action games (including public goods game variants) can increase efficiency, as measured by average group payoff, provided the cost of punishing is not too high. The findings suggest that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment. While the model is not an exact public goods game, its structure and payoff logic are closely related, and its results are directly relevant for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency. The paper does not address empirical game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, or communication, but it does provide strong evidence that the cost and effectiveness of punishment are critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Jones, M. A. (1999). The effect of punishment duration of trigger strategies and quasifinite continuation probabilities for Prisoners' Dilemmas. *International Journal of Game Theory*.

  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.

- source: Bruttel, L., & Eisenkopf, G. (2012). No contract or unfair contract: What's better?. *Journal of Socio-economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing a sequential prisoner's dilemma (PD) to a vertically integrated version with incentive-compatible contracts (ICC), the introduction of contracts—regardless of fairness—increases joint payoffs from an average of 7.33 (PD) to 9.33 (ICC). Even unfair contracts yield higher welfare (9.02) than the best possible matching in PD. The study also finds that individual reciprocity types in PD do not predict negative reciprocity (defection) in ICC.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player sequential prisoner's dilemma, introducing an incentive-compatible contract mechanism (analogous to a principal-agent or vertical integration structure) increases efficiency (joint payoff) compared to the baseline without contracts. The effect is robust to the fairness of the contract: even when the contract is unfair (unequal payoffs), efficiency is higher than in the baseline. The mechanism for increased efficiency is that contracts induce more cooperation, and the risk of 'punishment' via defection in response to unfairness is not large enough to negate the efficiency gains. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling a contract-like mechanism (even if not perfectly fair) in a similar environment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. However, the setting is a sequential PD, not a standard PGG, and punishment is not implemented as a costly action but as defection in response to unfairness.
