- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Peysakhovich, A., & Rand, D. G. (2016). Habits of Virtue: Creating Norms of Cooperation and Defection in the Laboratory. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Exposure to environments that support cooperation (via repeated PDs with high continuation probability and favorable payoffs) leads to higher prosociality in subsequent one-shot games, including the public goods game, trust game, dictator game, and ultimatum game. In a second experiment, exposure to cooperative environments increases third-party punishment of selfishness and reduces antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment. These effects are strongest among subjects who rely on intuitive (heuristic) decision-making. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional environments can shape prosocial and punishment behaviors in public-goods-game-like settings, and that these behaviors spill over into new contexts. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency. The design details (e.g., group size, punishment cost/tech, MPCR) are reported and can inform mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

- source: Villatoro, D., Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Nardin, L. G., Sabater-Mir, J., & Conte, R. (2014). The Norm-Signaling Effects of Group Punishment: Combining Agent-Based Simulation and Laboratory Experiments. *Social Science Computer Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when punishment is inflicted by the entire group, human subjects are more likely to cooperate (i.e., contribute to the public good) than when punishment is uncoordinated, even though the material cost to defectors is held constant. The authors attribute this to the norm-signaling function of group punishment, which is hypothesized to activate both instrumental and normative motivations for compliance. Agent-based simulations support the idea that norm salience, as modeled in the EMIL-I-A architecture, can explain the observed human behavior. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so the findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (contribution rates).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of punishment (specifically, whether it is coordinated by the group or uncoordinated) can significantly affect cooperation rates in public goods games, even when the material incentives are held constant. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that group punishment may lead to higher efficiency than uncoordinated punishment, but this is inferred from contribution rates rather than directly measured. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of group punishment structure and norm-signaling on cooperation, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency when combined with other sources.

- source: Yang, Q. X., & Yang, Y. L. (2024). A social monitoring mechanism for third-party judges promotes cooperation in evolutionary games. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that in repeated PGGs without punishment or reward, cooperation and group payoff decline over time, consistent with previous literature. The authors reconstruct a micro-dynamic model based on proportional changes in expected payoff, showing that players' willingness to cooperate depends on their current strategy and local cooperation levels. The model predicts the observed decline in cooperation and group payoff. The paper also documents a 'restart effect' in two-stage games, where cooperation temporarily increases at the transition point. The findings provide a framework for predicting the evolution of cooperation and group payoff in repeated PGGs without external interventions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on baseline efficiency and group payoff dynamics in repeated PGGs without punishment or reward. It is directly relevant for predicting control (no-punishment) efficiency as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and information structure. However, it does not provide any evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment-related variables. Thus, it is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it is highly informative for modeling the baseline (control) case.

- source: Ren, T. Y., & Zheng, J. J. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics in the spatial public goods game with tolerance-based expulsion and cooperation. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that in a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with four players and 20 rounds per part, groups that start with unequal endowments (two rich, two poor) and then experience redistribution to equality do not increase their group contributions or efficiency after redistribution. Group contributions in the post-redistribution phase are indistinguishable from those in groups that remain unequal, and both are significantly lower than in always-equal groups. The experience of initial inequality creates a persistent negative spillover, locking groups into low cooperation and efficiency even after equality is restored. The mechanism is traced to the former poor mimicking the low-contribution behavior of the former rich after redistribution. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms, simply reducing inequality via redistribution does not increase efficiency in public goods games if groups have previously experienced inequality. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, for games with similar design (no punishment, no reward, linear PGG, 4 players, 20+20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, no identification, no summary of individual contributions), the efficiency in the treatment with redistribution will not exceed that of the control with persistent inequality, and both will be lower than in always-equal groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGGs without punishment or reward, but do not inform about the effect of punishment.

- source: Jiang, L. L., Chen, Z., Perc, M., Wang, Z., Kurths, J., & Moreno, Y. (2023). Deterrence through punishment can resolve collective risk dilemmas in carbon emission games. *Chaos*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment simulating a collective-risk public goods game (carbon emission game), costly punishment of free-riders (with fines imposed probabilistically and costs shared by all group members) increases the probability that groups reach the collective target, but only when the punishment risk is high (p > 0.8). At low punishment risk, deterrence is weak, and group failure rates remain high. Larger groups are less likely to succeed at any given punishment risk, requiring higher punishment risk for similar deterrence. The introduction of punishment shifts most players to fair-share contributions, reducing both free-riding and maximal altruism. The effect of punishment on group success (a proxy for efficiency) is robust to different loss probabilities. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the probability of reaching the target and average investment are closely related and show a positive effect of punishment when sufficiently strong.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in collective-risk public goods games with a threshold target and probabilistic costly punishment, enabling punishment increases group success rates (and thus efficiency) only when the punishment risk is high. The effect is moderated by group size (larger groups require higher punishment risk) and is robust to different loss probabilities. For prediction, the presence and strength of punishment (punishment risk, fine size, cost structure), group size, and the absence of communication are key moderators. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low group success rates, especially as group size increases. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is strong and credible, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or infrequent.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: Punishment increases cooperation in one-shot, three-person PD games, both when imposed exogenously (costless) and endogenously (costly to the punisher). The effect of punishment is decomposed into a direct effect (altering incentives for the punished individual) and an indirect effect (raising expectations that others will cooperate, thus encouraging conditional cooperators). Both effects are of similar magnitude. In the endogenous punishment condition, the cost of punishment is small relative to the benefit from increased cooperation, resulting in a net positive benefit. However, the main outcome is contribution, not group efficiency or total payoff, though some calculations of net benefit are provided.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (even when costly to the punisher) increases cooperation in public-goods-like environments, and that the effect is due to both direct and indirect mechanisms. While efficiency (as defined by group payoff relative to maximum possible) is not directly reported, the paper's calculations of net benefit and cost-effectiveness suggest that punishment increases group welfare. The design is a one-shot, three-player, continuous-contribution PD with and without punishment, and with both exogenous and endogenous punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when both direct and indirect effects are present, and when punishment is not prohibitively costly. However, the lack of direct efficiency reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution and net benefit data.

- source: Hashim, M. J., Kannan, K. N., Maximiano, S., & Ulmer, J. R. (2014). Digital Piracy, Teens, and the Source of Advice: An Experimental Study. *Journal of Management Information Systems*.

  findings: Advice from parents, especially when they are punished for their child's piracy, increases purchasing and group profit and reduces piracy compared to other advice sources. Advice from record labels is the least effective and can even reduce group profit. Advice from a regulator is largely ignored. The effect of advice is strongest when the adviser has both a social tie and a stake in the outcome. It shows that exogenous punishment of a third party (parents) for a player's free-riding can increase public good provision and group profit, especially when combined with a social tie. However, it does not test endogenous peer punishment or standard PGG punishment institutions. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms may be more effective when they are socially salient and affect someone with a close tie to the player, but direct transfer to standard PGG with peer punishment is limited. Control efficiency (group profit without punishment) is reported, and the effect of introducing a punishment-like mechanism (parent penalty) can be inferred, but the mechanism differs from standard punishment. The absence of peer punishment, reward, or communication means the results are only adjacent for direct prediction of PGG punishment effects.

- source: Macleod, W. B., Lara, V. V., & Zehnder, C. (2025). Worker Empowerment and Subjective Evaluation: On Building an Effective Conflict Culture. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the effect of enabling punishment (conflict) in a principal-agent game with subjective evaluation is highly context-dependent. In the baseline, communication, and agreement (code of conduct) experiments, allowing workers to punish managers for not paying bonuses does not increase efficiency: the costs of conflict outweigh any small increases in effort, and total surplus is lower or unchanged compared to the no-punishment condition. Workers and managers fail to coordinate on an efficiency-enhancing norm, and punishment is often misapplied. However, when conflicts are institutionalized through a formal grievance process (grievance experiment), workers and managers coordinate more on a pay-for-output norm, effort increases significantly, and total surplus is higher in the punishment-enabled condition. Thus, punishment can enhance efficiency, but only when supported by strong institutional framing and norm coordination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform and is highly sensitive to the institutional environment, especially the presence of mechanisms that coordinate norms (such as a grievance procedure). In environments lacking such coordination, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency due to the direct costs of conflict. In contrast, when a formal grievance process is present, punishment can increase both effort and efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of institutionalized conflict resolution and norm-coordination mechanisms should be considered a key moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment) unless the institutional context is specified.

- source: Zhao, Y. K., Zhao, X. B., & Shen, Z. J. M. (2018). The hot-versus-cold effect in a punishment game: a multi-round experimental study. *Annals of Operations Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment compares two elicitation methods (strategy vs. direct-response) in a multi-round, two-player punishment game. The strategy method leads to significantly higher punishment rates (84% vs. 62%) and more frequent cooperative sender actions (S1: 68% vs. 44%). Combined payoffs (group welfare) are higher for S1 actions, and the higher frequency of S1 under the strategy method results in higher average group payoffs. Regression and learning model analyses show that the difference in punishment rates is due to mental accounting: the strategy method allows receivers to integrate the cost and expected benefit of punishment, increasing its perceived utility. Over repeated rounds, senders learn to avoid punishment by choosing S1 more often, further increasing group welfare. Reward rates are low and do not differ between methods. The findings suggest that, in repeated games, the strategic value of punishment (deterrence) is more important than the immediate emotional satisfaction of punishing, and that the design of the punishment mechanism (including decision timing) can significantly affect cooperation and group outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated two-player punishment games, higher punishment rates (enabled by the strategy method) lead to more cooperative sender behavior and higher group payoffs. While the game is not a standard public goods game, the structure is adjacent: one player can take a selfish or cooperative action, and the other can punish. The results suggest that, in repeated settings, enabling effective punishment (with high perceived strategic value) increases efficiency by deterring selfish behavior. For prediction tasks, this implies that, all else equal, punishment mechanisms that increase the perceived strategic value of punishment (e.g., via decision timing or repeated interaction) will lead to higher efficiency, especially when the baseline (control) efficiency is low due to frequent selfish actions. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, punishment-enabled games, but the mechanism may generalize to other repeated social dilemmas with punishment.

- source: Zhang, M., Ma, Y. J., Tao, Y., Wang, Z., Shi, L., & Wang, R. W. (2020). Awareness of wealth inequalities breeds animosity. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that in repeated PD games with costly punishment, the visibility of wealth information interacts with initial wealth inequality to affect both cooperation rates and average final wealth. When initial wealth is unequal, making wealth visible reduces cooperation and leads to lower average final wealth compared to when wealth is hidden. When initial wealth is equal, visibility has little effect on cooperation or final wealth. Punishment is available but rarely used, and its frequency does not significantly differ across treatments. Thus, in environments with inequality, hiding wealth information improves both cooperation and group payoff (final wealth), while visibility exacerbates inequality and reduces efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving efficiency in repeated PD or PGG-like games with punishment, this paper provides evidence that the visibility of wealth information is a strong moderator when initial endowments are unequal: making wealth visible reduces efficiency (average final wealth), while hiding it increases efficiency. The effect is not present when initial endowments are equal. The presence of punishment does not appear to moderate this effect, as punishment is infrequent and not significantly different across treatments. Thus, when modeling efficiency outcomes, consider both initial wealth inequality and information visibility as key moderators, especially in games with available but rarely used punishment.

- source: Goette, L., Huffman, D., Meier, S., & Sutter, M. (2012). Competition Between Organizational Groups: Its Impact on Altruistic and Antisocial Motivations. *Management Science*.

  findings: The experiment shows that in a neutral environment, punishment is used prosocially to enforce cooperation, especially when the victim of defection is an in-group member. When competition between groups is introduced, punishment becomes antisocial: out-group members are punished more, regardless of whether they cooperate or defect.
