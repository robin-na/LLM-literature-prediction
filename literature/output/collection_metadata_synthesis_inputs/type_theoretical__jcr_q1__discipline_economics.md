- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

The model gives explicit conditions relating group size, mpcr, and punishment cost to the likelihood of efficient cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, for typical lab PGG designs, adding punishment will shift efficiency from low (near zero) to high (near one), unless punishment is extremely costly or the group is very large and inequity aversion is rare. The model also suggests that the marginal effect of punishment is moderated by mpcr and punishment cost.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing a voluntary deposit-commitment mechanism in public goods games can fully implement efficient contribution profiles as equilibrium outcomes, even in the absence of strong external enforcement or punishment institutions. The mechanism works by making non-contribution costly (loss of deposit) only if all players commit, thus aligning individual incentives with group welfare. Theoretical results show that, under this mechanism, the group can achieve payoffs and efficiency levels that strictly Pareto-dominate the standard Nash equilibrium of zero contribution. The mechanism is robust to various extensions, including repeated games and alternative uses of forfeited deposits.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that self-commitment mechanisms (such as refundable deposits) can substitute for traditional punishment in public goods games, leading to higher efficiency and group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in environments where such a deposit-commitment mechanism is available and properly designed, the efficiency with 'punishment' (here, self-sanctioning via deposit) can approach the social optimum, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the deposit mechanism is feasible and all players can observe and verify commitments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or parameterized predictions, but the qualitative direction is clear: enabling credible self-sanctioning or commitment mechanisms can dramatically improve efficiency in public goods games.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2020). SUSTAINING COOPERATION: COMMUNITY ENFORCEMENT VERSUS SPECIALIZED ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of the European Economic Association*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the optimal way to sustain cooperation in repeated public-goods-like games depends on the effectiveness of the punishment technology. If specialized enforcers can punish at low cost and high impact, then pure enforcer punishment (with immediate return to cooperation) maximizes efficiency. If specialized enforcement is less effective, then combining enforcer punishment with community enforcement (e.g., temporary withdrawal of cooperation or ostracism) is optimal. The paper provides explicit characterizations of the maximum sustainable cooperation (efficiency) in each regime, and shows that the presence of effective specialized enforcement can increase group welfare compared to pure community enforcement. However, if the punishment technology is very weak, community enforcement alone is nearly optimal. The results generalize to both small and large groups, and to settings with imperfect monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of introducing punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on the effectiveness of the punishment technology and other game design parameters. It shows that, when punishment is highly effective (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the harm inflicted), enabling punishment can substantially increase efficiency by supporting higher cooperation. However, if punishment is costly or ineffective, the efficiency gain is limited, and community enforcement may be nearly as good. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to inform predictions about treatment efficiency as a function of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and discount factor. The results also highlight that the structure of punishment (one-time vs. repeated, enforcer vs. peer) and the possibility of ostracism or repentance are important moderators.

  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The analysis shows that the threat of the harshest possible group punishment (worst perfect equilibrium) is sufficient to deter defection, even when individual actions are not observable. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

When monitoring is imperfect, reducing the interest rate (making players more patient) increases the efficiency of equilibrium outcomes, potentially allowing near-first-best payoffs. Delaying information release can, counterintuitively, increase efficiency by reducing the cost of deterring deviations. The paper provides generalizations to n-player symmetric games and shows that these results are robust to more complex action and signal spaces.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public-goods-like games with punishment under imperfect monitoring. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the informativeness of monitoring, the timing of information, and the patience of players. In particular, enabling punishment does not guarantee high efficiency unless monitoring is sufficiently informative and the timing of information and action periods is chosen carefully. For prediction, the paper suggests that increasing patience (lower interest rate) is reliably associated with higher efficiency, but increasing the frequency of action or information updates can reduce efficiency when monitoring is imperfect. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to estimate efficiency given the relevant game design dimensions.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling social preference mechanisms (like reciprocity) may only improve efficiency if the relevant parameters (strength and prevalence of reciprocity) are high. The model does not address explicit punishment, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled games is limited, but it does show that endogenous social preferences can sometimes substitute for formal sanctions in supporting cooperation and efficiency.
