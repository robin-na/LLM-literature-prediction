- source: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T. (2023). Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

- source: Li, S. L., Du, C. P., Li, X. X., Shen, C., & Shi, L. (2024). Antisocial peer exclusion does not eliminate the effectiveness of prosocial peer exclusion in structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in structured populations, prosocial peer exclusion can uphold cooperation even in the presence of antisocial peer exclusion, except when exclusion costs are low. Antisocial peer exclusion alone suppresses cooperation, but when both types coexist, prosocial exclusion remains effective at sustaining cooperation through complex coexistence and cyclic dominance patterns. The results are reported in terms of cooperation rates and strategy frequencies, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings are robust across different network sizes and parameter sweeps, but are based on theoretical simulations rather than empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that prosocial peer exclusion can maintain high levels of cooperation in spatial public goods games, even when antisocial exclusion is possible, except at low exclusion costs. However, because the outcomes are reported as cooperation rates and not as efficiency or group payoff, the direct quantitative effect of exclusion on efficiency cannot be extracted. The results suggest that, in structured populations, enabling prosocial exclusion (a form of targeted sanctioning) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that the negative impact of antisocial exclusion does not fully undermine this effect. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect support: higher cooperation rates likely correspond to higher efficiency, but the mapping is not explicit. The findings are most relevant for settings with spatial structure, peer-based exclusion, and variable exclusion costs.

- source: Adams, Z., Ludwiczak, A., Sharma, D., & Osman, M. (2022). Verbal interaction in a social dilemma. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that the quality of communication—specifically, consensus-building language and alignment in chat—significantly predicts increases in cooperative behavior in a repeated public goods game. Groups with higher consensus scores in chat showed larger increases in contributions to the group pot in the next round. The analysis also shows that certain registers (gameshow, familial, socialist) are associated with more successful cooperation, while business and political registers are associated with less success. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that allowing chat/communication between rounds in a public goods game can increase cooperation, but the effect depends on the quality and style of the communication. However, it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is only indirectly informative: it supports the importance of communication (chat) as a moderator of cooperation, but does not address punishment or efficiency directly. It is most useful for understanding the role of chat/communication as a game design dimension.

- source: Dughera, S. (2022). The evolution of workplace control leadership, obedience and organizational performance. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The model finds that punishment (authoritarian leadership) can increase organizational performance relative to no control when workers are low-skilled or when monitoring is efficient, but is always less efficient than motivation (authoritative leadership) when workers are high-skilled and supervisors are charismatic. Multiple equilibria are possible: (1) 'Discipline' (universal cooperation with punishment), (2) 'Cooperation' (universal cooperation with motivation), (3) 'Conflict' (universal defection with punishment), and (4) mixed or cyclical regimes. The most efficient outcome is universal cooperation with motivation, but this requires high worker skill and supervisor charisma. Punishment can prevent the spread of shirking (the 'bad company' effect) by increasing procedural justice, but also reduces morale and is less efficient. Inefficient equilibria with widespread punishment and/or shirking can persist due to path dependence. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime is stable and analyzes the welfare properties of each.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in a public-goods-like team production setting, conditional on game design dimensions such as monitoring efficiency (punishment tech), team size (player count), and the presence/absence of reward (motivation/charisma). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency relative to no control when monitoring is effective and workers are not highly skilled, but is always less efficient than a well-functioning reward/motivation regime. The model gives explicit parameter boundaries for when punishment is effective, and highlights the importance of initial conditions and path dependence. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to no-punishment in some parameter regimes (especially with low worker skill and high monitoring efficiency), but that the maximum efficiency is achieved with motivation/charisma rather than punishment. The model is highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings where reward/motivation is not available or not effective.

- source: Esmaeili, P., Makui, A., Seyedhosseini, S. M., & Ghousi, R. (2022). The effect of probabilistic incentives to promote cooperation during the pandemics using simulation of multi-agent evolutionary game. *International Journal of Industrial Engineering Computations*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that probabilistic third-party incentives (reward and punishment) can trigger abrupt transitions from low to high cooperation in a networked volunteer dilemma game. Pure punishment is more effective than pure reward, but a combination of both (with a higher share for reward) is optimal for promoting cooperation at lower monitoring costs. The main reported outcome is the cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The findings are based on agent-based simulations and catastrophe theory analysis, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence and probability of punishment (and reward) can cause sharp increases in cooperation rates in volunteer dilemma games. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The results suggest that punishment (especially when combined with reward) can be a powerful lever for increasing cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency in similar public goods settings, but this is not directly demonstrated. The paper is most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of incentive mechanisms and the potential for nonlinear effects, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency.

- source: Gallo, E., Riyanto, Y. E., Roy, N., & Teh, T. H. (2022). Cooperation and punishment mechanisms in uncertain and dynamic social networks. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated networked Prisoner's Dilemma with 12 players per group and endogenous link formation/removal, the presence of reputational uncertainty (15% chance that intended actions are flipped) leads to a substantial decrease in both cooperation rate and welfare (group payoff), regardless of whether the network is updated slowly or quickly. Fast network updating (more frequent opportunities to change links) has only a marginally positive effect on welfare and cooperation, and does not offset the negative impact of uncertainty. The mechanism is that uncertainty makes players more lenient and forgiving in network punishment (less likely to remove links from defectors), which disrupts the formation of cooperative clusters. Action punishment (retaliatory defection) is also weakened under uncertainty. The study provides direct evidence that punishment mechanisms relying on accurate reputation signals are less effective in promoting efficiency when monitoring is noisy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in dynamic networked repeated games with endogenous partner selection, the effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (here, network punishment via link removal) in increasing efficiency is highly sensitive to the accuracy of reputation signals. When there is reputational uncertainty, punishment is less effective and group efficiency (welfare) is significantly reduced. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring should be treated as a major moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The results also indicate that simply increasing the frequency of punishment opportunities (e.g., faster network updating) does not robustly increase efficiency, especially when uncertainty is present. The findings are most directly relevant to networked PD games, but the mechanisms may transfer to PGG-like environments with endogenous partner choice and noisy monitoring.

- source: Bruner, J. P., & Smead, R. (2022). Tag-based spite with correlated interactions. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of observable tags and the possibility of conditioning behavior on tags (e.g., only harming out-group members) fundamentally changes the evolutionary dynamics of costly harming (spite) in the Prisoner's Delight game. Tags make pure (unconditional) spite less likely to evolve even when anti-correlation would otherwise favor it, but make conditional (tag-based) spite more likely in some regimes. Several stable equilibria are identified, including cooperative polymorphisms (mostly cooperation, little spite), pure spite, and a novel 'treacherous polymorphism' where spite is directed within groups. The presence of tags generally reduces the prevalence of widespread spiteful behavior, even under conditions that would otherwise favor it. The results are framed in terms of evolutionary stability and behavioral frequencies, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. The model is a two-player game (Prisoner's Delight) with costly harming, not a multi-player public goods game. The main outcomes are the evolutionary frequencies of behavioral types, not group efficiency or total payoff. However, the analysis is relevant for understanding how the structure of punishment-like mechanisms (costly harming, conditional on observable tags) and population structure (correlated/anti-correlated interactions) can affect the prevalence of cooperation and spite. The findings suggest that mechanisms allowing for conditional punishment (e.g., based on group identity) can both promote and suppress harmful behavior, depending on the context. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides indirect theoretical context about how punishment-like behaviors may interact with population structure and signaling, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence for efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments.

- source: Cheng, H. H., & Meng, X. Z. (2023). Evolution of cooperation in multigame with environmental space and delay. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary outcome (dominance or coexistence of cooperation, defection, and environmental space) depends on the mixture of games, the benefit structure of environmental space, and the presence of delay. Large benefits to cooperators from environmental space or reduced benefits to defectors can make pure cooperation the dominant strategy. Large delays can also shift the system from coexistence to pure cooperation. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, but environmental space can function as an indirect punishment by reducing defectors' payoffs. The outcomes are reported as frequencies of strategies, not as group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It does not manipulate or analyze explicit punishment mechanisms, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. However, it may offer indirect insights into how environmental factors or indirect 'punishment' (via reduced benefits to defectors) can affect the prevalence of cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only weakly informative, as it does not address the core dimensions of punishment or efficiency in a directly transferable way.

This stability is linked to how the norm treats justified punishment and cooperation with ill-reputed players. Its results are about the stability of cooperation under different reputation-based norms in indirect reciprocity models, not about payoffs or efficiency. The analysis is adjacent to the prediction task, as it concerns the maintenance of cooperation in the face of errors, but it does not report or model efficiency, group payoff, or surplus. Thus, it should not be used as direct evidence for efficiency prediction, but may provide indirect context about the robustness of cooperation under certain social norms.

- source: Haucap, J., Heldman, C., & Rau, H. A. (2024). Gender and cooperation in the presence of negative externalities. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that the presence of a negative externality (mutual cooperation harms a passive third party) reduces cooperation rates, especially among women. Women are less likely to cooperate when their cooperation harms others, and this is linked to higher guilt/shame aversion. Social information about the partner (gender, age, semesters studied) does not affect women's cooperation rates but reduces cooperation among men, especially when matched with a woman. There is no evidence of punishment, reward, or sanction mechanisms. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on cooperation rates and psychological correlates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. It is adjacent to the target because it studies a prisoner's dilemma with a negative externality, but there is no punishment or sanction mechanism, and no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported. The findings may inform expectations about gender differences in cooperation when externalities are present, but do not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is all-or-nothing, one-shot, with no communication, and no feedback beyond social information. Thus, its value for the downstream prediction task is limited to contextual insights about cooperation under negative externalities and gender, not about punishment or efficiency.

- source: Garfield, Z. H., Ringen, E. J., Buckner, W., Medupe, D., Wrangham, R. W., & Glowacki, L. (2023). Norm violations and punishments across human societies. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=weak | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that evidence for punishment of norm violations is common across societies, with physical, material, and execution punishments each present in about a third of societies, and reputational punishments somewhat less common. The presence of reputational punishment is associated with egalitarianism and the absence of food storage, while material punishment is associated with food storage and animal husbandry. Execution punishment is moderately associated with social stratification. There is little evidence for strong phylogenetic (cultural history) effects, suggesting that punishment systems adapt quickly to local socioecology. The study does not analyze or report any efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, nor does it use experimental economic games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is relevant for understanding the cross-cultural diversity of punishment systems and their socioecological correlates, but does not report on payoff-based outcomes or experimental manipulations of punishment. Its findings may be contextually informative for understanding the types of punishment that might arise in different socioecological settings, but it does not inform the quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in controlled game environments.
