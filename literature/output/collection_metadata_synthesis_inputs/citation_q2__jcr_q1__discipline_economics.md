- source: Ambrus, A., & Greiner, B. (2019). Individual, Dictator, and Democratic punishment in public good games with perfect and imperfect observability. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper experimentally compares three punishment institutions (individual, democratic, dictator) in repeated 5-person public goods games, under both perfect and imperfect monitoring. Democratic punishment, where a majority vote is required to punish, results in significantly higher average net profits (efficiency) and higher contributions than individual or dictator punishment. This is true in both monitoring conditions, though the efficiency advantage is somewhat reduced under noise. The improvement is due to a sharp reduction in anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators), while pro-social punishment (punishing defectors) is maintained. Dictator punishment does not outperform individual punishment in terms of efficiency. The study provides direct, quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment institution and monitoring noise on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games. It shows that the institutional design of punishment (democratic vs. individual vs. dictator) and the observability of contributions (perfect vs. noisy) are key moderators. Democratic punishment substantially increases efficiency compared to individual punishment, especially by reducing anti-social punishment. However, the efficiency gain is somewhat sensitive to monitoring noise. The paper provides exact values for average net profits under each condition, allowing for quantitative calibration of prediction models. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, observability, etc.) are all clearly specified, making the results highly transferable to similar prediction tasks.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

- source: Bühren, C., & Dannenberg, A. (2021). The demand for punishment to promote cooperation among like-minded people. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games is highly contingent on group type and information. Highly cooperative groups achieve near-optimal payoffs without punishment, and introducing punishment reduces efficiency due to its cost. Uncooperative groups only benefit from severe punishment if they are informed about their own composition and implement the institution early; otherwise, punishment is often misapplied or subverted, leading to low efficiency. Middle cooperators benefit from punishment only in the absence of information about group composition. Information about group cooperativeness allows groups to better match their institution choice to their needs, increasing overall efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment is not uniform and is moderated by group composition and information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games is not a simple function of the game design dimensions alone, but is critically moderated by group composition (cooperativeness) and information about that composition. For prediction tasks, this means that the same punishment institution can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency depending on whether the group is composed of high, middle, or low cooperators, and whether they know this. In the absence of such information, punishment is often over- or under-used, reducing efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction from this paper are the presence and cost of punishment, the severity/technology of punishment, the availability of information about group composition, and the baseline cooperativeness of the group. Control efficiency (no punishment) is a strong predictor: if control efficiency is high, adding punishment reduces efficiency; if control efficiency is low, adding severe punishment with information can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of comprehension and early institution choice for realizing efficiency gains from punishment.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

- source: Riehm, T., Fugger, N., Gillen, P., Gretschko, V., & Werner, P. (2022). Social norms, sanctions, and conditional entry in markets with externalities: Evidence from an artefactual field experiment. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that all-can-win intergroup competition (where multiple groups can be ranked first and avoid penalties) leads to large increases in cooperation and group output across all treatments. The effect is strongest when penalties for not being ranked first are sharp (T1, T2), supporting near-perfect cooperation and benefiting a majority of individuals, though not all. In these cases, average earnings are about the same with and without competition, as severe penalties for a few groups offset gains for the majority. In the weaker-penalty treatment (T3), cooperation increases less, and the effect on earnings is neutral. The results suggest that the design of the penalty function (i.e., the sharpness of incentives) is crucial for both cooperation and efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a rank-based, all-can-win intergroup competition mechanism in a standard linear public goods game can substantially increase cooperation and group output, especially when penalties for low-ranked groups are sharp. However, the effect on efficiency (average earnings) is not always positive: while most individuals benefit, severe penalties for a minority can offset group-level gains. For prediction, the sharpness of the penalty function (punishment tech) and the degree of coordination among groups are key moderators. If the control game (no competition) has low efficiency, introducing sharp all-can-win competition is likely to increase group output and may increase average earnings, but the risk of severe losses for some groups must be considered. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments with intergroup competition as a sanctioning mechanism.

- source: Stoop, J., van Soest, D., & Vyrastekova, J. (2018). Rewards and cooperation in social dilemma games. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that net-positive rewards (where the recipient gains more than the sender loses) dramatically increase cooperation and efficiency in standard public goods games (VCM), with most groups reaching high contribution levels when rewards are available. In contrast, in the Claim game—where players can undo others' contributions by taking from the public pool—rewards have only a marginal effect on cooperation, and average net contributions are not significantly different from zero. In the Claim game, rewards are often exchanged bilaterally and are not strongly linked to cooperative behavior in the social dilemma. The results suggest that the effectiveness of rewards in promoting efficiency depends critically on the structure of the social dilemma, particularly whether others can undo cooperative actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of reward mechanisms in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends on whether the game allows for negative actions (i.e., taking from the public pool) and whether the MPCR is certain. In standard VCMs (linear PGGs), rewards can substantially increase efficiency, but in environments where contributions can be undone (as in the Claim game or CPR games), rewards are much less effective. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a 'take' option or uncertain MPCR is a critical moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency with rewards if the game allows for undoing others' contributions. The findings do not inform about punishment effects, as punishment is not studied.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Fatas, E., Jimenez-Jimenez, F., & Morales, A. J. (2010). Blind Fines in Cooperatives. *Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that a blind, probabilistic punishment mechanism based on group performance can significantly increase average group quality (success ratio) in a cooperative production setting, but only when the cost of contributing high quality is not too high. In the LC treatment, average group quality rises from about 40% to about 70% of the maximum after punishment is introduced, while in the HC treatment, there is no significant improvement. The mechanism does not require individual monitoring, and its effectiveness depends on the underlying cost structure. The main outcome is group quality, which is closely related to efficiency, though not reported as explicit group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a group-level, blind punishment mechanism can substantially increase group performance (as measured by average quality/success ratio) in public-goods-like environments, but only when the cost of cooperation is not too high. For prediction tasks, the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive when the marginal cost of cooperation is low relative to the group benefit, and negligible when costs are high. The design features—no chat, fixed group size (n=4), 20 rounds per block, no individual monitoring, and punishment based on group outcome—are all relevant for mapping to prediction settings. The main limitation is that the outcome is group quality, not direct group payoff, but the two are tightly linked in this design.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

The effect is measured as a reduction in the average number of nets used per person. There is substantial antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators), which can undermine the effectiveness of punishment. However, the effect on efficiency is not directly measured; it must be inferred from extraction levels. The presence of antisocial punishment and lack of norm consensus can limit or even reverse the positive effect of punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency in mixed-group contexts than in homogeneous groups, but the risk of antisocial punishment and unclear norms can reduce or negate efficiency gains. - source: Freytag, A., Güth, W., Koppel, H., & Wangler, L. (2014). Is regulation by milestones efficiency enhancing? An experimental study of environmental protection. *European Journal of Political Economy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether adding intermediate contribution targets ('milestones') to a cumulative threshold public goods game increases efficiency, measured by group payoffs and the probability of reaching a final target. The game is played in groups of 5 over 6 rounds, with treatments varying the strictness of milestones, the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), and the probability of group loss if targets are missed. The main findings are: (1) In a scenario with no continuous efficiency gains (i.e., only reaching the target matters), strict milestones increase both contributions and the probability of reaching the target, thus enhancing efficiency. (2) In a scenario with continuous efficiency gains (i.e., contributions always increase payoffs), strict milestones actually reduce average contributions and efficiency, as groups focus on meeting milestones rather than maximizing payoffs. (3) In an intermediate scenario, the positive and negative effects cancel out. The effect of milestones is thus context-dependent, and their efficiency impact is not universally positive.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment-like milestone mechanisms on efficiency in public-goods-like games is highly sensitive to the underlying payoff structure. When the only way to generate group payoffs is to reach a threshold (no continuous efficiency gains), strict milestones (punishment for missing intermediate targets) can improve efficiency by increasing coordination and contributions. However, when continuous efficiency gains are present (i.e., contributions always increase payoffs), strict milestones can reduce efficiency, as they may distract from maximizing group welfare. For prediction, this means that the presence and structure of milestone/punishment mechanisms, the marginal per-capita return, and the risk of group loss must all be considered. Control efficiency (without milestones/punishment) is a strong moderator: if control efficiency is already high, milestones may not help and can even hurt. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions in cumulative threshold public goods games with milestone/punishment features.

- source: Kimbrough, E. O., & Rubin, J. (2015). Sustaining Group Reputation. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated trust game with random rematching and group reputation, the introduction of an in-group punishment mechanism (jury) leads to a significant increase in the amount sent by senders, which directly translates to higher total welfare/efficiency. Information sharing about group members' experiences also increases welfare, but mainly by reinforcing positive or negative group reputations (path dependence). The effects of punishment and information sharing are additive rather than superadditive or substitutive. The presence of punishment increases both the amount sent and the amount returned, with the largest gains in the treatment where both punishment and information sharing are present. The effect of punishment is primarily due to deterrence—the threat of being punished is enough to change behavior, not just actual punishment events.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in trust-game-like environments with group reputation and random rematching, enabling a punishment mechanism (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases efficiency (total welfare) relative to a control with punishment disabled. The effect is robust and additive with information sharing. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be higher than with punishment disabled, especially when punishment is feasible and not prohibitively costly. The results are most directly applicable to trust games but are relevant for adjacent public-goods-like settings where group reputation and in-group punishment are possible. The paper also provides evidence that the marginal per-capita return (here, the tripling of the amount sent) is important for welfare, and that communication (cheap talk) alone does not have a significant effect. The design dimensions most critical for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, information sharing, and the structure of the trust game.

  findings: The model shows that introducing sanctions (punishment) can increase efficiency in CPR games by enabling the enforcement of rules that prevent over-extraction (static inefficiency) and/or promote conservation (dynamic inefficiency). The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the strength of the sanctioning mechanism relative to the incentives to deviate from cooperation. When sanctions are strong enough, the first-best (fully efficient) outcome can be achieved. With limited sanctions, only partial improvements are possible, and sometimes increasing the resource's value or growth rate can make enforcement harder, reducing efficiency. If sanctions are strong enough relative to the incentives to deviate, enabling rules can be enforced and efficiency increases. If not, efficiency may not improve, or may even decrease if resource value/growth increases incentives to defect. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the logic of how punishment affects efficiency is highly transferable. The paper is especially informative for settings where the punishment mechanism is exogenous and bounded, and where resource dynamics (growth, conservation) are important. For prediction, the model suggests that the effect of enabling punishment depends on whether the sanctioning parameters cross key thresholds relative to the game's other parameters.

- source: Barron, D., & Guo, Y. N. (2021). THE USE AND MISUSE OF COORDINATED PUNISHMENTS. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in relational/trust games with coordinated punishment, the possibility of extortion (i.e., agents threatening to trigger punishment unless paid, regardless of their effort) can completely undermine cooperation and efficiency. In the baseline model with extortion, equilibrium effort and surplus are zero. However, if institutions can tie the ability to trigger punishment (leverage) to actual effort—such as through public signals of effort or strong dyadic relationships—then positive effort and efficiency can be restored, though often at the cost of rents to agents or the need for strong relationships. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not universally positive: it depends critically on whether misuse/extortion is possible and whether institutional features can prevent it.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated cooperation games is highly sensitive to the possibility of extortion and the institutional context. If punishment can be misused (e.g., via action-contingent threats), enabling punishment may reduce efficiency to zero. However, if the game design includes mechanisms that tie punishment power to actual cooperative behavior (e.g., public effort signals, strong dyadic relationships), punishment can support high efficiency. For prediction, this means that the presence or absence of extortion opportunities, and the presence of institutional safeguards, are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. Game design dimensions that affect the ability to make credible threats, the observability of effort, and the structure of relationships are especially important.
