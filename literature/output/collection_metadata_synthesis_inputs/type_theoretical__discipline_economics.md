  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment, when feasible and not prohibitively costly, enables groups to sustain full contribution to public goods and achieve high efficiency, even in large groups. The model provides conditions under which peer discipline is feasible and optimal, and shows that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on the cost and effectiveness of the punishment and audit technology. The model also shows that, in the absence of peer punishment, only small groups can achieve efficient outcomes, while large groups fail to contribute. The results are robust to various extensions, including renegotiation, spillover costs, and generalized auditing procedures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling peer punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that punishment and audit costs are not too high. The model gives explicit formulas for group utility as a function of game parameters, including player count, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in larger groups and when the punishment technology is effective and not too costly. The model also highlights the importance of the cost structure of punishment and auditing, and the quality of monitoring signals, for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment.

- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

- source: Huang, S. T., Dong, Y. L., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). THE OPTIMAL INCENTIVE IN PROMOTING COOPERATION: PUNISH THE WORST AND DO NOT ONLY REWARD THE BEST. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

- source: Acemoglu, D., & Wolitzky, A. (2021). A THEORY OF EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW. *Economic Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods environment, the introduction of coercive punishment (by a state or elite) increases total group output and can increase welfare, but also increases inequality when punishment is unequally applied (elite domination). When punishment is applied equally to all (equality before the law), both effort and welfare increase further, and inequality is reduced. For non-elites, welfare is single-peaked in the level of coercive punishment: some punishment increases their welfare by raising group output, but too much punishment reduces it by forcing excessive effort. The model predicts that limits on the extent of coercion, higher marginal returns to effort, a larger elite group, and greater political power for non-elites all make equality before the law (and thus higher efficiency) more likely. The effect of punishment on efficiency thus depends on its distribution and intensity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on both the intensity of punishment and whether it is applied equally to all. If punishment is available but only applied to non-elites, efficiency (total output) can increase, but so does inequality, and non-elites may be worse off if punishment is too harsh. If punishment is applied equally (equality before the law), both efficiency and equality improve, and non-elites are always better off. The model predicts that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the structure of who can punish whom (punishment_tech, show_punishment_id) are key moderators. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is moderate and/or equally applied, but can be negative for non-elites if punishment is too strong or unequally distributed. The model is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with and without punishment, especially when considering the distribution and intensity of punishment.

The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that, for typical lab PGG designs, adding punishment will shift efficiency from low (near zero) to high (near one), unless punishment is extremely costly or the group is very large and inequity aversion is rare. The model also suggests that the marginal effect of punishment is moderated by mpcr and punishment cost.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.
