- source: Wang, X. M., Couto, M. C., Wang, N. Y., An, X. M., Chen, B., Dong, Y. L., Hilbe, C., & Zhang, B. Y. (2023). Cooperation and coordination in heterogeneous populations. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endowment inequality is a significant obstacle to successful coordination in threshold public goods games, reducing the proportion of groups that reach the threshold and thus receive the group reward. Productivity inequality does not have a similar negative effect. Participants tend to coordinate on equal relative contributions, even when this is not payoff-maximizing. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or cooperation. The findings highlight the importance of focal points and fairness heuristics in equilibrium selection, and show that evolutionary game theory models may mis-predict outcomes unless they account for participants' initial expectations and heuristics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design. However, it provides informative context on how inequality (in endowment or productivity) affects coordination and group success in threshold public goods games. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it suggests that baseline efficiency (without punishment) is lower under endowment inequality. This could be relevant for understanding the baseline to which punishment effects would be compared in similar asymmetric games.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect of TPP corruption on efficiency is not measured. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.

- source: Singh, M., & Garfield, Z. H. (2022). Evidence for third-party mediation but not punishment in Mentawai justice. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that, in the Mentawai context, third-party punishment is absent: no cases were found where third parties (outside the victim, their close kin, or spouse) directly punished or demanded penalties from transgressors. Indirect or second-order sanctions by third parties are also not observed. Instead, penalties (tulou) are negotiated and paid primarily to restore dyadic cooperation, with kinship playing a major role in the likelihood and size of payments. Mediation by third parties (especially shamans and elders) is common, particularly in more severe or between-clan disputes, but the mediator's role is to facilitate negotiation, not to enforce punishment. The study suggests that, in this small-scale society, institutionalized penalties serve to repair relationships rather than to enforce group-level norms through punishment. The findings are based on retrospective interviews and ethnographic observation of 302 reliable cases of transgression and response.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The studied context is not a PGG or close variant, and the outcomes are not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that, in small-scale, kin-based societies, third-party punishment is rare or absent, and penalties serve to restore dyadic cooperation rather than to enforce group norms or increase group efficiency. If extrapolating to PGG-like settings, the results would suggest that the presence or absence of third-party punishment may depend on the social structure and the nature of cooperation (private vs. public goods), but the paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes. Thus, it is of limited use for the downstream prediction task, except as a caution that punishment mechanisms may not always function as assumed in small-scale, kin-based societies.

- source: Bar-On, K. K., & Lamm, E. (2023). The interplay of social identity and norm psychology in the evolution of human groups. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper proposes that norm psychology (the internalization of social norms) and social identity theory (the internalization of group identity) are deeply intertwined processes that together shape human norm-following behaviour. It suggests that norm adherence is flexible and context-dependent, influenced by both personal commitment to norms and the desire to maintain social identity. Punishment of norm violators is discussed as a social process (e.g., gossip, humour) that reinforces group identity and norm compliance. However, the paper does not address efficiency, payoff, or public goods game outcomes, nor does it provide empirical evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctions in economic games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not discuss game design dimensions, control or treatment efficiency, or economic outcomes. Its relevance is limited to high-level theoretical framing about the psychological and social mechanisms underlying norm enforcement and group behaviour. It may inform background understanding of why punishment and norm enforcement are important in human groups, but it does not provide actionable evidence for the downstream prediction task.
