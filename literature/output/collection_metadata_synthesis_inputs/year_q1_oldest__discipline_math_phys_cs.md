- source: Cressman, R., Song, J. W., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2012). Cooperation and evolutionary dynamics in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

- source: Zhuang, Q., Wang, D., Fan, Y., & Di, Z. R. (2012). Evolution of cooperation in a heterogeneous population with influential individuals. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: In a spatial PGG with influential individuals, both punishment and reward mechanisms (administered by influential individuals) can promote cooperation and increase mean payoff compared to the baseline with no such interventions. Rewarding cooperators is more effective than punishing defectors in terms of both promoting cooperation and increasing mean payoff, especially at low multiplication factors (low MPCR). The cost of punishment or reward is paid by society, not by the influential individuals. The presence of influential individuals as mere exemplars (always cooperating, no sanctioning power) actually reduces cooperation and does not improve efficiency. The model demonstrates that, for a given intervention cost, reward leads to higher group efficiency than punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct simulation evidence that, in a spatial PGG with influential individuals, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (mean payoff) compared to the baseline with no punishment, but enabling reward increases efficiency even more. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, but smaller than that of reward, for a given cost. The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. The model is theoretical/simulation-based, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted with caution.

- source: Wang, Y. F., Nakao, A., Vasilakos, A. V., & Ma, J. H. (2011). On the effectiveness of service differentiation based resource-provision incentive mechanisms in dynamic and autonomous P2P networks. *Computer Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: In dynamic, anonymous P2P environments modeled as public goods games, traditional punishment- and reward-based service differentiation mechanisms fail to sustain cooperation or high efficiency due to the presence of 'cooperator' mutants who contribute but do not punish or reward. Introducing a small entry fee and allowing voluntary participation enables the punishment-based scheme to achieve high average payoff and system efficiency, with punishers dominating the population most of the time. Reward-based schemes, even with entry fees, are less effective and more unstable, with defectors occupying the system more frequently. The effect is robust to group size, total population, and punishment cost. The average payoff in the punishment-based scheme approaches the ideal (but unfeasible) case of a reward-based scheme where rewarders can selectively reward only other rewarders and incur zero cost, but such selective rewarding is not feasible in anonymous, dynamic settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods game environments with anonymous, dynamic, and voluntary participation, punishment-based mechanisms with a small entry fee are much more effective at sustaining high efficiency (average payoff) than reward-based mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the control (no-punishment) condition is dominated by defectors. The results suggest that, if the game allows for voluntary entry and a small entry fee, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of group size, population size, or moderate changes in punishment cost. However, if there is no entry fee, neither punishment nor reward mechanisms are effective. The paper's results are most applicable to settings with anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, and may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

  findings: The paper finds that strong reciprocity—cooperation combined with costly punishment of defectors—can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, even when repeated interaction is unlikely. Theoretical results show that the ability to inflict punishment at low cost (high punishment effectiveness) and the presence of a sufficient fraction of strong reciprocators can induce cooperation among self-interested agents, raising group fitness. The model predicts that punishment increases group efficiency, especially when the cost to punishers is low and the impact on defectors is high. Laboratory evidence is reviewed to support these claims, showing that allowing punishment in public goods experiments prevents the decay of cooperation and increases average contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially when punishment is effective (high impact per cost) and not too costly for punishers. The model identifies key moderators: group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the probability of future interaction. The reviewed experimental evidence (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000) shows that punishment prevents the decline of cooperation and can even increase it to near full cooperation, implying a positive effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment will have a more positive effect on efficiency when the punishment technology is strong and the cost is low, and that group size and the structure of repeated interaction also matter.

- source: Wolff, I. (2012). Retaliation and the role for punishment in the evolution of cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with punishment and the possibility of retaliation, punishment can help induce partially cooperative equilibria and temporarily increase cooperation and group payoff, especially when mutation is present. However, punishment cannot sustain full cooperation in the presence of even small mutation rates, as the costs of punishment and the threat of retaliation allow non-punishing cooperators and defectors to invade. The efficiency gains from punishment are thus limited and parameter-dependent: punishment is most effective at intermediate continuation probabilities and when retaliation is not too strong. At high continuation probabilities or when retaliation is easy, punishment is driven out and cannot maintain high efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in repeated public goods games is highly sensitive to the possibility of retaliation and the evolutionary dynamics of strategy mutation. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, one should consider whether retaliation is possible, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the continuation probability (analogous to number of rounds or game length), and the presence of mutation or experimentation. Punishment can increase efficiency in some cases, but its effect is undermined by retaliation and high continuation probability. The model suggests that punishment is most likely to improve efficiency when it is effective (high punishment impact per cost), retaliation is costly or rare, and the game is not too long. If retaliation is easy and the game is long, punishment may not improve and may even reduce efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, allowing anti-social punishment (punishment of cooperators) prevents the evolution of cooperation in public goods games with costly punishment. In models where only pro-social punishment is possible, cooperation and punishment can co-evolve, leading to high group efficiency. However, when anti-social punishment is included, defecting strategies that punish others become dominant, and cooperation is not stable. This result holds across a wide range of parameter values and is robust to small behavioral errors. The findings highlight that the efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the absence of anti-social punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly contingent on the type of punishment allowed. If anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, as cooperation is not stable and defectors who punish others dominate. In contrast, if only pro-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of anti-social punishment as a design dimension is critical: if anti-social punishment is allowed, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment; if it is not allowed, efficiency gains are possible under the right parameter regimes.

For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.
