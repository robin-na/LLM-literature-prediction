- source: Engel, C. (2019). When Does Transparency Backfire? Putting Jeremy Bentham's Theory of General Prevention to the Experimental Test. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment manipulates the level of feedback about punishment and contributions in a standard linear public goods game with centralized punishment. Three feedback treatments (low, medium, high) vary the precision of information about others' contributions and punishment. The main finding is that full transparency (high treatment: individual-level information about both contributions and punishment) leads to significantly lower contributions than aggregate or less precise feedback. Information about punishment of others does not increase cooperation; instead, it can reduce it. Participants are more responsive to descriptive norms (others' contributions) than to injunctive signals (punishment). The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable, but reports total earnings descriptively.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with centralized punishment, increasing transparency about individual punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation (contributions), contrary to standard deterrence theory. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment with high transparency may not increase efficiency and could even reduce it, especially if the control (no punishment) game already has moderate contributions. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to contribution behavior. The design dimensions most relevant here are the feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), centralized punishment, and the linear PGG structure.

- source: De Geest, L. R., & Kingsley, D. C. (2021). Inequality and Peer Punishment in a Common-Pool Resource Experiment. *Strategic Behavior and the Environment*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Peer punishment in a nonlinear CPR game reduces extractions in both equal and unequal endowment settings, but its effect on efficiency (group earnings relative to the social optimum) depends on the distribution of endowments. In the equal endowment treatment, punishment leads to lower earnings due to high punishment costs and lack of coordination. In the unequal endowment treatment, punishment does not reduce average earnings and increases earnings for low-endowment participants, as groups coordinate around the Equal Earnings norm. Punishment is more targeted and less frequent in the unequal treatment, leading to higher efficiency. The effect of punishment is thus moderated by the presence of endowment inequality, which creates a focal point for coordination and more effective norm enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in CPR/public goods-like games depends on the distribution of endowments (inequality). When endowments are equal, punishment can reduce efficiency due to high punishment costs and poor coordination. When endowments are unequal, punishment is more effective, leading to higher efficiency, especially for low-endowment participants, as groups coordinate around a salient norm. For prediction, the presence of endowment heterogeneity should be considered a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when inequality is present and salient. Other design features (e.g., punishment technology, group size, number of rounds) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable insight is about the interaction between punishment and endowment inequality.

- source: Amirova, I., Petrick, M., & Djanibekov, N. (2022). Investment traps and resilience to shocks: An experimental study of Central Asian collective water governance. *Irrigation and Drainage*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat
  findings: The experiment found multiple stable equilibria (lock-ins) in collective investment in irrigation infrastructure. Communication (chat) treatments increased collective investment and made high-investment equilibria more likely, but did not guarantee them. Penalty (punishment) treatments, both low and high, reduced collective investment compared to baseline, contrary to theoretical expectations. The penalty treatments crowded out intrinsic motivation to cooperate, resulting in lower investment levels and less resilience to shocks. The main outcome is collective investment, which is a behavioral proxy for group efficiency, but the paper does not report explicit group payoffs or efficiency ratios.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public-good-like field experiment with real farmers, introducing punishment (penalties) can reduce collective investment and thus lower efficiency-related outcomes, compared to a baseline with no punishment. Communication (chat) increases investment, but does not guarantee high efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in similar field settings may decrease efficiency, especially when intrinsic motivation is important. The main outcome is collective investment, not explicit group payoff, but the relationship is close. The findings are most relevant for field settings with real-world participants and repeated games with investment as the public good.

- source: Herne, K., Kuyper, J., & Lappalainen, O. (2023). The Mode of Communication as a Driver of Sustainable and Equitable Asymmetric Common Pool Resource Use. *Environmental Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that both unstructured and structured (deliberative) communication robustly increase group contributions and the efficiency of resource generation in an asymmetric common pool resource game, compared to a no-communication baseline. Deliberation (structured communication) reduces the impact of player position on contributions, leading to more equitable participation, but does not significantly increase total efficiency or equality of extraction compared to unstructured communication. The study does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that communication—both unstructured and structured—substantially increases efficiency in asymmetric common pool resource games relative to a no-communication baseline. However, it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, so it does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The design and results are highly relevant for understanding the effects of communication and deliberation, but not for punishment. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly informative, though it does establish a high-efficiency baseline for games with communication.

- source: Gomez-Ruiz, L., & Sánchez-Expósito, M. J. (2020). The Impact of Team Identity and Gender on Free-Riding Responses to Fear and Cooperation Sustainability. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that strong team identity significantly reduces free-riding responses to fear and increases cooperation rates among men, but not among women. The effect of team identity on cooperation sustainability (maintenance of cooperation over time) is marginally significant for men and not significant for women. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. No formal punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on informal control (team identity) and gender differences. The findings suggest that team identity can be an effective informal mechanism to sustain cooperation among men in repeated social dilemmas with binary contribution choices and feedback, but not among women.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It studies a repeated binary-choice social dilemma (close to a public goods game) without any punishment or reward mechanism. The main manipulations are team identity and gender. The outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. Therefore, this paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it does provide contextual evidence about the role of informal mechanisms (team identity) and gender in sustaining cooperation. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation rates in binary-choice, feedback-rich, no-punishment settings, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Ferguson, E. (2021). Inequality averse and compassionate blood donor: implication for interventions. *Vox Sanguinis*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Blood donors are more likely to choose compensation (directly helping the victim) over punishment (sanctioning the perpetrator) in a one-shot 3PPC game, while organ donors are more likely to choose punishment. The compensation and punishment options are both costly to the third party, and the game is designed so that each £1 spent on compensation or punishment has a 2x effect. The study does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, but describes how different choices affect the distribution of payoffs. A follow-up experiment finds that an intervention message based on advantageous inequality aversion increases non-donors' willingness to take information about blood donation, with some evidence that guilt-based appeals may have negative effects on current donors.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about individual preferences for compensation versus punishment in a third-party context, which is adjacent to public goods games with punishment. The design is a one-shot, three-player game with both punishment and compensation options, and the main outcomes are behavioral choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the presence of a compensation option may reduce the use of punishment, and that individuals' philanthropic identities (e.g., blood donor vs. organ donor) influence their preference for direct help versus norm enforcement. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in repeated public goods games, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited and indirect.

- source: Qirko, H. (2020). Altruistic punishment in modern intentional communities. *Interaction Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that, in modern intentional communities, free-riding is often acknowledged as a problem and most communities have rules or norms to address it. However, only about half of the communities report actually enforcing these rules through third-party punishment, and even then, enforcement is context-dependent and often flexible. The data do not support the existence of a strong, inherited predisposition to engage in costly third-party punishment. Instead, punishment behavior appears to be shaped by contextual, self-maximizing considerations. No direct measures of group efficiency, welfare, or payoff are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It suggests that, in real-world cooperative groups with explicit rules and opportunities for punishment, actual use of costly third-party punishment is infrequent and context-dependent. This implies that the presence of punishment mechanisms does not guarantee their use or an increase in efficiency, especially outside of laboratory or game-like settings. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The findings are most useful for understanding the limits of external validity for lab-based punishment effects and for highlighting the importance of contextual moderators.

- source: Suzuki, K., & Ishiwata, R. (2022). Impact of a Carbon Tax on Energy Transition in a Deregulated Market: A Game-Based Experimental Approach. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study used a 4-player, 30-round online game simulating energy companies in a deregulated market, with and without a carbon tax (punishment for fossil fuel use). The carbon tax was introduced in the second half of the game and was known to players in advance. The main findings are: (1) The carbon tax significantly increased renewable energy usage after its implementation, accelerating the energy transition. (2) Total group profit (efficiency) was higher with the tax, but the difference was not statistically significant. (3) The tax did not significantly affect R&D investment or market price in aggregate, but did increase market price after the tax was imposed. (4) Information about future taxation alone did not change behavior or reduce uncertainty; only actual taxation affected actions. (5) Players' perceptions of uncertainty and obstacles to transition were not reduced by the tax. Overall, the carbon tax improved efficiency-related outcomes by promoting renewables and increasing group profit, but only after its implementation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism (carbon tax) in a competitive, multi-player, repeated game increases group efficiency (total profit) and accelerates the transition to socially optimal behavior (renewable energy use). The effect is most pronounced after the punishment is actually implemented, not merely announced. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in similar games is likely to increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is salient and directly affects payoffs. The game structure is adjacent to a public goods game, with competition and investment choices, so the findings are most applicable to PGG variants with market competition and investment dynamics. The effect size on efficiency is positive but may not always reach statistical significance, especially in small samples.

- source: Wei, J., Zhong, Z. W., Chen, H. R., Arango-Aramburo, S., & Zhao, X. L. (2025). Exploring green electricity certificate purchasing behavior via a laboratory experiment. *Renewable & Sustainable Energy Reviews*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether job rotation can reduce the ratchet effect in a principal-agent setting. Three treatments are compared: no rotation (baseline), exogenous rotation (forced switch), and endogenous rotation (principal can pay to switch). In the baseline, workers often restrict effort to avoid revealing private information, resulting in lower efficiency. Exogenous rotation nearly eliminates the ratchet effect, with almost all workers providing high effort and firms earning significantly more. Endogenous rotation, where the principal can pay a small cost to rotate workers, also reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort, but the increase in firm earnings is not statistically significant. The findings support the idea that job rotation (especially exogenous) can improve efficiency by mitigating strategic output restriction, and that even a costly, non-credible threat of rotation (punishment) can have positive behavioral effects.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment option (here, job rotation) can increase efficiency by reducing strategic shirking, especially when the punishment is exogenously imposed. The effect is weaker but still present when the punishment is endogenous and costly. The experimental design is not a standard public goods game, but the principal-agent structure with costly punishment and efficiency outcomes is closely related. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling punishment (even at a cost) is likely to increase efficiency, with the effect size depending on whether the punishment is exogenous or endogenous and on the costliness of punishment.

- source: Oria, P. A., Wijnands, M., Alaii, J., & Leeuwis, C. (2018). Options for sustaining solar-powered mosquito trapping systems on Rusinga Island, Western Kenya: a social dilemma analysis. *Bmc Public Health*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that community members prefer individual responsibility for maintaining the mosquito trapping systems, with selective support for small, self-selected group savings (not large-scale collective action). Distrust in leaders, fear of misappropriation, and lack of confidence in group sanctions are major barriers to collective solutions. Some success is seen in small, trusted women's savings groups that can impose sanctions (e.g., expelling non-compliant members), but large governing bodies or compulsory group contributions are widely distrusted. The study highlights the importance of trust, group size, and leadership in sustaining public goods, but does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides qualitative, contextual evidence about the challenges of sustaining public goods in real-world settings, especially regarding trust, group size, and sanctioning. It suggests that effective punishment or sanctioning is difficult without strong trust and group identity, and that collective action may fail in large, heterogeneous groups. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental data on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions of treatment efficiency in public goods games with or without punishment. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of social context and group structure as moderators of collective action and sanctioning effectiveness.

- source: Albergaria, M., & Saes, M. S. (2018). Measuring externalities in an information commons: the case of libraries. *Journal of Cleaner Production*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in a library (information commons) setting, users subject to non-monetary sanctions (professors, employees) generate negative externalities for users subject to monetary sanctions (students): an increase in non-student borrowing is associated with a nearly one-to-one decrease in student borrowing. Peer effects are positive but small. The analysis is based on observational data and does not report efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding behavioral externalities and the impact of different sanction types in common-pool resource settings, but do not directly inform about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its findings are limited to behavioral externalities (borrowing counts) in a field setting with different sanction types. While it highlights that sanction type can affect user behavior and externalities, it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the setting is only adjacent to public goods games. Thus, its value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited and indirect.

- source: Hirama, C., Zeng, Z. C., Nawa, N., & Fujiwara, T. (2022). Association between Cooperative Attitude and High-Risk Behaviors on the Spread of COVID-19 Infection among Medical Students in Japan. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*.

  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that students who self-identify as 'cooperative' or 'punishment' types (in a hypothetical task-sharing scenario) are significantly more likely to engage in high-risk behaviors (eating/drinking out) during the COVID-19 pandemic than 'noncooperative' types. There is no significant association between cooperative attitude and vaccine hesitancy. The 'punishment' type is defined as not cooperating and letting the partner do the task alone after being let down, which is interpreted as a desire to punish free riders. The study does not use a public goods game or measure efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The study uses a hypothetical scenario to classify cooperative attitudes, including a 'punishment' type, but does not implement or analyze any economic game, nor does it measure payoff, efficiency, or group outcomes. The findings are about associations between self-reported attitudes and real-world risk behaviors, not about the effect of punishment mechanisms in controlled game settings. Thus, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for the prediction task, though it may provide weak, indirect context about the social meaning of punishment attitudes.

- source: Chang, C. C., Kristensen, N. P., Nghiem, T. P. L., Tan, C. L. Y., & Carrasco, L. R. (2021). Cooperating with the future through natural resources restoration. *Sustainability Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that all groups overexploited the resource during extraction, but some pools were rescued through restoration. The likelihood of sustaining the resource pool was lower when previous generations were unsustainable. Individuals extracted more and had higher final payoffs when inheriting from unsustainable previous generations. Restoration behavior was positively associated with feelings of guilt and anger, but not with cognitive style. Deliberate (vs. intuitive) thinkers extracted more and had higher payoffs. The study does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, so it does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions in public-goods-game-like environments. It is not informative for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or analyzed. The findings are only indirectly relevant, as they pertain to restoration and intergenerational reciprocity, not to punishment or its efficiency effects.
