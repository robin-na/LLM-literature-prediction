- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in TPGG-like environments, especially when punishment is endogenous, low-cost, and shared.   findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.
