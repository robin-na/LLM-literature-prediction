- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in both one-shot and iterated public goods games with a punishment option, individuals who decide to punish quickly invest more in punishment, especially if they contributed more than their peers and feel highly upset. The spontaneous punishment effect is not moderated by dispositional pro-sociality (SVO), but by situational factors (above-average contribution and negative affect). The study does not analyze or report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoffs, so it does not provide evidence on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on the psychological and situational determinants of punishment behavior in public goods games, but does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. However, it does confirm that punishment is used more by above-average contributors and those experiencing negative affect, and that the cost-to-impact ratio of punishment is 1:4. The game design is a standard PGG with continuous contributions, four players, two rounds (Study 1) or one round (Study 2), no chat, and full information about others' contributions. The absence of efficiency or payoff analysis limits the paper's direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that in environments with time-varying productivity or delayed punishment, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and by extension, likely on efficiency) is weakened. For prediction, this paper supports the idea that punishment is less effective in unstable or delayed settings, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

- source: Gavrilets, S. (2021). Coevolution of actions, personal norms and beliefs about others in social dilemmas. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.

  findings: The model predicts that, in repeated public goods and related games, the long-term average group action and payoff converge to the Nash equilibrium based on material payoffs when there is no external authority. Social and cognitive factors (such as conformity, cognitive dissonance, and beliefs about others) affect the distribution and variance of actions and beliefs, but do not shift the group average away from the material-payoff optimum in the absence of authority. When an external authority promotes a particular action, group behavior can shift toward the promoted standard, sometimes increasing or decreasing efficiency depending on the alignment of the promoted action with the material optimum. The model does not include explicit peer punishment or sanctioning as a treatment variable, so it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a theoretical baseline for predicting efficiency in public goods and related games without explicit punishment mechanisms. It shows that, absent external authority or explicit sanctions, efficiency is determined by material payoffs, and social/cognitive factors mainly affect variance and convergence speed. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that, unless punishment is modeled as a material cost for non-conformity (as in the 'disapproval by peers' term), enabling punishment would require additional modeling or empirical evidence. The model is most useful for predicting control (no-punishment) efficiency and for understanding the role of soft normative pressures, but does not provide direct evidence for the effect of enabling explicit punishment on efficiency.

- source: Gavrilets, S., & Shrestha, M. D. (2021). Evolving institutions for collective action by selective imitation and self-interested design. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based models of collective action with institutionalized punishment by leaders, both selective imitation and self-interested design (foresight) in leaders can lead to the emergence of effective institutions that increase cooperation, group production, and payoffs (efficiency). The presence of leaders who monitor and punish free-riders increases group effort and payoffs compared to groups without leaders or punishment. The effect is strongest when the benefit-to-cost ratio is high, punishment is not too costly, and leaders value future payoffs. The model also shows that the details of the updating mechanism (imitation vs. foresight) matter most when the benefit-to-cost ratio is intermediate and leaders heavily discount the future. In 'us vs. nature' games, increased monitoring and production lead to higher payoffs for all; in 'us vs. them' games, overproduction can reduce payoffs for subordinates. The model provides direct evidence that institutionalized punishment can increase efficiency in public-goods-like settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling institutionalized punishment by leaders in public-goods-like games increases group efficiency and payoffs, especially when the benefit-to-cost ratio is high and punishment is not too costly. The effect is robust to variations in group size, punishment cost, and the leader's share of rewards. For prediction tasks, the presence of leader-administered punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment), with the magnitude depending on the benefit-to-cost ratio, punishment cost, and whether leaders value future payoffs. The model also suggests that the effect of punishment is less positive or even negative if punishment is very costly or if leaders do not value future payoffs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in games with institutionalized punishment, especially when the control efficiency is low due to free-riding.

For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of punishment on efficiency is negative when control efficiency is high, and less negative or possibly weakly positive when control efficiency is low, but the overall efficiency with punishment rarely exceeds the cooperative baseline. - source: Chávez, C. A., Murphy, J. J., & Stranlund, J. K. (2018). Managing and defending the commons: Experimental evidence from TURFs in Chile. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
