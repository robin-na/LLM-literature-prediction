- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The chapter reviews evidence that, in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment opportunities (as in Fehr and Gächter 2000) leads to higher contribution rates and prevents the typical decay of cooperation seen in repeated games without punishment. It explains these results using models of social preferences, especially inequality aversion. However, the chapter does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes directly. The main findings are that punishment increases cooperation (contribution rates) and that social preference models can explain both altruistic and selfish behaviors depending on the context (e.g., presence of competition).
  prediction_guidance: This chapter provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation, which is often associated with higher efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the chapter supports the qualitative expectation that punishment increases cooperation, but does not allow for precise prediction of efficiency changes. The evidence is most useful for understanding mechanisms and for qualitative directionality, not for quantitative prediction.

- source: Vincent, A. (2017). Punishment and status in collective action: How status hierarchies foster optimal punishment use. *Sociology Compass*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that status hierarchies can endogenously coordinate punishment in public goods games, leading to more optimal (i.e., less costly and more effective) use of punishment. High status actors are more likely to be expected to punish when punishment is seen as group-oriented, and their punishment is more likely to promote subsequent cooperation and reduce the need for further punishment. The author suggests that these dynamics can help groups realize public goods more efficiently, but this is a theoretical claim rather than an empirically demonstrated effect. The paper also discusses why typical experimental designs may fail to observe these effects, due to their suppression of status emergence and communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical arguments and literature synthesis suggesting that the presence of status hierarchies and the assignment of punishment responsibility to high status actors could improve the efficiency of punishment in public goods games by reducing unnecessary or counterproductive punishment and fostering cooperation. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game designs allowing status differentiation, communication, or leadership roles may see more efficient punishment use, but this is not quantified. The paper is less useful for direct prediction of efficiency from game design dimensions in the absence of empirical data.

The design and results may be contextually informative for understanding baseline cooperation rates in the absence of punishment, but do not inform the efficiency impact of enabling punishment.

- source: Tang, T. Z., & Ye, H. (2016). The Blessing of Sexuality: Evolution of Altruism with Mating Preference. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.

  findings: The simulation results show that altruistic punishment alone does not sustain high levels of cooperation; defectors tend to dominate unless sexual attractiveness (mating preference for altruists) is present. When both punishment and sexual attractiveness are enabled, altruists (cooperators and punishers) become dominant, and the mean altruistic investment approaches its maximum. Variable population size and individual variation in investment further amplify the prevalence of altruism. The model is a close variant of a public goods game, but the main outcomes are population composition and mean investment, not efficiency or group payoff. Thus, the findings are most relevant for predicting the conditions under which punishment can sustain high cooperation, but less directly informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment, when combined with additional incentives (such as sexual attractiveness or mating preference for altruists), can lead to high levels of cooperation in public-goods-like environments. However, in the absence of such additional incentives, punishment alone may not sustain cooperation. The model does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes from punishment. Instead, it suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may depend on the presence of complementary mechanisms that reward altruists. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment's positive effect on efficiency is conditional and may be weak or absent without additional incentives.

- source: Asgharpourmasouleh, A., Sadeghi, A., & Yousofi, A. (2017). A Grounded Agent-Based Model of Common Good Production in a Residential Complex: Applying Artificial Experiments. *Sage Open*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The agent-based model simulates a common good game in a residential complex, with agent strategies grounded in qualitative fieldwork. The model shows that in blocks with more defectors, common good production and payoffs collapse. Artificial experiments demonstrate that (1) introducing punishers in place of defectors, (2) increasing all agents' desirability for the common good, and (3) combining both interventions, all increase common good production and total payoffs. The most dramatic improvement occurs when both punishers are introduced and desirability is maximized, resulting in all agents adopting punishing strategies and achieving the highest payoffs and common good production. Thus, punishment mechanisms, especially when agents value the common good, can transform a low-efficiency, low-payoff equilibrium into a high-efficiency, high-payoff one.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (and increasing the number of punishers) in a public-goods-like environment can increase efficiency and group payoffs, especially when agents also value the common good. The model is not a standard PGG but is structurally similar, with continuous contributions, punishment, and payoff functions analogous to PGGs. The results suggest that, all else equal, introducing punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in settings where baseline cooperation is fragile or low. The effect is amplified if agents' intrinsic motivation (desirability) for the common good is high. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low.

- source: Gaudeul, A., Crosetto, P., & Riener, G. (2017). Better stuck together or free to go? Of the stability of cooperation when individuals have outside options. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment varied the ease of exit from a public project (barriers to exit) in a repeated, two-player, stochastic public-good-like game. When exit was easier (lower barriers), participants exited more frequently, but this did not increase efficiency or average payoffs. In fact, average individual welfare was lower in treatments with lower barriers to exit. The main reason was excessive exit, driven by pessimistic beliefs about the peer's likelihood of leaving, rather than accurate cost-benefit analysis. The study finds that exit is used both as a response to perceived exploitation and as a punishment for failure, but these mechanisms do not improve group efficiency. The findings suggest that making exit easier undermines group stability and reduces welfare, contrary to some theoretical expectations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-good-like environments with imperfect monitoring and stochastic outcomes, making exit easier (i.e., lowering barriers to exit) reduces efficiency and average payoffs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of exit options (a form of sanction or punishment via exclusion/self-exclusion) on efficiency. The results suggest that, unless exit is tightly controlled or costly, its availability will likely decrease group welfare due to excessive and inefficient use. For prediction tasks, the presence and ease of exit should be treated as a negative moderator of efficiency, even when the control (no-exit) game already has moderate efficiency. The study does not test classic costly punishment (e.g., deduction points), but its findings on exit as a sanction are highly informative for settings where exit or exclusion is a possible response to non-cooperation.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: Antoci, A., & Zarri, L. (2015). Punish and perish?. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary success and efficiency impact of punishment in a prisoner's dilemma with explicit punishment and reward options depend on the type of strong reciprocity present. Altruistic punishment of defectors (SNR) can support cooperation and high average payoff, but only if punishers are sufficiently numerous; otherwise, defectors dominate and efficiency collapses. Altruistic rewarding (SPR) leads to persistent coexistence of types and fluctuating efficiency. Symmetric strong reciprocity (punishing defectors and rewarding cooperators) is maladaptive: it leads to the extinction of cooperators and punishers, resulting in universal defection and minimal efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (PNP) or both defectors and non-punishing cooperators (HSNR) can robustly enforce cooperation and maintain high efficiency, with the PNP strategy being especially robust. The presence of unconditional cooperators who do not punish can undermine the evolutionary stability of punishment-based cooperation. The model shows that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universally positive and can be negative or fragile depending on the sanctioning structure and population heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments is highly contingent on the details of the punishment and reward mechanisms and the behavioral heterogeneity of the population. In prediction tasks, the presence of explicit reward options, the type of punishment (who is punished), and the initial population composition are critical moderators. Punishment of defectors alone can increase efficiency if enough punishers are present, but this equilibrium is fragile. Adding reward can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (second-order free riders) is more robust in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is essential to consider not just the presence of punishment but also the structure of available sanctions and rewards, and the possibility of second-order free riding. The findings caution against assuming that enabling punishment will always increase efficiency; under some conditions, it can be maladaptive.

- source: Nasrallah, W. F., & Cheaib, K. A. (2016). An equilibrium model of how regulative and normative institutions influence micro-economic and organizational behavior. *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The theta-pi model demonstrates that both punishment (regulative institutions) and empathy (normative institutions) can increase the efficiency of economic interactions by expanding the set of transactions that are mutually beneficial and deterring predation. The model provides explicit conditions under which markets are efficient (all cooperate), collapse (all defect or abstain), or are path-dependent/oscillatory. Punishment (pi) is especially effective in high-temptation, low-sucker-loss environments, while empathy (theta) is more effective when losses from being cheated are high. When some actors are exempt from punishment (elites), efficiency declines and can collapse if the exempt group is too large. Overlapping institutions and partial coverage can lead to complex dynamics, including cycles of cooperation and predation. The model quantifies the marginal value of increasing institutional strength and provides phase diagrams for different transaction types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (increasing pi) in a public-goods-like environment will increase efficiency, especially when the temptation to defect is high and the cost to victims is not overwhelming. The model clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment depends on its universality and the structure of payoffs, and that partial or selective enforcement can undermine efficiency. The findings suggest that, all else equal, increasing the probability or effectiveness of punishment will shift the equilibrium from market collapse or low efficiency to high efficiency, provided the institutional coverage is broad. The model also highlights that empathy-based mechanisms can substitute for punishment in some environments, but are less effective when temptation is high and losses are low. For prediction tasks, the model's phase diagrams and threshold conditions can be mapped to game design dimensions such as punishment cost, effectiveness, and universality, supporting the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency in public-goods-like games.

The mechanism is that agents develop reputations for toughness, deterring defection and reducing the need for actual fighting. While efficiency is not directly measured, the increase in mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant outcome) suggests that efficiency would also increase. The results are most relevant for coordination games adjacent to public goods games, rather than standard PGGs. - source: Koenig, B. L., & Riley, C. M. (2017). To what reference point do people calibrate cost-free, third-party punishment?. *Personality and Individual Differences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: Across three experiments, participants assigned preferred punishments (fines) to a perpetrator in a theft scenario, with victim loss and perpetrator gain manipulated independently. Both victim loss and perpetrator gain influenced punishment preferences, with participants tending to select the larger of the two as a reference point.   prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. However, it is informative about the psychological calibration of punishment preferences in third-party contexts, showing that people often prefer punishments that more than offset both victim loss and perpetrator gain. This suggests that, in environments where third-party punishment is possible and costless, punishment levels may be set high, potentially deterring transgressions more strongly than would be predicted by models based solely on harm or gain. The findings are most relevant for understanding the psychological underpinnings of punishment, not for quantitative prediction of efficiency in PGG-like games.

- source: D'Exelle, B., & Riedl, A. (2013). Resource Allocations and Disapproval Voting in Unequal Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Disapproval voting as a punishment mechanism in unequal groups does not consistently improve efficiency or prosocial allocations. Instead, its effectiveness is highly sensitive to institutional details. When villagers have only private information about their share and voting is public, disapproval rates are low and representatives often use a minimal coalition (exclude-one) strategy, which avoids disapproval and maintains higher group earnings, though at the cost of excluding the poorest villager. When allocations are common knowledge or voting is secret, disapproval rates are higher, leading representatives to frequently switch to the exclude-all strategy, which is inefficient as it maximizes the representative's payoff at the expense of the group. The punishment mechanism can thus backfire, reducing efficiency, especially when disapproval is frequent. The study highlights the importance of information structure and voting rules in moderating the effects of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the effect of punishment mechanisms (here, disapproval voting) on efficiency in resource allocation games is not universally positive and depends critically on the design dimensions, especially information disclosure and the publicity of voting. In settings where punishment is possible but disapproval is rare (private information, public voting), efficiency is higher, but this is achieved by excluding the least powerful group member. In settings where punishment is more likely (common information, secret voting), efficiency is lower due to frequent costly disapproval and representatives' defensive strategies. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effect is moderated by how information and punishment are structured.
