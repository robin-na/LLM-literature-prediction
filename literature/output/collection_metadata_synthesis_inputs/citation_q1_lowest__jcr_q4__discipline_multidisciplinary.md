- source: Selterman, D. (2019). Altruistic Punishment in the Classroom: An Update on the Tragedy of the Commons Extra Credit Question. *Teaching of Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that adding an altruistic punishment option (students can sacrifice their own points to remove points from a random over-consumer) increases the rate at which groups successfully avoid the 'tragedy of the commons' (i.e., more classes stay under the 10% threshold and receive points) compared to prior years without punishment. However, the study does not directly measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The main findings are that the punishment option increases cooperation (fewer students choose the selfish option), and that students report positive attitudes and increased understanding of social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods dilemma can increase group cooperation and the likelihood of group success (i.e., receiving the reward). However, because the outcome is binary (group gets points or not) and not a continuous efficiency or payoff measure, the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task. The evidence suggests that punishment can improve group outcomes in similar all-or-nothing, threshold-based social dilemmas, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency change. The design is also classroom-based and not a standard repeated PGG, so caution is warranted in generalizing to other settings.

- source: Sequeira, M. E. (2023). Fairness, partner choice, and punishment: An ethnographic study of cooperative behavior among children in Helsinki, Finland. *Ethos*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=weak | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that among Helsinki-based children, fairness is a central value, and children resolve conflicts or perceived unfairness primarily through partner choice strategies (avoiding or disengaging from unfair partners) rather than through punishment. Both children and adults show a strong aversion to punishment, which is rare in both peer and adult-child interactions. Autonomy and independence are emphasized in socialization, and norm enforcement is achieved through persuasion and shaming rather than coercion or punitive measures. The study does not use experimental games or report on efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments, as it is an ethnographic observational study of real-world child interactions, not a controlled experiment or game. However, it does provide strong qualitative evidence that in this context, punishment is rare and not socially valued as a means of sustaining cooperation. If one were to extrapolate, it suggests that in environments with strong norms of autonomy and aversion to punishment, introducing punishment mechanisms may not increase cooperation or efficiency, and may even be resisted or ignored. However, the lack of payoff or efficiency data means this paper cannot be used for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.
