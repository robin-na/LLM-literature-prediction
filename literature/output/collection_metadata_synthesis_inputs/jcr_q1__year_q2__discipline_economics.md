- source: Dickinson, D. L., Masclet, D., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Norm enforcement in social dilemmas: An experiment with police commissioners. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Grechenig, K., & Thöni, C. (2016). Information-sensitive Leviathans. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend strongly on the information environment and the type of punishment institution. Under perfect information, decentralized punishment leads to the highest contributions and profits (efficiency), outperforming centralized punishment and no punishment. As information becomes less accurate (signals about others' contributions are noisy), both contributions and profits decline in all institutions, but especially in DecPun. Under moderate noise, CenPun can attract more citizens and sometimes matches or slightly exceeds DecPun in efficiency, provided the authority avoids punishing cooperators. Under high noise, neither punishment institution yields substantial efficiency gains over no punishment. The key moderator is the extent to which punishment is misapplied to cooperators, which is more likely in DecPun under noise. The paper provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes with punishment enabled versus disabled, across different institutional and informational settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the information environment and the punishment institution's design. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially decentralized) can substantially increase efficiency under perfect information. However, as information becomes noisy, the efficiency gain from punishment is reduced, and the difference between decentralized and centralized punishment narrows. The presence of a central authority who avoids punishing cooperators can maintain or improve efficiency under moderate noise, but not under high noise. For prediction, the paper suggests that the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost/tech, and especially the information structure (accuracy of contribution signals) are critical moderators. The results also imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on these dimensions.

- source: Drouvelis, M., & Grosskopf, B. (2016). The effects of induced emotions on pro-social behaviour. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study uses a one-shot voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) public goods game with and without punishment, manipulating both the announcement of punishment and participants' emotional state (anger vs. happiness). The main findings are: (1) Announcing the punishment stage increases contributions and average net earnings, confirming that punishment can improve efficiency in the short run. (2) Induced anger reduces contributions and increases the severity of punishment, leading to lower average net earnings (i.e., lower efficiency) compared to the happy condition. (3) The efficiency benefit of punishment is present but is diminished when subjects are angry, as harsher punishment increases costs and reduces net group payoffs. (4) The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured directly via average net earnings, and the design allows for clean causal inference due to the one-shot structure and between-subjects manipulation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a one-shot, three-player VCM with punishment, the introduction of a punishment stage (when announced) increases group efficiency (average net earnings) compared to the same game without punishment. The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends on the emotional state of participants: it is larger when subjects are happy and smaller (but still positive) when subjects are angry, due to increased punishment costs. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, controlling for baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the effect can be moderated by factors (such as mood) that affect the severity and frequency of punishment.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., & Masclet, D. (2015). Emotion venting and punishment in public good experiments. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard 4-player, 10-round public goods game with punishment (costly to punisher, more costly to target), the authors introduce several treatments allowing for different levels of emotion venting before punishment decisions: (1) Baseline (no venting), (2) Waiting (5-min cooling-off), (3) Waiting & Emotion (cooling-off plus self-reported emotions and unsent messages), and (4) Virtual Punishment (all previous plus non-binding virtual punishment). All treatments have punishment enabled. The main findings are: (a) All venting treatments reduce the amount and frequency of costly punishment compared to baseline; (b) The simple cooling-off period (Waiting) increases final payoffs (efficiency) relative to baseline, as reduced excessive punishment outweighs any loss in contributions; (c) More extensive venting (Virtual Punishment) reduces both punishment and contributions, and only increases efficiency in the early rounds, not in the long run; (d) The relationship between emotion venting and efficiency is non-linear (inverted-U): moderate venting is optimal for efficiency, while too little or too much venting is suboptimal. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so all results are relative to punishment-enabled baselines.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard lab PGG with punishment, the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the emotional context of punishment decisions. If punishment is enabled without any venting, excessive punishment can reduce efficiency. Introducing a simple cooling-off period (moderate venting) reduces excessive punishment and increases efficiency. However, if venting is too extensive (e.g., virtual punishment), punishment is reduced too much, contributions fall, and efficiency gains are not sustained. For prediction, this suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is maximized when the game design includes a moderate opportunity for emotional cooling-off before punishment, but not when venting is so extensive that it undermines the deterrence function of punishment. The results are most relevant for standard 4-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGGs with punishment enabled and no communication.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2016). Judicial error and cooperation. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that error-free formal sanctions increase both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public goods game. When judicial errors are introduced (either type I or type II), both cooperation and efficiency decline. The effect on efficiency is more negative for type I errors than for type II errors, due to the wasteful punishment of cooperators. The negative effect of errors is stronger when the error probability is higher and when errors convert a deterrent sanction regime into a non-deterrent one. The effects of type I and type II errors on contributions are symmetric, but type I errors reduce group earnings more. Subjects show a clear preference for avoiding type I errors, but this preference is fully explained by income maximization rather than additional psychological aversion.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the introduction of punishment with judicial error reduces efficiency in public goods games, and that the magnitude of the efficiency loss depends on the type and probability of error, as well as the deterrence strength of the sanction regime. For prediction, the findings imply that enabling punishment increases efficiency only if sanctions are deterrent and error-free; otherwise, efficiency may decrease, especially with type I errors or high error probability. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the probability and type of judicial error, and whether sanctions are deterrent. Control efficiency (without punishment) is also reported, allowing for direct comparison.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2014). Self-Organization for Collective Action: An Experimental Study of Voting on Sanction Regimes. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, groups could vote between no sanctions (NS), informal sanctions (IS: peer punishment), and formal sanctions (FS: centralized punishment with fixed and variable costs, deterrent or non-deterrent). Both IS and deterrent FS substantially increased group efficiency and earnings relative to NS. IS were highly cost-effective, yielding higher returns per point spent on sanctions than FS in most conditions. IS were especially effective when chosen by vote, with group earnings and efficiency about 30% higher than when IS were imposed exogenously. Non-deterrent FS also increased efficiency when chosen by vote, but less so than IS. High fixed costs for FS reduced both their efficiency and their popularity. Reducing the effectiveness of IS (increasing cost per unit punishment) decreased their efficiency somewhat, but IS remained popular and relatively effective. Allowing IS and FS to coexist did not substantially change these patterns. Overall, IS can be as or more effective than FS in promoting efficiency, especially when adopted democratically.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with 5 players, enabling informal peer punishment (IS) can increase group efficiency to levels comparable to or exceeding those achieved by formal sanctions, especially when IS are chosen by vote. The efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost-effectiveness of the sanction regime, the fixed and variable costs of punishment, and whether the regime is adopted endogenously. High fixed costs for FS reduce their efficiency advantage. IS are most effective when punishment is relatively cheap and well-targeted. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling IS is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, and that the effect is robust to moderate changes in punishment technology and institutional coexistence. The control (no-sanction) efficiency is typically well below full cooperation, while IS and deterrent FS can approach or reach high efficiency, with IS often being more cost-effective.

- source: Kube, S., Schaube, S., Schildberg-Hörisch, H., & Khachatryan, E. (2015). Institution formation and cooperation with heterogeneous agents. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with three players, the introduction of an institution formation stage (via unanimity voting) that, if implemented, imposes binding contribution obligations enforced by deterrent sanctions, leads to large increases in efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the maximum possible). For homogeneous groups, a symmetric institution (equal obligations) is almost always implemented and achieves efficiency above 90%. For heterogeneous groups, a symmetric institution is often rejected by low-benefit players due to inequality aversion, but an asymmetric institution (with obligations tailored to equalize payoffs) is much more likely to be implemented, though at a cost to total efficiency. In all cases, efficiency is higher when the institution is implemented than when it is not. The main moderators are the distribution of marginal per-capita returns (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) and the symmetry of institutional obligations. The study does not manipulate punishment cost or technology directly, as the institution, if implemented, is enforced with sufficiently strong sanctions to ensure compliance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with three players and 20 rounds, the introduction of a binding, deterrent-sanction institution (enabled by unanimity voting) increases efficiency relative to the baseline game without such an institution. The effect is strongest when the institution is both efficient and equitable (i.e., symmetric for homogeneous groups, asymmetric for heterogeneous groups). When the institution is rejected (often due to inequality aversion), efficiency is lower, but still generally higher than in the baseline. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, binding punishment institution (with high compliance) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on group heterogeneity and the design of institutional obligations. The study does not provide evidence on the effect of varying punishment cost or technology, as these are not manipulated; the punishment regime is always fully deterrent. Control efficiency (without institution) is reported and can be used as a baseline for prediction.

- source: Dai, Z. X., Hogarth, R. M., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Ambiguity on audits and cooperation in a public goods game. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with centralized sanctions triggered by audits, the study manipulates the frequency and regularity of audits (continuous vs. intermittent) and the severity of sanctions. Under ambiguity (unknown audit probability), intermittent audits with high fines (but lower frequency) produce similar or higher contributions and significantly higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audits with low fines. After audits are withdrawn, groups exposed to intermittent audits maintain higher cooperation and efficiency for longer, as their beliefs about audit probability decay more slowly. High frequency-high severity (continuous audits with high fines) also sustains cooperation and efficiency, but at higher cost. Reducing audit frequency or sanction severity too much undermines these effects. The results suggest that, for a given expected cost of sanction, intermittent audits with high severity are more efficient than continuous audits with low severity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 3-player, 50-round linear PGG with centralized punishment, intermittent (irregular) audit schemes with high severity (punishment effectiveness) and sufficient frequency lead to higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audit schemes with lower severity, especially after audits are withdrawn. The effect is robust as long as audit frequency and severity are not too low. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency and other game dimensions constant, enabling punishment via intermittent, ambiguous audits with high severity will increase efficiency more than continuous, predictable audits with low severity. The paper also shows that both audit frequency and punishment effectiveness (severity) are key moderators: too few audits or too low severity reduces the efficiency benefit. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with centralized punishment.

- source: Wolitzky, A. (2013). Cooperation with Network Monitoring. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. For pure public goods, efficiency is typically increasing in group size; for divisible public goods, it is typically decreasing. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: Dal Bó, E., & Dal Bó, P. (2014). Do the right thing: The effects of moral suasion on cooperation. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a series of lab experiments using a two-player linear public goods game, the authors show that (1) moral suasion (exposure to a moral message) increases contributions and group payoffs, but the effect is short-lived when punishment is not available; (2) when a punishment stage is added (where players can pay to reduce their partner's payoff), the effect of moral suasion becomes persistent, with higher and sustained contributions and higher group payoffs; (3) punishment alone increases contributions compared to no-punishment, but does not prevent a decline over time; (4) the combination of punishment and moral suasion produces the highest and most persistent cooperation and payoffs; (5) the mechanisms include both changes in expectations about others and changes in preferences. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, but does report group payoffs and their changes, which are closely related.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player linear public goods game with random rematching, adding a punishment stage (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases group payoffs and sustains higher cooperation, especially when combined with moral suasion. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, and the effect is amplified when moral suasion is present. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are well specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with and without punishment. The paper is less informative about settings with more players, field contexts, or different punishment technologies, but is highly informative for standard lab PGGs.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

- source: Aimone, J. A., Iannaccone, L. R., Makowsky, M. D., & Rubin, J. (2013). Endogenous Group Formation via Unproductive Costs. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Allowing subjects to self-select into groups with lower private returns (sacrifice) leads to higher contributions and, crucially, higher group and individual payoffs in high-sacrifice groups compared to no-sacrifice groups. The mechanism works by screening out free-riders and encouraging conditional cooperators to join and contribute more. If a game design allows for endogenous sorting into groups with different private returns (sacrifice), expect higher efficiency in high-sacrifice groups due to screening and substitution effects.
