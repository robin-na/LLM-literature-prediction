- source: Deng, K. Y., Li, Z. Z., Kurokawa, S., & Chu, T. G. (2012). Rare but severe concerted punishment that favors cooperation. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that implementing rare but severe concerted punishment in a Public Goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable and increase group efficiency. The threshold probability for punishment to be effective decreases as the severity of punishment increases and as the population size increases. The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

- source: Kurokawa, S., Wakano, J. Y., & Ihara, Y. (2010). Generous cooperators can outperform non-generous cooperators when replacing a population of defectors. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player public goods games, reciprocators who are 'generous' (i.e., tolerate some defection before retaliating) can outperform less generous reciprocators and defectors in terms of average payoff and fixation probability, especially when group size is at least 4. The optimal generosity level depends on the benefit-to-cost ratio and group size. Generosity facilitates the emergence of cooperation (replacement of defectors by reciprocators) but does not help maintain cooperation once established. The model provides explicit conditions for when generosity is advantageous and quantifies the optimal generosity for maximizing the likelihood of cooperation's evolutionary success.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with n ≥ 4, the design of reciprocation strategies (specifically, the degree of generosity/tolerance for defection) can significantly affect the efficiency outcome when cooperation is replacing defection. While the model does not include explicit punishment mechanisms (such as costly punishment), it analyzes a form of contingent cooperation that functions as a sanctioning mechanism (withholding cooperation from defectors). The findings suggest that, for predicting efficiency in games with repeated interaction and contingent cooperation, allowing some tolerance for defection (generosity) can increase the likelihood of efficient cooperative outcomes, especially in the emergence phase. However, the model does not address explicit costly punishment or its direct efficiency effects, so its relevance to games with explicit punishment is adjacent rather than exact.

- source: Bowles, S., & Gintis, H. (2004). The evolution of strong reciprocity: cooperation in heterogeneous populations. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The model shows that in groups where some members are willing to incur costs to punish norm violators (strong reciprocators), high levels of cooperation and group efficiency are sustained. Without punishment, groups devolve to all-Selfish agents and low efficiency. The presence of punishment leads to a stable mix of Reciprocators, Cooperators, and Selfish agents, with low average shirking rates and high group payoffs. The model is robust to changes in group size, cost of punishment, and other parameters. The findings support the idea that costly punishment is a key mechanism for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling costly punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases group efficiency and average payoffs, relative to control conditions without punishment. The model is not an empirical experiment but simulates a wide range of parameter values, showing that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in group size, cost of punishment, and other design features. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism (even with moderate cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, especially in settings with repeated group interaction and no central authority. The model's structure and outcomes are directly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games.

- source: Kendal, J., Feldman, M. W., & Aoki, K. (2006). Cultural coevolution of norm adoption and enforcement when punishers are rewarded or non-punishers are punished. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both reward-based and punishment-based metanorms can enable the stable coexistence and fixation of norm-adopting punishers, leading to high group efficiency (mean fitness) under a wide range of parameter values. The presence of a metanorm (either rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) is necessary for this outcome; without it, the only stable equilibrium is full defection (no norm adoption, no punishment). The Reward Model is generally more effective than the Punishment Model in enabling the population to reach the efficient equilibrium, especially when starting from low frequencies of norm-adopters and punishers. The probability of fixation of the efficient equilibrium is higher under the Reward Model, and the region of attraction is larger. The models also show that cultural drift can facilitate the transition from the inefficient to the efficient equilibrium, especially in small populations and when costs of norm observation and punishment are low. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and are relevant to public goods game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (especially when reinforced by a metanorm, such as rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) can shift a population from an inefficient equilibrium (low efficiency, no cooperation) to an efficient one (high efficiency, full cooperation and sanctioning), provided the costs and benefits are favorable. The results suggest that, in public goods game-like environments, the introduction of punishment (with or without additional metanorms) can substantially increase group efficiency, especially if the cost of punishing is not too high and the benefit to punishers is sufficient. The Reward Model (rewarding punishers) is particularly effective, suggesting that institutional or social mechanisms that reward sanctioning may be more robust in promoting efficiency than those that only punish non-punishers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the cost/benefit structure and initial conditions. However, the results are theoretical and assume well-mixed populations and specific forms of cultural transmission.

- source: Castro, L., & Toro, M. A. (2008). Iterated prisoner's dilemma in an asocial world dominated by loners, not by defectors. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated N-person Prisoner's Dilemma games, the presence of a 'loner' strategy (voluntary opt-out for a fixed payoff) changes the evolutionary dynamics: loners can displace defectors, and conditional cooperators (TFT0) can invade a population of loners if the benefit of cooperation is high enough and the number of repeated interactions is sufficient. The model demonstrates that cooperation can be established and maintained in sizable groups without the need for explicit punishment, provided that individuals can opt out. The paper does not model or test punishment directly, but discusses its potential role as an additional mechanism for sustaining cooperation in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that voluntary participation (the loner strategy) can enable high-efficiency outcomes in repeated public-goods-like games, even without punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency, as punishment is not modeled. The findings suggest that in environments where voluntary participation is possible, the need for punishment to sustain cooperation may be reduced. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the baseline efficiency in control games with voluntary participation, but not about the incremental effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

- source: Jaffe, K. (2004). Altruism, altruistic punishment and social investment. *Acta Biotheoretica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper uses agent-based simulations to examine the effects of altruistic punishment on aggregate wealth (efficiency) in artificial societies. It finds that while punishment increases generosity and norm compliance, it reduces aggregate wealth compared to societies without punishment, unless the punished behaviors trigger synergistic benefits for society. The effect is observed in both structured (with division of labor) and unstructured societies, though structured societies are more susceptible to social control via punishment. The study concludes that punishment is a strong mechanism for norm enforcement but is not, by itself, efficiency-enhancing.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides evidence that enabling punishment in a public-goods-like environment (here, an agent-based resource-sharing simulation) increases norm compliance and generosity but reduces group efficiency (aggregate wealth), unless there are additional synergistic benefits from the enforced behaviors. The negative effect on efficiency is robust to variations in social structure and punishment intensity. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper suggests that, in the absence of explicit synergistic returns, punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to the control. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is costly and does not directly generate group-level surplus.

- source: Davis, D. D., & Holt, C. A. (1999). Equilibrium cooperation in two-stage games: Experimental evidence. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the use of contingent punishment/reward strategies in the second stage of a two-stage game is strongly associated with higher rates of cooperation in the first stage. This effect is robust across different treatments, including those where the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium or is Pareto dominated. The structure of the second-stage equilibria does not reliably predict cooperation rates. Instead, initial cohort propensities and first-stage payoff structure are more important. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability and use of punishment/reward strategies can increase cooperation rates in two-stage games, even when the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The findings suggest that behavioral responses to punishment opportunities are important, but the translation to efficiency outcomes is not addressed. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can support cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Chen, M. L. (2012). The Effect of Leader Reward and Punishment Behaviors on Subordinates' Budget Reports. *Engineering Economist*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that hands-off (no reward or punishment) is least effective in reducing budgetary slack. Punishment alone reduces slack more than reward alone, but the combination of reward and punishment (carrot-stick) is most effective. The effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is large relative to the benefit of slack creation. Rewards alone, regardless of size, have little effect. Requiring a factual assertion (honesty) also reduces slack. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (slack/honesty).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment, especially when combined with reward, reduces dishonest behavior (budgetary slack) in a principal-agent reporting game. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it is a one-superior/one-subordinate budget reporting game, not a multi-player public goods game. The findings are most useful for understanding the behavioral impact of punishment and reward on honesty/slack, but less so for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Jones, M. A. (1999). The effect of punishment duration of trigger strategies and quasifinite continuation probabilities for Prisoners' Dilemmas. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.

- source: KRAINES, D., & KRAINES, V. (1993). LEARNING TO COOPERATE WITH PAVLOV - AN ADAPTIVE STRATEGY FOR THE ITERATED PRISONERS-DILEMMA WITH NOISE. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that Pavlovian strategies, which adapt their probability of cooperation based on positive and negative payoffs (interpreted as rewards and punishments), can achieve high average payoffs and are robust to noise in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. Pavlov can exploit overly cooperative opponents (like All-C), is not easily exploited except by always-defect strategies, and can recover mutual cooperation after errors more quickly than Tit-for-Tat (TFT). The optimal learning rate (parameter n) balances exploitability and speed of learning, with n=3 or 4 performing best in simulations. The paper provides detailed payoff tables and Markov chain analyses for various strategy matchups, but does not study group interactions, explicit peer punishment, or public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in public goods games, as it does not study group games, peer punishment, or any of the relevant game design dimensions. However, it is informative about how strategies that adapt to positive and negative payoffs (interpreted as endogenous rewards and punishments) can lead to high efficiency and mutual cooperation in repeated dyadic dilemmas. The findings suggest that adaptive learning can be robust to noise and can outperform simple reciprocity (TFT) in some environments. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address the effect of enabling punishment mechanisms or any of the design dimensions in public goods games.

- source: Hagen, E. H., & Hammerstein, P. (2006). Game theory and human evolution: A critique of some recent interpretations of experimental games. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that current interpretations of experimental games, especially those invoking strong reciprocity and cultural group selection, are limited by insufficient understanding of how participants cognitively frame the games and categorize other players. It highlights that both explicit features (such as punishment options) and implicit cues (such as social context or subtle signals) can strongly influence cooperation and punishment behavior. The authors caution against drawing strong conclusions about the evolution of cooperation or the existence of group-selected norms from experimental game results, given the variability in framing and the lack of direct evidence about participants' cognitive processes. The paper does not provide findings on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative findings relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does, however, highlight that behavioral outcomes in such games are highly sensitive to framing, implicit cues, and cultural context, which may moderate or confound the effects of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests caution in generalizing from experimental results, especially when game design dimensions do not capture the full range of contextual and framing variables that influence behavior. The paper does not report or synthesize efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it offers limited direct support for efficiency prediction.
