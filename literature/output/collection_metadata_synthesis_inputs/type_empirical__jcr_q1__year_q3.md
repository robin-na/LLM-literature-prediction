- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

Democratic punishment, where a majority vote is required to punish, results in significantly higher average net profits (efficiency) and higher contributions than individual or dictator punishment. This is true in both monitoring conditions, though the efficiency advantage is somewhat reduced under noise. The improvement is due to a sharp reduction in anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators), while pro-social punishment (punishing defectors) is maintained. Dictator punishment does not outperform individual punishment in terms of efficiency. The study provides direct, quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment institution and monitoring noise on group efficiency.

It shows that the institutional design of punishment (democratic vs. individual vs. dictator) and the observability of contributions (perfect vs. noisy) are key moderators. Democratic punishment substantially increases efficiency compared to individual punishment, especially by reducing anti-social punishment. However, the efficiency gain is somewhat sensitive to monitoring noise. The paper provides exact values for average net profits under each condition, allowing for quantitative calibration of prediction models. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, observability, etc.) are all clearly specified, making the results highly transferable to similar prediction tasks.

- source: Bühren, C., & Dannenberg, A. (2021). The demand for punishment to promote cooperation among like-minded people. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games is highly contingent on group type and information. Highly cooperative groups achieve near-optimal payoffs without punishment, and introducing punishment reduces efficiency due to its cost. Uncooperative groups only benefit from severe punishment if they are informed about their own composition and implement the institution early; otherwise, punishment is often misapplied or subverted, leading to low efficiency. Middle cooperators benefit from punishment only in the absence of information about group composition. Information about group cooperativeness allows groups to better match their institution choice to their needs, increasing overall efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment is not uniform and is moderated by group composition and information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games is not a simple function of the game design dimensions alone, but is critically moderated by group composition (cooperativeness) and information about that composition. For prediction tasks, this means that the same punishment institution can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency depending on whether the group is composed of high, middle, or low cooperators, and whether they know this. In the absence of such information, punishment is often over- or under-used, reducing efficiency. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction from this paper are the presence and cost of punishment, the severity/technology of punishment, the availability of information about group composition, and the baseline cooperativeness of the group. Control efficiency (no punishment) is a strong predictor: if control efficiency is high, adding punishment reduces efficiency; if control efficiency is low, adding severe punishment with information can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of comprehension and early institution choice for realizing efficiency gains from punishment.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors.   prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.
