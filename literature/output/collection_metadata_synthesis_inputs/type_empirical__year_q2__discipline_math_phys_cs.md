# Paper Set Evidence Digest

Each item below is a compact paper-level analysis digest. Use only this digest.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Peysakhovich, A., & Rand, D. G. (2016). Habits of Virtue: Creating Norms of Cooperation and Defection in the Laboratory. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Exposure to environments that support cooperation (via repeated PDs with high continuation probability and favorable payoffs) leads to higher prosociality in subsequent one-shot games, including the public goods game, trust game, dictator game, and ultimatum game. In a second experiment, exposure to cooperative environments increases third-party punishment of selfishness and reduces antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment. These effects are strongest among subjects who rely on intuitive (heuristic) decision-making. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional environments can shape prosocial and punishment behaviors in public-goods-game-like settings, and that these behaviors spill over into new contexts. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency. The design details (e.g., group size, punishment cost/tech, MPCR) are reported and can inform mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

- source: Villatoro, D., Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Nardin, L. G., Sabater-Mir, J., & Conte, R. (2014). The Norm-Signaling Effects of Group Punishment: Combining Agent-Based Simulation and Laboratory Experiments. *Social Science Computer Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when punishment is inflicted by the entire group, human subjects are more likely to cooperate (i.e., contribute to the public good) than when punishment is uncoordinated, even though the material cost to defectors is held constant. The authors attribute this to the norm-signaling function of group punishment, which is hypothesized to activate both instrumental and normative motivations for compliance. Agent-based simulations support the idea that norm salience, as modeled in the EMIL-I-A architecture, can explain the observed human behavior. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so the findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (contribution rates).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of punishment (specifically, whether it is coordinated by the group or uncoordinated) can significantly affect cooperation rates in public goods games, even when the material incentives are held constant. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that group punishment may lead to higher efficiency than uncoordinated punishment, but this is inferred from contribution rates rather than directly measured. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of group punishment structure and norm-signaling on cooperation, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency when combined with other sources.

- source: Drouvelis, M., Metcalfe, R., & Powdthavee, N. (2015). Can priming cooperation increase public good contributions?. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that priming participants with cooperative concepts (via a word search) leads to significantly higher contributions in a one-shot public goods game compared to a neutrally primed control group. The effect is robust to controls for gender, nationality, and field of study, and does not appear to be mediated by beliefs about others' contributions. The main outcome is contribution level; average earnings are reported but not analyzed in detail. No punishment, reward, or sanction mechanisms are present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism. Its findings are not directly transferable to the prediction of efficiency with or without punishment. However, it does provide evidence that subtle environmental interventions (like priming) can increase contributions in a standard linear PGG, which may be contextually relevant for understanding other non-incentive-based interventions. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not informative about punishment effects.

- source: Martinsson, P., Villegas-Palacio, C., & Wollbrant, C. (2015). Cooperation and social classes: evidence from Colombia. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that both high and medium-low social class groups contribute about 40% of their endowment in a one-shot public goods game, with no significant difference in unconditional contributions or beliefs about others' contributions. The distribution of contributor types (conditional cooperators, free riders, etc.) is also similar between groups. However, medium-low social class members are more likely to contribute when others contribute nothing (i.e., higher baseline conditional cooperation). The study does not include punishment, reward, or repeated interaction, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide a clear description of a standard one-shot linear public goods game with four players, continuous contributions, and MPCR of 0.4, which may be useful as a control or baseline for comparison with studies that do include punishment. The findings on contribution behavior by social class may inform expectations about baseline cooperation in similar environments, but cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Noussair, C. N., van Soest, D., & Stoop, J. (2015). Punishment, reward, and cooperation in a framed field experiment. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing punishment or reward opportunities does not increase cooperation compared to baseline: average catch and effort are statistically indistinguishable across treatments. Punishment is directed at non-cooperators, but does not induce greater cooperation in subsequent periods. Reward is used more frequently than punishment, but is not targeted based on recipient cooperation. The results suggest that, in this field setting, peer punishment and reward are ineffective at increasing cooperation, in contrast to many laboratory PGG studies. No direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff are reported, but the behavioral proxies (catch and effort) do not improve with punishment or reward.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a field setting with a common-pool resource framed as a social dilemma, enabling peer punishment (and reward) does not increase cooperation or its behavioral proxies, compared to a baseline with no sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency observed in many lab PGGs may not generalize to field environments with similar incentive structures but different context, framing, or real-world constraints. However, because the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited; it is most informative as a caution about generalizability and as evidence that punishment may not always improve group outcomes, even when the game structure is similar to a PGG.

- source: Hashim, M. J., Kannan, K. N., Maximiano, S., & Ulmer, J. R. (2014). Digital Piracy, Teens, and the Source of Advice: An Experimental Study. *Journal of Management Information Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Advice from parents, especially when they are punished for their child's piracy, increases purchasing and group profit and reduces piracy compared to other advice sources. Advice from record labels is the least effective and can even reduce group profit. Advice from a regulator is largely ignored. The effect of advice is strongest when the adviser has both a social tie and a stake in the outcome. The study does not include standard peer punishment or reward mechanisms; the only 'punishment' is an exogenous penalty to parents in one treatment. The game is a close variant of a public goods game, but not a standard PGG. Efficiency-related outcomes (group profit, purchasing) are reported, but not as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that exogenous punishment of a third party (parents) for a player's free-riding can increase public good provision and group profit, especially when combined with a social tie. However, it does not test endogenous peer punishment or standard PGG punishment institutions. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms may be more effective when they are socially salient and affect someone with a close tie to the player, but direct transfer to standard PGG with peer punishment is limited. Control efficiency (group profit without punishment) is reported, and the effect of introducing a punishment-like mechanism (parent penalty) can be inferred, but the mechanism differs from standard punishment. The absence of peer punishment, reward, or communication means the results are only adjacent for direct prediction of PGG punishment effects.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: Leibbrandt, A., & López-Pérez, R. (2014). Different carrots and different sticks: do we reward and punish differently than we approve and disapprove?. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that monetary punishment is used more often and more intensely than non-monetary disapproval, but monetary reward and non-monetary approval are used at similar rates. The determinants of monetary punishment/reward are primarily payoff-based (inequity aversion), while non-monetary approval/disapproval is more strongly driven by reciprocity and norm-based considerations. Strict payoff equality reduces disapproval and increases approval. The experiment is not a repeated public goods game but a set of one-shot, two-player allocation games, so efficiency or group payoff outcomes are not reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about the psychological and motivational underpinnings of punishment and reward, distinguishing between monetary and non-monetary forms. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes in a repeated public goods context, nor does it manipulate or report most of the game design dimensions relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments. Its main value is in clarifying that the impact of punishment/reward on behavior may depend on whether the mechanism is monetary or non-monetary, and that motivations differ. For direct prediction of efficiency effects in PGGs, this paper is only indirectly informative.

- source: Liu, J., & Riyanto, Y. E. (2017). Information transparency and equilibrium selection in coordination games: an experimental study. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Revealing information about the existence and structure of centralized punishment or reward mechanisms in a stag hunt game increases the rate of mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant equilibrium) compared to a control where only final payoffs are shown. The effect is stronger for punishment than for reward. The mechanism works both by increasing beliefs in others' cooperation and, for punishment, by directly increasing cooperative choices. In contrast, when the underlying game remains a prisoner's dilemma (i.e., the punishment/reward is too small to change the equilibrium), revealing the mechanism does not increase cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in mutual cooperation implies higher group welfare in the context of the stag hunt game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that, in two-player, repeated stag hunt games with centralized punishment or reward, making the institutional mechanism transparent increases cooperation rates, especially when punishment is highlighted. While efficiency is not directly measured, the increase in mutual cooperation (the payoff-dominant outcome) suggests that efficiency would also increase. The results are most relevant for coordination games adjacent to public goods games, rather than standard PGGs. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment and making its mechanism salient is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such information, especially when the punishment is strong enough to change the equilibrium structure.

- source: Li, L. F., & Xiao, E. (2014). Money Talks: Rebate Mechanisms in Reputation System Design. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when feedback reporting is costly, market efficiency (proportion of efficient trades and participant earnings) declines. Introducing a rebate mechanism, where sellers can offer a partial rebate to buyers for leaving feedback, increases efficiency—especially when sellers frequently offer rebates. The rebate acts as a signal of seller cooperativeness, increasing buyers' willingness to purchase and sellers' willingness to ship. The effect is robust even when the rebate does not fully cover the reporting cost. The more often sellers offer rebates, the higher the efficiency and earnings for both buyers and sellers. If sellers do not offer rebates, efficiency can be even lower than in the no-rebate, high-cost condition. Over time, sellers learn to offer rebates more frequently, further improving efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a mechanism for sellers to precommit to (partially) covering buyers' feedback costs increases efficiency in a repeated trust game with feedback, especially when sellers frequently use the mechanism. The effect is strongest when the rebate is offered often, and the mechanism works even if the rebate is less than the full reporting cost. For prediction, if a game design allows for a precommitment rebate mechanism and sellers are likely to use it, efficiency should be higher than in a comparable game with high feedback costs and no rebate. The control efficiency (with high feedback cost and no rebate) is a lower bound; the treatment efficiency (with rebate) will be higher, approaching the efficiency of the low-cost or automatic feedback conditions as rebate frequency increases. The findings are most directly relevant to trust/reputation games with feedback as a public good, and less directly to standard PGGs or punishment games, but the mechanism is analogous to reward/precommitment interventions in PGGs.

- source: Schram, A., & Charness, G. (2015). Inducing Social Norms in Laboratory Allocation Choices. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that providing impartial advice about what one 'ought to do' (shared understanding) only affects behavior when choices are made public, i.e., when social (not just moral) norms are salient. In the advice + public condition, dictators are significantly less selfish than in other conditions. Advice alone (private) does not change behavior, nor does public observability alone without advice. The effect is interpreted as the creation of a social norm, enforced by the threat of social disapproval (shame), not by material punishment. There is no direct measurement or analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus. The findings are relevant for understanding norm induction and the role of observability, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. It does not implement punishment, rewards, or repeated interaction, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. However, it does provide informative context about how social norms (via advice and observability) can shift behavior toward less selfish choices in allocation games. For prediction tasks, the main takeaway is that nonmaterial social sanctions (public observability) combined with shared understanding can induce prosocial behavior, but the absence of payoff-based outcomes and the lack of a PGG or punishment mechanism limit its direct applicability.
