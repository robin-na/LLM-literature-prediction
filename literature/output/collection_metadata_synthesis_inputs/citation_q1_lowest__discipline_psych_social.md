- source: Zhang, Z. Y., Elvis, N. T., Wang, J. W., & Hou, G. L. (2024). Can Leading by Example Alone Improve Cooperation?. *Behavioral Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.

The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.

Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.
