- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Dannenberg, A., & Levin, S. A. (2022). Punishment institutions selected and sustained through voting and learning. *Nature Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

- source: Rosas, A. (2008). The return of reciprocity: a psychological approach to the evolution of cooperation. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. Exclusion-based punishment (TPR) is more stable and efficient, provided defectors cannot exploit cooperators. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency. Theoretical predictions are made for experimental settings, suggesting that people will avoid over-punishment and switch methods when possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Rusch, H. (2012). An evolutionary perspective on the long-term efficiency of costly punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

- source: Brick, K., & Visser, M. (2010). Meeting a national emission reduction target in an experimental setting. *Climate Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Voluntary cooperation alone is insufficient to meet a group emission reduction target in a public good game with heterogeneous players. Allowing communication increases average contributions and the proportion of groups meeting the target (from 35% to 50%), but also increases polarization between full cooperation and free-riding. Introducing a tax-based sanction (automatic fine for under-contribution) leads to near-universal compliance with the target (88–91% of groups meet the target), but also crowds out voluntary contributions above the minimum required. The effect of taxation on contributions above the minimum differs by player type: for capital, taxation reduces excess contributions; for labour, taxation can encourage excess contributions relative to baseline. The study does not directly analyze efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the compliance rates and average earnings suggest that tax-based sanctions are highly effective at achieving the group target.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that automatic, rule-based sanctions (taxes/fines for under-contribution) are highly effective at increasing compliance with group targets in public good games, even with heterogeneous players. Communication alone improves cooperation but is less reliable than sanctions. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of institutional (not peer) punishment on group compliance and contributions, especially in settings with asymmetric player types and marginal returns. However, because the study does not report efficiency in the standard sense (group payoff as a share of maximum possible), its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. Still, the high compliance rates under tax-based sanctions suggest that efficiency would be much higher with punishment enabled than in the voluntary or communication-only conditions.

- source: Rommel, J. (2015). What can economic experiments tell us about institutional change in social-ecological systems?. *Environmental Science & Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that allowing third-party punishment of traders (who cause negative externalities) leads to a significant reduction in market entry rates compared to the baseline, especially for sellers. About 86% of observers are willing to punish, spending on average more than half their endowment to do so. The conditional entry treatment also reduces market entry, as about a quarter of participants condition their entry on others' behavior, reflecting norm conformity. The main outcome is the proportion of participants who choose to trade (i.e., cause the externality), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings show that punishment can enforce social norms and reduce harmful market behavior, but the study does not measure efficiency as defined in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of costly punishment reduces the frequency of norm-violating behavior (trading that causes negative externalities) in a large market setting. However, because the outcome is market entry rate (not group efficiency or total payoff), the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games. The punishment mechanism is effective at deterring harmful behavior, especially among sellers, and most observers are willing to punish at a cost. For prediction tasks, the main signal is that enabling punishment reduces the rate of norm violation, but the mapping to efficiency or group payoff in standard PGGs is indirect. The market structure, one-shot design, and externality to a third party differ from standard PGGs, so caution is needed in transferring quantitative effects.

- source: Woodcock, S., & Heath, J. (2002). The robustness of altruism as an evolutionary strategy. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment mechanisms, such as enforcement and ostracism, dramatically increase the robustness of altruism in evolutionary simulations of the prisoner's dilemma. While group selection and imitation can help sustain altruism under certain conditions (e.g., small group size, high imitation rates), punishment allows altruism to persist in much larger groups and under a wider range of conditions. The key mechanism is that punishment increases correlation among altruists, allowing them to interact more with each other and less with defectors. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes; it focuses on the evolutionary persistence of altruism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment mechanisms can greatly increase the persistence of altruism (cooperation) in evolutionary environments similar to the prisoner's dilemma. However, because the main outcome is the proportion of altruists rather than group efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency in public goods games. The results suggest that, all else equal, adding punishment mechanisms is likely to increase cooperation and thus could plausibly increase efficiency, but the paper does not quantify this effect in payoff terms. The design dimensions most relevant here are the presence and effectiveness of punishment, group size, and the structure of interaction (e.g., group partitioning, imitation).

- source: Xu, J., Song, Y. B., & van der Schaar, M. (2014). Sharing in Networks of Strategic Agents. *Ieee Journal of Selected Topics in Signal Processing*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that distributed rating-based sanctioning protocols can fully or nearly fully restore efficiency in repeated network sharing games with strategic agents, provided certain incentive compatibility conditions are met. When the network's connectivity is not too high and agents' discount factors are sufficient, the protocol achieves the social optimum (PoA=1). As network connectivity increases or agents become more impatient, efficiency declines but remains higher than with direct reciprocity mechanisms. The protocol is robust to imperfect monitoring and can be tuned for dynamic networks. Theoretical and simulation results show that the protocol outperforms Tit-for-Tat and similar direct punishment schemes, especially in heterogeneous or asymmetric networks.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that distributed, rating-based punishment mechanisms can substantially increase efficiency in repeated networked cooperation environments, often achieving the social optimum. The effect is most pronounced when the network is not too highly connected and agents are sufficiently patient. The protocol's performance is robust to imperfect monitoring and network dynamics. For prediction tasks, the presence of a distributed, indirect punishment mechanism (ratings affecting future access) is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially compared to direct, bilateral punishment. The results are most directly applicable to networked repeated games with costly sharing, which are adjacent to but not identical to standard public goods games.

- source: O'Connor, C. (2016). The Evolution of Guilt: A Model-Based Approach. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that guilt, modeled as a behavioral trait leading to cooperation, apology, and reparative actions, can be individually adaptive in evolutionary games. In both stag hunt and prisoner's dilemma settings, guilt-proneness increases the likelihood of cooperation, which in turn raises individual and group payoffs. When punishment for defection is present, guilt-proneness further increases individual fitness by reducing the risk of being punished. The models show that costly or honest signals of apology (as a proxy for guilt) can stabilize cooperation and prevent the invasion of defectors or 'fakers.' The efficiency of the group (measured as average fitness or payoff) is higher in populations where guilt-proneness and apology are present, especially when punishment mechanisms exist.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment mechanisms, when combined with behavioral traits like guilt-proneness and apology, can increase efficiency (group payoff) in social dilemma games. While it does not directly model public goods games, the findings are adjacent and suggest that enabling punishment and supporting honest signals of intent (like apology) are likely to increase efficiency relative to control conditions without punishment. The models also highlight the importance of the cost and credibility of punishment and apology mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that introducing punishment (especially with some cost or honest signaling) will increase efficiency, provided that the game environment allows for reciprocity and repeated interaction.

- source: Rosas, A. (2008). Multilevel selection and human altruism. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that the evolution of human altruism depends on the degree to which individuals (or their social/psychological mechanisms) can control assortative grouping, such as by punishing defectors and maintaining group cohesion. It claims that in human societies, mechanisms like punishment, reputation, and social norms can, in principle, prevent subversion from within and make altruism individually adaptive. The discussion references public goods games and the importance of punishment for sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and social mechanisms might enable stable cooperation in public goods settings by engineering assortative grouping and deterring free-riders. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or variants. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of mechanisms (like punishment and reputation) that allow groups to control subversion from within, suggesting that such mechanisms could, in principle, improve group outcomes. For direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in experimental public goods games, this paper is not directly informative.

- source: Forber, P., & Smead, R. (2016). The Evolution of Spite, Recognition, and Morality. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that mechanisms enabling recognition and conditional behavior can support both altruism and spite, but with important asymmetries. Recognition based on exogenous signals (e.g., greenbeard effects) is more evolutionarily stable with spite than with altruism, because mimics threaten altruism more than spite. In contrast, reciprocity based on past behavior (as in repeated games) is more conducive to the evolution of altruism than spite. The models show that reciprocal altruism evolves readily in repeated games, while reciprocal spite is rare. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff, but rather the evolutionary stability and frequency of these behaviors.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its models are adjacent, focusing on the evolutionary conditions for the emergence and stability of conditional harming (spite) and helping (altruism) behaviors, and the coevolution of recognition mechanisms. While it discusses repeated games and conditional strategies, it does not report or predict group efficiency, welfare, or payoff outcomes. The main relevance is conceptual: it suggests that the evolutionary stability of punishment-like behaviors depends on the mechanism of recognition and the type of conditionality (signal-based vs. reciprocity), but it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence for efficiency effects in PGG-like games.

The paper discusses how gene-culture co-evolutionary theory is used to provide ultimate explanations for the evolution of SR, while neuroeconomics is invoked for proximate explanations. However, it does not report or synthesize quantitative effects on efficiency, payoff, or welfare, and thus offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task.

- source: Heath, J., & Rioux, C. (2018). Recent trends in evolutionary ethics: greenbeards!. *Biology & Philosophy*.

  findings: The paper finds that many recent philosophical and scientific models that attempt to explain large-scale human cooperation by introducing commitment or punishment mechanisms (such as strong reciprocity) tacitly rely on greenbeard mechanisms, which are considered implausible in the human case due to their vulnerability to exploitation by 'falsebeards.' Its relevance to the prediction task is limited to theoretical critique: it cautions that models relying on punishment to sustain cooperation may be structurally vulnerable unless they avoid greenbeard-like mechanisms. For prediction tasks based on game design dimensions and control efficiency, this paper offers only indirect, high-level theoretical context and does not inform parameter-level predictions.

- source: Chen, W. M., Quan, J., Wang, X. J., & Liu, Y. (2023). Evolutionary dynamics from fluctuating environments with deterministic and stochastic noises. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical model of evolutionary dynamics in the hawk-dove game under fluctuating environments with both payoff and demographic noise. Through simulation, it finds that both deterministic and stochastic fluctuations can promote cooperation, sometimes even reversing selection so that cooperation dominates. Deterministic fluctuations are more effective than stochastic ones at promoting cooperation. The mechanism is that noise can create unfair time-averaged payoffs that favor cooperation. High variance in noise promotes cooperation but makes its maintenance less stable. The results are robust to the type of noise distribution. The paper briefly extends the analysis to the prisoner's dilemma, finding similar qualitative results. No public goods game, punishment, or explicit group efficiency analysis is present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not study public goods games, punishment, or group efficiency. Its findings about noise and cooperation in the hawk-dove and prisoner's dilemma games may be of limited indirect relevance for understanding how environmental uncertainty affects cooperation, but not for the specific prediction task involving punishment and efficiency.
