- source: Dong, Y. L., Sasaki, T., & Zhang, B. Y. (2019). The competitive advantage of institutional reward. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

- source: Jiao, Y. H., Chen, T., & Chen, Q. (2020). Probabilistic punishment and reward under rule of trust-based decision-making in continuous public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing probabilistic punishment and reward (i.e., punish/reward with probability rho < 1) into a continuous PGG can maintain high levels of cooperation and mean payoff (efficiency), even when the cost of punishment/reward is high. Traditional (always executed) punishment/reward leads to collapse of cooperation and low efficiency when costs are high. There is an optimal execution probability (rho ≈ 0.6) and moderate fine/bonus size (f = r = 1) that maximize mean payoff and cooperation, saving about 40% of the cost compared to always executing punishment/reward. If either the execution probability or the fine/bonus is too low, cooperation and efficiency remain low. The model shows that probabilistic execution is robust to high costs and can avoid the collapse of cooperation seen in traditional models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in continuous PGGs on structured populations, enabling punishment (and reward) with a moderate probability (rather than always) can increase group efficiency compared to both no punishment and always-on punishment, especially when costs are high. The effect is robust across a range of synergy factors (mpcr), costs, and fine/bonus sizes. For prediction, the presence of probabilistic punishment (with moderate execution probability and moderate cost/fine) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to the control (no punishment) and to traditional always-on punishment, particularly when punishment/reward costs are nontrivial. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the presence and cost of punishment, the effectiveness of punishment, and whether execution is probabilistic or deterministic.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of prosocial punishment in unstructured and structured populations and in the presence of antisocial punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that prosocial punishment can evolve and promote cooperation (and thus high efficiency) in public goods games, even without auxiliary mechanisms like network structure or voluntary participation. The efficiency of the group (measured by which strategy dominates and thus the average group payoff) is increased when prosocial punishment dominates. The evolution of prosocial punishment (and thus efficiency) is facilitated by higher returns to the public good (mpcr), higher punishment effectiveness, and optimal punishment cost. Allowing unused punishment resources to be redistributed (non-wasteful punishment) further facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency. Antisocial punishment can dominate under low punishment cost or low public good return, reducing efficiency. Network structure can both hinder and facilitate the evolution of punishment, but under favorable conditions, it helps prosocial punishment dominate. The model provides phase diagrams mapping out these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment can increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective, the cost is optimal (not too low or high), and the returns to the public good are high. The model also shows that if punishment is too cheap, antisocial punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency, while if it is too costly, punishment does not evolve. The presence of a reward mechanism (redistribution of unused punishment resources) further increases the likelihood of high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions such as mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward. The results are theoretical and should be interpreted as providing mechanistic and qualitative guidance rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2019). Evolution of coordinated punishment to enforce cooperation from an unbiased strategy space. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, the ability to observe the presence of punishment institutions is crucial for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff. When institutions are not observable, anti-social punishment can invade and reduce cooperation and efficiency. When institutions are observable, a small minority of pro-social institution supporters can sustain high levels of cooperation and group payoff, even in the presence of anti-social punishment strategies. The model demonstrates that the evolutionary outcome is robust to the inclusion of all possible strategies, and that the coexistence of institution supporters and opportunists is stable. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore context-dependent: positive when observability and conditional strategies are present, negative or neutral when they are absent.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on whether players can observe the presence of punishment institutions and condition their behavior accordingly. If the game design allows for observability and conditional strategies, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. If observability is absent, enabling punishment—especially if anti-social punishment is possible—may reduce efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of supporting institutions and group size affect the probability of institution implementation and thus the efficiency outcome. For prediction, the presence or absence of observability/conditionality, the possibility of anti-social punishment, and the cost structure are key moderators.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Evolution of cooperation in a hierarchical society with corruption control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a hierarchical public goods game, the presence of corrupt punishment can maintain civilian cooperation but undermines altruistic punishment and reduces population mean payoff. Introducing corruption control, where altruistic punishers can also punish or exclude corrupt punishers, increases both cooperation and efficiency. Asymmetrical punishment (social exclusion of corrupt punishers) is more effective than symmetrical punishment (equal fines for corrupt punishers and defectors) in promoting both cooperation and population mean payoff. The model provides explicit formulas and phase diagrams showing how efficiency depends on group size, punishment cost, fine, bribe, cop proportion, and the probability of spiteful corruption. The results are robust across a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with hierarchical roles and corruption. It shows that the introduction of punishment (especially with corruption control) increases efficiency, and that the effectiveness depends on the design dimensions such as group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the structure of punishment (symmetrical vs. asymmetrical). The explicit formulas and phase diagrams can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game parameters. The results are most applicable to settings with hierarchical roles and the possibility of corrupt punishment, but the general findings about the positive effect of well-designed punishment on efficiency are likely to transfer to standard PGGs with punishment.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Evolution of cooperation under punishment. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both autonomous and consensual punishment can promote cooperation and increase average payoffs in public goods games. Consensual punishment (where a group vote determines whether defectors are punished) can be more effective than autonomous punishment when the consensus threshold is low and the willingness to punish is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation dominates defection in terms of average payoffs. The effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the group size, the marginal per-capita return, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the consensus threshold for punishment. The paper also shows that increasing the cost of punishment or the group size can make autonomous punishment more effective relative to consensual punishment, while increasing the fine or the marginal return favors consensual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given game design parameters such as group size, number of rounds (implicitly infinite in the model), marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the decision rule for punishment (autonomous vs. consensual, and the consensus threshold). The explicit payoff formulas and comparative statics allow for quantitative or qualitative prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment (especially with low consensus thresholds and high willingness to punish) will increase efficiency, but the effect is moderated by punishment cost, group size, and the consensus rule.

- source: Wang, Q., Liu, L. J., & Chen, X. J. (2020). Evolutionary Dynamics of Cooperation in the Public Goods Game with Individual Disguise and Peer Punishment. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment increases cooperation and group payoff (efficiency) in public goods games, but this effect is undermined if defectors can cheaply disguise themselves to avoid punishment. When the cost of disguise is high, or when punishment is strong (high fine, low cost), punishment is effective at promoting cooperation and high group payoff. Second-order punishment further stabilizes cooperation and punishment strategies, even in the presence of disguise. In finite populations, the stationary distribution of strategies shows that low disguise cost leads to defection (low efficiency), while high disguise cost or strong punishment leads to cooperation or punishment (high efficiency). Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the interplay between punishment parameters and the cost/effectiveness of disguise.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the fine imposed, and the possibility/cost of disguise by defectors. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency due to high defection, enabling punishment will increase efficiency only if punishment is strong enough and disguise is not too cheap. If disguise is cheap, punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The model also shows that second-order punishment can further increase efficiency by stabilizing punishment strategies. For prediction, the paper supports that lower punishment cost, higher punishment effectiveness, and higher fine on defectors all increase the efficiency gain from punishment, but this is moderated (and can be negated) by the ability of defectors to disguise at low cost.

- source: Podder, S., Righi, S., & Pancotto, F. (2021). Reputation and punishment sustain cooperation in the optional public goods game. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the OPGG, punishment alone (including anti-social punishment) does not increase cooperation or group fitness, and can even reduce them by increasing the prevalence of loners. Reputation alone can increase cooperation and fitness, but only under specific social norms (e.g., when defectors are assigned a strictly worse reputation than loners). The combination of reputation and punishment leads to the highest levels of cooperation and population fitness, with most agents adopting conditional cooperation strategies and rarely using punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size, synergy factor, punishment cost and effectiveness, and other parameters. The findings suggest that reputation and punishment interact synergistically to sustain cooperation and improve group outcomes, but that reputation can substitute for costly punishment in many cases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in optional public goods games, the addition of punishment alone (especially when anti-social punishment is possible) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and may even reduce it. However, when reputation mechanisms are also present—especially those that allow agents to distinguish defectors from loners—punishment and reputation together can synergistically increase cooperation and group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the presence and nature of reputation mechanisms and social norms. In the absence of reputation, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency; with reputation, especially under certain norms, enabling punishment can have a more positive effect. The model's robustness checks indicate these findings are not highly sensitive to group size, rounds, or punishment cost within the tested ranges.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Conditional punishment is a double-edged sword in promoting cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.

- source: Flores, L. S., Fernandes, H. C. M., Amaral, M. A., & Vainstein, M. H. (2021). Symbiotic behaviour in the public goods game with altruistic punishment. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that in a spatial PGG, the introduction of altruistic punishment allows for the survival of cooperation in a wider range of parameters than in the classic PGG. For low punishment costs, clusters of punishers can survive and support cooperation. At intermediate punishment costs, both punishers and cooperators are vulnerable to defectors. At high punishment costs, a symbiotic spatial structure emerges where neither cooperators nor punishers can survive alone, but together they can outcompete defectors by forming clusters with punishers in the core and cooperators at the boundary. The effect of punishment on cooperation is thus highly dependent on the cost and the spatial structure. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff directly, but the persistence of altruistic strategies is used as a proxy for cooperative success.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in spatial PGGs is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the spatial structure of interactions. While it does not report efficiency directly, it shows that punishment can expand the parameter region where cooperation survives, especially when spatial clustering allows for symbiosis between cooperators and punishers. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that punishment is most effective at low cost, can be detrimental at intermediate cost, and can again be effective at high cost if spatial symbiosis is possible. The results are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing first-order costly punishment into a finite-population public goods game can shift the system from an all-defection equilibrium to a coexistence of cooperation and punishment, provided that both the punishment intensity and the return on investment are large enough. This shift implies higher group welfare and efficiency, as the all-defection state is inefficient. The model provides a detailed mapping of parameter regions where cooperation is stochastically stable.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in finite-population public goods games, enabling costly punishment (with sufficient intensity and return on investment) can substantially increase the probability of the system being in cooperative or mixed cooperative-punisher states, which are more efficient than all-defection. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence and strength of punishment, as well as the return on investment (mpcr), are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. However, the paper does not report direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must be inferred from the stability of cooperative states. Second-order punishment adds little except in narrow parameter regions. The results are most applicable to settings matching the model's assumptions (finite, well-mixed populations, no communication, discrete strategies).
