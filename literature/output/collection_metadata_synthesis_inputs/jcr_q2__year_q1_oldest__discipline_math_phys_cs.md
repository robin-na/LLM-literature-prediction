- source: Zhuang, Q., Wang, D., Fan, Y., & Di, Z. R. (2012). Evolution of cooperation in a heterogeneous population with influential individuals. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a spatial PGG with influential individuals, both punishment and reward mechanisms (administered by influential individuals) can promote cooperation and increase mean payoff compared to the baseline with no such interventions. Rewarding cooperators is more effective than punishing defectors in terms of both promoting cooperation and increasing mean payoff, especially at low multiplication factors (low MPCR). The cost of punishment or reward is paid by society, not by the influential individuals. The presence of influential individuals as mere exemplars (always cooperating, no sanctioning power) actually reduces cooperation and does not improve efficiency. The model demonstrates that, for a given intervention cost, reward leads to higher group efficiency than punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct simulation evidence that, in a spatial PGG with influential individuals, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (mean payoff) compared to the baseline with no punishment, but enabling reward increases efficiency even more. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, but smaller than that of reward, for a given cost. The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. The model is theoretical/simulation-based, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted with caution.

The effect is present for both perfectly rational and boundedly rational agents. The effect is robust to changes in the rationality parameter K. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to defection. The specific parameterization (N=6, r=3.6, c=1.25, punishment fine and cost) is informative for quantitative prediction.

- source: Sigmund, K., Hauert, C., Traulsen, A., & De Silva, H. (2011). Social Control and the Social Contract: The Emergence of Sanctioning Systems for Collective Action. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Punishment (peer or pool) can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, especially when participation is voluntary. (2) Pool punishment with second-order punishment is particularly effective, leading to stable, high-efficiency cooperative regimes. (3) Peer punishment can also support cooperation, but is less robust and can be undermined by second-order free riders. (4) Compulsory participation (no option to abstain) undermines the effectiveness of punishment, resulting in dominance by defectors and low efficiency. (5) The costs of punishment (especially pool punishment) can reduce efficiency if not balanced by increased cooperation, but with sufficient rounds or strong imitation, efficiency gains are realized. (6) The structure of the punishment mechanism (peer vs. pool, presence of second-order punishment) and the option for voluntary participation are key moderators of the efficiency effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when participation is voluntary and when pool punishment with second-order punishment is available. The model shows that the design dimensions—such as player count, number of rounds, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and whether participation is voluntary—critically shape the efficiency outcome. In particular, pool punishment with second-order punishment and voluntary participation are predicted to yield the highest efficiency. Peer punishment can also increase efficiency, but is less stable and more vulnerable to free-riding. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency, enabling punishment (especially with the above features) is likely to substantially increase efficiency. However, if punishment is costly and not well-targeted, or if participation is compulsory, efficiency gains may be limited or negative.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games and similar social dilemmas, the introduction of punishment (even costly, altruistic punishment) reliably increases cooperation and group payoff, moving outcomes closer to the social optimum. Theoretical and computational models (e.g., Homo Egualis utility, learning automata) and empirical studies are reviewed, all supporting the claim that punishment transforms the equilibrium from inefficient defection to more efficient cooperation. The paper also notes that while punishment increases efficiency, it is not always evolutionarily stable due to the possibility of second-order free-riders. Additional mechanisms such as reputation, volunteering, and network structure can further support cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and review-based support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games and similar environments will increase group efficiency (payoff relative to the cooperative optimum), compared to control games without punishment. The effect is robust across a range of game structures, player counts, and repeated interactions, as long as punishment is possible and salient. The paper also highlights that the magnitude of the effect may depend on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of reputation mechanisms, and the network structure of interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when compared to the Nash equilibrium of defection in the absence of punishment.

- source: Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2012). Self-organization of punishment in structured populations. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Adaptive punishment in spatial public goods games enables the spontaneous emergence of rare, localized punishers who respond to the spread of defection. This mechanism maintains smooth interfaces between cooperators and defectors, invigorates spatial reciprocity, and prevents the emergence of cyclic dominance that would otherwise allow defectors to persist. As a result, adaptive punishment can achieve full cooperation (pure C phase) in parameter regions where, without punishment, only defectors would survive. The cost of punishment, when adaptively deployed, is globally negligible, making the mechanism highly efficient. Compared to steady (non-adaptive) punishment, adaptive punishment is more effective at eliminating defectors and does so with lower cost.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that adaptive, context-sensitive punishment mechanisms in spatially structured public goods games can substantially increase the likelihood of full cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency, even when punishment is costly. The effect is especially pronounced when punishment is inexpensive, but remains positive even for costly punishment. The findings suggest that models or interventions that allow for adaptive, rather than fixed, punishment are likely to yield higher efficiency than those with no punishment or with steady punishment. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, so predictions should be based on the qualitative pattern that adaptive punishment expands the parameter space for high efficiency.

- source: Helbing, D., Szolnoki, A., Perc, M., & Szabó, G. (2010). Punish, but not too hard: how costly punishment spreads in the spatial public goods game. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in spatial public goods games, the introduction of punishing cooperators (PC) or punishing defectors (PD) can promote the spread of cooperation, but through different mechanisms. For punishing cooperators, increasing the punishment fine monotonically increases the cooperation level up to a threshold, after which non-punishing cooperators and defectors are eliminated. For punishing defectors, there is a non-monotonic (reentrant) effect: increasing the fine initially increases cooperation, but too high a fine reduces the fraction of punishing defectors, causing cooperation to collapse and the system to revert to defection. The results are presented as phase diagrams and time-evolution of strategy fractions, not as direct efficiency or group payoff measures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and type of punishment mechanism, as well as the punishment cost and fine, can have large effects on the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games. However, because the outcomes are reported as fractions of strategies and cooperation level, not as group efficiency or total payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency. The results suggest that enabling punishment (especially by punishing cooperators) can increase cooperation and likely efficiency, but the relationship is mediated by the spatial structure and the specific punishment parameters. The non-monotonic effect for punishing defectors highlights the importance of tuning punishment severity. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment can improve efficiency in structured populations, but the mapping to efficiency must be inferred from cooperation levels.
