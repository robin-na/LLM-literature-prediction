- source: Ohdaira, T. (2025). The second-order probabilistic pool punishment proportional to the payoff difference can solve the punishment problem of previous studies. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that the second-order probabilistic pool punishment proportional to payoff difference leads to higher average payoff than both standard pool punishment (which suffers from fixed costs) and peer punishment (which suffers from high per-punishment costs) in spatial public goods games. This mechanism is less effective at fully eliminating second-order free riders, but allows for coexistence of punishers and cooperators with high group efficiency. The results are robust across different network structures (regular and randomized ring lattices).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that a probabilistic, payoff-difference-based pool punishment mechanism can increase group efficiency relative to both standard pool and peer punishment, under the specified game parameters (n=5, r=3, c=1, punishment cost G=0.3, punishment impact B=1, peer punishment cost gamma=0.3, peer punishment impact beta=1). The findings suggest that, for spatial PGGs with these parameters, enabling this form of punishment will increase efficiency compared to no punishment or to standard punishment mechanisms. The results are most applicable to settings with continuous contribution, no communication, and fixed group size and structure. The study is simulation-based, so real-world generalizability may be limited, but the mechanism-level insight is strong for theoretical prediction.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Gao, S. P., & Li, N. (2023). Preference reversal and the evolution of cooperation. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.

(2) Moderate labor subsidies for executors (who implement reward/punishment) are optimal for forming stable alliances of cooperators and executors, excluding defectors. (3) Excessive reward (relative to punishment) or excessive subsidies can destabilize cooperation and allow defectors to invade. (4) The presence of a corruption (bribery) mechanism has little effect on cooperation levels when subsidies are high, but can slow the increase of cooperation as taxation increases. (5) The main outcomes are the steady-state fractions of each strategy; average payoffs are derived but not the focus. It is directly relevant for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms (and their funding) interact to affect cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum. The results suggest that moderate, well-funded punishment (and reward) mechanisms, especially when executors are compensated, can stabilize cooperation, but excessive reward or subsidy can backfire. The presence of corruption can reduce the effectiveness of punishment, but this effect is mitigated by higher subsidies. For prediction tasks, this paper informs the expected direction and possible moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency.

- source: Gao, S. P., Suo, J. H., & Li, N. (2025). Cooperation under endogenous punishment in the spatial public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that endogenous punishment can promote cooperation in spatial public goods games when the network reciprocity alone is insufficient (i.e., when the enforcement factor is below the threshold for cooperation in the absence of punishment). The effectiveness of punishment depends on the willingness of cooperators to punish (probability p), the cost and fine of punishment, and the network degree. There is a critical value of p above which cooperation can be sustained. However, when network reciprocity is already strong enough to support cooperation, adding endogenous punishment can actually hinder cooperation or have no additional effect. The evolutionary dynamics can be bi-stable, with both all-cooperator and all-defector states possible depending on initial conditions. The model provides explicit threshold formulas for when cooperation is favored.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on how endogenous punishment, punishment cost and effectiveness, and network structure interact to affect the likelihood of achieving full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) in spatial public goods games. While it does not report empirical efficiency values, it gives explicit formulas for the thresholds at which cooperation becomes stable or dominant, as a function of the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and network degree. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that enabling punishment can increase efficiency (by supporting all-cooperation) when the control game is below the cooperation threshold, but may have little or even negative effect when the control game already achieves high cooperation due to network reciprocity. The model is most informative for structured populations with endogenous, probabilistic punishment.
