- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Forsyth, P. A. I., & Hauert, C. (2011). Public goods games with reward in finite populations. *Journal of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in finite populations, allowing individuals to reward contributors to the public good can initiate and maintain a significant minority of cooperators, even though the majority of the population is typically composed of non-cooperating shirkers. Reward is effective at jump-starting cooperation but is unable to stabilize it in the long run due to exploitation by second-order free-riders. The average group payoff (efficiency) is increased relative to pure defection, but full efficiency is not achieved. The effectiveness of reward is largely independent of group size when key parameters are scaled appropriately. The paper does not provide new results on punishment, but notes that punishment is more effective at stabilizing cooperation once established.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence on the effects of reward (not punishment) in public goods games. It shows that positive incentives can increase efficiency above the baseline of mutual defection, but do not achieve full efficiency due to the persistence of second-order free-riders. The findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, reward alone is insufficient to fully stabilize cooperation and maximize efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with reward enabled, but only indirectly inform predictions about punishment. The analysis of group size, MPCR, and reward parameters is informative for understanding how these dimensions affect efficiency with reward. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for cases where the treatment is reward (not punishment), and less directly informative for punishment-enabled games.

- source: Broom, M., Lafaye, C., Pattni, K., & Rychtár, J. (2015). A study of the dynamics of multi-player games on small networks using territorial interactions. *Journal of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that the fixation probability of cooperators in the public goods game is generally less than 1/N (where N is the number of players), indicating that cooperation is disfavored under the birth-death process in these small networked populations. The fixation probability is strongly influenced by the mean temperature (a measure of how often individuals are replaced), with higher temperatures amplifying selection and lower temperatures suppressing it. The specific graph structure (network topology) has some effect, but mean temperature is a more important determinant. The results are derived for small graphs (3-4 players), and the framework is generalizable to larger graphs in principle. No punishment, reward, or sanctioning mechanisms are included in the models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. It does, however, provide theoretical insights into how network structure and group size affect the evolutionary success of cooperation versus defection in public goods games. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, but it may provide contextual background on how structural features (such as player count and group formation) influence cooperation in the absence of punishment.

- source: POLLOCK, G. B. (1988). POPULATION-STRUCTURE, SPITE, AND THE ITERATED PRISONERS-DILEMMA. *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that punishment-based strategies (TFT/NTFT) can maintain high group efficiency and suppress defection when the population is structured into multiple groups or dyads, allowing intergroup competition. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation and high payoffs is reduced as the scope of spite (the number of groups a defector can affect) increases or as population structure becomes less partitioned. In the limit of a single group (no structure), punishment cannot prevent defection and efficiency collapses. The analysis generalizes to n-person games, showing that the same logic applies: partitioned populations with group-level competition allow punishment to sustain cooperation and high efficiency, while unstructured populations do not.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments will increase efficiency (group payoff) relative to control, but only when the game design includes sufficient population structure (multiple groups, limited overlap in group membership, or mechanisms for intergroup competition). If the game is a single group or highly overlapping, punishment will not improve efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the marginal per-capita return, group size, and population structure can be specified, and they highlight the importance of these dimensions as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

- source: Nhim, T., Richter, A., & Zhu, X. Q. (2019). The resilience of social norms of cooperation under resource scarcity and inequality - An agent-based model on sharing water over two harvesting seasons. *Ecological Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model shows that strong punishment (high punishment strength) and high social capital (frequent encounters) support the emergence and resilience of cooperation in a common-pool resource dilemma. However, resource scarcity, high variability in resource inflow, and increased inequality (in land or skill) undermine cooperation, making collapse of social norms more likely. The system tends toward either full cooperation or full defection, with little evidence for stable mixed equilibria. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the collapse of cooperation is interpreted as a breakdown of socially optimal resource use.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that punishment can sustain cooperation in public-goods-like resource dilemmas, but its effectiveness is strongly moderated by resource scarcity, variability, and inequality. The model is not a direct PGG but is structurally similar, with a focus on seasonal water allocation and peer punishment. The main outcome is the presence or absence of cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff, so the paper is most useful for predicting the conditions under which punishment will be effective at sustaining cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency gains. The findings suggest that in environments with high scarcity, variability, or inequality, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (if any) is likely to be weaker or absent.

- source: Ubeda, F., & Duéñez-Guzmán, E. A. (2011). POWER AND CORRUPTION. *Evolution*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a Prisoner's Dilemma with costly punishment, allowing for corrupt punishers and power asymmetries enables the evolution of stable states with both cooperation and corruption. When the social cost of punishing a punisher (q+d) is less than the cost of punishing a civilian (p), and within a certain range, a mixed equilibrium emerges with both cooperating civilians and corrupt policers. In this equilibrium, the population mean payoff (efficiency) is higher than in the all-defect equilibrium, and can approach the fully cooperative payoff as power asymmetries increase. The model demonstrates that costly punishment can increase both cooperation and efficiency, especially when punishers are protected by power asymmetries. The likelihood of reaching the efficient equilibrium depends on the relative costs and effectiveness of punishment. The results challenge the view that costly punishment is always inefficient, showing that under the right conditions, it can benefit the group even when punishers are themselves corrupt.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the structure of punishment costs and power asymmetries. If punishment is costly but power asymmetries exist (punishers are less vulnerable to punishment or punishment is more effective against non-punishers), then enabling punishment can increase efficiency above the control (no-punishment) baseline. The model suggests that the efficiency gain is larger when the punishment of defectors is strong and the punishment of punishers is weak. The findings are most directly applicable to Prisoner's Dilemma settings, but the logic is relevant for public goods games with similar punishment structures. The paper does not address all game design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, communication), but provides direct, mechanistic insight into how punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and power asymmetries moderate the efficiency impact of punishment.

- source: Bshary, R., & Bronstein, J. L. (2011). A General Scheme to Predict Partner Control Mechanisms in Pairwise Cooperative Interactions Between Unrelated Individuals. *Ethology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The paper provides a decision-tree framework linking specific game structures (defined by investment, payoff structure, symmetry, repetition, and partner choice) to the partner control mechanisms that can stabilize cooperation. Punishment is predicted to be a viable control mechanism in repeated interactions without partner choice, especially when both players can invest and defect. The framework also highlights the roles of reward, sanctions, partner switching, and indirect reciprocity in other game structures. The review does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment, but it does clarify the conditions under which punishment is theoretically expected to promote cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for identifying which game structures are theoretically compatible with punishment as a mechanism for stabilizing cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on the efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods or similar games. For the downstream prediction task, the framework can help map which design dimensions (e.g., repeated interactions, partner choice, symmetry) are necessary for punishment to be a relevant mechanism, but it cannot inform the magnitude or direction of efficiency changes. The paper is most useful for contextualizing when punishment is likely to be effective, not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Boehm, C. (2014). The moral consequences of social selection. *Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that in mobile hunter-gatherer societies, group punishment—ranging from shaming and ostracism to capital punishment—has been a central mechanism for enforcing cooperation and curbing antisocial behaviors such as bullying, theft, and cheating. These sanctions are argued to have shaped the evolution of human conscience and moral norms, favoring individuals who are generous, honest, and humble. The author suggests that such punitive social selection has been critical for the emergence of egalitarian, cooperative societies, and for the evolutionary success of altruistic traits. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative analysis of efficiency or group payoffs in public goods game-like settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and ethnographic context for the role of punishment in promoting cooperation and suppressing free-riding in small-scale societies. It supports the idea that punishment can be a powerful tool for maintaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper offers indirect support for the positive role of punishment in sustaining cooperation, especially in small, face-to-face groups, but does not allow for parameter-specific predictions based on game design dimensions.

- source: Du, E., & Chang, S. W. C. (2015). Neural components of altruistic punishment. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper reviews evidence that altruistic punishment is driven by neural processes related to inequity aversion, cost-benefit analysis, and social reference framing. It highlights the involvement of brain regions such as the insula, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex in these processes. The review draws on studies using ultimatum, dictator, and trust games, but does not focus on public goods games or report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding the psychological and neural basis of punishment decisions, but not for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public goods game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is useful for understanding the cognitive and neural mechanisms that may underlie punishment behavior, which could inform mechanistic models, but it does not report or synthesize payoff-based outcomes or moderators relevant to efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers only indirect, mechanistic context and does not inform quantitative or qualitative predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Singh, M., & Boomsma, J. J. (2015). Policing and punishment across the domains of social evolution. *Oikos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper proposes that punishment and policing behaviors are maintained when the inclusive fitness benefits to the punisher outweigh the costs, and when these benefits are greater than those from bystanding. The framework is applied to a range of biological and social systems, showing that power, relatedness, and reproductive options drive variation in who punishes. In systems with strong power asymmetries, dominants are more likely to punish; in systems with high relatedness and low individual reproductive prospects, policing is more likely to be corrective and group-beneficial. The paper highlights that the same inclusive fitness logic can explain punishment across diverse domains, but does not provide empirical data or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a conceptual framework for understanding the conditions under which punishment is likely to emerge and be maintained in social groups, emphasizing the roles of power, relatedness, and reproductive options. However, it does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. For the downstream prediction task, the paper may inform mechanistic expectations about when punishment is likely to be effective or who will implement it, but it does not supply data or direct evidence to calibrate efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions.

- source: Sasaki, T., Okada, I., & Nakai, Y. (2016). Indirect reciprocity can overcome free-rider problems on costly moral assessment. *Biology Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in indirect reciprocity models with costly assessment, cooperation is not stable unless there is a mechanism to sanction or exclude those who evade the cost of assessment. By introducing a pre-assessment stage that labels non-payers as 'nasty' and excludes them from receiving help, the model shows that a cooperative equilibrium (where everyone pays for assessment and helps others) can be locally stable if the benefit of helping exceeds the cost of assessment. This mechanism is analogous to pool punishment in public goods games and is effective under leading social norms. The findings are theoretical but directly address efficiency and the conditions for stable cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that institutional mechanisms to sanction or exclude cost-evaders (such as pre-assessment or pool punishment) can significantly improve efficiency in social dilemma games with costly monitoring or punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a pre-assessment or institutionalized punishment mechanism is likely to shift efficiency from low (collapse of cooperation) to high (stable cooperation), provided the net benefit of cooperation is positive. The results are robust across different social norms and suggest that the design of the punishment/sanctioning mechanism is a key moderator of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Raihani, N. J. (2014). Hidden altruism in a real-world setting. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: show_other_summaries
  findings: The main empirical finding is that donors to an online fundraising platform are more likely to conceal their identity when making donations that are either much lower or much higher than the norm for a given appeal. The paper interprets this as evidence that individuals may hide norm-violating cooperative behavior (especially high donations) to avoid social costs such as punishment or ostracism, as has been observed in public goods game experiments. However, the study does not directly measure punishment, sanctions, or efficiency, and the context is real-world charitable giving rather than a controlled public goods game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that social punishment or ostracism can target both low and high contributors in cooperative settings, which may limit the extent of visible over-contribution. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment mechanisms on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that social costs for norm-violating contributions (including over-contribution) may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes. Its main value is in highlighting the potential for negative social responses to both under- and over-contribution.

- source: Aguilar, E. G., & Akçay, E. (2018). Gene-Culture Coinheritance of a Behavioral Trait. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The main finding is that for traits inherited both genetically and culturally, cultural selection can drive the evolution of a trait even when genetic selection opposes it, provided the variance in culture is high relative to genetic variance. The model is applied to altruism, showing that cultural selection can favor the spread of altruistic behaviors even when they are genetically maladaptive. The framework also demonstrates that cultural transmission can modify the effect of genetic selection itself. The paper does not address punishment, sanctions, or efficiency in public goods games, nor does it report empirical or simulated outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how cultural and genetic selection interact in the evolution of altruism, which is conceptually adjacent to cooperation in public goods games. However, it does not address punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze the game design dimensions relevant to public goods games. Its main relevance is in highlighting that cultural mechanisms (such as social learning) can enable the spread of cooperative or altruistic behaviors even when they are costly in terms of genetic fitness, especially when cultural variance is high. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public goods games with or without punishment, this paper offers only indirect, high-level theoretical context.
