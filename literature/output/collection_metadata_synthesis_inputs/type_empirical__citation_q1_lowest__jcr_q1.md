- source: Jiang, X., & Wang, X. H. (2024). Gender difference in leaders framed with responsibility or authority: An experimental study. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a leader with the power to punish increases group contributions and efficiency compared to the no-leader baseline. When the leader's role is framed as responsibility (rather than authority), and especially when the leader is selected by voting, female leaders are more effective at increasing group income and efficiency than male leaders. The responsibility framing increases both leader contributions and the effectiveness of punishment, but the positive effect on efficiency is robustly observed only in the voting stage for female-led groups. In other conditions (authority framing, male leaders, or self-selected leaders), the efficiency gains from punishment are smaller or not significant. The study also shows that leader contributions are a more important driver of efficiency than punishment per se, as punishment can offset its own benefits through costs. Overall, the presence of punishment increases efficiency, but the magnitude depends on leader gender, framing, and selection method.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a public good game increases efficiency, especially when the leader is female, the leadership role is framed as responsibility, and the leader is selected by voting. The game design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency gains from punishment are: presence of a leader with punishment power, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (4), number of rounds (5), and information structure (no chat, summary feedback). The control (no-leader) efficiency is reported, and the treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency is higher, particularly in the responsibility/voting/female leader condition. For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when the leader is prosocial (as selected or induced by responsibility framing), and that leader contributions are a key mechanism. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with punishment, especially with comparable group size, round number, and punishment parameters.

- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., Gross, J., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., van Dijk, E., & de Dreu, C. K. W. (2023). Discriminatory punishment undermines the enforcement of group cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

- source: Duell, D., Mengel, F., Mohlin, E., & Weidenholzer, S. (2024). Cooperation through collective punishment and participation. *Political Science Research and Methods*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring (noisy signals about individual contributions, but perfect information about total group contribution), the authors experimentally compare five regimes: no punishment, peer-to-peer punishment, collective punishment, peer-to-peer punishment with commitment, and collective punishment with commitment. The key finding is that only collective punishment with commitment (where group members can opt in to a regime that automatically punishes the whole group if total contributions fall short) leads to significantly higher group payoffs and cooperation rates compared to both no punishment and peer-to-peer punishment. Peer-to-peer punishment does not improve payoffs or cooperation relative to no punishment in this noisy environment. The positive effect of collective punishment with commitment is especially pronounced in the later rounds, suggesting a learning or coordination effect. The study also shows that the ability to participate in the decision to implement collective punishment (i.e., voting/commitment) is crucial for its effectiveness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling collective punishment with commitment in a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling collective punishment with commitment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff) substantially, especially over time, provided the punishment is collective, pre-committed, and participation in the institution is possible. The effect is not observed for peer-to-peer punishment or for collective punishment without commitment. The experimental design and reporting allow for direct mapping to prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, given the game design dimensions.

- source: Chen, J. C., D'Attoma, J., Fonseca, M. A., & Malezieux, A. (2025). Promoting Cooperation in an Unequal World: Experimental Evidence on the Role of Transparency and Punishment. *Journal of Business Ethics*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across two lab experiments using a four-player linear public goods game with unequal endowments (determined by merit and luck), the paper finds that: (1) Peer punishment increases contributions and group payoffs, but only when endowments are transparent. (2) When endowments are secret, punishment has a limited effect on cooperation and does not reduce inequality; advantaged participants contribute a lower fraction of their endowment and earn much higher payoffs. (3) When endowments are made public, punishment becomes much more effective at increasing contributions and reducing inequality, especially among the advantaged. (4) Willingness to pay for secrecy is higher among advantaged participants, especially when their advantage is due to luck. (5) Overall, transparency is necessary for punishment to improve efficiency and mitigate the negative effects of inequality.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on whether endowments (wealth) are transparent. In games with secret endowments, punishment has little effect on group payoff or efficiency; in games with public endowments, punishment significantly increases contributions and group payoffs, especially among the advantaged. For prediction, the presence of transparency (showing endowments or relevant summaries) is a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when transparency is high. The game uses continuous contributions, no chat, and a fixed MPCR of 0.4. Punishment is costly (cost 1, impact 3), and there is no reward option. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar design features.
