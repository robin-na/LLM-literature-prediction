- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2009). Public Goods Provision and Sanctioning in Privileged Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares standard (normal) and privileged public goods groups, with and without the option for decentralized costly punishment. In normal groups (all members have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment substantially increases contributions and group earnings, especially in later rounds. In privileged groups (one member has a high marginal return, others low), punishment increases contributions and earnings only slightly, and it increases inequality. The main reason is that punishment is less targeted and less effective in privileged groups: high-benefit members punish in an unstructured way, and low-benefit members respond weakly to punishment. As a result, the efficiency gains from punishment are much smaller in privileged groups than in normal groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends strongly on group composition, specifically on whether the group is homogeneous (normal) or contains privileged members (heterogeneous marginal returns). For standard linear PGGs with 3 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, punishment increases efficiency, especially in later rounds. However, if the group is privileged (one high-benefit, two low-benefit), the efficiency gain from punishment is much smaller, and inequality increases. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to account for heterogeneity in marginal returns. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher in privileged groups, but the treatment effect of punishment is much weaker than in normal groups.

- source: Decker, T., Stiehler, A., & Strobel, M. (2003). A comparison of punishment rules in repeated public good games - An experimental study. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that collective punishment rules generally induce more punishment activity and higher contributions than the individual rule. The most severe collective rule (maxi) achieves the highest contributions but not the highest efficiency, as the costs of punishment offset the gains from increased cooperation. Efficiency (group profit after punishment costs) does not differ significantly between rules, and the relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is reversed compared to the relationship with contributions. Emotional punishment, especially in the most severe rule, can damage efficiency. Justice (as measured by profit-contribution correlation and variance) improves with more severe collective rules. Participants are more likely to support collective rules if they expect higher profits and if the rule requires a higher degree of consent (e.g., unanimity).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public good game with punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not guarantee higher efficiency or group profit. The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the severity and structure of the punishment rule: more severe rules can increase cooperation but may reduce efficiency if punishment costs are high, especially due to emotional punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency with punishment is non-monotonic and depends on the balance between increased cooperation and punishment costs. Game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology, and the collective/individual nature of punishment are critical moderators. The paper's results caution against assuming that more severe or more available punishment will always improve efficiency.

- source: Fehr, E., Fischbacher, U., & Gächter, S. (2002). Strong reciprocity, human cooperation, and the enforcement of social norms. *Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the introduction of a costly punishment option leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation and a 40% increase in aggregate monetary payoff, even after subtracting the costs of punishment. Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency unravel over time, with most subjects defecting by the end. With punishment, cooperation is sustained or even increases, and group earnings approach the social optimum. The effect is observed both in stable groups (partner design) and in randomly re-matched groups (stranger design), though the effect is stronger in stable groups. The paper also shows that punishment is targeted at defectors and is proportional to the degree of free-riding. The findings are robust across cultures and with high monetary stakes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard public goods game with known MPCR, group size, and round structure leads to a large, positive effect on group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust to group composition (fixed or random), and the paper provides enough detail on the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) to inform predictions for similar designs. The findings support the prediction that, controlling for control-game efficiency, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently effective and observable.

- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Price, M. E. (2008). The resurrection of group selection as a theory of human cooperation. *Social Justice Research*.

  findings: The review highlights that experimental public good games and related economic games (including with punishment) show that people often contribute more than predicted by self-interest and sometimes punish non-cooperators at a cost to themselves, even when reciprocity and reputation are ruled out. This pattern is observed across diverse societies. However, the review argues that these behavioral outcomes do not necessarily indicate group selection or strong reciprocity as an evolved adaptation, and that the experimental settings may not reflect ancestral environments. It reviews behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment) from experimental games, but does not report or synthesize efficiency, group payoff, or welfare data. The main value for prediction is contextual: it affirms that punishment is present in many public good game experiments and that it affects contribution behavior, but it does not quantify or analyze efficiency effects. The review also cautions that experimental game results may not generalize to real-world or evolutionary contexts.

- source: Fatas, E., Morales, A. J., & Ubeda, P. (2010). Blind justice: An experimental analysis of random punishment in team production. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a public goods game with a novel random punishment mechanism: after each round, each group member is excluded from the group benefit with probability 1-R, where R is the normalized group contribution. This 'blind' punishment does not target individual defectors. The experiment compares three conditions: standard VCM (no punishment), random punishment without redistribution (RP), and random punishment with redistribution (RPR). Results show that both RP and RPR significantly increase contributions compared to VCM. However, only RPR increases group earnings (efficiency), as RP's punishment cost outweighs the contribution gains. The effect of random punishment is similar whether introduced after a baseline or from the start. Behavioral analysis shows that high contributors reduce their contributions if punished, but overall, conditional cooperation persists and the net effect is positive for contributions. The mechanism does not fully prevent the decline in contributions over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that random, non-individually-targeted punishment can increase contributions in a standard public goods game, but its effect on efficiency depends on whether the penalty is redistributed. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism that is not individually targeted but is probabilistically linked to group performance will likely increase contributions, but may not increase efficiency unless the punishment cost is recycled within the group. The effect is robust to order and does not depend on communication or knowledge of individual contributions. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5, and the group size is 4. The findings suggest that, for similar designs, enabling random punishment will increase contributions and may increase efficiency if the punishment cost is not lost to the group.

  findings: The study finds that in both public goods and resource dilemma games, the presence of fear (risk of zero payoff for cooperation) interacts with individual trust to affect cooperation rates. High trusters cooperate more than low trusters when fear is present, but this difference disappears when fear is absent. Removing fear increases cooperation among low trusters in resource dilemmas, but decreases cooperation among high trusters in public goods games. The paper does not experimentally manipulate or measure punishment, but discusses its theoretical relevance.   prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is useful for understanding how trust and fear interact to affect cooperation rates, but does not manipulate or measure punishment, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The discussion suggests that punishment may be more relevant in no-fear situations, but this is not tested. Thus, the paper offers only indirect, contextual support for prediction tasks involving punishment and efficiency.

- source: Guillen, P., Fatas, E., & Brañas-Garza, P. (2010). Inducing efficient conditional cooperation patterns in public goods games, an experimental investigation. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The introduction of a grim trigger strategy threat (via possible automata) leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (by 40% before a surprise restart and 50% after) compared to a standard repeated public goods game. Contributions in the threat treatment are highly polarized (either full or zero), and the pattern of conditional cooperation changes: in the threat treatment, the minimum contribution in the previous round predicts current contributions, consistent with grim trigger logic. However, contributions still decline over time, and the effect is driven by belief manipulation rather than actual punishment or sanctions. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the higher contributions imply higher group payoffs, net of the absence of punishment costs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that belief in the possibility of strict conditional cooperation (grim trigger) can substantially increase contributions in repeated public goods games, even without actual punishment. However, because there is no real punishment mechanism (no cost, no assignment of punishment, no reduction of others' payoffs), the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to enabling punishment. The results suggest that the mere threat or belief in sanctions can have large effects on cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency impact of actual punishment mechanisms, especially considering the cost of punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, but the absence of real punishment means the findings should be used as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Webb, J., & Foddy, M. (2004). Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts. *Small Group Research*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

- source: POLLOCK, G. B. (1988). POPULATION-STRUCTURE, SPITE, AND THE ITERATED PRISONERS-DILEMMA. *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*.

  findings: The paper finds that punishment-based strategies (TFT/NTFT) can maintain high group efficiency and suppress defection when the population is structured into multiple groups or dyads, allowing intergroup competition. - source: Guillen, P., Schwieren, C., & Staffiero, G. (2007). Why feed the Leviathan?. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that a centralized sanctioning mechanism (CSM) in a step-level public good game leads to a dramatic increase in the rate at which groups successfully provide the public good (i.e., reach the threshold). In the baseline (no CSM), groups almost never reach the threshold after a few rounds. With CSM, the threshold is reached in nearly 90% of rounds.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a centralized punishment mechanism in a threshold (step-level) public good game increases the rate of successful public good provision, both during and after the punishment phase. The effect persists even after punishment is removed, supporting the idea of an 'educational' or coordination effect. The CSM is costly, so the net efficiency gain depends on the balance between increased public good provision and the cost of the mechanism. For prediction, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to substantially increase efficiency (proxied by provision rate) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially in step-level games with coordination problems. The effect is robust to whether removal is automatic or by vote. However, the paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must use provision rate as a proxy.

- source: Whitmeyer, J. M. (2004). The group control catastrophe. *Sociological Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. Instead, for intermediate monitoring efficacy, compliance can collapse to zero, creating a bifurcation with two possible equilibria (full compliance or full defection), depending on initial conditions (e.g., the number of initial cooperators).   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on group compliance and payoffs in public-goods-like environments is highly non-linear and can depend critically on monitoring efficacy, initial conditions, and the structure of sanctions. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.
