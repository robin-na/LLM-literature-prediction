- source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in a population with probabilistic corrupt enforcers and violators. *Mathematical Models & Methods in Applied Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. If the expected loss equals the bribe, the system exhibits neutral cycles (limit cycles) with periodic oscillations in cooperation and efficiency. If the expected loss exceeds the bribe, the system can either converge to all-defection (low efficiency) or to a heteroclinic cycle with cyclic dominance, which may still allow for periods of cooperation. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings. The findings are directly informative for mapping game design parameters (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, bribe size, probability of corruption) to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Liu, L. J., Wang, S. X., Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2018). Evolutionary dynamics in the public goods games with switching between punishment and exclusion. *Chaos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a well-mixed infinite population PGG, both peer-based and pool-based punishment and exclusion mechanisms can promote cooperation and increase average group payoff (efficiency) compared to defection. When individuals use a switching strategy—punishing defectors when they are few and excluding them when they are many—there is a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and sanctioning strategists, leading to higher average payoffs. The efficiency gains are maximized at intermediate switching thresholds. Pure punishment (high threshold) leads to dominance of defectors and low efficiency, while pure exclusion (low threshold) can lead to oscillatory coexistence but still supports higher efficiency than no sanctions. The model explicitly tracks average payoff as the key outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment (and/or exclusion) in a standard PGG increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to the control (no sanctions), especially when the sanctioning mechanism is flexible (switching between punishment and exclusion based on the number of defectors). The effect is sensitive to the threshold for switching, the costs and effectiveness of punishment/exclusion, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The model is fully specified for N=5, r=3, c=1, and various punishment/exclusion costs, and can be used to predict efficiency under different game design dimensions. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design parameters in PGG-like environments.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both decentralized punishment and reward can drive a networked public goods game to high cooperation, but the minimal cumulative cost required depends on the initial cooperation level. If the initial fraction of cooperators is low, reward is more cost-effective; if it is high, punishment is more cost-effective. The study provides analytical and simulation results for the optimal incentive protocols and the conditions under which full cooperation is stable. The main outcome is the cumulative cost of incentives needed to reach a target cooperation level, not group efficiency or total payoff per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in networked public goods games. While it does not report group efficiency directly, it analyzes the cost (incentives spent) required to achieve high cooperation, which is closely related to efficiency when incentive costs are subtracted from group payoffs. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment (in terms of cost to achieve cooperation) depends on initial conditions and the structure of the game (network, group size, synergy factor). For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is not always the most efficient intervention; its relative efficiency compared to reward depends on the starting state of the group. The detailed modeling of incentive cost and cooperation dynamics can inform parameterizations of efficiency in similar networked PGG environments.

However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting how design dimensions like punishment cost and punishment effectiveness affect cooperation rates, and by extension may suggest (but not demonstrate) positive effects on efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides indirect support for the idea that lower punishment cost and higher punishment effectiveness (via synergy) should increase efficiency, but this is inferred from cooperation rates, not measured payoffs.

The findings are most useful for predicting the effect of punishment on cooperation rates, and only indirectly suggest that efficiency may increase when cooperation is high. For the downstream prediction task (treatment efficiency from design dimensions plus control efficiency), this paper provides only indirect, mechanistic support, not quantitative or direct evidence.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2018). Punishment and inspection for governing the commons in a feedback-evolving game. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment and inspection mechanisms can promote cooperation and prevent overexploitation in a common-pool resource game, but their ability to improve group efficiency (resource sustainability) depends critically on the intrinsic growth rate of the resource. If the resource renews too slowly, even perfect cooperation enforced by strong punishment leads to resource depletion and low efficiency. At moderate growth rates, strong punishment and inspection can achieve both high cooperation and sustainable resource levels, but weak institutions result in mixed strategy populations and depleted resources. At high growth rates, the resource is always sustainable regardless of punishment, and punishment only affects the fraction of cooperators versus defectors. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is context-dependent and can be absent or even negative if resource dynamics are unfavorable.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like common-pool resource games is highly contingent on the ecological context, especially the resource's intrinsic growth rate. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is crucial to consider not only the game design dimensions (such as punishment cost and effectiveness) but also the underlying dynamics of the resource. In settings where the resource cannot recover quickly, punishment may not improve efficiency even if it increases cooperation. In more favorable ecological conditions, punishment can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, but only if it is sufficiently effective (high detection probability and/or fine). Thus, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universal and must be interpreted in light of resource dynamics.

- source: Yan, F., Chen, X. J., Qiu, Z. P., & Szolnoki, A. (2021). Cooperator driven oscillation in a time-delayed feedback-evolving game. *New Journal of Physics*.

  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment (probabilistic detection and fine for defectors) can stabilize cooperation and maintain the common resource at sustainable levels, but only if the product of detection probability and fine (pβ) is sufficiently large relative to the cost of cooperation or the reinvestment rate. When punishment is strong, the system converges to a stable, efficient state with high cooperation and resource sustainability. When punishment is weak, defectors dominate and the resource is depleted. If cooperator reinvestment is proportional to endowment and there is a time delay in environmental feedback, the system can exhibit persistent oscillations in cooperation and resource levels, especially when punishment is moderate and resource growth is not too fast or too slow. The critical threshold for punishment effectiveness and the impact of time delay are analytically characterized. The model provides explicit conditions under which punishment improves or fails to improve group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment (with known cost and effectiveness) affects group efficiency in a common-pool resource game with feedback dynamics. The results are most relevant for adjacent PGG-like environments with renewable resources and environmental feedback. The model gives explicit formulas for the stationary group payoff/resource level as a function of punishment parameters, player count, resource growth, and reinvestment structure. For prediction, the key insight is that increasing punishment effectiveness (higher pβ) generally increases efficiency up to a threshold, above which full cooperation and sustainability are achieved. However, if punishment is too weak, efficiency remains low. Time delay in feedback can destabilize the system and induce oscillations, but does not always reduce average efficiency. The findings are most transferable to settings with institutional punishment, renewable resources, and feedback between group action and group payoff.
