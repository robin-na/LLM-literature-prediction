- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

However, if punishment is relatively cheap or the game is played for many rounds, efficiency losses may be mitigated or reversed. The review provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the importance of punishment cost, number of rounds, and the structure of punishment opportunities as moderators of the efficiency effect. It also cautions that the presence of counter-punishment or 'perverse' punishment can further reduce efficiency.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Parks, C. D., Joireman, J., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2013). Cooperation, Trust, and Antagonism: How Public Goods Are Promoted. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*.

  findings: The paper finds that both punishment and reward mechanisms are effective at promoting cooperation in public goods games and similar social dilemmas, with meta-analytic evidence indicating that their effects on cooperation are roughly equal and of medium size. The presence of punishment opportunities leads to higher contributions to public goods over time, and thus to higher group efficiency and collective welfare. The effectiveness of punishment is moderated by factors such as trust (more effective in high-trust societies), group size (less effective in large groups), and the iterated nature of the game (more effective in repeated interactions). The review also notes potential downsides of punishment, such as undermining intrinsic motivation or evoking retaliation, but concludes that, overall, structural solutions like punishment are robust tools for increasing cooperation and efficiency in public goods settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper should inform prediction by reinforcing that, across a wide range of public goods game designs, enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and collective welfare compared to control conditions without such mechanisms. The effect is robust across many studies and is supported by meta-analytic evidence. However, the magnitude of the effect may depend on moderators such as group size, trust, and the iterated nature of the game. The review provides indirect evidence for the predictive value of design dimensions such as punishment cost, identifiability, communication, and group size. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, especially in high-trust, small-group, iterated settings.

- source: dos Santos, M., & Wedekind, C. (2015). Reputation based on punishment rather than generosity allows for evolution of cooperation in sizable groups. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper shows that in simulated public goods games, a reputation system based on punitive actions (punishment scoring) allows for the evolution and maintenance of high cooperation frequencies in large groups, even when errors in reputation assessment are present. In contrast, reputation based on generosity (contributions) or rewards is much less effective: generosity-based reputation rarely leads to cooperation in large groups, and reward-based systems are unstable due to invasion by non-rewarders. The model assumes binary contribution and punishment choices, with payoffs used for evolutionary selection, but does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff as outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment-based reputation mechanisms are more effective than generosity- or reward-based reputation for sustaining cooperation in public goods games, especially as group size increases and errors in reputation assessment become more likely. However, because the main outcome is cooperation frequency rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting the likely positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, especially in large groups and error-prone environments. The model assumes all-or-nothing contribution and punishment choices, no communication, and full observability of actions (except for errors in reputation memory). The results are most informative for predicting the effect of punishment in large, anonymous, repeated PGGs with reputation tracking, but do not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency.

The results also warn that adding rewards without second-order incentives can reduce cooperation, which may imply lower efficiency, but this is not directly measured.

However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.
