- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. - source: Gürdal, M. Y., Gürerk, & Yahsi, M. (2021). Culture and prevalence of sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Lim, W., & Zhang, J. P. (2020). Endogenous Authority and Enforcement in Public Goods Games. *B E Journal of Theoretical Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing an endogenous authority with punishment power in a linear public goods game significantly increases group efficiency and average earnings compared to a no-authority baseline. In the experiment, with N=4, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical prediction) or 12.6% (empirical, due to positive baseline contributions) to 40.9% with authority; with N=8, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical) or 16.6% (empirical) to 47.7%. The social cost of contesting for authority is lower in larger groups (18.95% for N=8 vs 32.2% for N=4). Authorities tend to punish the lowest contributor and exercise most of their punishment power. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across treatment orders. Authorities contribute less than others but still contribute positively. The mechanism is effective at raising efficiency, especially as group size increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a punishment-enabled authority in a linear public goods game. The design dimensions (player_count, mpcr, punishment_tech, etc.) are clearly specified and manipulated. The efficiency gains from punishment are substantial and increase with group size, while the social cost of authority selection decreases with group size. The control (no authority) efficiency is empirically measured, and the treatment (authority/punishment enabled) efficiency is reported, allowing for direct mapping to the prediction task. The findings suggest that, for similar game parameters, enabling punishment via an endogenous authority will significantly increase efficiency, with the effect size moderated by group size and the cost structure of authority selection.

- source: Glöckner, A., Kube, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2018). The joint benefits of observed and unobserved social sanctions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players over ten rounds, the authors compare four conditions: no punishment, observed punishment only, unobserved (delayed) punishment only, and both punishment channels available. Both observed and unobserved punishment alone increase contributions but do not improve efficiency (average payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline, due to the costliness of punishment. However, when both punishment channels are available, group efficiency is significantly higher than in all other conditions. This is because the threat of unobserved punishment increases the effectiveness of observed punishment, leading to higher cooperation with less punishment expenditure. The combination of punishment channels thus overcomes the typical efficiency losses seen in standard lab PGG punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the punishment institution. Standard punishment (observed or unobserved alone) does not improve efficiency over the control, but the combination of both channels leads to a large positive effect. For prediction, this means that the presence of multiple punishment channels (observed and unobserved) is a key moderator: if only one is present, efficiency is not improved; if both are present, efficiency is substantially increased. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the punishment technology (timing/observability), cost, and whether multiple channels are available. Control efficiency (no punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless the institutional details of punishment are considered.

When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors.   prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.
