- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., Masclet, D., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Norm enforcement in social dilemmas: An experiment with police commissioners. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Kingsley, D. C. (2016). Endowment heterogeneity and peer punishment in a public good experiment: Cooperation and normative conflict. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that peer punishment increases contributions and group earnings in public goods games with homogeneous endowments, but not in games with heterogeneous endowments. In homogeneous groups, punishment mitigates the decay of cooperation and raises average contributions and pre-punishment earnings, though post-punishment earnings are lower due to the cost of punishment. In heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase contributions or earnings, and punishment costs further reduce efficiency. The results suggest that normative conflict arising from endowment heterogeneity undermines the effectiveness of peer punishment as a self-governance mechanism for promoting efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on endowment homogeneity. When endowments are homogeneous, enabling punishment increases contributions and can increase efficiency (though punishment costs may offset gains). When endowments are heterogeneous and endowment information is not available in the punishment stage, enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency, and punishment costs reduce group earnings. For prediction, the presence of endowment heterogeneity (without endowment information in punishment) should be expected to moderate or eliminate the positive effect of punishment on efficiency, even if control efficiency is known. The game is a continuous-contribution, interior-solution PGG with 4 players, 15 rounds, no chat, and punishment cost/impact of 1:3.

- source: Gürerk (2013). Social learning increases the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Boosey, L., & Isaac, R. M. (2016). Asymmetric network monitoring and punishment in public goods experiments. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment compares three network structures (Complete, Circle, Asymmetric) in a repeated linear public goods game with decentralized punishment. In all treatments, punishment is possible, but the network structure determines who can punish whom. The Asymmetric network features one under-monitored player (D) who can only be punished by one other player (C), while C is responsible for punishing all others. The main findings are: (1) Average contributions and efficiency are significantly lower in the Asymmetric network than in the Complete or Circle networks; (2) The drop in efficiency is driven entirely by the under-monitored player D, who contributes less and retaliates more against punishment; (3) There is no evidence that players with sole punishment responsibility (C) punish their unique target (D) more harshly; (4) Retaliatory (anti-social) punishment is more frequent from the under-monitored player, deterring effective punishment and reducing efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that network asymmetry can undermine the positive effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment network is a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency in public goods games. When punishment is possible but monitoring/punishment is asymmetric (i.e., some players are under-monitored), efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or eliminated, primarily due to lower contributions and increased retaliation by under-monitored players. In symmetric networks (Complete, Circle), punishment supports higher contributions and efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the symmetry and coverage of the punishment network. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is known, the expected efficiency gain from punishment will be lower or even negative in asymmetric/patched networks compared to symmetric ones, even with identical punishment parameters.

- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2013). Legitimate punishment, feedback, and the enforcement of cooperation. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (unrestricted punishment, full feedback), (2) Full R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, full feedback), and (3) Partial R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, partial feedback). Restricting punishment to only allow higher contributors to punish lower contributors (legitimate punishment) increases both cooperation and efficiency compared to unrestricted punishment. The highest efficiency and earnings are observed in the Full R. treatment, where both legitimacy and full feedback are present. In the Baseline, a significant share of punishment is antisocial, which reduces efficiency. When feedback about high contributors is removed (Partial R.), the positive effect of legitimate punishment on efficiency and cooperation is greatly reduced. Thus, both legitimacy and feedback are necessary for punishment to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, restricting punishment to legitimate cases (i.e., only higher contributors can punish lower contributors) and providing full feedback about all group members' contributions both increase efficiency and group earnings compared to unrestricted punishment. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling unrestricted punishment may not increase efficiency due to antisocial punishment, but enabling legitimate punishment with full feedback is likely to yield a substantial efficiency gain. The effect is much weaker or absent if feedback about high contributors is not provided. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the structure of punishment and information feedback.

- source: Leibbrandt, A., Ramalingam, A., Sääksvuori, L., & Walker, J. M. (2015). Incomplete punishment networks in public goods games: experimental evidence. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study experimentally manipulates punishment network structure and punishment capacity in a repeated linear public goods game with four players per group. Treatments include no-punishment, complete punishment, pairwise punishment, and untouchable punishment networks, as well as variants with increased punishment capacity. The main findings are: (1) Complete punishment networks lead to higher contributions than no-punishment or pairwise networks, but not necessarily higher group earnings after accounting for punishment costs. (2) Pairwise punishment networks are less effective at increasing contributions and do not improve efficiency. (3) Increasing punishment capacity in incomplete networks does not significantly improve contributions or efficiency. (4) The structure of the punishment network is more important than the absolute punishment capacity for group outcomes. (5) Targeted revenge is a major driver of antisocial punishment. (6) The presence of an 'untouchable' (who cannot punish or be punished) does not significantly reduce the willingness of others to contribute, but untouchables contribute less and earn more.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment network (who can punish whom) is a critical moderator. Enabling punishment in a complete network increases contributions but does not necessarily increase efficiency due to the cost of punishment. Incomplete networks (e.g., pairwise) do not yield efficiency gains and may even reduce efficiency. Increasing punishment capacity without changing network structure does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, it is crucial to account for both the network structure and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless network structure and punishment costs are considered.

- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Grechenig, K., & Thöni, C. (2016). Information-sensitive Leviathans. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend strongly on the information environment and the type of punishment institution. Under perfect information, decentralized punishment leads to the highest contributions and profits (efficiency), outperforming centralized punishment and no punishment. As information becomes less accurate (signals about others' contributions are noisy), both contributions and profits decline in all institutions, but especially in DecPun. Under moderate noise, CenPun can attract more citizens and sometimes matches or slightly exceeds DecPun in efficiency, provided the authority avoids punishing cooperators. Under high noise, neither punishment institution yields substantial efficiency gains over no punishment. The key moderator is the extent to which punishment is misapplied to cooperators, which is more likely in DecPun under noise. The paper provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes with punishment enabled versus disabled, across different institutional and informational settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the information environment and the punishment institution's design. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially decentralized) can substantially increase efficiency under perfect information. However, as information becomes noisy, the efficiency gain from punishment is reduced, and the difference between decentralized and centralized punishment narrows. The presence of a central authority who avoids punishing cooperators can maintain or improve efficiency under moderate noise, but not under high noise. For prediction, the paper suggests that the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost/tech, and especially the information structure (accuracy of contribution signals) are critical moderators. The results also imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on these dimensions.

  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Kamei, K., & Putterman, L. (2015). In broad daylight: Fuller information and higher-order punishment opportunities can promote cooperation. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies how the availability of higher-order punishment (punishing punishers or non-punishers) affects cooperation and efficiency in repeated public goods games. The key findings are: (1) When higher-order punishment is enabled with full information (who punished whom), fixed group member IDs, and explicit display of past history, both cooperation and efficiency are significantly higher than in the control with only first-order punishment.   prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect depends on the information structure and punishment design. If punishment is enabled with full information about who punished whom, fixed IDs, and explicit history, efficiency is likely to increase relative to the control. If punishment is enabled with only ego-centric information, a dedicated counter-punishment stage, and no history, efficiency may decrease. In other cases, the effect may be neutral. The findings suggest that the game design dimensions—especially information about punishment, identifiability, and history—are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between treatments are clear.

  findings: This study experimentally compares two centralized punishment institutions in a linear public goods game: absolute punishment (all violators punished) and relative punishment (only the worst violators punished). If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and the punishment is non-deterrent, enabling relative punishment is likely to increase efficiency more than enabling absolute punishment. - source: Masclet, D., Noussair, C. N., & Villeval, M. C. (2013). THREAT AND PUNISHMENT IN PUBLIC GOOD EXPERIMENTS. *Economic Inquiry*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a linear public goods game with costly punishment, adding a stage where players can issue non-binding threats to punish (conditioned on others' contribution levels) increases average contributions and, in the latter periods, increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to a baseline with punishment but no threats. However, the cost of punishment initially offsets the benefit of higher contributions, so efficiency only improves in later rounds. Overall, threats can improve efficiency, but only under certain conditions and mainly in longer interactions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with punishment, adding a non-binding threat stage can increase efficiency (group payoff) relative to a baseline with punishment but no threats, but only in the latter periods of repeated play. The efficiency gain is modest and depends on the absence of second-order punishment (punishing those who fail to carry out threats). If second-order punishment is possible, efficiency falls to or below baseline. The effect is also sensitive to punishment effectiveness (punishment technology). For prediction, this suggests that enabling threats (structured, non-binding, and observable) can increase efficiency above the control (punishment-only) baseline, but only in longer games and only if threats are not themselves subject to enforcement. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (higher punishment technology).
