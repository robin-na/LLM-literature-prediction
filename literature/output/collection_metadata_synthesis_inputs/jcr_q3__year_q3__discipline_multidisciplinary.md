- source: Hou, G. L., Wang, F., Shi, J. Y., Chen, W. J., & Yu, J. (2019). Which is the ideal sanction for cooperation? An experimental study on different types of third-party sanctions. *Psych Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a standard PGG increases group earnings and cooperation compared to a baseline with no sanctions. The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Liu, Q., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2018). Evolutionary Public Goods Game on Evolving Random Networks. *Journal of the Korean Physical Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that allowing informed cooperators to rewire their network connections at a cost can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation in public goods games on random networks. Higher rates of network rewiring (by informed cooperators) lower the threshold for cooperation to emerge, especially under adverse conditions (low synergy factor). The system exhibits distinct phases (full cooperation, full defection, coexistence, and segregated sub-networks), and the transition between these phases depends on the synergy factor and rewiring rate. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not implement or analyze punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. Instead, it studies the effect of costly information and network rewiring on cooperation rates. While the findings suggest that network adaptation can promote cooperation, they do not provide evidence about efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor about the effect of punishment. Thus, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency, though it may be contextually relevant for understanding alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms.

- source: Wang, S., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2018). Non-Fixed Investment in Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Journal of the Korean Physical Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that allowing non-fixed investment in voluntary PGGs leads to complex rock-scissors-paper dynamics among cooperators, defectors, and loners. Increasing the maximum possible investment ratio can initially promote cooperation and increase average payoff, but further increases may reduce cooperation due to increased temptation to defect. Higher interest rates (MPCR) and lower loner payoffs promote both cooperation and higher average payoffs. The memory length (sensitivity to past payoffs) also moderates outcomes: moderate memory promotes higher average payoffs and cooperation, while very short or very long memory is detrimental. The system always converges to a stable equilibrium regardless of initial conditions. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present or discussed as interventions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in PGG-like environments, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the model or analysis. It is therefore not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. However, it does provide detailed evidence on how other design dimensions (investment flexibility, MPCR, loner payoff, memory) affect efficiency and cooperation in voluntary PGGs without punishment. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not relevant.

- source: Gomez-Ruiz, L., & Sánchez-Expósito, M. J. (2020). The Impact of Team Identity and Gender on Free-Riding Responses to Fear and Cooperation Sustainability. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that strong team identity significantly reduces free-riding responses to fear and increases cooperation rates among men, but not among women. The effect of team identity on cooperation sustainability (maintenance of cooperation over time) is marginally significant for men and not significant for women. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. No formal punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present; the study focuses on informal control (team identity) and gender differences. The findings suggest that team identity can be an effective informal mechanism to sustain cooperation among men in repeated social dilemmas with binary contribution choices and feedback, but not among women.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It studies a repeated binary-choice social dilemma (close to a public goods game) without any punishment or reward mechanism. The main manipulations are team identity and gender. The outcomes are behavioral (contribution rates, free-riding responses), not efficiency or group payoff. Therefore, this paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it does provide contextual evidence about the role of informal mechanisms (team identity) and gender in sustaining cooperation. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation rates in binary-choice, feedback-rich, no-punishment settings, but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Zhang, Q. H., Chen, Y. Y., Tao, Y., Farid, T., & Ma, J. H. (2019). How Consistent Contributors Inspire Individuals to Cooperate: The Role of Moral Elevation and Social Value Orientation. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: This study demonstrates that introducing a consistent contributor (CC) into a step-level public-goods-dilemma game increases both cooperation and group/individual earnings. The effect is robust across both pro-social and pro-self participants, though the underlying psychological mechanisms differ: pro-selfs are influenced via increased moral elevation, while pro-socials respond directly to the CC's behavior. The design does not include punishment or sanction mechanisms, but provides strong evidence that motivational (role model) interventions can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanction mechanisms, introducing a consistent contributor (role model) can increase group efficiency (payoff) in step-level public-goods games. The findings are most relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar motivational interventions, but do not inform the effect of punishment. The design and results can serve as a control or baseline for understanding efficiency in the absence of punishment, and highlight the potential for non-punitive mechanisms to improve outcomes.

- source: Liu, W. W., & Yang, J. N. (2018). The Evolutionary Game Theoretic Analysis for Sustainable Cooperation Relationship of Collaborative Innovation Network in Strategic Emerging Industries. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that government intervention through incentives and punishments can significantly influence the frequency of positive cooperation between enterprises and URIs in collaborative innovation networks. When the sum of incentives and punishments exceeds the total additional cost (TAC) of cooperation, positive cooperation becomes stable. If incentives and punishments are too low, negative cooperation prevails. The model does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, but focuses on the conditions for stable cooperation strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that punishment (and incentives) can increase the frequency of cooperative behavior in a multi-agent innovation network, but it does not quantify effects on efficiency or group payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves free-riding and cooperation, but the outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies) rather than payoff-based. The findings suggest that, in similar environments, increasing punishment and incentives above a threshold can shift the system toward more cooperation, which may imply higher efficiency, but this is not directly modeled or measured.

- source: Berger, J. (2021). Social Tipping Interventions Can Promote the Diffusion or Decay of Sustainable Consumption Norms in the Field. Evidence from a Quasi-Experimental Intervention Study. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that providing regular normative feedback about the prevalence of sustainable behavior (use of mugs) can trigger rapid norm change in the field, either increasing or decreasing sustainable behavior depending on the initial prevalence. In one cafeteria with a high initial rate of sustainable choices, the intervention led to a sharp increase in sustainable behavior, consistent with a social tipping dynamic. In another with a low initial rate, the same intervention led to a sharp decline (a 'boomerang effect'). Control conditions (appeal only or no intervention) showed little or no persistent change. The study does not report on efficiency, payoffs, or group welfare, and the outcome is strictly behavioral (proportion of sustainable choices).
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The intervention is not a PGG or close variant, and there is no punishment or sanctioning mechanism. The outcome is behavioral (norm adoption), not payoff-based. However, the study is relevant for understanding how feedback and norm interventions can shift group behavior in social dilemma contexts, and it highlights the importance of initial conditions for intervention success. For the downstream prediction task, this paper offers only indirect, contextual evidence about norm change dynamics, not about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment.

- source: Huang, L. B., DeScioli, P., & Murad, Z. (2021). Pulling for the Team: Competition Between Political Partisans. *Evolutionary Psychological Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In deterministic contest games where participants can spend money to increase their team's chance of winning a prize, people exert significantly less effort when in teams (3-on-3 or best-of-3) than when competing individually (1-on-1), with about a 20% reduction in effort. This pattern holds both for arbitrarily assigned teams and for teams based on political partisanship, even under strong partisan cues and during a highly salient election period. The results support theories of shirking and free-riding in group settings and challenge the idea that group identity or partisanship alone is sufficient to mobilize greater costly effort. No evidence is provided about the effects of punishment, communication, or other mechanisms that might increase group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group/team settings in contest-like environments lead to lower total effort (and thus lower group payoff/efficiency) compared to individual settings. The findings are adjacent to public goods games: the contest game is not a standard PGG, but the group incentive structure and the opportunity for free-riding are similar. The results suggest that, without punishment, efficiency is lower in group settings. However, the paper does not test punishment, so it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that group identity or partisanship alone is not sufficient to overcome shirking. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in the absence of punishment, group efficiency will be lower than in individual settings, and that adding group identity cues is unlikely to increase efficiency without additional mechanisms.
