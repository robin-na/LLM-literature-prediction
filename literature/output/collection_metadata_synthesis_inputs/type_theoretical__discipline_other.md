- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Rusch, H. (2012). An evolutionary perspective on the long-term efficiency of costly punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.
