- source: Engel, C. (2019). When Does Transparency Backfire? Putting Jeremy Bentham's Theory of General Prevention to the Experimental Test. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment manipulates the level of feedback about punishment and contributions in a standard linear public goods game with centralized punishment. Three feedback treatments (low, medium, high) vary the precision of information about others' contributions and punishment. The main finding is that full transparency (high treatment: individual-level information about both contributions and punishment) leads to significantly lower contributions than aggregate or less precise feedback. Information about punishment of others does not increase cooperation; instead, it can reduce it. Participants are more responsive to descriptive norms (others' contributions) than to injunctive signals (punishment). The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable, but reports total earnings descriptively.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with centralized punishment, increasing transparency about individual punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation (contributions), contrary to standard deterrence theory. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment with high transparency may not increase efficiency and could even reduce it, especially if the control (no punishment) game already has moderate contributions. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to contribution behavior. The design dimensions most relevant here are the feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), centralized punishment, and the linear PGG structure.

- source: Pedroso, M. (2021). Blind Cooperation: The Evolution of Redundancy via Ignorance. *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that redundancy in group cooperation can evolve without targeted punishment or coercion if individuals are ignorant of others' strategies and/or face harsh environments. In a threshold public goods game, when players do not know what others will do, they cooperate with an intermediate probability, leading to more cooperators than strictly necessary (redundancy). Harsher environments (higher group-level cost of failure) increase the probability of cooperation and thus redundancy. In contrast, if individuals are well-informed about others' strategies, redundancy does not evolve without punishment. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or group payoff, but discusses these concepts in relation to the model.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how information availability and environmental harshness affect cooperation and redundancy in threshold public goods games. It suggests that punishment is not strictly necessary for redundancy or intermediate cooperation rates if players are ignorant of others' actions. However, it does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff with or without punishment, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment parameters. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of information and environmental context as moderators, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

- source: Goodman, J. R. (2023). The problem of opportunity. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based models of the Prisoner's Dilemma with punishment (strong reciprocity), cooperation can be maintained at high levels when only overt defectors are present. However, when agents can mimic cooperators or defect covertly (evading detection and punishment), the population can be dominated by individuals who are not true cooperators, even though the observed rate of cooperation remains high. The models highlight that the opportunity to defect undetected undermines the effectiveness of punishment-based strategies in ensuring genuine prosociality. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but raises concerns that high observed cooperation rates in experimental games may not indicate high group welfare or internalized prosocial preferences.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical caution for interpreting high cooperation rates in punishment-enabled games as evidence of increased efficiency or group welfare. It shows that punishment can maintain high cooperation in simple models, but that the introduction of undetectable defection strategies (mimicry, covert defection) can allow free-riding to persist without reducing the observed cooperation rate. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment may not always translate to higher efficiency, especially if the game design allows for undetected defection. However, the models are dyadic PDs, not public goods games, and do not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction is not possible from this paper.

- source: Steimanis, I., Hofmann, R., Mbidzo, M., & Vollan, B. (2020). When female leaders believe that men make better leaders: Empowerment in community-based water management in rural Namibia. *Journal of Rural Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The introduction of conditional neutral punishment—where agents punish neighbors with the opposite strategy if their own payoff is below the neighborhood average—substantially increases the fraction of cooperators in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game. The effect is robust to variations in the temptation to defect and the cost and effectiveness of punishment. Cooperative punishers form protective clusters that shield cooperators, while antisocial punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) is largely ineffective. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperation, not group payoff or efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that conditional neutral punishment can increase cooperation rates in spatial social dilemmas, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the results are only indirectly informative: higher cooperation rates typically—but not always—imply higher efficiency, especially in prisoner's dilemma-like settings. However, without explicit efficiency or payoff data, the magnitude and reliability of efficiency gains from punishment in this design cannot be directly inferred. The spatial structure, conditionality of punishment, and the absence of communication or reward mechanisms are important contextual features.

- source: Fischborn, M. (2023). The Evolutionary Roots of Moral Responsibility. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that moral responsibility judgment evolved as an adaptation that enabled groups of cooperative individuals to hold free riders responsible more safely by acting in a coordinated way. It distinguishes between two senses of justification in responsibility judgment (demand and permission) and hypothesizes that the permission sense evolved to allow individuals to assess social support for punishment, making collective punishment episodes safer and more stable. The account is situated within Tomasello's two-step theory of the evolution of morality and Boehm's ethnographic work on hunter-gatherer punishment practices. The paper critiques Mameli's account of second-order punishment and meta-emotional dispositions, arguing that coordinated group punishment (rather than second-order punishment) is the key evolutionary innovation. The paper does not provide empirical evidence or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes, but offers a conceptual framework for understanding the robustness and negative bias of responsibility practices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or data for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is a theoretical account of the evolutionary function of punishment and responsibility judgment, emphasizing the importance of coordination and social support in making punishment episodes safer and more stable. While it discusses the role of punishment in stabilizing cooperation and addressing free rider problems, it does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes. Therefore, its value for the downstream prediction task is limited to providing conceptual context about why punishment may have evolved to support cooperation, but it does not inform quantitative or design-specific predictions about efficiency effects in experimental games.

- source: Wei, J., Zhong, Z. W., Chen, H. R., Arango-Aramburo, S., & Zhao, X. L. (2025). Exploring green electricity certificate purchasing behavior via a laboratory experiment. *Renewable & Sustainable Energy Reviews*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether job rotation can reduce the ratchet effect in a principal-agent setting. Three treatments are compared: no rotation (baseline), exogenous rotation (forced switch), and endogenous rotation (principal can pay to switch). In the baseline, workers often restrict effort to avoid revealing private information, resulting in lower efficiency. Exogenous rotation nearly eliminates the ratchet effect, with almost all workers providing high effort and firms earning significantly more. Endogenous rotation, where the principal can pay a small cost to rotate workers, also reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort, but the increase in firm earnings is not statistically significant. The findings support the idea that job rotation (especially exogenous) can improve efficiency by mitigating strategic output restriction, and that even a costly, non-credible threat of rotation (punishment) can have positive behavioral effects.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment option (here, job rotation) can increase efficiency by reducing strategic shirking, especially when the punishment is exogenously imposed. The effect is weaker but still present when the punishment is endogenous and costly. The experimental design is not a standard public goods game, but the principal-agent structure with costly punishment and efficiency outcomes is closely related. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling punishment (even at a cost) is likely to increase efficiency, with the effect size depending on whether the punishment is exogenous or endogenous and on the costliness of punishment.

- source: Lean, C. H., & Jones, C. J. (2023). The evolution of multispecies populations: a multilevel selection perspective. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical model showing that in multispecies mutualisms, group-structured dispersal (MLS1) can stabilize cooperation (mutualism) against cheater invasion, even when mutualism is individually costly. The model is applied to obligate and facultative mutualisms, arguing that metacommunity structure and weight-of-numbers dispersal can allow mutualists to outcompete cheaters at the community level. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or payoffs, but suggests that mutualistic communities are more productive and can proliferate under MLS1 dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is primarily theoretical and does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public-goods-game-like environments with or without punishment. However, it is relevant in that it discusses how group structure, dispersal, and the presence of cheaters affect the stability of cooperation (mutualism) in multispecies settings. The mechanisms discussed (e.g., metacommunity dispersal, weight-of-numbers advantage) are analogous to factors that can affect efficiency in PGGs, but the paper does not quantify these effects or relate them to specific game design dimensions. It does not analyze punishment as a discrete, costly action, but rather as community-level selection against cheaters. Thus, its value for the prediction task is mainly conceptual and mechanistic, not quantitative.

- source: Albergaria, M., & Saes, M. S. (2018). Measuring externalities in an information commons: the case of libraries. *Journal of Cleaner Production*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in a library (information commons) setting, users subject to non-monetary sanctions (professors, employees) generate negative externalities for users subject to monetary sanctions (students): an increase in non-student borrowing is associated with a nearly one-to-one decrease in student borrowing. Peer effects are positive but small. The analysis is based on observational data and does not report efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding behavioral externalities and the impact of different sanction types in common-pool resource settings, but do not directly inform about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its findings are limited to behavioral externalities (borrowing counts) in a field setting with different sanction types. While it highlights that sanction type can affect user behavior and externalities, it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the setting is only adjacent to public goods games. Thus, its value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited and indirect.

- source: Li, J. Q., Dang, J. W., Zhang, J. L., Chen, Z. Q., & Dehmer, M. (2022). Degree of satisfaction-based adaptive interaction in spatial Prisoner's dilemma. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that allowing individuals to adapt the intensity of their interactions based on satisfaction with payoffs from neighbors can greatly increase the level of cooperation in a spatial Prisoner's Dilemma. There are optimal values of the satisfaction parameter (\alpha) that maximize cooperation, and both too low and too high values of \alpha reduce cooperation. The mechanism works by creating positive feedback between satisfaction and interaction among cooperators, leading to robust cooperative clusters. However, the study does not include punishment, reward, or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the prediction of efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanctioning, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. Its findings on adaptive interaction intensity and cooperation in spatial Prisoner's Dilemma are only weakly relevant for predicting the effects of punishment in PGGs. The main value is in illustrating that adaptive partner selection or interaction intensity can promote cooperation, but this is not directly transferable to the efficiency effects of punishment.
