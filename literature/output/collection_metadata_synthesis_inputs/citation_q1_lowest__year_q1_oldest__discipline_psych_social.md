- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: Webb, J., & Foddy, M. (2004). Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts. *Small Group Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule. Voting for structural change (switching sanctioning systems) is predicted by individual profit and fines, not by group-level resource preservation. The study highlights that the impact of sanctioning systems on efficiency and payoff is heterogeneous and depends on both the structure of the sanction and the distribution of resource access.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

- source: Whitmeyer, J. M. (2004). The group control catastrophe. *Sociological Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. Instead, for intermediate monitoring efficacy, compliance can collapse to zero, creating a bifurcation with two possible equilibria (full compliance or full defection), depending on initial conditions (e.g., the number of initial cooperators). The 'catastrophe' is robust across a range of group sizes, sanction levels, internal control efficacies, and production function shapes (except linear). The equilibrium with high compliance yields higher payoffs for group members than the low-compliance equilibrium. The cost to the agent of obtaining compliance can be minimized by using only one type of sanction (individual or collective) and by leveraging initial cooperation to reach the high-compliance equilibrium, especially at moderate monitoring efficacy. However, the model does not directly report efficiency in the sense of group payoff relative to the maximum possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on group compliance and payoffs in public-goods-like environments is highly non-linear and can depend critically on monitoring efficacy, initial conditions, and the structure of sanctions. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.

- source: Samid, Y., & Suleiman, R. (2008). Effectiveness of Coercive and Voluntary Institutional Solutions to Social Dilemmas. *New Issues and Paradigms in Research on Social Dilemmas*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment shows that introducing a moderately coercive authority (with moderate fines for non-participation and moderate fees for participation) increases the rate of authority support and contributions compared to non-coercive or exploitative authority. However, when coercion becomes excessive (high fees and/or fines), groups may still converge to high authority support, but this does not necessarily improve efficiency, as the cost of coercion can outweigh the benefits. The non-coercive authority is ineffective unless social comparison is reduced (partial feedback), in which case cooperation increases. The main findings are about behavioral convergence to equilibria (all-defect, partial authority, all-authority) rather than direct efficiency or group payoff. The results highlight the importance of balancing the cost and effectiveness of coercive mechanisms to avoid undermining the benefits of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for predicting the effect of punishment (coercive authority) on efficiency in public-goods-like games. It suggests that moderate punishment (moderate fines and fees) can increase cooperation and authority support, which are likely to improve efficiency, but excessive punishment (high fees/fines) can lead to high compliance at the cost of efficiency, as the costs may outweigh the benefits. The paper does not report direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so predictions must be inferred from behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, authority support) and the structure of the payoff matrices. The findings also indicate that feedback about others' payoffs (social comparison) can reduce the effectiveness of non-coercive mechanisms. For prediction, the paper supports the idea that moderate, mutually agreed punishment is most likely to improve efficiency, while excessive punishment may not.

- source: Loukopoulos, P., Eek, D., Gärling, T., & Fujii, S. (2006). Palatable punishment in real-world social dilemmas? Punishing others to increase cooperation among the unpunished. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that intermittent punishment (where only some group members are punished for defection in a given round) increases cooperation rates among those not directly punished, compared to a no-punishment baseline. This 'spillover effect' is robust to changes in the framing of the decision (individual vs. group focus) and to information about prior cooperation by others. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

- source: Rosenbaum, S. M., Billinger, S., & Stieglitz, N. (2012). Safeguarding Common-Pool Resources in Transition Economies: Experimental Evidence from Central Asia. *Journal of Development Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that average contributions to the public good are high and nearly identical in both Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, despite differences in market integration and transition stage. The experiment is a one-shot, anonymous, five-player VCM with no punishment or reward options. The results suggest that, in the absence of sanctioning mechanisms, group size, anonymity, and market integration do not significantly affect contribution rates in this context. The paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, and does not manipulate or analyze punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism was present or analyzed. It does, however, provide contextual evidence about baseline contribution rates in a one-shot, five-player, anonymous VCM with MPCR=0.4 and no communication or sanctions. For prediction tasks, this paper is informative only for estimating control (no-punishment) efficiency in similar game designs, but provides no evidence on the effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Corriveau, L. (2012). Game theory and the kula. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in a repeated, circular n-player game (the 'game of kula'), the unique Nash equilibrium of the one-shot game is inefficient (all defect). However, when the game is repeated indefinitely and players are sufficiently patient, efficient (fully cooperative) equilibria can be sustained by social norms that attach punishment to deviations. The model formalizes three types of punishment-based norms: direct reciprocity (punished by the harmed party), indirect reciprocity (punished by a third party), and mixed reciprocity (punished by both). The mixed norm is shown to be the most robust, existing under the widest range of parameters. The analysis is entirely theoretical, but it provides formal conditions under which punishment enables efficient cooperation, and shows that the presence of punishment strategies can transform an inefficient game into an efficient one.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in repeated, multi-player social dilemma games (with circular or networked structure) can increase efficiency, provided players are sufficiently patient and punishment is credible. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the results generalize the repeated prisoner's dilemma. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is possible and observable, and where players have high discount factors, the introduction of punishment mechanisms can shift the equilibrium from inefficient (defection) to efficient (cooperation), thus increasing group payoff. The paper also clarifies that the structure of punishment (direct, indirect, mixed) affects the robustness of cooperation, with mixed norms being most robust. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, networked social dilemmas, especially as the number of players increases and as the possibility of both direct and indirect punishment exists.

- source: Evans, R., & Thomas, J. P. (2001). Cooperation and punishment. *Econometrica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper proves that in repeated common interest games (with no discounting), the mere presence of cooperative (nondraconian) strategies in the set of possible strategies does not guarantee efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcomes in equilibrium. However, if the set of possible strategies includes at least one 'draconian' strategy (i.e., a strategy that is willing to punish noncooperation to the maximum extent, such as min-maxing the opponent forever after a deviation), then all Nash equilibria are efficient. The paper also shows that this result is robust to certain computability restrictions and discusses the limits of these results under discounting and other technical assumptions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated games with a structure similar to public goods or common interest games, the availability of sufficiently severe punishment (i.e., the ability to impose maximal punishment at low or no cost) is both necessary and sufficient for punishment to induce efficient outcomes in equilibrium. If the game design allows for draconian punishment strategies, then enabling punishment should lead to high efficiency, regardless of the control efficiency. If only mild or 'cooperative' punishment is possible, efficiency gains are not guaranteed. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted games, but the logic is relevant for understanding the role of punishment severity and credibility in other settings.

- source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

- source: Heller, W. B., & Sieberg, K. K. (2008). Functional unpleasantness: the evolutionary logic of righteous resentment. *Public Choice*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation can be sustained in populations where some individuals are willing to punish cheaters, even if those punishers are themselves not cooperative. The evolutionary stability of cooperation depends on the relative costs of punishing, being punished, and being cheated. When the cost of punishing is less than the cost of being cheated, punishment can support a stable mix of cooperators and punishers, increasing overall efficiency compared to a world with only cheaters. If punishment is too costly, cheaters dominate and cooperation collapses. The model demonstrates that the existence of punishment mechanisms—regardless of the punishers' motives—can improve group welfare and efficiency in collective action settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in collective action games (including public goods game variants) can increase efficiency, as measured by average group payoff, provided the cost of punishing is not too high. The findings suggest that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment. While the model is not an exact public goods game, its structure and payoff logic are closely related, and its results are directly relevant for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency. The paper does not address empirical game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, or communication, but it does provide strong evidence that the cost and effectiveness of punishment are critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Jones, M. A. (1999). The effect of punishment duration of trigger strategies and quasifinite continuation probabilities for Prisoners' Dilemmas. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.

- source: Ziegler, R. (1997). The normative structure of solidarity and inequality. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both dyadic and collective solidarity games (including public-goods-like settings), strategies that combine defection with active sanctioning of others (Hypocritical Cooperation) can robustly enforce high levels of solidarity (cooperation), provided the costs of sanctioning are not too high. However, this can also lead to 'overcompliance' or excessive norm enforcement unless checked by counterstrategies (Full Opposition). The evolutionary outcome depends on the mix of strategies, the costs and effectiveness of sanctions, and the distribution of 'poor' and 'rich' in the population. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that informal punishment can sustain cooperation under certain conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that informal punishment mechanisms can sustain cooperation in public-goods-like environments, especially when the cost of sanctioning is not prohibitive and when the population includes actors motivated to enforce norms even while defecting themselves. However, the findings are based on simulation and focus on the prevalence of strategies and 'level of solidarity' rather than explicit group efficiency or payoff. The results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency will depend on the cost and effectiveness of sanctions, the strategic mix in the population, and the degree of inequality. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency, so its value for direct prediction is limited to qualitative insights about when punishment is likely to be effective.

- source: Cisarovsky, G., Bshary, A., Bouzelboudjen, M., & Bshary, R. (2012). Spatial Group Structure as Potential Mechanism to Maintain Cooperation in Fish Shoals of Unrelated Individuals. *Ethology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count
  findings: The paper finds that anthias in natural shoals have partially non-overlapping home ranges, and blennies (parasites) show site fidelity, attacking more frequently in certain areas. Individuals more exposed to the blenny may benefit more from punishing (chasing) the blenny, making punishment potentially self-serving rather than purely altruistic. This spatial structuring could help maintain the public good of punishment even in large, unrelated groups. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or direct fitness outcomes; it infers potential payoff implications from spatial and behavioral patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that spatial structure and local exposure can moderate the effectiveness and self-serving nature of punishment in public-goods-like dilemmas in natural systems. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a controlled way. For prediction tasks, the main value is in highlighting that spatial structure and heterogeneity in exposure can affect the distribution and potential benefits of punishment, but the paper does not provide direct input for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in standard PGG designs.

- source: Stiff, C. (2008). Are They Bothered? How the Opportunity to Damage a Partner's Reputation Influences Giving Behavior in a Trust Game. *Journal of Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The opportunity to leave feedback (damage a partner's reputation) increases the amount participants are willing to give in a one-shot trust game, but only when the partner is perceived to care about their reputation. This effect is strongest when the partner's future interaction group consists of strangers rather than friends. The effect of feedback opportunity and group familiarity on giving is mediated by the participant's perceived control over the partner's reputation. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare, and the design is a dyadic trust game rather than a public goods game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that reputation-based indirect sanctions (opportunity to leave feedback) can increase cooperative behavior (giving/trust) in dyadic trust games, especially when the target is reputation-sensitive. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of reputation-based sanctions depends on the perceived concern for reputation and the social context of future interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper is only adjacent: it supports the idea that indirect, reputation-based sanctions can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency effects or direct evidence for public goods games with explicit punishment mechanisms.
