- source: Greenwood, G. W., Abbass, H. A., & Petraki, E. (2018). When is altruistic punishment useful in social dilemmas?. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment in a public goods game is only effective at promoting cooperation (and thus improving group outcomes) if the frequency of punishers exceeds a threshold determined by the cost of contribution and the punishment level. If this threshold is not met, defection remains the dominant strategy and group efficiency does not improve. The analysis provides explicit formulas for the returns to each strategy and the minimum frequency of punishers required for punishment to be effective. The findings imply that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to improve efficiency; the population composition and punishment parameters are critical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public goods games. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional: punishment only increases efficiency if the proportion of punishers is above a threshold that depends on the cost of contribution and the punishment level. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on the game parameters (contribution cost, punishment cost/impact) and the frequency of punishers. The explicit formulas can be used to estimate the required conditions for punishment to be effective, but the paper does not provide empirical efficiency values or group payoff data.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & D'Amico, V. (2021). A preference to learn from successful rather than common behaviours in human social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Participants in a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4, groups of 4, 6 rounds) were more motivated to learn from and copy successful (high-earning) behaviors than to conform to common behaviors. When shown both the average (common) contribution and the average contribution of the highest earners from a previous experiment, participants contributed significantly less than when shown only the common behavior. This decline in cooperation was not explained by simply knowing about free riders, but specifically by the desire to learn from success. Most participants preferred to see information about successful behaviors over common behaviors. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the lower contributions in the 'success' information condition imply lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games without punishment or reward, providing information about successful (high-earning) behaviors leads to lower cooperation and thus likely lower efficiency, compared to providing only information about common behaviors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of success-based social information (even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward) can undermine efficiency. However, the study does not manipulate or analyze punishment directly, and so its direct relevance to predicting the effect of enabling punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of social information and learning in public goods games, and suggest that efficiency may be lower when participants can observe and learn from successful free-riding.

- source: Liu, X. L., Li, J. B., Wang, G. R., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., & Fu, X. G. (2020). Transcranial direct current stimulation of the rLPFC shifts normative judgments in voluntary cooperation. *Neuroscience Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study demonstrates that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects both participants' normative judgments about appropriate contributions and their actual contributions in a one-shot, linear, asymmetric public goods game. Anodal stimulation (increasing rLPFC excitability) leads to higher normative judgments and higher contributions, while cathodal stimulation (decreasing rLPFC excitability) leads to lower judgments and contributions, relative to sham. The effect is robust across both 'rich' and 'poor' player types. No punishment, sanction, or reward mechanisms are present in the game, and no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Its findings are relevant to understanding the neural basis of normative judgment and voluntary cooperation, but not to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions reported (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds) may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this study should not be used as direct evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

- source: Morsky, B., & Akçay, E. (2021). False beliefs can bootstrap cooperative communities through social norms. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that inaccurate, overly optimistic beliefs about the level of cooperation can bootstrap and sustain high cooperation in communities governed by conditional cooperation norms, even in anonymous settings. The model shows that the dynamics of joining, learning, and leaving the community can create bistability, abrupt transitions, or cycles in cooperation rates and community size. High cooperation can be achieved and maintained if naive (optimistic) individuals are present in sufficient numbers, and if learning the true (lower) level of cooperation is slow or if disillusioned individuals can easily leave. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward, but discusses how mechanisms that increase transparency or allow for punishment may interact with these dynamics. The findings are relevant for understanding how social norms and belief management can foster cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how belief management and social norm dynamics can affect cooperation rates in public-goods-game-like environments, especially in the absence of explicit punishment or reward. It suggests that mechanisms which slow learning about true cooperation levels or allow for easy exit can paradoxically increase cooperation by maintaining a pool of optimistic, conditionally cooperative individuals. However, because the model does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency/payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The discussion of punishment is conceptual and highlights possible trade-offs between transparency (which may reduce cooperation by revealing low compliance) and incentive mechanisms (which may increase or decrease cooperation depending on beliefs). For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of belief dynamics and social norms as moderators, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Chen, S., Shi, J. C., Yang, X. L., Ye, H., & Luo, J. (2019). Modulating Activity in the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex Changes Punishment in the 3-Player Prisoner's Dilemma: A Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Study. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that modulating DLPFC activity via tDCS can significantly decrease the amount of punishment assigned by cooperators to defectors (both in second-party and third-party punishment), depending on the stimulation mode. One bilateral stimulation (left anodal/right cathodal) increases cooperation rates. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding neural mechanisms of punishment and cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or prisoner's dilemma games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, as it does not report these outcomes. It does, however, provide detailed evidence on how punishment behavior and cooperation rates are affected by neural modulation in a 3-player prisoner's dilemma with punishment. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment behavior can be modulated by factors unrelated to game design (e.g., neural stimulation), and that cooperation can be increased by certain interventions. However, without efficiency or payoff data, the paper's direct value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited.

- source: Gallier, C., Langbein, J., & Vance, C. (2018). Non-binding Restrictions, Cooperation, and Coral Reef Protection: Experimental Evidence from Indonesian Fishing Communities. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that non-binding recommendations, especially those from an external source (e.g., NGO or scientist), can reduce extraction effort in a CPR game, but this effect is highly context-dependent and only significant in the most diverse community (Bunaken). Democratic recommendations have a weaker effect, and leader-based recommendations have no effect. There is no punishment or sanctioning in any treatment. The main outcome is extraction effort, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that, in the absence of enforcement, non-binding recommendations alone are often insufficient to promote cooperation, except in certain socio-cultural contexts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or CPR games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in any treatment. The findings are relevant for understanding the limits of non-binding recommendations in the absence of enforcement, but do not inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment. The game design dimensions are well described, but the absence of punishment means this study is not directly informative for the downstream prediction task focused on punishment effects.

- source: Kayser, K., & Lampert, A. (2021). Survivor's dilemma: The evolution of cooperation in volatile environments. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in the survivor's dilemma, cooperation can be evolutionarily stable if the benefit-to-cost ratio is high and/or environmental volatility is low. In these cases, mutual cooperation leads to higher expected lifespan (efficiency) for all. However, if the benefit-to-cost ratio is low or volatility is high, defection dominates and efficiency is lower. The model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms; instead, indirect punishment occurs because defectors lose partners and thus reduce their own survival prospects. The findings are presented for both 2- and 3-player games, with detailed analysis of parameter regions where cooperation or defection is stable. The results are directly about efficiency (expected lifespan) and its dependence on game parameters, but do not address the effect of enabling or disabling explicit punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence about how efficiency (expected lifespan) in repeated social dilemmas depends on group size, benefit-to-cost ratio, and environmental volatility. It does not directly address the effect of enabling or disabling punishment, as no explicit punishment mechanism is present. However, it does show that indirect forms of 'punishment' (loss of cooperative partners) can sustain cooperation and efficiency under certain conditions. For prediction tasks involving explicit punishment in public goods games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that efficiency gains from cooperation depend strongly on the benefit-to-cost ratio and the likelihood of future interaction (volatility), and that even without explicit punishment, cooperation can sometimes be stable. The model is adjacent to PGGs and does not manipulate or analyze punishment as a treatment.

It reviews the development of theories such as kin selection, reciprocal altruism, indirect reciprocity, and strong reciprocity, including the role of punishment in maintaining cooperation. The discussion references public goods games and related experimental paradigms, noting that punishment can help sustain cooperation, but the paper does not provide new data or direct analysis of efficiency or payoff outcomes. Instead, it synthesizes and critiques the conceptual foundations of the field.

Its value for prediction is limited to contextual and theoretical framing: it highlights the importance of considering group selection, the evolution of punishment, and the limitations of models based solely on self-interest. However, it does not inform specific game design dimensions or their quantitative effects on efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper may be useful for understanding the broader theoretical context but does not provide actionable evidence for model calibration.

- source: Shimura, H., & Nakamaru, M. (2018). Large group size promotes the evolution of cooperation in the mutual-aid game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in the mutual-aid game (an 'all for one' group helping structure), larger group size and more repeated interactions (rounds) both promote the evolution and stability of cooperation, as measured by the expected payoff of conditional cooperators relative to defectors. This is in contrast to the standard public goods game, where larger groups typically undermine cooperation. The effect is robust to implementation and perception errors, and is strongest under certain reputation assessment rules. The paper does not include punishment or costly sanctioning mechanisms; all incentives are mediated by reputation and conditional cooperation. The findings are directly about efficiency (payoff) outcomes, not just cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in mutual-aid games with indirect reciprocity and reputation-based conditional cooperation (but no explicit punishment), increasing group size and number of rounds both increase the likelihood that cooperation (and thus high efficiency) will evolve and persist. The results are not directly transferable to standard public goods games with punishment, but they suggest that the structure of the game (e.g., 'all for one' vs 'all for all') and the presence of reputation-based mechanisms can strongly moderate the effect of group size and repetition on efficiency. Since punishment is not present, the paper does not inform the effect of enabling punishment per se, but it does provide a baseline for efficiency in a related class of games without punishment.

- source: Laird, R. A. (2018). Sequential interactions-in which one player plays first and another responds-promote cooperation in evolutionary-dynamical simulations of single-shot Prisoner's Dilemma and Snowdrift games. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Sequential interactions in single-shot Prisoner's Dilemma and Snowdrift games allow for the emergence of conditional strategies, which can increase the average payoff of the population compared to simultaneous play. In the spatial Prisoner's Dilemma, sequential play greatly expands the parameter range (cost-benefit ratio) over which cooperation and higher payoffs are possible. In the Snowdrift game, sequential play leads to high average payoffs across the full parameter range. The effect is due to the structure of the game and the possibility of conditional responses, not to punishment, reward, or repeated interaction.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. However, it is highly informative about the effect of sequential versus simultaneous play in two-player social dilemmas (Prisoner's Dilemma and Snowdrift), showing that sequential play can increase efficiency by enabling conditional strategies. Since the paper does not include punishment or sanctions, it should not be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it may be useful for understanding the role of game structure and timing in cooperation and efficiency.

- source: Tan, S. L. (2019). Proximity inheritance explains the evolution of cooperation under natural selection and mutation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that proximity inheritance in a spatial Prisoner's Dilemma model leads to the emergence and maintenance of high levels of cooperation, even in the presence of mutation. Cooperation is favored when offspring are placed within the neighborhood of their parents, and the effect is robust to variations in temptation of defection, selection intensity, and mutation rate. The model demonstrates that dynamic clustering of cooperators can arise, and that cooperation collapses if proximity inheritance is removed (i.e., if offspring are placed randomly or at the site of the dead individual). However, the paper does not analyze group payoff, efficiency, or welfare, and does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. It does not include punishment or efficiency outcomes, and the game structure is a spatial Prisoner's Dilemma rather than a public goods game. The findings may be contextually informative for understanding how spatial structure and inheritance of proximity can promote cooperation, but they do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.
