- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Pancotto, F., Righi, S., & Takács, K. (2023). Voluntary play increases cooperation in the presence of punishment: a lab in the field experiment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiments show that punishment increases contributions in public goods games, both when participation is compulsory and when it is optional. The effect is strongest when participation is compulsory and punishment is available. Optional participation (opt-out) alone does not significantly increase contributions compared to compulsory participation. Loners (those who opt out) contribute less when forced to participate, but are not responsible for anti-social punishment. Instead, low contributors (free-riders) are the main source of anti-social punishment. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the contribution data suggest that punishment increases group welfare, especially in compulsory settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in discrete public goods games with 4 players, 1:3 punishment technology, and no communication, enabling punishment increases contributions, especially when participation is compulsory. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions strongly suggests that efficiency would also increase with punishment, particularly in compulsory games. The design dimensions are well specified, allowing for mapping to prediction tasks, but the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff data means that predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes. The results are most informative for settings with similar group size, discrete contributions, and punishment technology.

- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Effects of majority-vote reward mechanism on cooperation: a public good experimental study. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The majority-vote reward mechanism significantly increases group efficiency and cooperation compared to both peer reward and no reward, in both homogeneous and heterogeneous MPCR settings. Peer reward increases efficiency and cooperation only in homogeneous groups. In heterogeneous groups, only the majority-vote reward mechanism leads to a significant increase in efficiency. The efficiency gains are due to higher contributions and more willingness to pay reward costs under the majority-vote mechanism. The paper provides direct evidence on efficiency outcomes (Table 6), showing that the majority-vote reward mechanism achieves the highest efficiency among the tested mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players, 6 rounds per condition, continuous contribution, no chat, and full information, adding a majority-vote reward mechanism (with a 1:3 cost-to-reward ratio) increases efficiency relative to the control (no reward) and peer reward conditions. Peer reward also increases efficiency in homogeneous groups, but not in heterogeneous groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially regarding the presence and type of reward mechanism, MPCR homogeneity, and the absence of punishment. The results suggest that, in the absence of punishment, majority-vote reward is a robust way to increase efficiency, especially when group members have heterogeneous returns from the public good.

- source: Buso, I. M., Di Cagno, D., Gueth, W., & Spadoni, L. (2025). Voluntary Partnerships for Equally Sharing Contribution Costs. *B E Journal of Theoretical Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Voluntary cost-sharing partnerships in a PGG-like environment significantly increase average contributions and prevent the typical decay of cooperation over repeated rounds. Partners contribute more than outsiders, and the presence of the partnership mechanism (whether endogenous or exogenous) leads to higher group contributions and payoffs than the standard PGG. The effect is strongest for larger partnerships. The mechanism does not involve punishment or reward, but rather a self-commitment to cost-sharing. Efficiency (in terms of group payoff relative to the social optimum) is not directly reported, but the data show that average contributions are about 50% of the endowment with partnerships, compared to about 32% in the standard PGG. The voluntary nature of partnership formation has a modest additional effect, especially for small partnerships.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional mechanisms allowing voluntary cost-sharing partnerships can substantially increase group contributions and payoffs in PGG-like settings, even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the presence of such a partnership mechanism should be expected to yield higher efficiency than standard PGGs with similar MPCR and group size. The effect is robust to whether partnership formation is endogenous or exogenous, except for very small partnerships. The findings are most directly relevant for settings where cost-sharing or coalition formation is possible, but do not directly inform about punishment effects. The results suggest that, in the absence of punishment, other institutional mechanisms can also enhance efficiency, and that the design dimensions (player count, MPCR, endogenous/exogenous group formation) interact with the mechanism's effectiveness.

- source: Li, Y. Z., Luo, J., Niu, H., & Ye, H. (2023). When punishers might be loved: fourth-party choices and third-party punishment in a delegation game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the paper shows: (1) Helpers are more trusted and preferred as partners in trust games, but punishers are more likely to be offered higher amounts in ultimatum games due to their deterrence signal, resulting in higher actual payoffs for punishers. (2) In a delegation game, when the proposer's knowledge of the delegate's punishment history is made explicit, fourth-party players prefer to select punishers as their delegates, and this choice leads to higher expected payoffs for both the delegate and the principal. (3) The effect is contingent on the visibility of the punishment signal; when the proposer's information is limited, helpers are preferred. Thus, punishment increases efficiency/payoff in environments where its deterrence is visible and relevant.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in games adjacent to public goods games (third-party punishment, ultimatum, and delegation games), enabling punishment increases the efficiency (payoff) of punishers and those who select them as delegates, especially when the punishment signal is visible to others. The effect is context-dependent: punishment is less effective in trust-based settings but more effective in settings where deterrence is valuable and observable. For prediction tasks, the presence of a visible punishment mechanism, the structure of the game (ultimatum/delegation vs. trust), and the information available to other players are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is lower than with punishment in deterrence-relevant settings.

- source: Herne, K., Lappalainen, O., Setälä, M., & Ylisalo, J. (2022). Accountability as a Warrant for Trust: An Experiment on Sanctions and Justifications in a Trust Game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment used a 2x2 design (punishment/no punishment x justification/no justification) in a three-player trust game (two senders, one responder, repeated stranger matching, 6 rounds). The opportunity for senders to punish responders (at a cost: 1 point spent reduces responder's payoff by 3) did not significantly increase the amounts sent or returned, nor did it improve group efficiency. In fact, net earnings for responders were lower in punishment treatments due to the cost of punishment, and group efficiency was not improved. Justification requirements (responders must explain their choices) increased contributions and returns, but this effect was not robust when controlling for the amount received. The combination of punishment and justification did not outperform justification alone in terms of efficiency. The main effect of punishment was to reduce responder payoffs via costly sanctions, not to increase group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a trust-game-like environment with costly punishment (punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment does not increase group efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of punishment. The design is adjacent to public goods games (not a standard PGG, but a trust game with two senders and one responder, continuous contributions, no communication, 6 rounds, known horizon, no rewards). The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be lower than or similar to the control (no punishment), unless the punishment is costless or much more effective. The paper also shows that justification requirements can increase contributions, but this does not translate into higher efficiency when punishment is present. Thus, for prediction, enabling costly punishment in similar trust or PGG-adjacent games is likely to reduce or not improve efficiency relative to control.

- source: Pevnitskaya, S., & Ryvkin, D. (2022). The effect of options to reward and punish on behavior in bargaining. *Journal of Economics & Management Strategy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that adding costly punishment and/or reward options for responders in ultimatum bargaining changes both proposer and responder behavior. When only reward is available, proposers make lower offers and efficiency drops. When only punishment is available, acceptance of low offers increases, and efficiency for low offers is higher than baseline. When both reward and punishment are available, both offers and efficiency are highest. The use of reward and punishment is rare and mostly symbolic, so efficiency losses from their direct use are minimal. The main efficiency effects come from changes in offer and acceptance behavior, not from the direct cost of sanctions. The results suggest that punishment options can increase efficiency in settings with low offers, while reward options alone may reduce efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence on how the availability and cost/effectiveness of punishment and reward options affect efficiency in a two-player bargaining environment. While not a public goods game, the findings are relevant for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in asymmetric, bilateral settings with costly sanctions. The results show that costly punishment (with 1:1 cost-impact ratio) can increase efficiency by increasing acceptance of low offers, especially when offers are low, but that reward alone can reduce efficiency. The combination of both instruments yields the highest efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the presence of reward, the cost/effectiveness of punishment, and the baseline offer/acceptance norms. The findings are most transferable to settings with costly, direct, one-to-one punishment and reward, and less so to multiplayer or public goods environments.

- source: Lec, F. L., Matthey, A., & Rydval, O. (2023). Punishing the weakest link - Voluntary sanctions and efficient coordination in the minimum effort game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that voluntary costly punishment opportunities in a repeated minimum effort game (with 8 players per group, 16 rounds, and a two-stage design) significantly increase both effort levels and group efficiency compared to a baseline with no punishment and a treatment with costless disapproval (communication). In the punishment treatment, after an initial drop, average group payoff (net of punishment costs) rises and eventually exceeds the control, with about a third of groups reaching or approaching the Pareto-optimal equilibrium. Punishment is mainly directed at minimum-effort players, and its use is frequent. Disapproval/communication alone produces only a temporary or weak improvement. The results show that decentralized sanctions can serve as an effective coordination device in Pareto-ranked coordination games, with a net positive effect on efficiency in the longer run.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated weakest-link (minimum effort) games with large groups and a history of inefficient coordination, enabling voluntary costly punishment leads to a robust and lasting increase in group efficiency, even after accounting for the direct costs of punishment. The effect is stronger and more persistent than that of costless communication. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, holding control efficiency and other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment (with a 2:1 fine-to-cost ratio and anonymous assignment) will increase average efficiency, especially in settings with multiple rounds, large groups, and no prior communication. The initial rounds after introducing punishment may see a drop in net efficiency, but this reverses as groups coordinate on higher efforts. The results are most directly applicable to coordination games with similar structure, but provide adjacent evidence for public goods games.

- source: Gueth, W., & Otsubo, H. (2023). When Efficiency Requires Arbitrary Discrimination: Theoretical and Experimental Analysis of Equilibrium Selection. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that third-party discriminatory sanctioning (especially when preannounced and when agents differ in wealth) increases the likelihood that the two agents coordinate to avoid mutual harm, resulting in higher group efficiency. When the third party sanctions equally, agents are less likely to coordinate efficiently. The effect is robust to the order protocol (sequential vs. simultaneous), but is strongest when the sanctioning schedule is observable. Asymmetric wealth increases the tendency for the third party to sanction the richer agent more, confirming the 'deep-pocket' hypothesis. Overall, the ability to use discriminatory punishment, and to preannounce it, enables higher efficiency in this coordination dilemma.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a coordination game adjacent to the public goods game, enabling a third party to use discriminatory punishment (especially when the punishment schedule is observable) increases group efficiency by facilitating coordination and avoiding mutual harm. The findings suggest that, in similar environments, the design dimensions of punishment observability and the ability to discriminate in punishment can be leveraged to improve efficiency. The results are most relevant for settings where a third party can precommit to a punishment schedule and where agents may differ in wealth or status. For prediction tasks, the presence of observable, flexible, and potentially discriminatory punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to control conditions without such punishment.

- source: Calabuig, V., Jiménez-Jiménez, N., Olcina, G., & Rodriguez-Lara, I. (2024). Coordinated and uncoordinated punishment in a team investment game. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares coordinated and uncoordinated punishment in a team investment game with two investors and one allocator. Coordinated punishment (where both investors must punish to inflict a penalty) increases joint investment and allocator return relative to uncoordinated punishment. However, this does not translate into higher efficiency, as the increased frequency of joint punishment in the coordinated condition destroys the potential efficiency gains. The highest efficiency (sum of total payoffs) is observed in the no-punishment condition. There are no significant efficiency differences between coordinated and uncoordinated punishment treatments after accounting for punishment costs. Thus, punishment—regardless of its form—does not improve efficiency in this setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a team investment game with binary investment and an allocator, enabling punishment (either coordinated or uncoordinated) reduces efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are: binary (all-or-nothing) investment, two investors and one allocator (asymmetric roles), fixed punishment cost, and varying punishment effectiveness. The findings suggest that, even if punishment increases cooperation or returns, the cost and frequency of punishment can offset these gains, leading to lower or unchanged efficiency. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, and the specific form of punishment (coordinated vs uncoordinated) does not significantly alter this outcome in this design. The results are most directly applicable to asymmetric, binary-contribution, repeated games with fixed matching and no communication.

  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement via grim punishment can sustain efficient cooperation in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with random matching, provided the cost of cooperation is not too low or too high. Specifically, if the cost-to-benefit ratio exceeds a threshold (less than 0.5), punishment is always credible and cooperation is an equilibrium for any population size and discount factor. For any environment, there exists a 'target cost of cooperation' that enables coordination on cooperation and makes punishment effective. When the cost of cooperation is endogenous, players can use it as a coordination device to achieve efficient outcomes. The results are entirely theoretical and provide explicit payoff-based conditions for when punishment increases group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with random matching and private monitoring, the introduction of credible punishment (grim trigger) can increase group efficiency to the cooperative level, provided the cost of cooperation is within a specific range. The key moderators are the cost-to-benefit ratio, population size, and discount factor. The results suggest that, for a wide range of parameter values, enabling punishment will move efficiency from the defection baseline to the cooperative optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the cost of cooperation (analogous to MPCR in PGGs), population size, and the presence of punishment. However, the model is not a literal public goods game but an adjacent repeated PD, so some caution is warranted in direct quantitative transfer.

- source: Uchida, S., Sasaki, T., Yamamoto, H., & Okada, I. (2024). Evolution of Pay-It-Forward in the Presence of the Temptation to Free-Ride. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in a donation game with institutional punishment, the evolution of cooperation and pay-it-forward strategies is strongly affected by the cognitive model of the players. Under EUT, only strong punishment (high fine or high probability) can sustain cooperation, and pay-it-forward strategies are not stable. Under PT, even weak or infrequent punishment is sufficient to deter free-riding, because players overweight small probabilities of being punished. This leads to the emergence and stability of pay-it-forward strategies and higher average payoffs (efficiency) in the population. The model shows bi-stability, with both cooperative and non-cooperative equilibria possible depending on parameters and initial conditions, but overall, punishment increases efficiency more under PT than under EUT.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games (here, a donation game) will increase group efficiency, especially when players are subject to prospect-theoretic biases. The effect is more pronounced when punishment is mild or infrequent, as real human players may overweight the risk of being punished. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the payoff-based outcomes are directly analyzed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (even with low probability or low severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, particularly if the control game has low efficiency due to free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of psychological factors (probability weighting) in moderating the effect of punishment.

- source: Kamei, K., & Tabero, K. (2025). Free riding, democracy, and sacrifice in the workplace: Evidence from a real-effort experiment. *Journal of Economics & Management Strategy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a real-effort lab experiment with teams of three, groups could either democratically vote or have randomly imposed a policy to reduce the private return from shirking (playing Tetris). The reduction was small and nondeterrent. Teams in the democratic (ENDO) condition exhibited significantly higher work productivity (correct answers per minute of teamwork) than those in the random (EXO) condition, regardless of whether the reduction policy was enacted. This effect (the 'dividend of democracy') was about 20% and persisted over time. However, democracy also led to more shirking (less work time), so total group output and payoffs did not significantly increase. The reduction policy itself had little effect, as shirking remained privately optimal for almost all teams. Communication analysis showed that support for the reduction policy was driven by intentions to work and beliefs about deterring others' shirking, but the policy's weakness limited its impact.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a democratic process (even with a weak, nondeterrent sanction) can substantially increase work productivity (efficiency per unit work time) in team-based, real-effort social dilemmas. However, unless the sanction is strong enough to change private incentives, total group output and payoffs may not increase, as increased productivity is offset by increased shirking. For prediction, the presence of a democratic process is a strong positive moderator of efficiency per work time, but the effectiveness of punishment/sanction depends on its deterrence strength. The experiment's design is adjacent to PGGs: it features a real-effort team task with revenue sharing and a private alternative activity, but not a standard PGG or explicit punishment tokens.

- source: Bellucci, G. (2022). A Model of Trust. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper formalizes trust as a trade-off between vulnerability (potential loss) and positive expectations of a partner's behavior, using a computational model applied to the investment game. Simulations show that trustful investors (high trust attitude) invest more and are more likely to recover trust after breaches, but are also more vulnerable to exploitation. It is a theoretical modeling paper focused on the investment game (trust game), which is adjacent but not equivalent to the public goods game. The model and its simulations may inform understanding of how psychological factors (trust, expectations, loss aversion) affect investment and payoffs in dyadic trust settings, but do not address punishment, sanctions, or group-level efficiency in multi-player public goods contexts. Therefore, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games.
