- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Clark, J., & Dickinson, D. L. (2020). The effect of sleep on public good contributions and punishment: Experimental evidence. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a standard VCM with 3-person groups and 10 rounds per treatment, the introduction of peer punishment increases contributions for sleep-restricted subjects but not for well-rested subjects. Without punishment, contributions do not differ by sleep condition. There is no significant difference in punishment behavior between sleep-restricted and well-rested subjects. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are robust to various compliance and attrition corrections, but some results are sensitive to the sleep deprivation measure used.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of punishment on contribution behavior in a standard VCM, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper confirms that, in a 3-player, 10-round VCM with standard punishment (linear cost, known identities), the introduction of punishment increases contributions, especially for sleep-restricted subjects. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed, the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, and the behavioral results are likely to correlate with efficiency, but the lack of direct payoff analysis is a limitation.

- source: Okada, I., Yamamoto, H., Akiyama, E., & Toriumi, F. (2021). Cooperation in spatial public good games depends on the locality effects of game, adaptation, and punishment. *Scientific Reports*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that localizing either the game interaction or the punishment scope in a spatial PGG model leads to robust cooperation, while globalizing both undermines cooperation. The adaptation (learning) process also interacts with these effects: global adaptation can help spread cooperation if the regime is already cooperative, but can also help defectors invade if cooperation is fragile. The model uses expected payoffs to update strategies, but does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome. The findings are most directly about cooperation rates and the stability of cooperative regimes under different locality conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that local punishment and local game interactions are important for sustaining cooperation in spatial PGGs, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results are useful for understanding which game design features (local vs global interaction, punishment scope) are likely to support high cooperation, which may correlate with higher efficiency, but the lack of explicit payoff or efficiency outcomes limits its direct predictive value for the efficiency task.

- source: Battu, B. (2021). Evolution of altruistic punishments among heterogeneous conditional cooperators. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with a population of heterogeneous conditional cooperators, the evolution of altruistic punishment (where more willing cooperators are also more willing to punish) can stabilize high levels of cooperation. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation depends on the inflicted cost of punishment and the frequency with which punishment opportunities arise. When punishment is frequent and the inflicted cost is high, the population evolves toward more cooperative and punitive types, and donation rates are high. When punishment is infrequent or the inflicted cost is low, cooperation collapses. The model also shows that it is not necessary to punish all free riders equally; targeting persistent (selfish) free riders is sufficient. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but higher donation rates are interpreted as indicative of higher group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in repeated public goods games with conditional cooperators can substantially increase cooperation rates, especially when punishment is sufficiently costly to the target and opportunities to punish are frequent. However, because the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, its quantitative value for predicting efficiency is limited. The main actionable insight is that punishment is most effective when it is frequent and sufficiently severe, and that selective (generous) punishment targeting persistent free riders is sufficient to stabilize cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with high inflicted cost and frequent opportunities) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, but the magnitude cannot be directly inferred from this paper.

It is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. However, it does provide indirect evidence that mechanisms other than punishment (such as the presence of prosocial 'dedicators' and emotional contagion) can promote cooperation and higher contributions. For the prediction task, this paper is not directly relevant, as it does not manipulate or analyze punishment, nor does it report efficiency outcomes in the required sense.

- source: Heine, F., & Strobel, M. (2020). Reward and punishment in a team contest. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares reward and punishment mechanisms in a team contest game (with and without between-group competition). Groups of four play 15 rounds, deciding how much to contribute to a group lottery (contest) and then can reward or punish teammates at a cost. The main findings are: (1) Both punishment and reward mechanisms lead to over-contribution relative to Nash equilibrium, causing significant efficiency losses (rent dissipation). (2) Punishment does not increase efficiency; it reduces it, as punishing is costly and does not significantly increase contributions. (3) Rewarding increases contributions more than punishment, but is welfare-neutral (cost to giver equals benefit to receiver). (4) The contest environment (with an opponent group) leads to even greater inefficiency and lower average payoffs than the non-contest environment. (5) The R&P treatment (both reward and punishment available) in the contest environment is the least efficient, with the highest overspending and lowest mean income. (6) Receiving reward increases subsequent contributions, but punishment does not. (7) Overall, the presence of punishment does not solve the efficiency problem in this environment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a team contest environment with public-good-like features, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency and may worsen it, especially when both reward and punishment are available. The effect of punishment is less positive (more negative) on efficiency compared to reward or baseline. The contest structure (between-group competition) amplifies inefficiency. For prediction, if the control game (no punishment) is already inefficient due to over-contribution, enabling punishment is likely to further reduce efficiency, not improve it. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, known MPCR, punishment cost and tech, no identification of punishers, no communication) are all specified and match standard lab contest/PGG designs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled in similar environments.

- source: Liao, Y. L., Zhang, L., Lei, S. Y., Song, M. Z., Deng, W. K., & Hu, D. F. (2021). RETRACTED: Third-Party Punishment Mechanism and Corporate Cooperation in Environmental Investment: Experiments on Public Goods Game (Retracted Article). *Discrete Dynamics in Nature and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares a threshold public goods game with and without third-party punishment (TPP), using groups of three participants (framed as enterprises) making resource investment decisions toward a group threshold. The TPP mechanism automatically punishes group members whose investment rate is below the group mean. The presence of TPP leads to higher mean resource investment amounts, higher individual and group investment rates, and a substantially higher success rate of cooperation (from 55.57% to 76.85%). Regression analysis confirms that TPP has a significant positive effect on investment behavior, even after controlling for individual characteristics and measured preferences for interaction and equity (IEP).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a third-party punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods game increases group investment and the probability of achieving the public good. For prediction tasks, the presence of TPP (punishment enabled) should be expected to yield higher efficiency or group payoff than the control (punishment disabled), especially in small groups (n=3) with a threshold structure and automatic, impersonal punishment. The results are most directly applicable to threshold PGGs with automatic third-party punishment and no communication. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency-related outcomes under these design conditions.

- source: Berger, U., & De Silva, H. (2021). Evolution of deterrence with costly reputation information. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study shows that in a repeated two-player deterrence game (with roles of challenger and defender), the possibility of costly punishment (retaliation) can lead to higher defender payoffs and social welfare, but only when reputation information is minimal (e.g., only the last action is observable and costly to obtain). When reputation information is rich (e.g., challengers can observe a defender's empirical fighting frequency over many rounds), deterrence breaks down, punishment loses its deterrent effect, and defender payoffs collapse. Agent-based simulations confirm that defender payoffs decline as the amount of reputation information increases, with a sharp drop when information becomes too rich. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is context-dependent: positive under minimal information, negative or null under rich information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments depends critically on the information structure: punishment increases efficiency (defender payoffs) when reputation information is minimal and costly, but not when information is rich and easily available. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the design dimension 'information about others' past actions' (analogous to 'show_other_summaries' or 'show_punishment_id') is a key moderator: enabling rich, low-cost information can undermine the efficiency benefits of punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to two-player deterrence games, but the mechanisms may generalize to PGG variants where punishment and reputation interact.

- source: Riedl, A., Rohde, I. M. T., & Strobel, M. (2021). Free neighborhood choice boosts socially optimal outcomes in stag-hunt coordination problem. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that free neighborhood choice in a repeated stag-hunt game (coordination problem) substantially increases the frequency of efficient coordination and group welfare compared to imposed (fixed) neighborhoods. In the fixed-neighborhood condition, groups mostly converge to the inefficient equilibrium, while in the free-choice condition, efficient outcomes become dominant over time. The ability to exclude inefficient players (a form of endogenous, costly social sanction) is the key mechanism: excluded players are much more likely to switch to efficient behavior, and overall exclusion is infrequent but sufficient to sustain high efficiency. Welfare gains in the free-choice condition are significantly higher, especially in later rounds, despite exclusion being costly. The results suggest that even limited, endogenous social sanctions (via exclusion) can robustly promote efficient group outcomes in coordination settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling endogenous, costly exclusion (a form of social sanction) in a repeated coordination game can dramatically increase group efficiency, even when exclusion is rare and costly. While the setting is a stag-hunt (coordination) game rather than a public goods game, the mechanism—costly exclusion of inefficient actors—parallels punishment in PGGs. The findings suggest that, in environments where players can choose their partners and exclusion is possible but costly, efficiency can be much higher than in fixed-group settings. For prediction tasks, this implies that the presence of endogenous exclusion or partner choice should be expected to have a large positive effect on efficiency, especially over repeated rounds, even if the cost of exclusion is nontrivial. However, the results may not transfer directly to standard PGGs with explicit punishment stages, as the underlying game is a coordination problem, not a social dilemma.
