- source: Rand, D. G., Dreber, A., Ellingsen, T., Fudenberg, D., & Nowak, M. A. (2009). Positive Interactions Promote Public Cooperation. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, both punishment and reward mechanisms can maintain high levels of cooperation and efficiency compared to a control with no targeted interactions. However, reward leads to higher total payoffs than punishment, as punishment is costly and does not increase efficiency beyond what is achieved by reward. When both reward and punishment are available, groups that use reward more achieve higher payoffs, while groups that use punishment more achieve lower payoffs. The results suggest that positive reciprocity (reward) is more effective than negative reciprocity (punishment) for promoting efficiency in repeated public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with persistent group identity and known group membership, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to a control, but enabling reward is equally or more effective and leads to higher group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment alone should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no-punishment controls, but not as much as reward or combined reward/punishment treatments. The effect of punishment is less positive for efficiency than reward, and when both are available, punishment may even reduce efficiency due to its cost. The findings are most applicable to repeated, fixed-group, non-anonymous settings with continuous contribution choices and no communication.

- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2006). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with endogenous institution choice, participants could choose between a sanctioning institution (with both positive and negative sanctions) and a sanction-free institution. Initially, most participants preferred the sanction-free institution, but over time, nearly all migrated to the sanctioning institution. In the sanctioning institution, cooperation and efficiency increased steadily, reaching over 95% of the maximum possible group payoff in the final period, while the sanction-free institution's efficiency fell to zero. The presence of punishment (negative sanctions) was the key driver of sustained high cooperation and efficiency. The study demonstrates that sanctioning institutions have a strong competitive advantage and can resolve the social dilemma by attracting and converting even initially noncooperative individuals.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game dramatically increases efficiency compared to a control condition without punishment, even when participants can freely choose between institutions. The design closely matches canonical PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is explicitly measured. The findings support strong positive predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in repeated games with migration or institution choice. The specific punishment cost and effectiveness parameters, as well as the presence of both positive and negative sanctions, are informative for parameterizing prediction models.

- source: Rockenbach, B., & Milinski, M. (2006). The efficient interaction of indirect reciprocity and costly punishment. *Nature*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that when participants can choose between groups with or without costly punishment, and with or without an indirect reciprocity (reputation) mechanism, most eventually prefer the group with both punishment and reputation. The combination (PUN&IR) leads to higher contributions, more efficient use of punishment (less frequent, more targeted), and significantly higher efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) than punishment alone (PUN). The destructive effect of punishment on efficiency is largely mitigated by the presence of reputation, as punishment is used less but more effectively. Full contributors in the combined treatment achieve payoffs close to the social optimum, and even the least cooperative subjects are more cooperative in the combined treatment. The results suggest that punishment and reputation are complementary, not substitutes, and their interaction provides a low-cost, high-efficiency solution to social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, adding a reputation/indirect reciprocity mechanism increases efficiency beyond what is achieved by punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the presence of both punishment and reputation should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, especially when punishment is costly and group members can observe each other's contributions and reputational history. The efficiency boost is due to reduced punishment frequency, more targeted punishment, and increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when both punishment and reputation mechanisms are present.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., & Villatoro, D. (2013). Punish and Voice: Punishment Enhances Cooperation when Combined with Norm-Signalling. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with 4-person groups over 30 rounds, the study compares three treatments: (1) punishment only, (2) norm-signaling (message) only, and (3) both punishment and norm-signaling (sanction). In the critical rounds where treatments differ (rounds 11-20), the sanction treatment yields significantly higher average net earnings (31% higher than punishment, 16% higher than message) and higher contributions, with less frequent and less intense punishment. The efficiency gains in the sanction treatment are attributed to the combination of explicit norm communication and the credible threat of punishment, which together increase norm salience and reduce the need for costly punishment. The findings are robust in both human experiments and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with costly punishment, the addition of norm-signaling (explicit communication of normative expectations) to punishment substantially increases group efficiency compared to punishment alone. The efficiency gains are due to both higher cooperation and lower punishment costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of norm-signaling or communication channels that make norms salient can be a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of punishment, the ability to send norm-signaling messages, and the structure of feedback about others' actions. The control efficiency (without punishment or norm-signaling) is lower than in the combined treatment. The results imply that, all else equal, enabling both punishment and norm-signaling will yield higher efficiency than enabling punishment alone.

- source: Wu, J. H., Balliet, D., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2016). Gossip Versus Punishment: The Efficiency of Reputation to Promote and Maintain Cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares the effects of punishment and gossip on cooperation and efficiency in a four-round public goods game. Punishment (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3) did not significantly increase overall cooperation and significantly reduced individual earnings (efficiency) compared to control. Gossip (costless) increased both cooperation and efficiency. When both mechanisms were present, gossip offset the negative efficiency impact of punishment. The study also finds that gossip, but not punishment, has positive spillover effects on trust and trustworthiness in a subsequent trust game. The results suggest that punishment, at moderate cost, is less efficient than gossip for promoting cooperation, and that gossip can mitigate the efficiency losses from punishment.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, four-round PGG with a punishment mechanism (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3, anonymous, no chat, continuous contributions), enabling punishment reduces efficiency (total/individual earnings) compared to control. The negative effect is robust, and the presence of a costless gossip mechanism can counteract this loss. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG designs, especially when punishment is moderately costly and anonymous. The results also suggest that the efficiency cost of punishment is not offset by increased cooperation, except possibly in the final rounds, and that gossip is a more efficient mechanism for promoting cooperation.

- source: Gross, J., Méder, Z. Z., Okamoto-Barth, S., & Riedl, A. (2016). Building the Leviathan - Voluntary centralisation of punishment power sustains cooperation in humans. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study introduces a 'power transfer game'—a repeated public goods game with punishment, where group members can voluntarily transfer their punishment power to others. In groups of five, three conditions were compared: (1) endogenous (voluntary) power transfer, (2) exogenous (mirrored, but not voluntary) power transfer, and (3) fixed (no transfer, standard peer punishment). Only the endogenous condition, where group members could choose whom to empower, led to sustained or increasing cooperation and higher group earnings, approaching the social optimum. In the fixed and exogenous conditions, cooperation and group earnings declined over time. The mechanism works because non-punishing cooperators delegate power to those willing to punish, and centralisation of power in the hands of pro-social punishers deters free riding. The study provides direct evidence that voluntary centralisation of punishment power can efficiently sustain cooperation and group welfare in public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with peer punishment, allowing voluntary centralisation of punishment power (i.e., enabling group members to transfer their punishment effectiveness to others) substantially increases group efficiency compared to standard decentralised peer punishment. The effect is not observed when centralisation is imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a voluntary power transfer mechanism is a key moderator: with it, efficiency is much higher than in standard peer punishment, even when the underlying MPCR, punishment cost, and other parameters are held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in games with and without voluntary centralisation of punishment power.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Duong, M. H., & Han, T. A. (2021). Cost efficiency of institutional incentives for promoting cooperation in finite populations. *Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in finite-population PGGs, the expected total cost to an institution of achieving a minimum cooperation level via punishment or reward can be exactly calculated and optimized. The cost efficiency of punishment versus reward depends on the required cooperation threshold: for low cooperation targets, reward is more cost-efficient; for high targets, punishment is more cost-efficient, with the threshold typically around 50% cooperation. The cost functions exhibit phase transitions as the intensity of selection changes. The analysis provides explicit formulas and algorithms for determining the optimal incentive size and minimal institutional cost, given the game parameters, population size, and selection intensity. The results are validated by numerical simulations and apply to both the PGG and the donation game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, theoretically grounded formulas for predicting the efficiency (cost-effectiveness) of institutional punishment (and reward) in finite-population PGGs, as a function of player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, selection intensity, and the required cooperation level. It shows that the efficiency of punishment depends critically on the required cooperation threshold and the selection intensity. For prediction tasks, the paper enables calculation of the minimal institutional cost (and thus efficiency) for any specified game design, provided the relevant parameters are known. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the institution can set the punishment/reward level to achieve a target cooperation rate, and where the main outcome of interest is the total cost to the institution (i.e., efficiency in terms of budget spent per unit of cooperation achieved). The findings are less directly applicable to settings where individual payoffs or group welfare (rather than institutional cost) are the main outcome, but the cost formulas can be mapped to group efficiency if the institutional cost is subtracted from total group payoffs.

- source: Chen, X. J., Sasaki, T., Brännström, & Dieckmann, U. (2015). First carrot, then stick: how the adaptive hybridization of incentives promotes cooperation. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. This policy outperforms pure reward or pure punishment in both effectiveness (range of conditions for full cooperation) and efficiency (cumulative cost to reach/restore cooperation). The findings hold for both well-mixed and spatial populations, and are robust to changes in group size, incentive leverage, spatial structure, and other model details. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.

- source: Sasaki, T., Brännström, Dieckmann, U., & Sigmund, K. (2012). The take-it-or-leave-it option allows small penalties to overcome social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, allowing optional participation (the 'take-it-or-leave-it' option) enables even small penalties to establish a globally stable cooperative norm, maximizing group welfare. In contrast, compulsory participation requires much larger penalties to achieve the same effect, and can leave the group trapped in full defection if initial cooperation is low. Rewarding also promotes cooperation, but requires much higher costs to achieve full cooperation and does not eliminate the possibility of stable coexistence between cooperators and defectors. The efficiency gains from punishment with optional participation are robust across several model variants, including user-pays and capped punishment. The key outcome is that punishment, when combined with voluntary participation, is a highly efficient mechanism for overcoming social dilemmas in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling optional participation (voluntary entry) dramatically increases the efficiency of institutional punishment: much smaller penalties suffice to achieve full cooperation and maximize group welfare. The model is directly relevant to settings where the game design allows players to opt out, and where punishment is administered by an institution rather than peers. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing institutional punishment with optional participation will yield a much larger increase in efficiency than with compulsory participation, and that the cost required to achieve this is much lower than for reward-based incentives. The results are robust to variations in group size, cost structure, and punishment allocation, making them broadly applicable to prediction tasks involving these design dimensions.

When given a choice, groups using majority voting overwhelmingly select the more efficient second-order punishment institution, while groups using individual migration (foot voting) initially avoid it, resulting in lower payoffs. The effect is robust across both theoretical predictions and experimental results. The findings also show that the method of institutional choice (majority voting vs. individual migration) moderates the realized efficiency, with majority voting more reliably leading to the efficient outcome. For prediction tasks, the presence of second-order punishment, the method of institutional choice, and the group’s prior experience with institutional regimes are important moderators. The paper’s detailed reporting of payoffs under both control and treatment conditions makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Sasaki, T., Uchida, S., & Chen, X. J. (2015). Voluntary rewards mediate the evolution of pool punishment for maintaining public goods in large populations. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary public goods games with large populations, pool punishment cannot emerge from rare initial conditions due to the second-order free rider problem. However, if a voluntary reward fund is available and especially if it rewards those who contribute to pool punishment, then pool punishment can emerge and become stable, leading to high group efficiency (i.e., the population converges to all-punisher state). The reward mechanism acts as a mediator, facilitating the transition from free riding to stable punishment-supported cooperation. Once established, pool punishment is self-sustaining and the reward fund is no longer needed. The findings are robust to variations in group size, benefit multipliers, and cost parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of a reward mechanism, especially one that rewards contributors to punishment, is a key moderator for the emergence and stability of efficient outcomes in public goods games with punishment. In the absence of such a reward, punishment alone cannot overcome the second-order free rider problem in large populations, and efficiency remains low. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the presence or absence of a reward mechanism (and its design) is critical. Other dimensions such as group size, MPCR, and cost parameters are explored and found not to qualitatively change the main result: without reward, punishment does not increase efficiency from a free-rider baseline; with reward, punishment can emerge and efficiency increases dramatically.

- source: Perc, M. (2012). Sustainable institutionalized punishment requires elimination of second-order free-riders. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. If second-order free-riders are not punished sufficiently, the punishment institution cannot be sustained, and efficiency remains low. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

  findings: The main finding is that in optional public goods games, allowing all forms of punishment (including antisocial punishment) leads to the breakdown of the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group efficiency. Punishment does not increase average payoffs or cooperation rates; instead, it is used by all strategy types to protect against invasion, resulting in a 'tragedy of the commons' where costly punishment is widespread and destructive. Only when punishment is weak (low impact per cost) and the public good is highly efficient (high multiplier) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained. Theoretical results are supported by large-scale simulations and are robust to parameter variation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the set of available punishment strategies. If only pro-social punishment (cooperators punishing defectors) is allowed, punishment can greatly increase efficiency. However, if antisocial punishment is possible, punishment does not improve efficiency and can even reduce it. The effect is robust across player count, group size, and a wide range of parameters. The efficiency impact of punishment is more positive when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is high and punishment is weak (low punishment impact per cost). For prediction, the presence of antisocial punishment options and the effectiveness of punishment are key moderators.
