- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Hou, G. L., Wang, F., Shi, J. Y., Chen, W. J., & Yu, J. (2019). Which is the ideal sanction for cooperation? An experimental study on different types of third-party sanctions. *Psych Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a standard PGG increases group earnings and cooperation compared to a baseline with no sanctions. The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E. (2019). The effect of decision timing on the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish noncooperation: Sanctioning the past, the present, or the future. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The main finding is that people are less willing to assign costly rewards or punishments when they must decide before observing others' (non)cooperation, compared to after. This effect is robust across both dichotomous and continuous public goods game variants, and for both reward and punishment, though the effect is stronger for punishment. The effect is not explained by differences in emotional reactions. The results are interpreted as evidence for nonconsequential reasoning: uncertainty about future behavior reduces decisiveness and willingness to sanction. The study does not measure or report group efficiency or total payoffs, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to use costly punishment (and reward) in public goods games is lower when sanctions must be decided before observing others' behavior, compared to after. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding when punishment is likely to be used, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Fraser, S., & Nettle, D. (2020). Hunger Affects Social Decisions in a Multi-Round Public Goods Game but Not a Single-Shot Ultimatum Game. *Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 20-round PGG (10 rounds without punishment, 10 with punishment), groups of four played with either all members having eaten breakfast or all having skipped breakfast. In the no-punishment phase, hungry participants contributed more initially and were more likely to match their contributions to those of their groupmates in subsequent rounds. In the punishment phase, hungry participants punished less, especially when group contributions were high, making punishment less effective at boosting contributions. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the reported results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides informative behavioral evidence about how punishment and hunger interact to affect contribution and punishment behavior in a standard PGG. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment is less effective at increasing contributions when participants are hungry, which may imply a smaller efficiency gain from punishment in such contexts, but this is not quantified in payoff terms. The game design dimensions are well specified and match standard PGG with punishment, so the behavioral patterns may inform models that link contribution and punishment behavior to efficiency, but direct efficiency data are lacking.

- source: Akdeniz, A., & van Veelen, M. (2021). The evolution of morality and the role of commitment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that commitment is a central mechanism for the evolution of cooperation, punishment, and other moral behaviors in humans. It reviews evidence and theory suggesting that commitment to punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and prisoners' dilemmas, even when punishment is costly. The authors contend that standard models based on population structure or repetition do not fully explain observed human behavior, and that commitment (and the ability to recognize commitment in others) is necessary for the evolution of costly punishment and conditional cooperation. The paper discusses the implications for efficiency and group welfare in a theoretical sense but does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical results on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for why punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and similar environments, emphasizing the role of commitment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the idea that the presence of punishment (and the ability to commit to punishment) can change the strategic environment and potentially increase cooperation, but it does not allow for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of commitment and recognizability of commitment for punishment to be effective.

However, it also highlights that the effectiveness of punishment is undermined by moral disagreement and counter-punishment, which can lead to conflict and breakdown of cooperation. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and by implication, efficiency) in standard public goods games under conditions of agreement and low punishment cost, but that this effect is fragile to disagreement and retaliation dynamics. The paper does not provide quantitative or direct evidence on efficiency outcomes, so its value is mainly in highlighting moderators and mechanisms.

- source: Gavrilets, S. (2021). Coevolution of actions, personal norms and beliefs about others in social dilemmas. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model predicts that, in repeated public goods and related games, the long-term average group action and payoff converge to the Nash equilibrium based on material payoffs when there is no external authority. Social and cognitive factors (such as conformity, cognitive dissonance, and beliefs about others) affect the distribution and variance of actions and beliefs, but do not shift the group average away from the material-payoff optimum in the absence of authority. When an external authority promotes a particular action, group behavior can shift toward the promoted standard, sometimes increasing or decreasing efficiency depending on the alignment of the promoted action with the material optimum. The model does not include explicit peer punishment or sanctioning as a treatment variable, so it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a theoretical baseline for predicting efficiency in public goods and related games without explicit punishment mechanisms. It shows that, absent external authority or explicit sanctions, efficiency is determined by material payoffs, and social/cognitive factors mainly affect variance and convergence speed. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that, unless punishment is modeled as a material cost for non-conformity (as in the 'disapproval by peers' term), enabling punishment would require additional modeling or empirical evidence. The model is most useful for predicting control (no-punishment) efficiency and for understanding the role of soft normative pressures, but does not provide direct evidence for the effect of enabling explicit punishment on efficiency.

- source: Morsky, B., & Akçay, E. (2021). False beliefs can bootstrap cooperative communities through social norms. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that inaccurate, overly optimistic beliefs about the level of cooperation can bootstrap and sustain high cooperation in communities governed by conditional cooperation norms, even in anonymous settings. The model shows that the dynamics of joining, learning, and leaving the community can create bistability, abrupt transitions, or cycles in cooperation rates and community size. High cooperation can be achieved and maintained if naive (optimistic) individuals are present in sufficient numbers, and if learning the true (lower) level of cooperation is slow or if disillusioned individuals can easily leave. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward, but discusses how mechanisms that increase transparency or allow for punishment may interact with these dynamics. The findings are relevant for understanding how social norms and belief management can foster cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how belief management and social norm dynamics can affect cooperation rates in public-goods-game-like environments, especially in the absence of explicit punishment or reward. It suggests that mechanisms which slow learning about true cooperation levels or allow for easy exit can paradoxically increase cooperation by maintaining a pool of optimistic, conditionally cooperative individuals. However, because the model does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency/payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The discussion of punishment is conceptual and highlights possible trade-offs between transparency (which may reduce cooperation by revealing low compliance) and incentive mechanisms (which may increase or decrease cooperation depending on beliefs). For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of belief dynamics and social norms as moderators, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Nikias, A., & Sy, A. (2021). Mutual monitoring in multi-period teams: an experimental investigation. *Team Performance Management*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that lowering the cost of punishment in a two-period team production game (with payoffs analogous to a prisoner's dilemma) increases both the frequency of punishment and the rate of working (cooperation) in both periods. When punishment is costless, participants are more likely to punish non-cooperators, and this threat leads to higher cooperation. The design ensures that higher working rates directly translate to higher group output and expected utility, so efficiency is higher when punishment is less costly. The effect is statistically significant for both periods. The study isolates the effect of punishment cost by holding other factors constant and using a one-shot horizon to rule out future gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player, two-period team production game with mutual monitoring and contingent punishment, reducing the cost of punishment increases cooperation and thus group efficiency. The design is closely related to public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, and the mapping from working to group payoff is explicit. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that lower punishment cost (punishment_cost dimension) will lead to higher efficiency when punishment is enabled, holding other design features constant. The findings are most directly applicable to small, short-horizon, mutual monitoring settings without communication or rewards.

- source: Cardella, E., Kugler, T., Anderson, J., & Connolly, T. (2019). Is greed contagious? Four experimental studies. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The main finding is that in a two-person CPR dilemma, participants increase their own withdrawals in response to high withdrawals by their partner, both when interacting with the same partner and with a new partner. However, this behavioral contagion is not mediated by perceptions of greed: participants' ratings of their partner's greediness do not predict their own subsequent behavior after controlling for observed withdrawals. Information about an opponent's greedy reputation (as rated by a previous partner) does not affect new participants' behavior. Attempts to manage one's own reputation for greed (when warned it will be shared) are weak and not statistically significant. The mechanism for behavioral contagion appears to be norm learning or direct response to observed behavior, not to perceptions or reputational information. No punishment, sanction, or reward options are present, and no group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes are analyzed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games, as no punishment or sanction mechanism is present in any condition. The design is a two-person CPR dilemma with continuous withdrawal choices, no communication, and no explicit efficiency or group payoff analysis. The findings are relevant for understanding behavioral contagion and norm learning in the absence of punishment, but do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results may be useful as a control or baseline for environments without punishment, showing that behavioral contagion (increased defection in response to observed defection) can occur, but do not address the effect of introducing punishment.

- source: Szekely, A., Andrighetto, G., Payette, N., & Tummolini, L. (2020). Aggression, Conflict, and the Formation of Intimidating Group Reputation. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that adding a peer punishment mechanism (with moderate cost and effectiveness) to a group setting where reputation is at stake will likely decrease efficiency compared to a control without punishment, at least over a moderate number of rounds. The design is not a standard public goods game, but is a social dilemma with conflict and punishment, so results are most transferable to adjacent settings.
