- source: Wu, Z. H., & Sun, Y. (2022). How to Treat Gossip in Internet Public Carbon Emission Reduction Projects?. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that gossip can promote cooperation in public goods game environments, even under heterogeneous interpersonal relationships. Among the three mechanisms for dealing with gossip (punishment only, punishment with reputation compensation, and punishment with monetary compensation), punishment with monetary compensation is most effective at increasing cooperation rates and total contributions. The model also identifies optimal parameter ranges for tolerance, penalty time, and rebirth coefficient to maximize cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (cooperation speed, total contributions) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that monetary compensation for those wrongly affected by gossip, combined with punishment for false gossipers, can best promote cooperative behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing punishment (especially with monetary compensation for those wrongly affected) can increase cooperation rates and total contributions in public-goods-game-like environments. However, because the outcomes are not reported as efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting a more positive effect of punishment on cooperation and contribution behavior, rather than on efficiency per se. The model's design and parameter exploration may inform predictions about the likely direction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require caution. The paper is most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms and the relative effectiveness of different punishment/compensation schemes.

- source: Liu, W. W., & Yang, J. N. (2018). The Evolutionary Game Theoretic Analysis for Sustainable Cooperation Relationship of Collaborative Innovation Network in Strategic Emerging Industries. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that government intervention through incentives and punishments can significantly influence the frequency of positive cooperation between enterprises and URIs in collaborative innovation networks. When the sum of incentives and punishments exceeds the total additional cost (TAC) of cooperation, positive cooperation becomes stable. If incentives and punishments are too low, negative cooperation prevails. The model does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, but focuses on the conditions for stable cooperation strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that punishment (and incentives) can increase the frequency of cooperative behavior in a multi-agent innovation network, but it does not quantify effects on efficiency or group payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves free-riding and cooperation, but the outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies) rather than payoff-based. The findings suggest that, in similar environments, increasing punishment and incentives above a threshold can shift the system toward more cooperation, which may imply higher efficiency, but this is not directly modeled or measured.

- source: Andrews, J., & Davidson, D. J. (2013). Cell-Gazing Into the Future: What Genes, Homo heidelbergensis, and Punishment Tell Us About Our Adaptive Capacity. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that punishment is a fundamental mechanism for stabilizing cooperation in large groups, enabling the evolution and maintenance of pro-social norms and group-level adaptation. It synthesizes evidence from evolutionary theory, ethnography, and game theory to claim that humans' unique adaptive capacity is rooted in our ability to cooperate, which is supported by both formal and informal punishment of defectors. The paper does not present new empirical findings or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes, but it does highlight the importance of punishment for sustaining cooperation and adaptive capacity in complex social systems.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment mechanisms are important for sustaining cooperation in group settings, which is relevant to public goods games and their variants. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in experimental games. The arguments may inform the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase cooperation and potentially efficiency, but the paper does not address how specific game design dimensions (such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost) moderate these effects. Thus, its value for quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency is limited to high-level theoretical justification for including punishment as a positive moderator of cooperation.

- source: Angourakis, A., Santos, J. I., Galán, J. M., & Balbo, A. L. (2015). Food for all: An agent-based model to explore the emergence and implications of cooperation for food storage. *Environmental Archaeology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The model demonstrates that cooperation in food storage is most likely to emerge and persist when (1) cooperative storage is at least as efficient as household storage, (2) there is moderate to high dependency on storable food, and (3) social norms regulating access to the cooperative stock are neither too strict nor too lax. Too strict enforcement (high reciprocity requirement) can undermine cooperation by excluding agents who fail to contribute due to bad luck, while too lax enforcement allows free-riding to undermine the common stock. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff in the public goods sense, but shows that cooperation increases the probability of avoiding shortages and, under certain conditions, can lead to surplus. The findings suggest that moderate sanctioning (via access control) is optimal for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff in a public goods game with explicit punishment, it models a closely related common-pool resource dilemma where access to the common resource is conditional on past cooperation (a form of sanction). The results suggest that intermediate levels of sanctioning (not too strict, not too lax) maximize cooperation and system performance (in terms of shortage avoidance and potential surplus). For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment or exclusion mechanisms that are too harsh may reduce efficiency by discouraging recovery from bad luck, while too little enforcement allows free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of storage efficiency and dependency on the resource, which may map onto MPCR and baseline efficiency in public goods games. However, direct quantitative predictions of efficiency with and without punishment are not available from this model.

- source: Suratin, A., Utomo, S. W., Martono, D. N., & Mizuno, K. (2023). Indonesia's Renewable Natural Resource Management in the Low-Carbon Transition: A Conundrum in Changing Trajectories. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that Indonesia's NRM practices are primarily allocation-focused and legal-normative, which limits their effectiveness in promoting sustainable, cooperative behavior. By integrating the PD/game theory approach into a socio-ecological governance framework, the authors argue that institutions can be designed to foster trust, reciprocity, and cooperation. They highlight that social punishment (e.g., social disapproval) may be more effective than financial sanctions in promoting norm compliance and cooperation, and that non-utilitarian values (e.g., stewardship, feelings of guilt) should be incorporated into NRM incentive structures. However, the paper does not present new empirical data or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides conceptual and theoretical support for the idea that punishment (especially social punishment) can increase cooperation in NRM dilemmas, and that institutional and value-based design features are important moderators. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or quantitative predictions of efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment in PGG-like games. For downstream prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment may depend on whether it is social or financial, the presence of trust and reciprocity, and the integration of non-utilitarian values, but it does not provide direct evidence to calibrate efficiency outcomes. Its main value is in highlighting relevant mechanisms and moderators for consideration in empirical synthesis.

- source: CAMPBELL, D. T. (1991). A NATURALISTIC THEORY OF ARCHAIC MORAL ORDERS. *Zygon*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists
  findings: The paper theorizes that the universal presence of supernatural sanction systems (rewarding and punishing afterlives) in archaic civilizations functioned to curb individual selfishness and support group-level cooperation, overcoming the problem of genetic competition among cooperators. It suggests that such beliefs and associated costly rituals (e.g., wasteful royal funerals) reinforced the credibility of these sanction systems, thereby enhancing group cohesion and effectiveness. The argument is that these group-level cultural adaptations, including moral norms and invisible sanction systems, were selected because they increased the survival and success of social groups, even if they imposed costs on individuals. However, the paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that sanction systems (punishment and reward, especially supernatural or invisible ones) can enhance group-level cooperation and cohesion, potentially increasing group effectiveness or efficiency in a broad sense. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. Its relevance is mainly conceptual, supporting the plausibility of positive effects of sanction systems on group outcomes, but it does not inform specific game design dimensions or control efficiency values.

  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper proposes that intense human cooperation and resource sharing, conceptualized as a 'regular hexagonal pyramid' of interconnected individuals, is the most effective way to reduce CO2 emissions and achieve sustainability. It applies this model to scenarios for 2030, suggesting that targeting individual high emitters and allowing low emitters to increase emissions is a fair and effective approach. The paper is entirely theoretical and does not present data or experimental results. There is no analysis of efficiency, payoff, or the effects of punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical or experimental evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not discuss punishment, sanctions, or game design dimensions such as player count, rounds, or payoff structures. Its conceptual arguments about cooperation and resource sharing may be contextually relevant to the broader literature on cooperation, but it does not inform quantitative prediction tasks involving efficiency or payoff outcomes in controlled game settings.
