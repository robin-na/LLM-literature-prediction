- source: Fehr, E., Gächter, S., Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2010). Institutions Fostering Public Goods Provision. *Selten School of Behavioral Economics: a Collection of Essays in Honor of Reinhard Selten*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment opportunities in public goods games increase cooperation and contributions, but typically reduce efficiency and group payoffs in standard (short) experimental settings due to the destruction of resources through costly punishment. In most experiments with 10 or fewer rounds, average payoffs are lower with punishment than without. However, in longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when punishment is combined with effective reputation mechanisms, efficiency can be higher with punishment. Endogenous institution choice leads most participants to eventually select punishment institutions, which then sustain high cooperation, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed unless the time horizon is long or reputation is leveraged. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed and highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as number of rounds and the presence of reputation mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive or negative, but depends critically on the number of rounds (time horizon) and the presence of reputation or endogenous institution choice. For standard short games (10 rounds or fewer), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to control. For longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when combined with reputation mechanisms, punishment can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of endogenous institution choice, which can foster high cooperation and, under the right conditions, higher efficiency. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the number of rounds and the presence of reputation or endogenous choice mechanisms are key moderators.

- source: Liu, X. S., & Guo, W. L. (2010). Persistent cooperators in nature. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with compulsory participation, if contributors are willing to pay a second cost to retrieve a portion of the public good (persistent cooperation), this can functionally act as punishment and promote cooperation. When the second cost is not too high relative to the benefit, persistent cooperators can dominate the population, leading to higher average payoffs and more stable cooperation. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of benefit-to-cost ratios and the size of the second cost) under which persistent cooperation is evolutionarily stable, risk-dominant, or advantageous. When the second cost is high, persistent cooperators act as traditional costly punishers and are less favored. The abundance of persistent cooperators is used as a proxy for group welfare and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment-like mechanisms (modeled as a second cost paid by persistent cooperators) can increase the prevalence of cooperation and, by extension, group efficiency, as long as the cost of punishment is not too high relative to the benefit. The model gives explicit parameter thresholds for when punishment is beneficial. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling punishment (with moderate cost and sufficient benefit) in a public goods game is likely to increase efficiency compared to a control without punishment, especially when the punishment cost is not excessive. The model also highlights the importance of the ratio between the benefit of cooperation, the cost of contributing, and the cost of punishment in determining the outcome. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions of efficiency, and its results are theoretical rather than empirical.

For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: Zhao, L., Ye, X. J., Huang, Z. G., Sun, J. T., Yang, L., Do, Y., & Wang, Y. H. (2010). Promotion of cooperation in the form C0C1D classified by 'degree grads' in a scale-free network. *Journal of Statistical Mechanics-theory and Experiment*.

  findings: The paper finds that in scale-free networks, cooperation can persist even when the PGG enhancement factor r < 1, due to a mechanism where high-degree ('hub') cooperators (C0) are imitated by their neighbors (C1), who may receive negative payoffs but still support the hub. The average payoff of agents is analyzed as a function of their network position (degree grads), showing that only the hubs (C0) receive positive payoffs, while most other cooperators (C1) have negative payoffs. The system-level average payoff increases with r, but the persistence of cooperation at low r is due to network structure and imitation, not payoff incentives. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present or analyzed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions on efficiency or payoff in public goods games. It is useful for understanding how network structure and imitation can sustain cooperation and affect payoff distribution, but it does not inform predictions about the impact of enabling punishment. For the downstream prediction task (treatment efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is not directly relevant, as punishment is not present or discussed as a mechanism.

  findings: The study finds that the evolution of cooperation in a spatial public goods game is strongly affected by the interplay of population density, learning range, and mobility range. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators; no efficiency or group payoff results are reported.

It is relevant for understanding how spatial structure, mobility, and learning range affect cooperation rates, but not for payoff-based efficiency outcomes or the effect of punishment. Therefore, it should not be weighted heavily in predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment.

- source: Webb, J., & Foddy, M. (2004). Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts. *Small Group Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule. Voting for structural change (switching sanctioning systems) is predicted by individual profit and fines, not by group-level resource preservation. The study highlights that the impact of sanctioning systems on efficiency and payoff is heterogeneous and depends on both the structure of the sanction and the distribution of resource access.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

- source: Whitmeyer, J. M. (2004). The group control catastrophe. *Sociological Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. Instead, for intermediate monitoring efficacy, compliance can collapse to zero, creating a bifurcation with two possible equilibria (full compliance or full defection), depending on initial conditions (e.g., the number of initial cooperators). The 'catastrophe' is robust across a range of group sizes, sanction levels, internal control efficacies, and production function shapes (except linear). The equilibrium with high compliance yields higher payoffs for group members than the low-compliance equilibrium. The cost to the agent of obtaining compliance can be minimized by using only one type of sanction (individual or collective) and by leveraging initial cooperation to reach the high-compliance equilibrium, especially at moderate monitoring efficacy. However, the model does not directly report efficiency in the sense of group payoff relative to the maximum possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on group compliance and payoffs in public-goods-like environments is highly non-linear and can depend critically on monitoring efficacy, initial conditions, and the structure of sanctions. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.

- source: McEvoy, D. M. (2012). Paying to punish on a free ride: member and nonmember punishments in voluntary coalitions. *Applied Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a public good game with voluntary coalition formation, allowing voluntary punishment (where any player can punish any other) does not increase public good provision relative to no punishment. However, when coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance (quasi-voluntary punishment), public good provision increases significantly. Both coalition members and nonmembers are equally likely to punish noncompliance, and the extent of nonmember punishment does not change when members can credibly enforce compliance. The main outcomes are contribution levels and punishment frequencies; efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that simply enabling voluntary punishment (without credible commitment) is not sufficient to increase efficiency or public good provision in coalition-based public good games. Only when punishment is structured so that coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance does cooperation (and likely efficiency) increase. For prediction, the presence of a credible, committed punishment mechanism is a key moderator; voluntary, non-committed punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes.

It suggests that moderate punishment (moderate fines and fees) can increase cooperation and authority support, which are likely to improve efficiency, but excessive punishment (high fees/fines) can lead to high compliance at the cost of efficiency, as the costs may outweigh the benefits. The paper does not report direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so predictions must be inferred from behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, authority support) and the structure of the payoff matrices. The findings also indicate that feedback about others' payoffs (social comparison) can reduce the effectiveness of non-coercive mechanisms. For prediction, the paper supports the idea that moderate, mutually agreed punishment is most likely to improve efficiency, while excessive punishment may not.

The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

- source: Janus, T., & Lim, J. J. (2009). Sticks and carrots: Two incentive mechanisms supporting intra-group cooperation. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper proves that in a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma, the introduction of either collective punishment (Nash reversion by all after any defection) or reward mechanisms (transfers contingent on cooperation) can sustain full cooperation as a subgame perfect equilibrium, even when bilateral incentive compatibility constraints are violated. This means that group efficiency (welfare) can be maintained at the cooperative level under broader conditions than in the absence of such mechanisms. The results are general and apply to settings where players interact repeatedly and can observe each other's actions, and where the game is either infinite or has an unknown end point.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency by sustaining cooperation, even when cooperation would not be sustainable without such mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially collective punishment with Nash reversion) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment, provided players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor) and can observe actions. The model is adjacent to public goods games (it is a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma), so the results are most directly applicable to small-group, repeated, fully observable settings. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it does establish that the direction of effect is positive for efficiency when punishment is enabled.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in two-player finitely repeated social dilemma games, the introduction of a well-designed punishment mechanism (specifically, a contract that imposes a fine for final-period deviation) can move the group from inefficient (all-defect) equilibria to fully efficient (all-cooperate) equilibria. The effect is robust to renegotiation if the punishment is contingent on final-period behavior and the fine is large enough to outweigh the gain from deviation. The result is directly relevant for predicting that enabling such a punishment mechanism will increase efficiency to the maximum possible, provided the contract is enforceable and the fine is sufficiently large.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in repeated, multi-player social dilemma games (with circular or networked structure) can increase efficiency, provided players are sufficiently patient and punishment is credible.
