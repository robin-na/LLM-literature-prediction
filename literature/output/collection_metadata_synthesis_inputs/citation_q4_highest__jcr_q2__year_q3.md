- source: Arechar, A. A., Gächter, S., & Molleman, L. (2018). Conducting interactive experiments online. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that enabling peer punishment in a repeated four-person public goods game (20 rounds: 10 without, 10 with punishment) substantially increases average contributions and group efficiency in both laboratory and online (MTurk) samples. In the no-punishment condition, average contributions and earnings are lower and decay over time. When punishment is introduced, contributions jump and are sustained at higher levels, and average earnings per participant per period increase (lab: 22.64 to 26.91; online: 22.64 to 26.91, as reported). Punishment is mostly prosocial (cooperators punish defectors), and the effect is robust across both environments. The paper also finds that attrition (dropout) is not related to experimental condition or behavior, supporting the internal validity of the results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with peer punishment (cost 1, impact 3), enabling punishment increases group efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust across both lab and online samples, and the design matches canonical PGG-with-punishment studies. The paper's detailed reporting of game parameters, outcome measures, and the direct comparison of control and punishment conditions make it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A., & Gallier, C. (2020). The choice of institutions to solve cooperation problems: a survey of experimental research. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The review finds that (1) punishment institutions, when adopted, reliably increase both cooperation and group efficiency/payoff in public goods and related games; (2) the effect is strongest when the institution eliminates free-riding incentives and is not too costly; (3) the popularity and effectiveness of punishment institutions increase with repeated choice and learning; (4) endogenously chosen punishment institutions often yield higher efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, especially for weaker institutions, but the aggregate benefit of endogenous choice is ambiguous because some groups fail to implement the institution; (5) institutional cost and incomplete elimination of free-riding are key barriers to adoption and effectiveness; (6) most studies report both behavioral and payoff outcomes, with efficiency and group payoff being central dependent variables.
  prediction_guidance: This review provides strong evidence that, in public goods game-like environments, enabling punishment mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and payoffs, especially when the punishment is effective (eliminates free-riding) and not too costly. The effect is robust across a variety of game designs, player counts, and institutional choice mechanisms. For prediction, the presence of a punishment institution should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, with the magnitude moderated by punishment cost, effectiveness, and whether the institution is adopted. The review also highlights that repeated exposure, information, and learning opportunities increase the likelihood of institution adoption and thus efficiency gains. However, if the institution is costly or does not fully deter free-riding, efficiency gains may be limited or absent. The review's synthesis of many studies with detailed design features makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Wang, S. X., Liu, L. J., & Chen, X. J. (2021). Tax-based pure punishment and reward in the public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both decentralized punishment and reward can drive a networked public goods game to high cooperation, but the minimal cumulative cost required depends on the initial cooperation level. If the initial fraction of cooperators is low, reward is more cost-effective; if it is high, punishment is more cost-effective. The study provides analytical and simulation results for the optimal incentive protocols and the conditions under which full cooperation is stable. The main outcome is the cumulative cost of incentives needed to reach a target cooperation level, not group efficiency or total payoff per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in networked public goods games. While it does not report group efficiency directly, it analyzes the cost (incentives spent) required to achieve high cooperation, which is closely related to efficiency when incentive costs are subtracted from group payoffs. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment (in terms of cost to achieve cooperation) depends on initial conditions and the structure of the game (network, group size, synergy factor). For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is not always the most efficient intervention; its relative efficiency compared to reward depends on the starting state of the group. The detailed modeling of incentive cost and cooperation dynamics can inform parameterizations of efficiency in similar networked PGG environments.

- source: Capraro, V., & Perc, M. (2018). Grand Challenges in Social Physics: In Pursuit of Moral Behavior. *Frontiers in Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that cooperation in social dilemmas, as studied in public goods games and related models, is just one aspect of a broader class of moral behaviors. It reviews how statistical physics and network science have been used to study cooperation, including mechanisms like punishment and reward, and suggests that these methods can be extended to study other forms of moral behavior. The paper references prior work showing that mechanisms such as punishment, reward, and network structure can affect the evolution of cooperation, but does not provide new empirical or simulation results. Efficiency is mentioned as a moral preference, but not as a measured outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is useful for contextualizing which mechanisms and dimensions (e.g., punishment, reward, network structure) are considered important in the literature, and for mapping which game features have been studied in prior simulation and empirical work. However, it does not report or synthesize quantitative effects of punishment on efficiency or payoff, nor does it provide parameter-level evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Dong, Y. T., Hao, G., Wang, J., Liu, C., & Xia, C. Y. (2019). Cooperation in the spatial public goods game with the second-order reputation evaluation. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The introduction of a second-order reputation evaluation mechanism in a spatial public goods game leads to a significant increase in the frequency of cooperators, especially as the reputation threshold and the severity of reputation changes (step length) increase. The model demonstrates that high-reputation individuals become influential, spreading cooperation, while defectors lose reputation and influence. The main outcome is the stationary frequency of cooperators, not efficiency or group payoff. There is no explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanism; instead, reputation changes act as an indirect incentive structure. The findings are based on numerical simulations, not empirical or experimental data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include a punishment mechanism or report efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its findings on reputation mechanisms may be contextually informative for understanding how indirect incentives can promote cooperation, but they are not directly transferable to the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The paper is best used as contextual or mechanistic background rather than as direct evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Yan, F., Chen, X. J., Qiu, Z. P., & Szolnoki, A. (2021). Cooperator driven oscillation in a time-delayed feedback-evolving game. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment (probabilistic detection and fine for defectors) can stabilize cooperation and maintain the common resource at sustainable levels, but only if the product of detection probability and fine (pβ) is sufficiently large relative to the cost of cooperation or the reinvestment rate. When punishment is strong, the system converges to a stable, efficient state with high cooperation and resource sustainability. When punishment is weak, defectors dominate and the resource is depleted. If cooperator reinvestment is proportional to endowment and there is a time delay in environmental feedback, the system can exhibit persistent oscillations in cooperation and resource levels, especially when punishment is moderate and resource growth is not too fast or too slow. The critical threshold for punishment effectiveness and the impact of time delay are analytically characterized. The model provides explicit conditions under which punishment improves or fails to improve group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment (with known cost and effectiveness) affects group efficiency in a common-pool resource game with feedback dynamics. The results are most relevant for adjacent PGG-like environments with renewable resources and environmental feedback. The model gives explicit formulas for the stationary group payoff/resource level as a function of punishment parameters, player count, resource growth, and reinvestment structure. For prediction, the key insight is that increasing punishment effectiveness (higher pβ) generally increases efficiency up to a threshold, above which full cooperation and sustainability are achieved. However, if punishment is too weak, efficiency remains low. Time delay in feedback can destabilize the system and induce oscillations, but does not always reduce average efficiency. The findings are most transferable to settings with institutional punishment, renewable resources, and feedback between group action and group payoff.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Chen, X. J. (2018). Competition and partnership between conformity and payoff-based imitations in social dilemmas. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that the coevolution of payoff-based and conformity-based learning protocols in spatial social dilemma games can dramatically increase the parameter space where full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) is achieved, especially in the snowdrift and stag-hunt game regions. In the snowdrift game, the coevolutionary model allows for full cooperation at higher temptation and sucker's payoff values than the classic payoff-driven model. In the stag-hunt game, alliances between different types of cooperators can reverse the direction of evolution, leading to full cooperation in regions where defection would otherwise dominate. In the prisoner's dilemma, the coevolutionary model does not improve cooperation. The main outcome is that diversity in learning protocols can enhance collective payoff and efficiency, but this is achieved without any punishment or sanctioning mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. However, it is highly informative about how alternative mechanisms (specifically, the coevolution of learning protocols) can affect efficiency and cooperation in spatial social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that mechanisms other than punishment (such as conformity-based imitation) can also expand the region of high efficiency, but it does not inform the effect of punishment per se. Thus, it should not be used as direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, but may be useful for understanding the broader context of cooperation-promoting mechanisms.
