- source: Jiao, Y. H., Chen, T., & Chen, Q. (2020). Probabilistic punishment and reward under rule of trust-based decision-making in continuous public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing probabilistic punishment and reward (i.e., punish/reward with probability rho < 1) into a continuous PGG can maintain high levels of cooperation and mean payoff (efficiency), even when the cost of punishment/reward is high. Traditional (always executed) punishment/reward leads to collapse of cooperation and low efficiency when costs are high. There is an optimal execution probability (rho ≈ 0.6) and moderate fine/bonus size (f = r = 1) that maximize mean payoff and cooperation, saving about 40% of the cost compared to always executing punishment/reward. If either the execution probability or the fine/bonus is too low, cooperation and efficiency remain low. The model shows that probabilistic execution is robust to high costs and can avoid the collapse of cooperation seen in traditional models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in continuous PGGs on structured populations, enabling punishment (and reward) with a moderate probability (rather than always) can increase group efficiency compared to both no punishment and always-on punishment, especially when costs are high. The effect is robust across a range of synergy factors (mpcr), costs, and fine/bonus sizes. For prediction, the presence of probabilistic punishment (with moderate execution probability and moderate cost/fine) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to the control (no punishment) and to traditional always-on punishment, particularly when punishment/reward costs are nontrivial. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the presence and cost of punishment, the effectiveness of punishment, and whether execution is probabilistic or deterministic.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

- source: Wang, X. J., & Lv, S. J. (2019). The public goods game with shared punishment cost in well-mixed and structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment with shared cost among cooperators in a public goods game can promote and stabilize cooperation, thereby increasing group efficiency and average payoff, provided the penalty imposed on defectors is large enough compared to the punishment cost. In well-mixed populations, this can transform the game from one dominated by defection to one where full cooperation is stable. In structured populations, cooperation can emerge at lower fine thresholds and coexist with defection, but as the penalty increases, well-mixed populations become more favorable for cooperation than structured ones. The effect of punishment is thus generally positive for efficiency, but the benefit depends on the relative size of the fine, the punishment cost, and the group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment (with shared cost among cooperators) increases efficiency and group payoff when the fine is sufficiently large relative to the cost. The effect is modulated by group size (player_count), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech, i.e., fine per cost). Structured populations (networked interactions) can allow cooperation to emerge at lower fine thresholds, but as the fine increases, well-mixed populations yield higher efficiency. The findings support the prediction that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective and not too costly. The model is theoretical, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted as qualitative guidance.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Evolution of cooperation in a hierarchical society with corruption control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a hierarchical public goods game, the presence of corrupt punishment can maintain civilian cooperation but undermines altruistic punishment and reduces population mean payoff. Introducing corruption control, where altruistic punishers can also punish or exclude corrupt punishers, increases both cooperation and efficiency. Asymmetrical punishment (social exclusion of corrupt punishers) is more effective than symmetrical punishment (equal fines for corrupt punishers and defectors) in promoting both cooperation and population mean payoff. The model provides explicit formulas and phase diagrams showing how efficiency depends on group size, punishment cost, fine, bribe, cop proportion, and the probability of spiteful corruption. The results are robust across a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with hierarchical roles and corruption. It shows that the introduction of punishment (especially with corruption control) increases efficiency, and that the effectiveness depends on the design dimensions such as group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the structure of punishment (symmetrical vs. asymmetrical). The explicit formulas and phase diagrams can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game parameters. The results are most applicable to settings with hierarchical roles and the possibility of corrupt punishment, but the general findings about the positive effect of well-designed punishment on efficiency are likely to transfer to standard PGGs with punishment.

- source: Flores, L. S., Fernandes, H. C. M., Amaral, M. A., & Vainstein, M. H. (2021). Symbiotic behaviour in the public goods game with altruistic punishment. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in a spatial PGG, the introduction of altruistic punishment allows for the survival of cooperation in a wider range of parameters than in the classic PGG. For low punishment costs, clusters of punishers can survive and support cooperation. At intermediate punishment costs, both punishers and cooperators are vulnerable to defectors. At high punishment costs, a symbiotic spatial structure emerges where neither cooperators nor punishers can survive alone, but together they can outcompete defectors by forming clusters with punishers in the core and cooperators at the boundary. The effect of punishment on cooperation is thus highly dependent on the cost and the spatial structure. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff directly, but the persistence of altruistic strategies is used as a proxy for cooperative success.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in spatial PGGs is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the spatial structure of interactions. While it does not report efficiency directly, it shows that punishment can expand the parameter region where cooperation survives, especially when spatial clustering allows for symbiosis between cooperators and punishers. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that punishment is most effective at low cost, can be detrimental at intermediate cost, and can again be effective at high cost if spatial symbiosis is possible. The results are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and may not generalize to well-mixed or non-spatial settings.

- source: Greenwood, G. W., Abbass, H. A., & Petraki, E. (2018). When is altruistic punishment useful in social dilemmas?. *Biosystems*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment in a public goods game is only effective at promoting cooperation (and thus improving group outcomes) if the frequency of punishers exceeds a threshold determined by the cost of contribution and the punishment level. If this threshold is not met, defection remains the dominant strategy and group efficiency does not improve. The analysis provides explicit formulas for the returns to each strategy and the minimum frequency of punishers required for punishment to be effective. The findings imply that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to improve efficiency; the population composition and punishment parameters are critical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public goods games. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional: punishment only increases efficiency if the proportion of punishers is above a threshold that depends on the cost of contribution and the punishment level. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on the game parameters (contribution cost, punishment cost/impact) and the frequency of punishers. The explicit formulas can be used to estimate the required conditions for punishment to be effective, but the paper does not provide empirical efficiency values or group payoff data.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that commitment is a central mechanism for the evolution of cooperation, punishment, and other moral behaviors in humans. It reviews evidence and theory suggesting that commitment to punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and prisoners' dilemmas, even when punishment is costly. The authors contend that standard models based on population structure or repetition do not fully explain observed human behavior, and that commitment (and the ability to recognize commitment in others) is necessary for the evolution of costly punishment and conditional cooperation. The paper discusses the implications for efficiency and group welfare in a theoretical sense but does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical results on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for why punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and similar environments, emphasizing the role of commitment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the idea that the presence of punishment (and the ability to commit to punishment) can change the strategic environment and potentially increase cooperation, but it does not allow for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of commitment and recognizability of commitment for punishment to be effective.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Murase, Y., & Baek, S. K. (2018). Seven rules to avoid the tragedy of the commons. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with three players and implementation error, it is possible to construct deterministic memory-3 strategies that achieve mutual cooperation (efficiency) and defensibility (no player can be exploited), as well as distinguishability (outperforming unconditional cooperators). For n-person games, memory length at least n is required for such strategies. The strategies do not involve explicit punishment actions or costs, but use conditional cooperation and defection rules that can serve similar functions. The findings are theoretical and do not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling punishment mechanisms on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games, it is possible to achieve high efficiency through carefully designed deterministic strategies with sufficient memory, even in the presence of errors. However, it does not study the effect of explicit punishment mechanisms (such as costly punishment or sanctions) on efficiency, nor does it manipulate or compare games with and without punishment. Therefore, while the results are highly relevant for understanding the potential for cooperation and efficiency in repeated PGGs, they do not directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in experimental or empirical settings. The paper is most useful for understanding the theoretical limits of strategy design rather than the causal effect of punishment interventions.

  findings: The model predicts that, in repeated public goods and related games, the long-term average group action and payoff converge to the Nash equilibrium based on material payoffs when there is no external authority. Social and cognitive factors (such as conformity, cognitive dissonance, and beliefs about others) affect the distribution and variance of actions and beliefs, but do not shift the group average away from the material-payoff optimum in the absence of authority. When an external authority promotes a particular action, group behavior can shift toward the promoted standard, sometimes increasing or decreasing efficiency depending on the alignment of the promoted action with the material optimum. The model does not include explicit peer punishment or sanctioning as a treatment variable, so it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a theoretical baseline for predicting efficiency in public goods and related games without explicit punishment mechanisms. It shows that, absent external authority or explicit sanctions, efficiency is determined by material payoffs, and social/cognitive factors mainly affect variance and convergence speed. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that, unless punishment is modeled as a material cost for non-conformity (as in the 'disapproval by peers' term), enabling punishment would require additional modeling or empirical evidence. The model is most useful for predicting control (no-punishment) efficiency and for understanding the role of soft normative pressures, but does not provide direct evidence for the effect of enabling explicit punishment on efficiency.

However, because the model does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency/payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency.
