- source: Hou, G. L., Wang, F., Shi, J. Y., Chen, W. J., & Yu, J. (2019). Which is the ideal sanction for cooperation? An experimental study on different types of third-party sanctions. *Psych Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a standard PGG increases group earnings and cooperation compared to a baseline with no sanctions. The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that gossip can promote cooperation in public goods game environments, even under heterogeneous interpersonal relationships. Among the three mechanisms for dealing with gossip (punishment only, punishment with reputation compensation, and punishment with monetary compensation), punishment with monetary compensation is most effective at increasing cooperation rates and total contributions. The model also identifies optimal parameter ranges for tolerance, penalty time, and rebirth coefficient to maximize cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (cooperation speed, total contributions) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that monetary compensation for those wrongly affected by gossip, combined with punishment for false gossipers, can best promote cooperative behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing punishment (especially with monetary compensation for those wrongly affected) can increase cooperation rates and total contributions in public-goods-game-like environments. However, because the outcomes are not reported as efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting a more positive effect of punishment on cooperation and contribution behavior, rather than on efficiency per se. The model's design and parameter exploration may inform predictions about the likely direction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require caution. The paper is most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms and the relative effectiveness of different punishment/compensation schemes.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that allowing informed cooperators to rewire their network connections at a cost can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation in public goods games on random networks. Higher rates of network rewiring (by informed cooperators) lower the threshold for cooperation to emerge, especially under adverse conditions (low synergy factor). The system exhibits distinct phases (full cooperation, full defection, coexistence, and segregated sub-networks), and the transition between these phases depends on the synergy factor and rewiring rate. Instead, it studies the effect of costly information and network rewiring on cooperation rates. While the findings suggest that network adaptation can promote cooperation, they do not provide evidence about efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor about the effect of punishment. Thus, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency, though it may be contextually relevant for understanding alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms.

- source: Wang, S., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2018). Non-Fixed Investment in Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Journal of the Korean Physical Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that allowing non-fixed investment in voluntary PGGs leads to complex rock-scissors-paper dynamics among cooperators, defectors, and loners. Increasing the maximum possible investment ratio can initially promote cooperation and increase average payoff, but further increases may reduce cooperation due to increased temptation to defect. Higher interest rates (MPCR) and lower loner payoffs promote both cooperation and higher average payoffs. The memory length (sensitivity to past payoffs) also moderates outcomes: moderate memory promotes higher average payoffs and cooperation, while very short or very long memory is detrimental. The system always converges to a stable equilibrium regardless of initial conditions. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present or discussed as interventions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in PGG-like environments, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the model or analysis. It is therefore not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. However, it does provide detailed evidence on how other design dimensions (investment flexibility, MPCR, loner payoff, memory) affect efficiency and cooperation in voluntary PGGs without punishment. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not relevant.

- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Suzuki, K., & Ishiwata, R. (2022). Impact of a Carbon Tax on Energy Transition in a Deregulated Market: A Game-Based Experimental Approach. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study used a 4-player, 30-round online game simulating energy companies in a deregulated market, with and without a carbon tax (punishment for fossil fuel use). The carbon tax was introduced in the second half of the game and was known to players in advance. The main findings are: (1) The carbon tax significantly increased renewable energy usage after its implementation, accelerating the energy transition. (2) Total group profit (efficiency) was higher with the tax, but the difference was not statistically significant. (3) The tax did not significantly affect R&D investment or market price in aggregate, but did increase market price after the tax was imposed. (4) Information about future taxation alone did not change behavior or reduce uncertainty; only actual taxation affected actions. (5) Players' perceptions of uncertainty and obstacles to transition were not reduced by the tax. Overall, the carbon tax improved efficiency-related outcomes by promoting renewables and increasing group profit, but only after its implementation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism (carbon tax) in a competitive, multi-player, repeated game increases group efficiency (total profit) and accelerates the transition to socially optimal behavior (renewable energy use). The effect is most pronounced after the punishment is actually implemented, not merely announced. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in similar games is likely to increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is salient and directly affects payoffs. The game structure is adjacent to a public goods game, with competition and investment choices, so the findings are most applicable to PGG variants with market competition and investment dynamics. The effect size on efficiency is positive but may not always reach statistical significance, especially in small samples.

- source: Angourakis, A., Santos, J. I., Galán, J. M., & Balbo, A. L. (2015). Food for all: An agent-based model to explore the emergence and implications of cooperation for food storage. *Environmental Archaeology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The model demonstrates that cooperation in food storage is most likely to emerge and persist when (1) cooperative storage is at least as efficient as household storage, (2) there is moderate to high dependency on storable food, and (3) social norms regulating access to the cooperative stock are neither too strict nor too lax. Too strict enforcement (high reciprocity requirement) can undermine cooperation by excluding agents who fail to contribute due to bad luck, while too lax enforcement allows free-riding to undermine the common stock. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff in the public goods sense, but shows that cooperation increases the probability of avoiding shortages and, under certain conditions, can lead to surplus. The findings suggest that moderate sanctioning (via access control) is optimal for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff in a public goods game with explicit punishment, it models a closely related common-pool resource dilemma where access to the common resource is conditional on past cooperation (a form of sanction). The results suggest that intermediate levels of sanctioning (not too strict, not too lax) maximize cooperation and system performance (in terms of shortage avoidance and potential surplus). For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment or exclusion mechanisms that are too harsh may reduce efficiency by discouraging recovery from bad luck, while too little enforcement allows free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of storage efficiency and dependency on the resource, which may map onto MPCR and baseline efficiency in public goods games. However, direct quantitative predictions of efficiency with and without punishment are not available from this model.
