- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of prosocial punishment in unstructured and structured populations and in the presence of antisocial punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that prosocial punishment can evolve and promote cooperation (and thus high efficiency) in public goods games, even without auxiliary mechanisms like network structure or voluntary participation. The efficiency of the group (measured by which strategy dominates and thus the average group payoff) is increased when prosocial punishment dominates. The evolution of prosocial punishment (and thus efficiency) is facilitated by higher returns to the public good (mpcr), higher punishment effectiveness, and optimal punishment cost. Allowing unused punishment resources to be redistributed (non-wasteful punishment) further facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency. Antisocial punishment can dominate under low punishment cost or low public good return, reducing efficiency. Network structure can both hinder and facilitate the evolution of punishment, but under favorable conditions, it helps prosocial punishment dominate. The model provides phase diagrams mapping out these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment can increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective, the cost is optimal (not too low or high), and the returns to the public good are high. The model also shows that if punishment is too cheap, antisocial punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency, while if it is too costly, punishment does not evolve. The presence of a reward mechanism (redistribution of unused punishment resources) further increases the likelihood of high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions such as mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward. The results are theoretical and should be interpreted as providing mechanistic and qualitative guidance rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2019). Evolution of coordinated punishment to enforce cooperation from an unbiased strategy space. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, the ability to observe the presence of punishment institutions is crucial for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff. When institutions are not observable, anti-social punishment can invade and reduce cooperation and efficiency. When institutions are observable, a small minority of pro-social institution supporters can sustain high levels of cooperation and group payoff, even in the presence of anti-social punishment strategies. The model demonstrates that the evolutionary outcome is robust to the inclusion of all possible strategies, and that the coexistence of institution supporters and opportunists is stable. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore context-dependent: positive when observability and conditional strategies are present, negative or neutral when they are absent.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on whether players can observe the presence of punishment institutions and condition their behavior accordingly. If the game design allows for observability and conditional strategies, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. If observability is absent, enabling punishment—especially if anti-social punishment is possible—may reduce efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of supporting institutions and group size affect the probability of institution implementation and thus the efficiency outcome. For prediction, the presence or absence of observability/conditionality, the possibility of anti-social punishment, and the cost structure are key moderators.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Conditional punishment is a double-edged sword in promoting cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.

- source: Otten, K., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N. (2020). Heterogeneous groups cooperate in public good problems despite normative disagreements about individual contribution levels. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a linear public goods game with peer punishment and heterogeneous returns, experimentally induced normative disagreement within groups does not reduce cooperation (measured by average contributions) or increase punishment. Instead, the average normative view within a group predicts the group's contribution level. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but notes that joint payoffs are maximized at full contribution, and that observed contribution levels are lower than this maximum, consistent with underprovision. The presence of punishment maintains cooperation at moderate levels, but does not eliminate the effect of group-mean norms. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of normative disagreement in a linear PGG with peer punishment, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, nor does it include a no-punishment control. For prediction tasks, it shows that, in a 3-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGG with punishment (cost 1, impact 3), with full information about others' contributions and payoffs but anonymous punishment, group-mean normative views are a strong predictor of contribution levels. The presence of punishment maintains moderate cooperation, but the paper does not allow direct estimation of the efficiency gain from enabling punishment versus disabling it. The results suggest that, under these parameters, punishment supports cooperation even in the presence of normative disagreement, but the efficiency effect relative to no-punishment is not measured.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Weber, T. O., Weisel, O., & Gächter, S. (2018). Dispositional free riders do not free ride on punishment. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that the availability of punishment leads dispositional free riders (DFR) to increase their contributions to the public good to levels similar to dispositional conditional cooperators (DCC). Both DFR and DCC are equally likely to punish non-cooperators, and their punishment expenditures and motives are similar, regardless of whether punishment reduces payoff inequality (3:1 ratio) or not (1:1 ratio). The presence of punishment thus increases cooperation among DFR, but the paper does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, focusing instead on behavioral outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a one-shot public goods game increases contributions among individuals who would otherwise free ride, and that both free riders and conditional cooperators are willing to punish non-cooperators. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does, however, support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation even among those predisposed to free ride, which is likely to translate into higher efficiency in similar game designs. The lack of direct efficiency data is a limitation for quantitative prediction.

- source: Shirado, H., & Christakis, N. A. (2020). Network Engineering Using Autonomous Agents Increases Cooperation in Human Groups. *Iscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that introducing bots that facilitate the breaking of ties with defectors ('disengaged' intervention) in dynamic network public goods games leads to a substantial increase in cooperation rates among human participants, compared to controls (no bots, bots that always cooperate, or tit-for-tat bots). The effect is robust even when only a single bot is present and when humans are aware of the bots' presence. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperative players per round. The study does not involve punishment or sanctions in the standard sense, but the network exclusion of defectors is interpreted as a form of decentralized ostracism.

Instead, it provides evidence about the effect of network engineering (ostracism via tie-breaking) on cooperation rates. The findings suggest that enabling players to break ties with defectors (or having bots facilitate this) can increase cooperation and, to some extent, the earnings of cooperators. However, because efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) is not directly measured or reported, and because punishment is not present, the paper's direct value for predicting efficiency under punishment is limited. It may be useful as adjacent evidence for the effects of exclusion or ostracism mechanisms.

However, it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. It is highly relevant for understanding the impact of commitment and negotiation structure, but not for the prediction task focused on punishment.

- source: Lee, J. H., Iwasa, Y., Dieckmann, U., & Sigmund, K. (2019). Social evolution leads to persistent corruption. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This theoretical study shows that while institutional punishment can, in principle, sustain cooperation and high payoffs in public-goods-like settings, the possibility of corruption (i.e., punishment institutions accepting bribes) leads to cycles or stable states of reduced cooperation and lower average payoffs. The model demonstrates that transparency and monitoring are necessary to maintain honest punishment, but these are costly and thus not always sustained. As a result, the efficiency gains from punishment are not robust: corruption can cause efficiency to fall well below the cooperative optimum, and the system may oscillate between periods of high and low efficiency depending on the prevalence of corruption and monitoring.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a cautionary theoretical perspective: enabling punishment in a public-goods-like game does not guarantee increased efficiency if the punishment institution can be corrupted. The efficiency benefit of punishment depends critically on the integrity of the institution and the cost and prevalence of monitoring. In prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on whether corruption is possible and how transparency is maintained. If the game design allows for institutional corruption or lacks mechanisms for transparency and monitoring, enabling punishment may not improve—and may even reduce—efficiency relative to the control. The model also highlights the importance of considering dynamic and cyclical effects, not just static outcomes.

- source: Gross, J., & Böhm, R. (2020). Voluntary restrictions on self-reliance increase cooperation and mitigate wealth inequality. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that when individuals can solve a collective action problem either individually (self-reliance) or collectively (public good), the availability of self-reliance crowds out cooperation and increases inequality, especially when self-reliance is relatively cheap. However, when groups are given the opportunity to vote to abolish the individual solution, they overwhelmingly do so under medium and high interdependence, which leads to higher rates of public goods provision, lower resource waste, higher group earnings, and reduced inequality. The effect is particularly strong when there is a risk of miscoordination (medium interdependence) or when group members differ in their ability to be self-reliant (wealth heterogeneity). The study does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms in the standard sense, but self-reliance by richer group members can function as a coercive device, pressuring poorer members to cooperate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional design features that restrict individual opt-out/self-reliance options can substantially increase efficiency and group earnings in public-goods-like environments, especially when the efficiency gains from cooperation are significant (i.e., when self-reliance is costly relative to the public good). While the study does not manipulate punishment, it shows that endogenous institutional restrictions (analogous to mandatory participation or exclusion of exit options) can have large positive effects on efficiency, similar to or greater than those observed for punishment in standard PGGs. For prediction, the presence or absence of self-reliance/exit options, and the ability to restrict them, should be considered as key moderators of efficiency, alongside standard game design dimensions.

  findings: The paper finds that both punishment (sanctions) and rewards can increase group welfare and promote safety compliance in a repeated innovation race game, but their effects depend on the underlying risk and incentive parameters. Reward mechanisms can also increase efficiency, especially when highly cost-effective, and are less likely to reduce efficiency through over-regulation. The findings are most directly applicable to innovation race or winner-take-all settings, but the mechanisms are adjacent to public goods games.
