- source: Li, M. Y., Kang, H. W., Sun, X. P., Shen, Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2022). Replicator dynamics of public goods game with tax-based punishment. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that tax-based punishment can sustain cooperation and increase group efficiency in a public goods game, provided the tax is sufficient to offset the cost of punishment. There is a critical threshold for the synergy factor (mpcr) and punishment fine, above which contributing strategies dominate and group efficiency is maximized. The model also shows that, while tax-based punishment and reward can achieve the same maximum cooperation level, the average payoff (efficiency) can be higher under tax-based punishment when punishment/reward parameters are modest relative to the returns from cooperation. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation and high efficiency are stable, and compares the efficiency of tax-based punishment, tax-based reward, and tax-based pure punishment models.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment (with tax support) on efficiency in public goods games. It shows that, for a given group size, number of rounds (infinite/replicator), mpcr, punishment cost, and tax, one can determine whether punishment will increase efficiency and by how much. The results are most applicable to infinite, well-mixed populations with compulsory tax and continuous contribution choices. The explicit conditions and payoff formulas can be used to predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions, especially for settings with tax-based punishment. The findings also clarify that, while cooperation rate and efficiency can be aligned, the average payoff (efficiency) is the key outcome, and it can be higher for tax-based punishment than for tax-based reward under certain parameter regimes.

- source: Sun, Z. B., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2023). State-Dependent Optimal Incentive Allocation Protocols for Cooperation in Public Goods Games on Regular Networks. *Ieee Transactions on Network Science and Engineering*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in structured populations (regular networks), the optimal allocation of a fixed institutional incentive budget between reward and punishment depends critically on the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment. When punishment is more efficient, pure punishment is optimal for maximizing cooperation and minimizing the payoff advantage of defectors. When reward is more efficient, pure reward is optimal. For intermediate efficiency ratios, an adaptive hybrid protocol—rewarding when cooperation is low, punishing when it is high—is optimal. These results are derived analytically and verified numerically. The findings differ from well-mixed populations, where the adaptive hybrid protocol is always optimal regardless of efficiency ratio. The main outcome is the expected payoff difference between defectors and cooperators, which is minimized under the optimal protocol, leading to higher group efficiency and cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games with institutional incentives on regular networks. The key moderator is the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment: if punishment is more efficient, enabling punishment (with a fixed incentive budget) increases efficiency and cooperation more than reward; if reward is more efficient, the reverse holds. For intermediate ratios, a state-dependent switch is optimal. The results are most applicable to games with institutional (not peer) incentives, fixed budgets, and structured (not well-mixed) populations. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (vs. reward or hybrid) given knowledge of the incentive budget, the efficiency of each incentive, and the network structure.

- source: Shen, Y., Yin, W. K., Kang, H. W., Zhang, H. G., & Wang, M. (2022). High-reputation individuals exert greater influence on cooperation in spatial public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper develops and analyzes a theoretical model of a public goods game with tax-based punishment and reward mechanisms. It shows that: (1) Tax-based rewards given directly to cooperators are more effective at promoting cooperation and group payoff than rewards given to punishers. (2) When rewards are used to support punishers, this can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce overall cooperation and efficiency, as punishers and cooperators end up in competition. (3) The effect of punishment and reward on efficiency is highly sensitive to the way these mechanisms are implemented, with some parameter regimes leading to lower group payoff than the baseline. (4) Increasing taxes (used as rewards for cooperators) generally increases the proportion of cooperators and system efficiency, but excessive rewards to punishers can be detrimental.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the design of the reward and punishment mechanisms, especially whether rewards are given to cooperators or punishers. For prediction, it suggests that enabling punishment does not always increase efficiency: if rewards are used to support punishers, efficiency may decrease compared to control. If tax-based rewards are given to cooperators, efficiency is more likely to increase. The model also provides explicit payoff functions and equilibrium conditions that can be used to estimate efficiency under different parameterizations (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost, reward structure). However, the results are theoretical and may not capture all behavioral nuances seen in experiments.

- source: Wang, X. J., Yang, Z. P., Chen, G. C., & Liu, Y. L. (2024). Enhancing cooperative evolution in spatial public goods game by particle swarm optimization based on exploration and q-learning. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that a reputation-based, probabilistic punishment mechanism can robustly promote cooperation and increase group payoffs in spatial public goods games, provided that the co-beneficiary capacity (number of individuals who share in the redistributed benefits) is moderate and that participants' retaliatory sentiment and reputation memory are within optimal ranges. Stronger retaliatory sentiment (lower gamma) and higher synergy factors (r) expand the parameter space where cooperation and high payoffs are sustained. The mechanism is robust to initial conditions and can allow punishers to achieve high payoffs, mitigating the typical cost of punishment. However, if the co-beneficiary capacity is too small (punishment is too weak) or too large (second-order free-riding emerges), or if reputation memory is too lenient or too short, cooperation and group payoffs collapse.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in spatial public goods games, enabling a reputation-based probabilistic punishment mechanism can substantially increase group payoffs and cooperation rates, especially when the marginal per-capita return is high and the punishment/reward mechanism is tuned to avoid both excessive exclusivity and excessive inclusivity. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of such a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, provided the co-beneficiary capacity and reputation memory are in the optimal range. The results are most directly applicable to structured (networked) populations with local interactions and reputation tracking.

- source: Wang, S. X., Chen, X. J., Xiao, Z. L., & Szolnoki, A. (2022). Decentralized incentives for general well-being in networked public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that both institutional punishment and reward, when applied optimally, can drive a population to full cooperation in a public goods game. However, the cumulative cost to the institution is lower for optimal punishment than for optimal reward across almost all initial conditions. The optimal control protocols for both incentives are derived analytically, and numerical examples confirm that these protocols minimize the cost of achieving cooperation compared to other fixed or heuristic strategies. The evolutionary trajectory of cooperation is identical under both optimal strategies, but punishment is more cost-efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in a standard public goods game with institutional incentives, enabling punishment (with optimally chosen intensity) is more cost-effective for achieving full cooperation than enabling reward, given the same game parameters. While the paper does not report group efficiency in the standard sense, the cumulative cost minimization is closely related to institutional efficiency. The results are most applicable to settings with well-mixed populations, no communication, continuous contribution, and institutional (not peer) punishment or reward. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutional punishment (with optimal tuning) will yield higher efficiency (lower cost to reach cooperation) than enabling institutional reward, and both are superior to no incentive. However, the model does not address peer punishment, finite groups, or stochastic effects.

The system can exhibit bistability (converging to either full cooperation with low punishment or full defection with high punishment), stable oscillations between cooperation and defection, or stable full cooperation/defection depending on the average per capita incentive and other parameters. The findings are based on theoretical analysis and numerical simulations of the replicator dynamics with feedback, not on empirical or experimental data. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the results suggest that under certain parameter regimes, adaptive punishment could lead to high efficiency (if cooperation is high and punishment costs are low). However, the absence of direct efficiency analysis means that predictions about efficiency effects must be made cautiously and indirectly, based on the reported cooperation dynamics. The model is most relevant for settings with institutional punishment, adaptive feedback, and infinite well-mixed populations, and less directly applicable to finite, experimental, or empirical settings.

Stronger punishment and higher economic potential generally expand the basin of attraction for cooperation, but the effect can reverse depending on who bribes more. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of corruption and the relative strength of punishment and economic potential are key moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but direct translation to efficiency outcomes requires caution.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that, when the game design includes a global exclusion mechanism with moderate or low cost and high effectiveness, one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also clarifies that if exclusion is too costly or ineffective, efficiency gains will not materialize. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of institutional punishment/exclusion in PGG-like environments.
