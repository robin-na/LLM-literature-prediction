- source: Zhuang, Q., Wang, D., Fan, Y., & Di, Z. R. (2012). Evolution of cooperation in a heterogeneous population with influential individuals. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a spatial PGG with influential individuals, both punishment and reward mechanisms (administered by influential individuals) can promote cooperation and increase mean payoff compared to the baseline with no such interventions. Rewarding cooperators is more effective than punishing defectors in terms of both promoting cooperation and increasing mean payoff, especially at low multiplication factors (low MPCR). The cost of punishment or reward is paid by society, not by the influential individuals. The presence of influential individuals as mere exemplars (always cooperating, no sanctioning power) actually reduces cooperation and does not improve efficiency. The model demonstrates that, for a given intervention cost, reward leads to higher group efficiency than punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct simulation evidence that, in a spatial PGG with influential individuals, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (mean payoff) compared to the baseline with no punishment, but enabling reward increases efficiency even more. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, but smaller than that of reward, for a given cost. The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. The model is theoretical/simulation-based, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted with caution.

- source: Xia, C. Y., Zhang, J. J., Wang, Y. L., & Wang, J. S. (2011). Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper demonstrates, through theoretical modeling and numerical simulation, that adding punishment to voluntary public goods games in finite populations with bounded rationality increases both the frequency of cooperation and the average payoff of the group. The effect of punishment is especially strong when the loner's payoff is not too high, and the model shows that punishment can suppress defectors and promote higher payoffs for cooperators and punishers. The results are compared to both compulsory PGG and VPGG without punishment, showing that the addition of punishment leads to higher cooperation and average payoffs. The findings are robust to variations in group size, rationality parameter, and loner's payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public goods games with voluntary participation, enabling punishment mechanisms increases average group payoff (i.e., efficiency) compared to the same game without punishment, especially when the loner's payoff is not too high. The model explicitly includes key design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds (implicitly infinite or repeated), punishment cost and effectiveness, and the presence of loners. The results suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, with the effect moderated by the value of the loner's payoff and the rationality parameter. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with voluntary participation, finite groups, and bounded rationality.

- source: Takács, K., & Janky, B. (2007). Smiling contributions:: Social control in a public goods game with network decline. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper develops a theoretical framework for public goods games with endogenous network change and social control mechanisms. It predicts that clustered networks reduce collective action and that the ability to delete ties leads to segregation between contributors and defectors. Social control is operationalized as the ability to send happy or sad smileys, which are intended to induce conformity or selective incentives. The experimental design is described, but no data or results are presented. The main dependent variable in the proposed experiments is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insights into how network structure and non-costly social control (smileys) might affect contribution behavior in public goods games. However, it does not provide empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it study costly punishment as typically defined in the literature. The model suggests that social control (even if non-costly) and network clustering can influence cooperation, but the lack of empirical results and the focus on non-payoff outcomes limit its direct utility for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions. The design dimensions are discussed in the context of the model and proposed experiment, but not tested empirically.

- source: Guillen, P., Fatas, E., & Brañas-Garza, P. (2010). Inducing efficient conditional cooperation patterns in public goods games, an experimental investigation. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The introduction of a grim trigger strategy threat (via possible automata) leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (by 40% before a surprise restart and 50% after) compared to a standard repeated public goods game. Contributions in the threat treatment are highly polarized (either full or zero), and the pattern of conditional cooperation changes: in the threat treatment, the minimum contribution in the previous round predicts current contributions, consistent with grim trigger logic. However, contributions still decline over time, and the effect is driven by belief manipulation rather than actual punishment or sanctions. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the higher contributions imply higher group payoffs, net of the absence of punishment costs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that belief in the possibility of strict conditional cooperation (grim trigger) can substantially increase contributions in repeated public goods games, even without actual punishment. However, because there is no real punishment mechanism (no cost, no assignment of punishment, no reduction of others' payoffs), the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to enabling punishment. The results suggest that the mere threat or belief in sanctions can have large effects on cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency impact of actual punishment mechanisms, especially considering the cost of punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, but the absence of real punishment means the findings should be used as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Noussair, C., & Soo, C. (2008). Voluntary contributions to a dynamic public good: Experimental evidence. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a dynamic public goods game where the MPCR increases or decreases based on group contributions, many groups are able to sustain high contributions and achieve high efficiency, even in the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms. The average efficiency is .671 in the high initial MPCR treatment and .632 in the low initial MPCR treatment, both substantially above the efficiency that would result from full free-riding. There is substantial heterogeneity: some groups converge to high contributions and high MPCR, while others decline to low contributions. The initial MPCR does not significantly affect long-run outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a baseline for the efficiency of dynamic public goods games without punishment or reward. It shows that, in such settings, efficiency can be substantially higher than in standard static public goods games, due to the dynamic feedback of contributions on the MPCR. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is useful for estimating control (no-punishment) efficiency in dynamic PGGs, but it does not provide any evidence about the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or discussed experimentally. Thus, it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, but it does suggest that dynamic feedback mechanisms can themselves sustain high efficiency in some groups.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing a continuous range of cooperation degrees (strategy diversity) in public goods games leads to higher average cooperation, especially when all players have equal initial wealth. The presence of strategy diversity prevents long-term exploitation and leads to the extinction of defectors at relatively low mpcr values.   prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include punishment or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its findings on strategy diversity and cooperation rates may be contextually informative for understanding how game structure affects cooperation, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

Its findings are limited to the effects of a depreciation mechanism on cooperation rates. It may be relevant for understanding how endogenous changes to the public good's value affect cooperation, but not for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment interventions.

- source: Congleton, R. D., & Vanberg, V. J. (2001). Help, harm or avoid? On the personal advantage of dispositions to cooperate and punish in multilateral PD games with exit. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in three-person repeated joint production games with exit, conditional cooperation and the ability to exit dysfunctional teams allow for higher average payoffs (efficiency) than unconditional defection. However, in short-lived enterprises, targeted punishment of defectors is necessary to sustain cooperation and high payoffs. When targeted punishment is strong enough, even in short-lived games, cooperation and efficiency are restored. The results are robust in both static and evolutionary (replicator dynamic) simulations. The study provides direct evidence that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong simulation-based evidence that enabling targeted punishment in small-group repeated public goods games with exit increases efficiency, especially when the number of rounds is small. The effect of punishment is more positive when the punishment is strong (high impact per cost) and can be targeted at defectors. The results suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of targeted punishment, the number of rounds, and the ability to exit are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher when exit and conditional cooperation are possible, but enabling punishment further increases efficiency, especially in short games. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Prietula, M. J., & Conway, D. (2009). The evolution of metanorms: quis custodiet ipsos custodes?. *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of a public-goods-like dilemma, the addition of metanorms (punishing those who do not punish defectors) is necessary for the emergence of a behavioral norm against defection. When only first-order punishment is available, defection dominates. When metanorms are present, defection rates drop and punishment rates rise, indicating norm emergence. Decoupling the strategies for punishing defectors and non-punishers does not prevent norm emergence and can reduce the overall level of punishment required. In group-structured games with unequal power, metanorms alone do not produce a norm against defection; only with decoupled sanctioning structures does the strong group enforce a norm against the weak group's defection, leading to group dominance rather than universal cooperation. The outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategy values (boldness, vengefulness), not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence of punishment and especially metanorms (second-order punishment) can dramatically reduce defection rates in public-goods-like environments, even when punishment is costly. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (defection, punishment) rather than efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that enabling punishment (especially with metanorms) is likely to increase efficiency in analogous empirical games, but the magnitude and exact efficiency outcomes are not reported. The paper also highlights that group structure and the sophistication of the punishment mechanism (e.g., decoupling punishment types) can moderate the effect, sometimes leading to group dominance rather than overall cooperation.

- source: Corgnet, B. (2012). PEER EVALUATIONS AND TEAM PERFORMANCE: WHEN FRIENDS DO WORSE THAN STRANGERS. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that using peer evaluations to allocate team profits in a real-effort team task leads to lower team performance (measured as a percentage of maximum possible profits) than an equal split rule. This effect is statistically significant and is stronger in teams where members are acquaintances or friends. The mechanism appears to involve a crowding-out of intrinsic motivation and increased negative reciprocity when peer evaluations are used among friends. The majority of peer assessments follow equality norms, but a significant minority use equity (contribution-based) norms. The results suggest that peer evaluation mechanisms may not always improve efficiency, especially in contexts with strong interpersonal relationships.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in small real-effort team settings with face-to-face communication and high social proximity, introducing a peer evaluation mechanism for allocating payoffs (a form of endogenous sanction/reward) can reduce efficiency compared to a fixed equal split. This is contrary to standard public goods game findings where punishment or reward options often increase efficiency. The negative effect is especially pronounced when team members are friends or classmates. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of introducing peer-based sanction/reward mechanisms on efficiency is context-dependent: in environments with high social ties and communication, such mechanisms may reduce efficiency relative to control. The findings are most relevant for small teams with real-effort tasks, face-to-face interaction, and endogenous allocation of payoffs via peer assessment.

- source: Song, Q. Q., Li, Z. P., Fu, C. H., & Wang, L. S. (2011). Optional contributions have positive effects for volunteering public goods games. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that introducing an option for low contributions in volunteering public goods games (VPG) increases the average payoff of the population (i.e., efficiency), compared to the standard VPG with only a single contribution level. This effect is observed in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, and is especially pronounced when the multiplication factor (r) is above a threshold that depends weakly on group size. The mechanism is that the low-contribution option induces more players to cooperate, which in turn increases the number of high contributors as well, leading to higher overall payoffs. The results are based on theoretical analysis and extensive simulations of evolutionary dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in VPG-like public goods games, allowing optional (especially low) contributions can increase group efficiency compared to a single, higher contribution requirement. While the paper does not study punishment or sanctions, it is highly informative for predicting efficiency in games where the main design dimension is the contribution structure (e.g., all-or-nothing vs. continuous, or optional vs. required). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, introducing more flexible contribution options (even without punishment) can increase efficiency, especially when the MPCR is above a moderate threshold. However, the paper does not address the effects of punishment, so it is not directly informative for the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Grimm, V., & Mengel, F. (2011). Matching technology and the choice of punishment institutions in a prisoner's dilemma game. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that a local, exogenous punishment institution can sustain high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency) when the matching structure (population viscosity) is high, i.e., when group members mostly interact with each other. As viscosity decreases (more random mixing), both cooperation and average payoffs decline. In the most separated case (x=0), average profits are 373 (out of a possible 400), while in the most mixed case (x=1), average profits drop to 230 (the mutual defection payoff is 200). A control with internalized punishment (punishment always applies) at low viscosity (x=2/3) yields higher average profits (243) than local punishment (230). Thus, punishment institutions can increase efficiency, but their effectiveness depends strongly on the matching structure and the scope of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on the matching structure (population viscosity) and the scope of punishment (local vs internalized). When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the presence of a punishment institution will increase efficiency only if the environment allows for sufficient group separation (high viscosity) or if punishment is internalized (applies in all matches). In low viscosity or random matching, local punishment is much less effective. The control efficiency (without punishment) is the mutual defection payoff (200), and the treatment efficiency with punishment can approach the cooperative payoff (400) under high viscosity, but remains low under random matching. The paper also shows that the technical details of punishment (local vs internalized) matter for efficiency outcomes.

- source: Bolle, F., Breitmoser, Y., & Schlächter, S. (2011). Extortion in the laboratory. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that when one player can punish another at low cost in a repeated extortion game, punishment is used frequently and harshly, especially as experience accumulates. The threat of punishment induces compliance with moderate demands, but actual punishment does not improve and may reduce future compliance. Over time, P's demands and income increase, while A's income decreases. The combined earnings of both players are about 80% of the maximum possible, indicating a notable efficiency loss compared to full cooperation. The results suggest that low-cost punishment leads to persistent, severe punishment and lower group efficiency, and that subject heterogeneity (different behavioral types) is important for understanding aggregate outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that in repeated asymmetric games with low-cost, high-impact punishment, the introduction of punishment reduces group efficiency compared to the maximum possible. The threat of punishment increases compliance with moderate demands, but actual punishment does not improve efficiency and may worsen it. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that when punishment is cheap and effective, efficiency is likely to be lower than in a fully cooperative scenario, and the presence of punishment can lead to persistent efficiency losses. The results are most directly relevant to asymmetric, dyadic games rather than standard symmetric public goods games, but the mechanisms (punishment cost, effectiveness, behavioral response) are informative for predicting efficiency in related environments.

- source: Tan, F. F., & Xiao, E. T. (2012). Peer punishment with third-party approval in a social dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment compares two treatments in a repeated prisoner's dilemma: (1) baseline, where players can directly punish each other, and (2) TPA (third-party approval), where a third party must approve any punishment before it is implemented. The TPA treatment results in significantly lower cooperation rates (0.47 vs. 0.70) and marginally significantly lower average earnings (23.52 vs. 25.30 tokens per period) compared to the baseline. Third-party approval reduces antisocial punishment but also reduces punishment of defectors, which appears to undermine the positive effect of punishment on cooperation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with peer punishment, introducing a third-party approval step for punishment reduces both cooperation and average earnings compared to direct peer punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that adding procedural hurdles or external approval to punishment mechanisms may decrease the efficiency gains typically associated with peer punishment, especially when antisocial punishment is infrequent. The findings are most directly relevant to adjacent settings (prisoner's dilemma rather than public goods), but the mechanism—peer punishment with and without third-party approval—maps closely to PGG-like environments. The control (baseline) efficiency is higher than in the treatment with third-party approval, indicating a less positive effect of punishment on efficiency when third-party approval is required.
