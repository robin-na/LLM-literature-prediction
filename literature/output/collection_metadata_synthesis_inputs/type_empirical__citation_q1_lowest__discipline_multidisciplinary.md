- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., Gross, J., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., van Dijk, E., & de Dreu, C. K. W. (2023). Discriminatory punishment undermines the enforcement of group cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

- source: Nakagawa, M., Lefebvre, M., & Stenger, A. (2022). Long-lasting effects of incentives and social preference: A public goods experiment. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that all three incentive mechanisms (monetary punishment, non-monetary reward, recommendation) significantly increase contributions to the public good during the policy phase. Monetary punishment is most effective in raising contributions, but this effect is not persistent: contributions drop sharply after the punishment phase ends. Non-monetary reward and recommendation also increase contributions, with some evidence of longer-lasting effects, especially for recommendation among certain types (e.g., free-riders). However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The study also finds that the effect of incentives varies by social preference type: monetary punishment increases contributions across all types, while non-monetary reward and recommendation mainly affect strong conditional cooperators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling monetary punishment in a repeated linear PGG increases contribution rates during the intervention, but the effect is not persistent after removal. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency outcomes is not possible. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified and match standard PGG lab experiments. The findings support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) during the intervention, but the lack of payoff/efficiency data means the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred. The results are most useful for predicting contribution rate changes, not efficiency per se.

For the downstream prediction task, the paper confirms that, in a 3-player, 10-round VCM with standard punishment (linear cost, known identities), the introduction of punishment increases contributions, especially for sleep-restricted subjects. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed, the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, and the behavioral results are likely to correlate with efficiency, but the lack of direct payoff analysis is a limitation.

In a Japanese online-worker sample, punishment may even be associated with lower cooperation. The absence of direct efficiency or group payoff data limits quantitative prediction, but the behavioral results suggest that, under these design parameters, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is likely minimal or absent. The study also highlights the importance of sample heterogeneity and the specific punishment parameters (cost and effectiveness) in moderating punishment effects.

- source: Posten, A. C., Ugurlar, P., Kube, S., & Lammers, J. (2025). Maintaining cooperation through vertical communication of trust when removing sanctions. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that when punishment institutions are removed from a public goods game, contributions (i.e., cooperation) typically drop sharply. However, if the authority communicates trust in participants' willingness to cooperate, this drop is substantially reduced, and contribution levels remain similar to those observed when punishment is still in place. This effect is observed for centralized, third-party, and peer punishment mechanisms. The effect of vertical trust communication persists beyond a single round and is mediated by increased trust in coplayers. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined, but the findings strongly suggest that vertical trust communication can substitute for punishment in maintaining cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the removal of punishment institutions in public goods games leads to a sharp decline in cooperation (contributions), but that this decline can be prevented if the authority communicates trust in participants' willingness to cooperate. While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, the close link between contribution rates and efficiency in standard PGGs suggests that vertical trust communication could maintain efficiency at levels similar to those with punishment, at least in the short term. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence or absence of vertical trust communication is a critical moderator when punishment is removed, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the main outcome is contribution, not efficiency.

- source: Heine, F., & Strobel, M. (2020). Reward and punishment in a team contest. *Plos One*.

  findings: The experiment compares reward and punishment mechanisms in a team contest game (with and without between-group competition). Groups of four play 15 rounds, deciding how much to contribute to a group lottery (contest) and then can reward or punish teammates at a cost. The main findings are: (1) Both punishment and reward mechanisms lead to over-contribution relative to Nash equilibrium, causing significant efficiency losses (rent dissipation).   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a team contest environment with public-good-like features, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency and may worsen it, especially when both reward and punishment are available. The effect of punishment is less positive (more negative) on efficiency compared to reward or baseline. The contest structure (between-group competition) amplifies inefficiency. For prediction, if the control game (no punishment) is already inefficient due to over-contribution, enabling punishment is likely to further reduce efficiency, not improve it. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, known MPCR, punishment cost and tech, no identification of punishers, no communication) are all specified and match standard lab contest/PGG designs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled in similar environments.
