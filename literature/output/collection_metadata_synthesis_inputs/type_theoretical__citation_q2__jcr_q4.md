- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

- source: Zhang, B. Y., & Pei, S. (2022). Game Theory and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Journal of the Operations Research Society of China*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper reviews theoretical and empirical literature on the evolution of cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It finds that punishment (both peer and institutional) can promote cooperation, especially in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, but at a cost that can reduce group welfare or average payoff. Peer punishment can curb free-riding but is costly and can lead to second-order free-riding and antisocial punishment, often resulting in lower group payoffs compared to no-punishment conditions. Institutional punishment can overcome some of these issues but is also costly. Reward mechanisms are generally less effective than punishment in moving populations from defection to cooperation, unless the reward is large. The paper emphasizes that combining reward and punishment can have a strong effect on promoting cooperation. Theoretical models predict that the effect of punishment on cooperation and payoff depends on the size and structure of the incentive, and that punishment can eliminate extremely selfish behavior but does not always increase group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based guidance that punishment mechanisms in public goods games can increase cooperation rates, but often at a cost that may reduce group efficiency or average payoff, especially in peer punishment settings. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the game (e.g., peer vs institutional punishment, compulsory vs voluntary participation). For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment is likely to increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, and in some cases may reduce it due to the costs of punishment. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates or new empirical data, so its value is mainly in providing qualitative, mechanism-based expectations and summarizing prior findings.

- source: Lee, J. H., & Iwasa, Y. (2014). Modeling socio-economic aspects of ecosystem management and biodiversity conservation. *Population Ecology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_tech
  findings: The paper's third model demonstrates that in social dilemma situations (akin to public goods or common-pool resource games), introducing punishment for selfish behavior can increase group welfare, especially when punishment is graduated (i.e., increases with the harm caused). The model shows that with evaluation errors and heterogeneity in player sensitivity to utility, graduated punishment is optimal for maximizing efficiency (group payoff). Severe, non-graduated punishment is only optimal under unrealistic assumptions. The findings support the use of graduated sanctions to improve efficiency in resource management games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public-goods-like environments with social dilemmas, enabling punishment (especially graduated punishment) increases group efficiency/welfare compared to no-punishment baselines, provided that punishment is not excessively severe and is sensitive to the harm caused. The model is abstract but maps closely to PGG-like games with two actions (cooperate/defect), and the efficiency outcome is directly analyzed. The findings are most applicable to settings where punishment can be graduated and where player heterogeneity and evaluation errors exist. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it gives qualitative guidance that enabling (graduated) punishment should increase efficiency relative to control.

- source: Castro, L., & Toro, M. A. (2008). Iterated prisoner's dilemma in an asocial world dominated by loners, not by defectors. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated N-person Prisoner's Dilemma games, the presence of a 'loner' strategy (voluntary opt-out for a fixed payoff) changes the evolutionary dynamics: loners can displace defectors, and conditional cooperators (TFT0) can invade a population of loners if the benefit of cooperation is high enough and the number of repeated interactions is sufficient. The model demonstrates that cooperation can be established and maintained in sizable groups without the need for explicit punishment, provided that individuals can opt out. The paper does not model or test punishment directly, but discusses its potential role as an additional mechanism for sustaining cooperation in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that voluntary participation (the loner strategy) can enable high-efficiency outcomes in repeated public-goods-like games, even without punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency, as punishment is not modeled. The findings suggest that in environments where voluntary participation is possible, the need for punishment to sustain cooperation may be reduced. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the baseline efficiency in control games with voluntary participation, but not about the incremental effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Voelkl, B. (2015). The evolution of generalized reciprocity in social interaction networks. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis demonstrates that introducing an effective penalty for unfaithfulness (selling outside the cooperative) leads to stable, efficient cooperative outcomes where all members sell to the cooperative. If the penalty is limited (not strong enough), full cooperation is unstable. When the penalty is strong (exceeds the extra profit from defection), the system converges to full cooperation, maximizing group payoff. Redistribution of penalties among faithful members further strengthens this effect, leading to a unique, stable Nash equilibrium with high efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions under which punishment stabilizes cooperation and maximizes group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in public-goods-like settings with continuous contribution and the possibility of punishment, increasing the effectiveness and/or strength of punishment increases group efficiency and stabilizes cooperation. The results are directly relevant for predicting that enabling or strengthening punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is strong enough to outweigh the gains from defection. The model also highlights that redistributing penalties among cooperators can further enhance efficiency and stability. However, the results are derived from a stylized, theoretical model rather than empirical data, and the setting is a marketing cooperative with continuous contribution rather than a canonical PGG, though the structure is closely related.

- source: Forges, F., Horst, U., & Salomon, A. (2016). Feasibility and individual rationality in two-person Bayesian games. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in two-person Bayesian games with a single informed player, the existence of a uniform punishment strategy (UPS) for the uninformed player guarantees the existence of feasible, individually rational (i.e., efficient/welfare-maximizing) solutions. These solutions can be implemented as Nash equilibria of appropriately designed noncooperative games. The set of such payoffs coincides with the set of joint plan equilibrium payoffs in the infinitely repeated game. When UPS does not hold, feasible, individually rational solutions may not exist. The results generalize to cases where both players have private information, under certain conditions. The paper provides theoretical foundations for how punishment strategies can support efficient outcomes in games with incomplete information, but does not provide empirical estimates or experimental results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that the ability to punish (specifically, the existence of a uniform punishment strategy) is crucial for achieving efficient, individually rational outcomes in two-person Bayesian games with incomplete information. For the downstream prediction task, the main implication is that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (punishment cost, punishment tech, and the ability to credibly threaten punishment) are key moderators of whether efficiency gains from punishment are possible. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical estimates, and its results are not specific to public goods games or experimental designs. It is most useful for understanding the theoretical boundary conditions under which punishment can support efficiency, rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes in experimental PGG-like environments.

- source: Madeo, D., & Mocenni, C. (2021). Consensus towards Partially Cooperative Strategies in Self-Regulated Evolutionary Games on Networks. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in Stag-Hunt and Chicken games on networks, consensus on partial cooperation can be globally stable and more rewarding (in terms of average payoff) than full cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma. The stability of full cooperation, full defection, or partial cooperation depends on the self-regulation parameter relative to network degree. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms; instead, it focuses on endogenous self-regulation. Payoff at equilibrium is analytically and numerically compared across game types and equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in networked games with self-regulation (but without explicit punishment or reward), partial cooperation can be globally stable and yield higher payoffs than full cooperation in the PD. However, because the model does not include punishment, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to enabling punishment in public goods games. The results are most relevant for understanding how internalized social preferences (modeled as self-regulation) affect efficiency, rather than the effect of exogenous punishment mechanisms.
