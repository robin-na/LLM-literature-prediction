- source: Ohdaira, T. (2022). The probabilistic pool punishment proportional to the difference of payoff outperforms previous pool and peer punishment. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the proposed probabilistic pool punishment mechanism, which ties the probability of paying the punishment cost to the difference in payoffs, results in a population with more punishers and fewer cooperators (who do not punish) compared to traditional pool punishment. This state is more robust to the invasion of defectors and maintains an average payoff that is comparable to or better than peer punishment. The results hold across both regular and random one-dimensional lattice structures. The study also notes that the fixed cost problem of pool punishment is solved, and that the mechanism is effective even when the number of defectors is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism—specifically, making punishment probabilistic and proportional to payoff differences—can improve group efficiency (average payoff) in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a more adaptive, probabilistic, and context-sensitive punishment regime (as opposed to fixed-cost or deterministic punishment) is likely to yield higher efficiency, especially in structured populations. The results are most directly applicable to games with 5-player groups, 1000-player populations, 600 rounds (generations), no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR and punishment cost parameters as specified. The study does not vary most game design dimensions, so its predictive value is strongest for the specific parameter regime tested.

- source: Wang, C. Q., Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2024). Evolutionary dynamics of any multiplayer game on regular graphs. *Nature Communications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper analytically demonstrates that in structured populations (regular graphs), the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer or pool) in public goods games can lead to the extinction of defection or to cyclic dominance among strategies, depending on the punishment type and strength. For peer punishment, if the punishment fine exceeds a critical threshold, defection is eliminated and only cooperators and punishers coexist, maximizing group efficiency. For pool punishment, strong punishment leads to cyclic dominance, preventing fixation of defection and maintaining higher group welfare. The paper provides explicit formulas for the critical punishment thresholds and shows that these effects are unique to structured populations and do not occur in well-mixed populations, where defection always retains a basin of attraction. The results are presented in terms of group payoff, mean payoff, and phase diagrams, all directly related to efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for predicting that, in public goods games on regular graphs, enabling punishment (peer or pool) will increase group efficiency and welfare, provided the punishment strength exceeds a critical threshold. The analytical results specify how the effect depends on the number of neighbors (player_count), the punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech), and the structure of the game (e.g., mpcr, payoff function). The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings with structured populations and multi-strategy dynamics. The explicit threshold formulas for punishment strength can be used to estimate when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency.

- source: Liu, L. J., Xiao, Z. L., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2022). Early exclusion leads to cyclical cooperation in repeated group interactions. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, the ability to exclude defectors early (after monitoring) leads to cyclical dominance among cooperators, defectors, and excluders, and maintains high average cooperation rates. If exclusion is delayed, defectors can dominate. The model shows that the timing of exclusion, monitoring cost, and discount factor are key moderators. While the model defines payoffs and uses them in evolutionary dynamics, the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies), not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task: it shows that enabling a form of punishment (social exclusion) can sustain high cooperation rates if implemented early, and that the timing and cost of exclusion are critical moderators. However, because the main outcomes are behavioral (not efficiency or group payoff), the findings should be used as informative-indirect evidence. The model's structure and parameterization (group size, rounds, exclusion cost, monitoring cost) can inform how similar design dimensions might affect the efficiency impact of punishment in repeated PGGs, but direct quantitative predictions about efficiency are not available.

- source: Liu, L. J., & Chen, X. J. (2022). Indirect exclusion can promote cooperation in repeated group interactions. *Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that indirect exclusion—where a game organizer probabilistically selects cooperators for future participation—can promote the stable coexistence of cooperators and defectors or even lead to the dominance of cooperation in repeated public goods games. The effectiveness of indirect exclusion increases with higher identification probability and more repeated rounds. The model avoids the second-order free-rider problem associated with costly direct exclusion. However, the main reported outcomes are the equilibrium fraction of cooperators and the stationary distribution of strategies, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that indirect exclusion mechanisms can increase the equilibrium level of cooperation in repeated public goods games, especially as the number of rounds and the probability of correctly identifying cooperators increase. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment (or exclusion) on efficiency. The results are most relevant for predicting changes in cooperation rates rather than efficiency, and the exclusion mechanism studied is costless and indirect, differing from standard costly punishment treatments.

- source: Pal, S., & Hilbe, C. (2022). Reputation effects drive the joint evolution of cooperation and social rewarding. *Nature Communications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that reputation effects are crucial for the evolution of cooperation and social rewarding in PGG-like environments. When individuals can observe or infer others' rewarding strategies (i.e., reputation is possible), social rewarding and cooperation co-evolve and are stable, leading to higher group payoffs and efficiency. Without reputation, cooperation and rewarding do not emerge, and antisocial rewarding may arise under certain conditions (e.g., high assortment or mutually beneficial rewards). In multiplayer (PGG) settings, similar results hold: reputation enables stable cooperation and social rewarding, while its absence leads to defection and low efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation and rewarding are payoff-maximizing, and shows that the presence of reputation mechanisms is a key moderator of efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling reputation effects (e.g., making rewarding behavior observable) in PGG-like games will increase efficiency when social rewarding is possible. The model directly links game design dimensions such as group size, marginal per-capita return, reward cost and effectiveness, and information structure (reputation) to the emergence of efficient cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of observable rewarding (reputation), sufficiently high reward effectiveness relative to cost, and the ability to target rewards to cooperators are critical for efficiency gains from reward mechanisms. The absence of reputation or the presence of antisocial rewarding can undermine efficiency. While the paper does not model punishment directly, it provides indirect evidence that the structure and observability of incentives (including punishment, by analogy) are key moderators of efficiency effects.

- source: Otten, K., Frey, U. J., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N. (2022). Human cooperation in changing groups in a large-scale public goods game. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that the number of newcomers in a group is negatively associated with the average percentage of resources contributed to the public good. This effect is strongest in small groups and at later time periods. The lower group contribution is primarily due to newcomers contributing less than incumbents, though their contributions increase as they spend more time in the group. The difference between newcomers and incumbents is partially explained by differences in private good levels and public good levels, but remains significant even after controlling for these factors. The study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment, and there is no evidence of sanctioning mechanisms being central to the game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide informative context about how group composition changes (newcomer entry) can reduce cooperation (as measured by contribution rates), especially in the absence of punishment or exclusion. For prediction tasks involving efficiency, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not report payoff-based outcomes or manipulate punishment. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation dynamics in large, dynamic groups without punishment.

- source: Han, T. A. (2022). Institutional incentives for the evolution of committed cooperation: ensuring participation is as important as enhancing compliance. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (punishment or reward) can promote the evolution of cooperation and commitment compliance in a social dilemma with a prior commitment stage. Reward is generally more effective than punishment at promoting cooperation, especially when the cost of commitment is high or the benefit-to-cost ratio is large. Allocating part of the incentive budget to encourage participation in the commitment (not just compliance) further increases cooperation, particularly when participation is costly. The presence of noise in participation decisions can also stabilize cooperation. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and are based on explicit payoff calculations and evolutionary stability analysis.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in social dilemma games with a prior commitment stage, enabling punishment (or reward) increases group efficiency (cooperation frequency) compared to no incentives, provided the incentive budget is sufficient. Reward is more effective than punishment, especially as the cost of participation increases. Allocating part of the incentive budget to encourage participation (not just compliance) further increases efficiency, especially when participation is costly. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in games with a commitment or opt-in stage, and the model's explicit payoff calculations can be mapped to efficiency outcomes. However, the model is based on a two-player, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with a commitment stage, not a standard repeated public goods game, so predictions should be transferred with caution to multi-player, repeated settings.

- source: Han, T. A., Duong, M. H., & Perc, M. (2024). Evolutionary mechanisms that promote cooperation may not promote social welfare. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of the Prisoner's Dilemma with peer or institutional incentives, punishment mechanisms (both peer and institutional) are often detrimental to social welfare, even when they increase cooperation. Peer punishment increases cooperation more than peer reward, but peer reward leads to higher social welfare. For institutional incentives, reward achieves positive social welfare across a broader range of parameters than punishment, even when both achieve similar cooperation levels. There is often an optimal, moderate level of incentive impact for maximizing social welfare, rather than maximizing cooperation or punishment strength. These findings are robust across mutation rates, selection intensities, and game configurations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments (here, the Prisoner's Dilemma) is likely to reduce efficiency (social welfare) compared to reward mechanisms, even if punishment increases cooperation. The effect is robust across a range of model parameters. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (especially with high impact-to-cost ratio) should be expected to have a less positive or even negative effect on efficiency relative to control, unless the punishment is extremely cost-effective and moderate in impact. The findings suggest that maximizing cooperation via punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency, and that reward mechanisms are generally preferable for social welfare. The results are based on theoretical models, not empirical data, but are directly relevant to the efficiency prediction task.

- source: Köster, R., Hadfield-Menell, D., Everett, R., Weidinger, L., Hadfield, G. K., & Leibo, J. Z. (2022). Spurious normativity enhances learning of compliance and enforcement behavior in artificial agents. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=other_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: Introducing a punishment regime for norm violations (e.g., eating a poisonous berry) enables agents to learn to avoid harmful actions more quickly, resulting in higher group efficiency (collective return) than in the absence of punishment. Adding a second, arbitrary 'silly' rule (punishment for eating a harmless berry) further increases group efficiency during the learning process, as it provides more opportunities for agents to practice enforcement and compliance, leading to faster and more robust avoidance of genuinely harmful actions. The effect is strongest during the intermediate learning phase, with the silly rule condition yielding the highest group payoffs. The study demonstrates that third-party punishment, even when applied to arbitrary rules, can enhance the emergence of efficient, norm-compliant behavior in artificial agent societies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a multiagent environment with norm violations increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust even when punishment is costly and decentralized. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the core mechanism—costly third-party punishment of norm violations—closely parallels PGG punishment treatments. The results suggest that, for similar environments, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially when the environment presents a credit assignment problem (delayed or opaque consequences of harmful actions). The effect is further enhanced if the environment provides more opportunities for agents to practice enforcement (e.g., via additional, even arbitrary, punishable actions). The findings are most directly applicable to settings where learning and credit assignment are challenging, and where punishment is visible and incentivized. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with immediate, transparent payoffs, but the qualitative direction (punishment increases efficiency) is likely to hold.

  findings: The paper finds that in a third-party punishment game followed by a trust game, humans earn less trust by sharing with or punishing bots than with humans. Trust-gain is attenuated but not eliminated in mixed human-bot collectives. Perceived consensus about cooperative norms predicts trust-gain, and experimentally making norm consensus salient increases trust-gain in some conditions. The main outcomes are trust and norm perception, not group efficiency or total payoff. The design is adjacent to public goods games, but the outcome focus is on trust and norm-following rather than efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. Its design is adjacent (third-party punishment game with binary sharing), and the main outcomes are trust-gain and norm perception, not efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment can increase trust in norm-followers, especially when norm consensus is salient, but do not quantify effects on group efficiency. The paper may inform mechanism design around norm salience and trust, but is not directly informative for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect of TPP corruption on efficiency is not measured. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.

  findings: The study finds that the inclusion of punishment as a strategy can, depending on parameter values (notably the cost of punishment and the temptation to defect), lead to the coexistence of punishers and defectors, dominance of punishers, or extinction of all strategies. Time delay can induce oscillatory or chaotic dynamics, and the ecological 'free space' variable can support the survival of cooperation-related strategies. However, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. The results may inform qualitative expectations about the conditions under which punishment can suppress defection or support cooperation, but cannot be used for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.

- source: Dimant, E., & Gesche, T. (2023). Nudging enforcers: how norm perceptions and motives for lying shape sanctions. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment increases with the size of the lie and is harsher when the lie increases inequity or is for personal gain, compared to when it restores equity. Norm-nudges (empirical or normative information about others' behavior or approval) increase punishment, especially when preexisting norms are ambiguous. The effect of norm-nudges is strongest in scenarios where the social norm is less clear. These findings are robust across a behavioral experiment, a vignette experiment, and a norm elicitation study. However, the study does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on how punishment behavior is shaped by norm-nudges and the context of norm violations, but it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it shows that punishment is sensitive to context and can be increased by norm-nudges, especially when norms are ambiguous. However, it does not provide evidence on whether or how punishment affects group efficiency or welfare, nor does it use a public goods game structure. Thus, it should be used as indirect evidence about the determinants of punishment behavior, not about efficiency effects.

- source: Singh, M., & Garfield, Z. H. (2022). Evidence for third-party mediation but not punishment in Mentawai justice. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that, in the Mentawai context, third-party punishment is absent: no cases were found where third parties (outside the victim, their close kin, or spouse) directly punished or demanded penalties from transgressors. Indirect or second-order sanctions by third parties are also not observed. Instead, penalties (tulou) are negotiated and paid primarily to restore dyadic cooperation, with kinship playing a major role in the likelihood and size of payments. Mediation by third parties (especially shamans and elders) is common, particularly in more severe or between-clan disputes, but the mediator's role is to facilitate negotiation, not to enforce punishment. The study suggests that, in this small-scale society, institutionalized penalties serve to repair relationships rather than to enforce group-level norms through punishment. The findings are based on retrospective interviews and ethnographic observation of 302 reliable cases of transgression and response.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The studied context is not a PGG or close variant, and the outcomes are not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that, in small-scale, kin-based societies, third-party punishment is rare or absent, and penalties serve to restore dyadic cooperation rather than to enforce group norms or increase group efficiency. If extrapolating to PGG-like settings, the results would suggest that the presence or absence of third-party punishment may depend on the social structure and the nature of cooperation (private vs. public goods), but the paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes. Thus, it is of limited use for the downstream prediction task, except as a caution that punishment mechanisms may not always function as assumed in small-scale, kin-based societies.
