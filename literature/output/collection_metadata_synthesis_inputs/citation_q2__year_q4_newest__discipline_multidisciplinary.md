- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Dickson, E. S., Gordon, S. C., & Huber, G. A. (2022). Identifying legitimacy: Experimental evidence on compliance with authority. *Science Advances*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that a central authority's costly action to improve the fairness of punishment (procedural fairness) increases citizens' willingness to contribute to a public good, even when material incentives are held constant. The effect is robust and persists over time. The mechanism is shown to be intrinsic (normative legitimation) rather than reciprocity or conditional cooperation. However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper provides strong evidence that legitimacy-enhancing actions by authorities can increase cooperation, but does not directly report on efficiency or total group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that features of the punishment regime that enhance perceived legitimacy (such as procedural fairness) can increase contribution rates in public goods games with centralized punishment. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar game designs, legitimacy-enhancing punishment regimes may increase efficiency indirectly via increased contributions, but the magnitude of the effect on efficiency is not quantified here. The paper is most useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators of cooperation under punishment, rather than for direct efficiency prediction.

- source: Schnell, E., & Muthukrishna, M. (2024). Indirect reciprocity undermines indirect reciprocity destabilizing large- scale cooperation. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a nested PGG environment with both local and global public goods, indirect reciprocity (reputation-based conditional aid) can stabilize cooperation at a single scale, but when multiple scales are present, local cooperation is more stable and can invade global cooperation. This occurs even when the global PGG has a higher theoretical maximum payoff. The model demonstrates that the presence of multiple, potentially conflicting reputations leads to a situation where local cooperation undermines global cooperation, reducing overall efficiency at the larger scale. The more fragmented the population (more local groups), the harder it is for global cooperation to be stable. Thus, indirect reciprocity, as typically modeled, cannot by itself sustain efficient, large-scale cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the structure of group membership and the scale at which reputational incentives operate are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from indirect reciprocity-based 'punishment' (here, conditional aid/reward) in PGG-like environments. Specifically, if the game design allows for multiple, nested groups and reputations can be built at the local level, enabling local cooperation, then enabling indirect reciprocity (punishment/reward) may not increase, and can even decrease, efficiency at the global (large-group) level. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of multiple group scales and the ability to build local reputations are key negative moderators of the efficiency effect of punishment/reward mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the control (no punishment/reward) game is inefficient, but the addition of indirect reciprocity does not guarantee higher efficiency at the largest scale.

The paper is most useful for understanding how reward design parameters affect cooperation rates and institutional costs, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled. It does not provide empirical or theoretical results on punishment, nor does it analyze efficiency as defined by group payoff relative to full cooperation.

However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for understanding the conditions under which punishment can prevent resource collapse or sustain cooperation in public-goods-like environments with ecological feedbacks. The model structure and qualitative findings may inform priors about when punishment is likely to be effective, but quantitative efficiency predictions are not possible from this paper.

- source: Mondal, S. S., Pathak, M., & Chakraborty, S. (2022). Reward versus punishment: averting the tragedy of the commons in eco-evolutionary dynamics. *Journal of Physics-complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both punishment and reward can prevent the tragedy of the commons in a dynamic resource game, but their effects on efficiency differ. Sufficiently strong punishment leads to a stable equilibrium with all cooperators and a fully replete resource, achieving the social optimum (maximum possible group payoff). In contrast, even with strong reward, some defectors persist, so the group does not reach the maximum efficiency. The model also shows that the effect of punishment or reward depends on the initial state and the intensity of the intervention, and that bistability (multiple possible long-run outcomes) can occur. The analysis is theoretical, using replicator dynamics and stability analysis, and is not based on empirical or experimental data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in dynamic resource games with feedback (and by extension, in public-goods-like environments), enabling punishment (with sufficient intensity) can increase group efficiency and achieve the social optimum, while enabling reward alone cannot fully eliminate inefficiency due to persistent defectors. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is a prisoner's dilemma with resource feedback, and where punishment or reward is implemented as a direct modification of payoffs. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment should be expected to increase efficiency more than enabling reward, especially when the goal is to reach the maximum possible group payoff. However, the model is theoretical and does not address practical implementation details or empirical constraints.

- source: Romano, A., Gross, J., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2024). The nasty neighbor effect in humans. *Science Advances*.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in contest or public-goods-like environments with an option for costly attack or punishment, the availability and use of such options can reduce group efficiency, especially when directed at in-group members. The effect is robust across cultures and group compositions. However, the contest/attack framing is not a standard PGG, so direct quantitative transfer to classic PGGs with norm-enforcing punishment should be cautious.
