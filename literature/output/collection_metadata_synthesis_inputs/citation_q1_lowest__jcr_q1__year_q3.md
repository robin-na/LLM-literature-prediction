- source: Harrell, A. (2019). Group leaders establish cooperative norms that persist in subsequent interactions. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (either peer or leader) increases contributions and group earnings compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, peer punishment leads to lower group earnings than leader punishment due to higher costs from distributed punishment (coordination problems, counter-punishment). Leader punishment is more efficient, but its effectiveness depends on the leader's own cooperative behavior. Leaders' contributions have a stronger effect on group cooperation and efficiency than peers' contributions in peer punishment. These effects persist even after the punishment institution is removed. The paper provides direct evidence on how punishment institution design (peer vs. leader) moderates the efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) relative to no-punishment, but the design of the punishment institution matters: centralizing punishment in a single leader yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower punishment costs and less welfare destruction. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus not only a function of whether punishment is enabled, but also of the structure (peer vs. leader), the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the behavior of the punisher(s). For prediction, this paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but peer punishment may reduce efficiency gains due to higher punishment costs, while leader punishment can maximize efficiency if the leader is cooperative.

- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

  findings: The experiment finds that, in a standard VCM with 3-person groups and 10 rounds per treatment, the introduction of peer punishment increases contributions for sleep-restricted subjects but not for well-rested subjects. Without punishment, contributions do not differ by sleep condition. There is no significant difference in punishment behavior between sleep-restricted and well-rested subjects. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are robust to various compliance and attrition corrections, but some results are sensitive to the sleep deprivation measure used.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of punishment on contribution behavior in a standard VCM, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper confirms that, in a 3-player, 10-round VCM with standard punishment (linear cost, known identities), the introduction of punishment increases contributions, especially for sleep-restricted subjects. However, because efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed, the paper cannot directly inform predictions about efficiency changes due to punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, and the behavioral results are likely to correlate with efficiency, but the lack of direct payoff analysis is a limitation.

- source: Langenbach, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Inherited Institutions: Cooperation in the Light of Democratic Legitimacy. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot, three-player linear public goods game with or without a centralized, non-deterrent punishment (automatic fine for less-than-full contribution). The institutional setting (punishment or not) is determined either by majority vote (Endo) or random draw (Exo) in the first generation, and this setting is inherited by a matched group in the second generation. In the first generation, contributions are higher when punishment is democratically adopted and lower when it is democratically rejected, compared to random assignment. In the second generation, the positive effect of democratic adoption disappears, but the negative effect of democratic rejection persists (marginally). The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or total earnings as a share of the social optimum, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized, non-deterrent punishment mechanism can increase contributions if it is democratically adopted, but only in the generation that participates in the decision. The effect does not persist to subsequent generations who inherit the institution. The negative effect of democratic rejection may persist. However, since the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, only contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is informative for mapping design to expected contribution behavior, but not for direct efficiency prediction. If contribution is used as a proxy for efficiency (assuming linearity and no punishment cost to the group), the results suggest that democratic adoption of punishment increases efficiency in the first generation, but not in the second.

The main value for efficiency prediction is the robust increase in cooperation frequency with punishment, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in standard PGGs, but the absence of direct efficiency or payoff analysis limits the strength of inference.

- source: Engel, C. (2019). When Does Transparency Backfire? Putting Jeremy Bentham's Theory of General Prevention to the Experimental Test. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment manipulates the level of feedback about punishment and contributions in a standard linear public goods game with centralized punishment. Three feedback treatments (low, medium, high) vary the precision of information about others' contributions and punishment. The main finding is that full transparency (high treatment: individual-level information about both contributions and punishment) leads to significantly lower contributions than aggregate or less precise feedback. Information about punishment of others does not increase cooperation; instead, it can reduce it. Participants are more responsive to descriptive norms (others' contributions) than to injunctive signals (punishment). The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable, but reports total earnings descriptively.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with centralized punishment, increasing transparency about individual punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation (contributions), contrary to standard deterrence theory. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment with high transparency may not increase efficiency and could even reduce it, especially if the control (no punishment) game already has moderate contributions. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to contribution behavior. The design dimensions most relevant here are the feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), centralized punishment, and the linear PGG structure.

- source: Battu, B. (2021). Evolution of altruistic punishments among heterogeneous conditional cooperators. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with a population of heterogeneous conditional cooperators, the evolution of altruistic punishment (where more willing cooperators are also more willing to punish) can stabilize high levels of cooperation. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation depends on the inflicted cost of punishment and the frequency with which punishment opportunities arise. When punishment is frequent and the inflicted cost is high, the population evolves toward more cooperative and punitive types, and donation rates are high. When punishment is infrequent or the inflicted cost is low, cooperation collapses. The model also shows that it is not necessary to punish all free riders equally; targeting persistent (selfish) free riders is sufficient. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but higher donation rates are interpreted as indicative of higher group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in repeated public goods games with conditional cooperators can substantially increase cooperation rates, especially when punishment is sufficiently costly to the target and opportunities to punish are frequent. However, because the model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, its quantitative value for predicting efficiency is limited. The main actionable insight is that punishment is most effective when it is frequent and sufficiently severe, and that selective (generous) punishment targeting persistent free riders is sufficient to stabilize cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with high inflicted cost and frequent opportunities) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, but the magnitude cannot be directly inferred from this paper.
