- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y. (2013). Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy. *Economic Record*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (b) In larger groups (n=8, 12), PPID yields the highest efficiency (80.60% and 78.05%), followed by AP (76.10% and 61.35%), with VCM lowest (68.60% and 63.30%). (c) In large groups, AP can reduce efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on group size and punishment institution. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A. (2016). Non-binding agreements in public goods experiments. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Punishment is used less frequently and more effectively in these settings, with little anti-social punishment. Punishment alone (without agreement) or with second-best agreements increases contributions but not payoffs, as punishment costs offset the gains. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

- source: Dorrough, A., Glöckner, A., & Lee, B. (2017). Race for Power in Public Good Games with Unequal, Unstable Punishment Power. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Rockenbach, B., & Wolff, I. (2016). Designing Institutions for Social Dilemmas. *German Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that when subjects are allowed to design institutions for a repeated public goods game, they initially favor punishment or redistribution mechanisms, but these are associated with lower efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) compared to rules that emphasize communication (framing, appeals) or participative elements (endogenous rule change). Over the course of the experiment, the use of punishment declines, and efficiency increases, suggesting that punishment is not an efficient solution in this context. Communication and participative rule features are positively associated with efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that punishment, as typically implemented by endogenous designers, tends to reduce efficiency, mainly due to its costs, and that designers learn to move away from it.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with endogenous institution design, enabling punishment mechanisms tends to reduce efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) baseline, especially when punishment is costly and not peer-administered. The effect is robust across multiple rounds and rule sets, with efficiency increasing as designers move away from punishment toward communication and participative mechanisms. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment (especially costly, centralized, or automatic forms) is likely to result in lower efficiency than the control, unless the punishment is highly refined or replaced by redistribution mechanisms with lower waste. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG environments.

- source: Hwang, S. H. (2017). Conflict technology in cooperation: The group size paradox revisited. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that coordinated punishment in public goods games can lead to a concentration effect, making it easier to sustain cooperation and achieve high group efficiency as group size increases. When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run. This result is robust to several variations, including the presence of unconditional punishment costs and some decay in coordination as group size increases. The model provides a theoretical explanation for empirical findings that larger groups can sometimes achieve higher cooperation and efficiency, contrary to standard free-rider logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2016). Cooperation and punishment in community-structured populations with migration. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in community-structured populations playing a linear PGG with punishment, the presence of group competition (where entire communities can replace each other based on average fitness) or fitness-based migration (where individuals are more likely to migrate if they are fitter) can sustain high levels of cooperation and punishment, suppressing defection in parameter regions where defection would otherwise dominate. Constant (random) migration does not have this effect. The transition between cooperative and defective regimes is sharper with migration, but only group competition and fitness-based migration expand the parameter space where cooperation and punishment are stable. The main reported outcomes are the frequencies of strategies, not efficiency or group payoff, but higher frequencies of cooperation and punishment imply higher group payoffs relative to defection-dominated regimes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task: in PGG-like environments with punishment, the presence of group competition or fitness-based migration mechanisms can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation and punishment, and thus are likely to increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control games without punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is based on the frequencies of cooperative and punishing strategies. The results suggest that, for the same game design dimensions, enabling punishment in a context with group competition or fitness-based migration will likely lead to higher efficiency than in well-mixed or constant migration settings. The findings are most relevant for models with large group sizes, high mutation and migration rates, and community structure.

- source: Greiff, M. (2013). Rewards and the private provision of public goods on dynamic networks. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The model demonstrates that in a repeated public goods game with rewards (where giving rewards is costly but beneficial to the receiver), the emergence of a social institution prescribing prosocial behavior (contribute and reward contributors) can lead to efficient public good provision, provided that rewards are only given to contributors and the network structure evolves quickly enough to allow exclusion of non-contributors. If rewards are also given to defectors, or if players update strategies too quickly, the system converges to low contribution and low efficiency. The model does not analyze punishment directly, but exclusion from rewards via network dynamics acts as an endogenous sanction. The findings are theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games with reward mechanisms, especially in dynamic network settings. It provides strong theoretical evidence that reward-based institutions can achieve high efficiency if rewards are targeted only at contributors and if the network allows for exclusion of non-contributors. However, it does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment, nor does it compare reward to punishment directly. For prediction tasks focused on punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that endogenous exclusion (a form of sanction) can substitute for explicit punishment, but does not quantify the effect of punishment per se. The model's results are most applicable to games with dynamic partner selection and reward, not to standard fixed-group punishment treatments.

Instead, they show non-monotonic patterns, often sustained by the presence of 'contribution makers' who lead reciprocators to increase their contributions. There is a strong restart effect in finitely repeated games but not in infinitely repeated games. The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but notes that groups with contribution makers achieve higher average contributions and, by implication, higher payoffs than groups without them.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide evidence on baseline contribution dynamics in standard VCMs with continuous contribution choices, fixed groups of 5, MPCR of 0.5, and no communication or punishment. The findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency (proxied by contribution rates) is sustained at moderate levels in infinitely repeated games, especially when 'contribution makers' are present. For prediction tasks, this paper is best used as a control/baseline for VCMs without punishment, not for estimating the effect of punishment.

- source: Reeves, T., Ohtsuki, H., & Fukui, S. (2017). Asymmetric public goods game cooperation through pest control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in asymmetric public goods games (modeled as pest control among farmers), the Nash equilibrium investment and resulting efficiency depend strongly on the discount factor and the number of players. There are threshold effects: only farmers close to the risk (frontier) contribute when the discount factor is low, while more distant farmers contribute only if the discount factor is high enough. Increasing the number of players does not always increase efficiency; in some parameter regions, adding more players can paradoxically reduce total investment and group welfare. Asymmetry leads to a disproportionate burden on those at greatest risk, making the system more vulnerable to shocks. The model does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how efficiency in asymmetric public goods games depends on player count, discount factor, and the structure of risk, but it does not address punishment or sanctioning. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is highly informative for baseline (control) efficiency in asymmetric, no-punishment settings, especially regarding the effects of group size and discounting. However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no such mechanism is present or analyzed. Its findings suggest that efficiency can be non-monotonic in group size and is sensitive to asymmetry and time preferences, which should be considered when predicting control efficiency, but it cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment.

- source: Olejarz, J. W., & Nowak, M. A. (2014). Evolution of staying together in the context of diffusible public goods. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in public goods games with diffusible goods can evolve if the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds a threshold determined by the probability of staying together (q) and the internal absorption efficiency (alpha_k). Clustering increases the efficiency of cooperation by allowing cooperators to preferentially benefit from their own public goods. The model also shows that if defectors and cooperators differ in their ability to absorb public goods, coexistence or bistability can occur. Introducing a cost to large clusters (gamma_k) can create an optimal cluster size for cooperation. The model provides explicit analytical conditions for these outcomes, but does not consider punishment, reward, or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for understanding how structural and spatial factors (such as clustering and internal absorption efficiency) affect the evolution of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games. However, it does not address punishment or sanctioning, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in control games without punishment, or in environments where clustering and spatial structure are the main mechanisms supporting cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how efficiency depends on clustering, internal benefit allocation, and the benefit-to-cost ratio, but offers no evidence about punishment effects.

- source: Lierl, M. (2016). Social sanctions and informal accountability: Evidence from a laboratory experiment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment studies a public goods game with two beneficiaries and one provider, where the provider can embezzle contributions. The main manipulation is the structure of costly, mutual sanctioning (punishment and reward) relationships: (1) no sanctioning, (2) only among beneficiaries, (3) between provider and one or both beneficiaries, and (4) combinations thereof. The key finding is that mutual sanctioning among beneficiaries increases public goods provision and efficiency compared to no-sanctioning control, but this effect disappears or reverses when the provider is also part of the sanctioning network. The highest efficiency is observed when only beneficiaries can sanction each other. When both beneficiaries and the provider can mutually sanction, efficiency is not improved and can be lower than control. Actual punishment/reward is rare; the effect is mainly anticipatory. The results challenge the idea that denser or more encompassing sanctioning networks always improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends critically on the network structure of sanctioning relationships. If only beneficiaries can punish each other, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to control. If the provider can also punish or be punished, the effect is reduced or negative. For prediction, the presence and direction of punishment effects should be conditioned on who can punish whom. The game is a close variant of a standard PGG, with the addition of a third-party provider who can embezzle, so results are most directly applicable to co-production or agency settings. Control efficiency (no-sanction) is reported, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) varies by network structure. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment is not always efficiency-improving; the details of the punishment network matter.

- source: Cason, T. N., & Gangadharan, L. (2016). Swords without covenants do not lead to self-governance. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment tested four treatments (baseline, punishment only, communication only, both) in a repeated nonlinear common pool resource game with groups of six. Peer punishment (costly, 3:1 fine-to-fee ratio, up to 5 points per target) did not significantly increase group yield or efficiency relative to baseline. Communication (anonymous chat, twice per session) led to dramatic and statistically significant increases in yield/efficiency, with most groups achieving near the social optimum. When both mechanisms were available, communication made punishment largely redundant and unused. The inefficacy of punishment is attributed to the complexity of the nonlinear environment and insufficient use of strong punishment. The findings challenge the generalizability of positive punishment effects from linear public goods games to more realistic, nonlinear settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in nonlinear common pool resource environments (with concave private benefits and endogenous group costs), enabling peer punishment does not improve efficiency over baseline. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar nonlinear games, one should expect little or no efficiency gain, even with a punishment technology that is effective in linear games. The presence of communication is a much stronger moderator, with large positive effects on efficiency. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the payoff structure (linear vs nonlinear), and that control efficiency alone is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering the game's nonlinearity and the availability of communication.
