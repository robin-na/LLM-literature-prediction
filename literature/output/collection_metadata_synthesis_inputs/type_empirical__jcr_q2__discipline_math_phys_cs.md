# Paper Set Evidence Digest

- source: Pi, J. X., Yang, G. H., & Yang, H. (2022). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in N-person snowdrift games with peer punishment and individual disguise. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. The group payoff (efficiency) is lowest in the complete punishment network (86.13 out of 160), and higher in the circle (94.81), homo-pairwise (95.39), and hetero-pairwise (92.01) networks. The structure of the punishment network thus significantly moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency. The study also finds that high-MPCR individuals contribute more than low-MPCR individuals, and that punishment severity is lower in the complete network. Antisocial punishment is present in all network types.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

- source: Wang, C. Q., & Huang, C. C. (2022). Between local and global strategy updating in public goods game. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing the option for costly punishment in a repeated public goods game increases group contributions and group payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline. This effect holds both when sanctions are observable (feedback given immediately) and when sanctions are unobservable (feedback delayed until the end). The mechanism is that subjects expect free riders to be punished and adjust their behavior to avoid being punished, even when they do not observe sanctions in real time. Sanctioning is mainly non-strategic and norm-driven, targeting free riders. The presence of punishment stabilizes cooperation and prevents the unraveling of contributions, with some groups achieving high cooperation and others not. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency (as measured by group payoffs and contributions as a fraction of maximum) is positive.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to whether punishment is observable or not during play, suggesting that the mere possibility of punishment, and the associated social norm, is sufficient to increase cooperation and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment to a repeated linear PGG with 4 players, 10 rounds, and MPCR=0.4 will increase efficiency, even if punishment is not immediately observable. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

- source: Yang, X. Q., Zhang, F., Wang, W. X., Zhang, D., Shi, Z. H., & Zhou, S. W. (2020). Effect of habitat destruction on cooperation in public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a weak, automatic punishment institution (where the lowest contributor in a group loses 1 yuan per round) does not significantly increase contributions compared to a control PGG or to a reward institution. There is no significant effect of matching or mismatching subjects' institutional preferences (punishment vs reward) with the institution they are assigned to. Subjects who prefer punishment tend to be lower contributors and have social preferences associated with anti-social or efficiency-reducing profiles. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff as a main outcome, and the punishment institution is designed to be strictly efficiency-reducing at the group level.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated linear PGG with weak, automatic, centralized punishment (punishment cost = 1, punishment tech = 1, imposed on lowest contributor), enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency compared to a control. The punishment is non-deterrent and strictly efficiency-reducing by design. Therefore, in similar game designs (weak, automatic, centralized punishment), one should not expect punishment to improve efficiency over the control. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is indirect and based on contribution rates and the institutional design. The findings are most relevant for settings with weak, automatic, non-peer punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment or stronger punishment regimes.

- source: Sun, X. P., Li, Y. B., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., Peng, J., Wang, H. Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2020). Co-Evolution of Complex Network Public Goods Game under the Edges Rules. *Entropy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that allowing players to break unsatisfactory connections and form new ones (edge rules) in a public goods game on a network leads to higher and more stable cooperation, especially at low gain factors (r=2). Both simulation and a 30-person lab experiment show that the fraction of cooperators increases and stabilizes under these rules, compared to a static network. The mechanism is interpreted as self-organized punishment (breaking ties with defectors) and reward (forming ties with cooperators), but there is no explicit costly punishment. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperators; efficiency or group payoff is not reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that endogenous network adaptation (breaking and forming ties based on satisfaction) can promote cooperation in public goods games, even at low MPCR. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of explicit punishment on efficiency. The 'punishment' here is not a costly sanction but the breaking of ties, which is structurally different from standard punishment mechanisms. The findings may be useful for understanding the effects of network fluidity or endogenous partner choice, but not for predicting efficiency changes from adding explicit punishment to a fixed-group PGG.

- source: Liao, Y. L., Zhang, L., Lei, S. Y., Song, M. Z., Deng, W. K., & Hu, D. F. (2021). RETRACTED: Third-Party Punishment Mechanism and Corporate Cooperation in Environmental Investment: Experiments on Public Goods Game (Retracted Article). *Discrete Dynamics in Nature and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares a threshold public goods game with and without third-party punishment (TPP), using groups of three participants (framed as enterprises) making resource investment decisions toward a group threshold. The TPP mechanism automatically punishes group members whose investment rate is below the group mean. The presence of TPP leads to higher mean resource investment amounts, higher individual and group investment rates, and a substantially higher success rate of cooperation (from 55.57% to 76.85%). Regression analysis confirms that TPP has a significant positive effect on investment behavior, even after controlling for individual characteristics and measured preferences for interaction and equity (IEP).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a third-party punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods game increases group investment and the probability of achieving the public good. For prediction tasks, the presence of TPP (punishment enabled) should be expected to yield higher efficiency or group payoff than the control (punishment disabled), especially in small groups (n=3) with a threshold structure and automatic, impersonal punishment. The results are most directly applicable to threshold PGGs with automatic third-party punishment and no communication. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency-related outcomes under these design conditions.

- source: Weng, Q. F., He, N. R., Hu, L. W., & Chen, X. J. (2021). Heterogeneous investment with dynamical feedback promotes public cooperation and group success in spatial public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that communication mechanisms—especially chain (one-on-one) communication and the combination of information display with chain communication—substantially increase the frequency and stability of successful group provision of the public good (EPGs via GECs) compared to a baseline with no intervention. Star network (information display only) and global communication also improve outcomes relative to baseline, but less so than chain communication. The most effective intervention is the combination of information display and chain communication. However, the study does not include any punishment or sanctioning treatments, and does not report group efficiency or total payoffs. The findings are thus about behavioral contributions and threshold success, not efficiency per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or variants. It is useful for understanding the effects of communication and information display on contribution behavior and threshold success in a TPGG, but cannot inform predictions about punishment-enabled efficiency. None of the experimental treatments include punishment, sanctions, or peer enforcement. Therefore, this paper should not be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency, but may be informative for the effects of communication and information structures in threshold public goods settings.

- source: Ando, H., Nishi, A., & Handcock, M. S. (2025). Statistical modelling of networked evolutionary public goods games. *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series A-statistics in Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that participants preferentially form and maintain network ties with others who also cooperate, and avoid forming ties that would close triangles (i.e., transitive closure is disfavored in tie formation). Participants also prefer to connect with others of similar wealth. The modeling approach (STERGM) is validated for small, repeated networked public goods games. However, the experiment does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, and does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design. It does, however, provide detailed information about a networked PGG variant with dynamic partner selection, which may be relevant for understanding baseline cooperation and network effects in the absence of punishment. For prediction tasks focused on punishment effects or efficiency outcomes, this paper offers only contextual or indirect value.

- source: Li, L. Y., Zhao, X. B., Xie, D., & Xiao, X. (2024). On difference between direct-response method and strategy method in decision-making: behavioural and neural evidence in a reward-punishment game. *Journal of the Operational Research Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment shows that the elicitation method (direct-response vs. strategy) significantly affects the rates of reward and punishment in a repeated two-player reward-punishment game. Specifically, the strategy method leads to higher rates of both reward and punishment. Behavioral modeling and neural evidence suggest that the ownership effect (overweighting of cost) suppresses reward in the direct-response method, while counterfactual thinking (comparison of possible outcomes) increases punishment in the strategy method. Strategic thinking is present in both methods but does not explain the difference between them. The study does not report efficiency or total payoff outcomes, and the game structure is not a public goods game or a direct variant.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the method of eliciting punishment and reward decisions (direct-response vs. strategy) can substantially affect the frequency of punishment and reward, with the strategy method increasing both. However, it does not report on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The game is a two-player repeated reward-punishment game, not a public goods game or close variant, and the main outcomes are behavioral rates, not payoffs. The findings may be relevant for understanding how elicitation methods could moderate punishment effects, but the lack of efficiency data and the different game structure limit its direct applicability to the prediction task.

- source: Pan, Q. H., Liu, X. S., Bao, H. L., Su, Y., & He, M. F. (2018). Evolution of cooperation through adaptive interaction in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the probability of disconnection (β) after defection leads to a significant increase in the fraction of cooperators in both simulation and human lab experiments. The effect is nonlinear: at low β, the effect is modest, but at higher β, cooperation increases sharply. The mechanism is interpreted as a punishment for defection, as defectors are excluded from interactions and thus lose opportunities for payoff. The system can transition between states of all-defector, all-cooperator, or coexistence depending on the parameters (temptation to defect, cost, and β). While payoffs are discussed and collected, the main reported outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not efficiency or total group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that a punishment-like mechanism (disconnection after defection) can substantially increase cooperation rates in a spatial prisoner's dilemma environment. However, it does not directly report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so its value for predicting efficiency is indirect. The findings suggest that enabling a punishment mechanism that reduces defectors' ability to interact (and thus earn payoff) can increase cooperation, which is likely to improve efficiency in similar environments, especially when the punishment is strong (high β). The design is adjacent to public goods games, so transfer to PGG-like settings should be cautious. The main predictive signal is that punishment mechanisms that exclude defectors can be effective, especially when the exclusion probability is high.

- source: Zhang, Q. W., Liu, J. Q., & Zhang, X. R. (2024). Reputation-based disconnection-reconnection mechanism in Prisoner's Dilemma Game within dynamic networks. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=other_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that a reputation-based mechanism, where players can disconnect from low-reputation neighbors and reconnect with others, significantly increases the frequency of cooperation in repeated PDG simulations on dynamic networks. The effect is stronger when the reputation factor (alpha) is high and when the network's information connectivity (environmental degree r, rewiring probability p) is high. Conversely, when the selection intensity (sigma) is high, the effect of reputation is diminished and cooperation collapses. The results are consistent across both square lattice and small-world network topologies. However, the study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. While it does not study public goods games or efficiency/payoff outcomes directly, it shows that reputation-based dynamic linking mechanisms can strongly increase cooperation rates in repeated PDG environments. The findings suggest that, in analogous PGG-like settings, mechanisms that allow for social selection or partner choice based on reputation may increase cooperation, which could plausibly translate into higher efficiency if payoffs are aligned with cooperation. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency changes. The results are most relevant for settings where dynamic partner selection or reputation-based exclusion is possible.

- source: Gao, J., Yan, Y., Li, J. Y., Bai, W. Q., & Geng, Y. Q. (2025). Research trends of cooperation in social dilemmas from 2003-2023: A bibliometric study using CiteSpace. *Adaptive Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper identifies that research on cooperation in social dilemmas is multidisciplinary and growing, with key topics including public goods games, punishment, reward, social value orientation, procedural fairness, and group cooperation. It highlights that punishment and reward are frequently studied as mechanisms to promote cooperation, and that public goods games are a common paradigm. However, the paper does not provide quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis of the effects of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games. Instead, it summarizes the literature's thematic structure and research frontiers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It is useful for understanding the breadth of research topics, the prominence of punishment and public goods games in the literature, and the importance of various design dimensions (e.g., group size, communication, network structure) as studied factors. However, it does not report or synthesize quantitative results on efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes, and thus cannot directly inform the prediction task. It may be useful for identifying influential works or trends to consult for more targeted evidence.

- source: Powers, S. T., Ekárt, A., & Lewis, P. R. (2018). Modelling enduring institutions: The complementarity of evolutionary and agent-based approaches. *Cognitive Systems Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that participants' choices in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma games are influenced by information about the other player's action and by individual personality traits. The disjunction effect (greater cooperation under uncertainty) is present but weaker than in previous studies and is not explained by a general bias to cooperate. Higher potential payoffs (high variability matrices) reduce the disjunction effect, suggesting more optimal responding when incentives are larger. Reward Responsiveness predicts more optimal (defecting) choices when the optimal strategy is to defect and the other player's action is known. Agreeableness predicts more cooperative choices when the optimal strategy is to cooperate. No evidence is found for a general bias toward cooperation, and the results are similar whether the task is framed as a social dilemma or a neutral card game. The study does not include punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes. Its findings on the influence of payoff variability and personality on cooperation/defection may be contextually informative for understanding individual decision-making in social dilemmas, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects or efficiency.
