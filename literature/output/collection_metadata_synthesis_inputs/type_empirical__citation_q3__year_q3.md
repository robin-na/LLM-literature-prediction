- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Gürerk, Lauer, T., & Scheuermann, M. (2018). Leadership with individual rewards and punishments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the addition of individual punishment or reward mechanisms (either centralized via a leader or decentralized among peers) leads to significantly higher contributions and higher net payoffs compared to the baseline game without such mechanisms. The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment is more effective than reward at sustaining high contributions over time, especially in the peer punishment treatment, where contributions even increase in the second half of the game. Net payoffs are highest in reward treatments (due to the reward mechanism's structure), but also higher in punishment treatments than in the baseline. The presence of a leader (who contributes first and can reward or punish) does not further increase contributions or payoffs compared to peer-based reward or punishment. The effect of punishment on increasing contributions and payoffs is robust, and punishment from leaders has a stronger effect on subsequent contributions than peer punishment. Anti-social punishment is less frequent when punishment is centralized with a leader.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, enabling individual punishment (with a cost/impact ratio of 1:3) increases both contributions and net payoffs (i.e., efficiency) compared to the baseline without punishment. The effect is robust whether punishment is centralized (leader) or decentralized (peers), and is not further enhanced by adding a leader who leads by example. The control (no punishment) efficiency is lower than in the punishment-enabled treatments. The findings support the prediction that, holding game design dimensions constant, enabling punishment will increase average efficiency. The specific cost and impact structure (1 point cost reduces target by 3 points) is important for generalization. Reward mechanisms also increase efficiency, but the structure of the reward mechanism in this experiment is payoff-increasing by design. The results are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar public goods game environments.

- source: Engl, F., Riedl, A., & Weber, R. (2021). Spillover Effects of Institutions on Cooperative Behavior, Preferences, and Beliefs. *American Economic Journal-microeconomics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Institutions that enforce cooperation in a public goods game (via a minimum contribution requirement, with full forfeiture for under-contribution) substantially increase group welfare in the regulated game and also increase welfare in an unregulated, otherwise identical game played by the same group. The direct effect on welfare is large, and the spillover effect is significant and economically meaningful (about 3-3.4 points on a 20-point endowment, or 30-40% of the direct effect). Both exogenous and endogenous institutions produce these effects, but the endogenous institution's spillover effect grows over time as the minimum requirement increases. The effect is mainly due to higher contributions among those already contributing, not by converting free riders. The positive effects on welfare and contributions persist after the institution is removed and extend to new partners. The mechanisms include increased beliefs about others' cooperativeness and stronger conditional cooperation preferences. No spillover is found to trust games. Heterogeneity exists: younger subjects and those with high positive reciprocity show smaller spillover effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a strong, central punishment institution (with full forfeiture for under-contribution) in a repeated linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, and MPCR 0.5. The control (no institution) game shows declining contributions and welfare over time, while the treatment (with punishment) achieves near-full cooperation and much higher welfare. Critically, the paper quantifies both the direct and spillover effects on group welfare, allowing for prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design. The results are robust across exogenous and endogenous institution types, though the time dynamics differ. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with similar group size, round structure, and punishment technology (centralized, all-or-nothing, no cost to punisher). The absence of spillover to trust games suggests limits to generalizability across domains. The evidence is strongest for games with continuous contribution choices, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Bruhin, A., Janizzi, K., & Thöni, C. (2020). Uncovering the heterogeneity behind cross-cultural variation in antisocial punishment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper shows that the effectiveness of peer punishment in public goods games is highly heterogeneous and depends critically on the composition of punishment types within groups. Using a finite mixture model, the authors classify subjects into three types: non-punishers (Type N), antisocial/free rider punishers (Type AF), and free rider-only punishers (Type F). The presence of Type AF subjects, who punish both low and high contributors, is strongly associated with lower group earnings and reduced efficiency. In contrast, groups with only Type F and Type N subjects achieve much higher efficiency. The number of Type AF subjects in a group explains a large share of the variance in group earnings, more than baseline cooperativeness or period effects. Cross-cultural differences in the prevalence of Type AF subjects explain much of the observed variation in the effectiveness of punishment across societies.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform: it depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishers (Type AF) in the group or population. In environments where antisocial punishment is common, enabling punishment can substantially reduce efficiency. In environments where only free rider punishment occurs, enabling punishment can increase or sustain efficiency. The paper provides quantitative evidence (e.g., groups with four Type AF subjects earn 68.7% less than groups with only Type F/N) and regression models showing that the number of Type AF subjects is a strong predictor of group earnings. Thus, for prediction, the cultural or group composition regarding punishment types is a critical moderator, and control game efficiency alone is insufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering this heterogeneity.

- source: Marcin, I., Robalo, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Institutional endogeneity and third-party punishment in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public good game with binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, groups could vote to introduce a third-party punishment institution. When punishment was enabled, a B-type player could assign costly deduction points to defectors (non-contributors), with each deduction point costing the punisher 1 point and reducing the defector's income by 3 points. The main experimental manipulation was whether the punishment institution was implemented endogenously (by majority vote) or exogenously (by random assignment, sometimes overruling the vote). The study finds that: (1) Punishment is significantly milder when the institution is endogenously chosen, but is more effective in inducing cooperation per unit of punishment; (2) Exogenous punishment is harsher, leading to similar or slightly higher cooperation in the long run, but at higher punishment cost; (3) Overall, group efficiency (total payoffs) is not significantly different between endogenous and exogenous punishment conditions; (4) The presence of punishment (vs. no punishment) increases cooperation and efficiency, but the method of institution selection does not further increase efficiency. The study provides direct evidence on efficiency, group payoff, and the moderating role of institutional endogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling third-party punishment in a public good game increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the way the punishment institution is implemented (endogenous vs. exogenous) does not significantly affect overall efficiency, even though it affects punishment severity and effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is a key driver of efficiency gains, but whether the institution is chosen by vote or imposed does not further increase group efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, binary contribution, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for mapping design to efficiency outcomes. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is lower than with punishment enabled, and the efficiency gain from punishment is robust to the method of institution selection.

- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

  findings: The experiment finds that, in a linear public goods game with peer punishment and heterogeneous returns, experimentally induced normative disagreement within groups does not reduce cooperation (measured by average contributions) or increase punishment. Instead, the average normative view within a group predicts the group's contribution level. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but notes that joint payoffs are maximized at full contribution, and that observed contribution levels are lower than this maximum, consistent with underprovision. The presence of punishment maintains cooperation at moderate levels, but does not eliminate the effect of group-mean norms. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of normative disagreement in a linear PGG with peer punishment, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, nor does it include a no-punishment control. For prediction tasks, it shows that, in a 3-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGG with punishment (cost 1, impact 3), with full information about others' contributions and payoffs but anonymous punishment, group-mean normative views are a strong predictor of contribution levels. The presence of punishment maintains moderate cooperation, but the paper does not allow direct estimation of the efficiency gain from enabling punishment versus disabling it. The results suggest that, under these parameters, punishment supports cooperation even in the presence of normative disagreement, but the efficiency effect relative to no-punishment is not measured.

- source: Engel, C., Kube, S., & Kurschilgen, M. (2021). Managing expectations: How selective information affects cooperation and punishment in social dilemma games. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

In environments where baseline cooperation is high, punishment is effective unless unfavorable information is provided, which undermines both cooperation and the effectiveness of punishment. In less cooperative environments, favorable information can help maintain cooperation and enhance the effect of pro-social punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the baseline expectations and informational context (not just structural game parameters) are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, predictions must be inferred from contribution data.
