- source: Nicklisch, A., & Wolff, I. (2011). COOPERATION NORMS IN MULTIPLE-STAGE PUNISHMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment implements a linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, and multiple endogenous punishment stages per round. Players can punish at a convex cost, and punishment reduces the target's payoff by 10% per point (up to 10 points). Two treatments are compared: BASIC (standard punishment) and OPINION (bystanders can approve/disapprove of punishment, but this has no direct payoff effect). The main findings are: (1) Average payoffs and efficiency remain above the Nash equilibrium and do not differ significantly between treatments or over time; (2) The opportunity for counter-punishment does not lead to a collapse in cooperation or efficiency; (3) Punishment is guided by an absolute full-contribution norm, and both punishers and bystanders apply similar standards; (4) Retaliation occurs but does not undermine overall efficiency; (5) The presence of bystander approval does not materially change efficiency or cooperation patterns. The results suggest that multi-stage punishment with changing groups can sustain cooperation and efficiency, even when counter-punishment is possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a four-player, ten-round linear PGG with continuous contributions, full information, and multi-stage punishment (with convex costs and 10% effectiveness per point), the introduction of punishment sustains or increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment Nash equilibrium. Efficiency remains stable and above the Nash level, and does not collapse even when counter-punishment is possible. The presence of bystander approval (OPINION treatment) does not significantly alter efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that in similar designs (with multi-stage punishment, convex costs, and changing groups), enabling punishment is likely to have a more positive effect on efficiency, and the risk of efficiency collapse due to counter-punishment is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in this class of games.

- source: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y. (2013). Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy. *Economic Record*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (b) In larger groups (n=8, 12), PPID yields the highest efficiency (80.60% and 78.05%), followed by AP (76.10% and 61.35%), with VCM lowest (68.60% and 63.30%). (c) In large groups, AP can reduce efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on group size and punishment institution. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A. (2016). Non-binding agreements in public goods experiments. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Punishment is used less frequently and more effectively in these settings, with little anti-social punishment. Punishment alone (without agreement) or with second-best agreements increases contributions but not payoffs, as punishment costs offset the gains. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

- source: Kamei, K. (2019). The power of joint decision-making in a finitely-repeated dilemma. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Joint decision-making in pairs, where members can communicate, leads to higher and more sustained efficiency (measured by average group payoff/contributions) than individual decision-making in finitely repeated two-player public goods games. This effect is strongest under partner matching (fixed pairs), where efficiency remains high until the end-game. Under stranger matching (random rematching), efficiency declines more rapidly, but is still higher than individual decision-making in early rounds if social distance is close. When compared to individual decision-making with informal punishment (where subjects can punish at a cost), joint decision-making achieves similar efficiency and average payoffs. The effect of joint decision-making is driven by communication and, to a lesser extent, by close social distance within pairs. Removing social distance (anonymous pairs) reduces efficiency, especially under stranger matching. The paper provides direct evidence on the efficiency impact of punishment institutions versus joint decision-making, and indirect evidence on the role of communication, matching protocol, and social distance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player finitely repeated public goods games, enabling informal punishment (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases efficiency to a level comparable to that achieved by joint decision-making in pairs with communication. The efficiency boost from punishment is robust under both partner and stranger matching, though the effect is stronger under partner matching. The design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency with punishment enabled are: player count (2), number of rounds (15), MPCR (0.8), punishment cost (0.5 per point), punishment tech (1.5 per point), and matching protocol (partner vs stranger). Communication within pairs (chat) is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially under partner matching. The paper's direct comparison of control (no punishment), punishment, and joint decision-making treatments makes it highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Rockenbach, B., & Wolff, I. (2016). Designing Institutions for Social Dilemmas. *German Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that when subjects are allowed to design institutions for a repeated public goods game, they initially favor punishment or redistribution mechanisms, but these are associated with lower efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) compared to rules that emphasize communication (framing, appeals) or participative elements (endogenous rule change). Over the course of the experiment, the use of punishment declines, and efficiency increases, suggesting that punishment is not an efficient solution in this context. Communication and participative rule features are positively associated with efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that punishment, as typically implemented by endogenous designers, tends to reduce efficiency, mainly due to its costs, and that designers learn to move away from it.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with endogenous institution design, enabling punishment mechanisms tends to reduce efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) baseline, especially when punishment is costly and not peer-administered. The effect is robust across multiple rounds and rule sets, with efficiency increasing as designers move away from punishment toward communication and participative mechanisms. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment (especially costly, centralized, or automatic forms) is likely to result in lower efficiency than the control, unless the punishment is highly refined or replaced by redistribution mechanisms with lower waste. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG environments.

- source: Dutta, R., Levine, D. K., & Modica, S. (2021). The whip and the Bible: Punishment versus internalization. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms leads to a large increase in group efficiency and welfare, as measured by group payoff and normalized utility. The model, calibrated to Fehr & Gächter (2000), matches the observed jump in average contributions and welfare when punishment is enabled. The theory explains that punishment is a costly but effective backstop to internalized norms, and that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the fraction of internalizers and the punishment technology. The paper also shows that, in the lab, the efficiency benefit of punishment is realized only after sufficient rounds for learning, and that the effect of punishment is robust across a range of parameter calibrations. The model further predicts that the relationship between societal value of cooperation and punishment is non-monotonic in the lab, but always positive in the field.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and quantitative evidence that, in standard public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and welfare relative to the no-punishment baseline. The model is directly calibrated to the Fehr & Gächter (2000) design (4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, specific punishment cost/tech), and matches the observed efficiency increase. The findings support the prediction that, for similar game designs, the efficiency with punishment enabled will be substantially higher than in the control. The paper also provides a framework for interpreting how design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, internalization) moderate the effect, though most direct evidence is for the canonical 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGG. The results are most directly applicable to lab PGGs with similar parameters, and less so to games with very different structures or field settings.

- source: Hwang, S. H. (2017). Conflict technology in cooperation: The group size paradox revisited. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that coordinated punishment in public goods games can lead to a concentration effect, making it easier to sustain cooperation and achieve high group efficiency as group size increases. When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run. This result is robust to several variations, including the presence of unconditional punishment costs and some decay in coordination as group size increases. The model provides a theoretical explanation for empirical findings that larger groups can sometimes achieve higher cooperation and efficiency, contrary to standard free-rider logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.

- source: DeAngelo, G., & Gee, L. K. (2020). Peers or police?: The effect of choice and type of monitoring in the provision of public goods. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment uses a 2x2 design to compare exogenous vs. endogenous monitoring and peer vs. group monitoring in a linear public goods game with punishment. When monitoring is exogenously imposed, both peer and group monitoring yield similar public good provision and net earnings. When monitoring is endogenously chosen, group monitoring collapses (due to free riders refusing to pay for monitoring), leading to a 44% drop in public good provision, but net earnings are not significantly lower because less is spent on punishment. Peer monitoring, however, is robust to endogenization: public good provision and net earnings remain high, and willingness-to-pay for monitoring is not strongly correlated with contribution level. The results are robust to changes in the punishment mechanism and the voting rule for group monitoring. The key implication is that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on the monitoring regime and whether monitoring is exogenous or endogenous.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the impact depends on the monitoring regime and whether monitoring is exogenously imposed or endogenously chosen. If monitoring is exogenous (automatic), punishment increases or maintains efficiency (via higher public good provision) regardless of whether monitoring is peer-based or group-based. If monitoring is endogenous and requires group consensus, punishment may fail to improve efficiency because monitoring is not implemented (especially in the presence of free riders), leading to low public good provision but not necessarily lower net earnings due to reduced punishment costs. Peer monitoring is robust to endogenization: punishment maintains efficiency even when monitoring is endogenous. Thus, when using game design dimensions to predict treatment efficiency, it is crucial to account for whether monitoring is exogenous or endogenous and the type of monitoring (peer vs. group). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper provides sufficient data to estimate the relative effect of punishment under different monitoring regimes.

- source: Chang, H. I., Dawes, C. T., & Johnson, T. (2018). POLITICAL INEQUALITY, CENTRALIZED SANCTIONING INSTITUTIONS, AND THE MAINTENANCE OF PUBLIC GOODS. *Bulletin of Economic Research*.

The findings suggest that the way punishment institutions are selected (random vs. democratic) may not matter for efficiency, unless political inequality is tied to economic wealth, which can reduce contributions. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment should be coded as increasing efficiency, but the selection mechanism (random vs. democratic) should not be expected to matter unless wealth-based voting is used.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.
