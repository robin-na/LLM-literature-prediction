- source: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C. (2014). Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.

- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

- source: Bell, A., Zhang, W., & Nou, K. (2016). Pesticide use and cooperative management of natural enemy habitat in a framed field experiment. *Agricultural Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot public goods game with three treatments: (1) Base (no punishment), (2) UCPun (conditional decision maker can be punished by an unconditional decision maker), and (3) CPun (conditional decision maker can punish an unconditional decision maker). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), but does lead to more sensitive punishment behavior. Responsibility effects (increased cooperation when given punishment power) are stronger than substitution effects. The overall effect of punishment on cooperation is positive, but the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment mechanisms affect conditional cooperation in a one-shot public goods game with four players, MPCR 0.4, and costless punishment. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings suggest that enabling punishment increases the responsiveness of cooperators to others' contributions and may increase overall cooperation, but also increases free riding. For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is likely to increase cooperation-related behaviors, but the net effect on efficiency is ambiguous due to the rise in free riders. The absence of direct efficiency analysis limits the ability to quantitatively predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency, but the behavioral mechanisms identified are informative for modeling.

- source: Hurlstone, M. J., Wang, S., Price, A., Leviston, Z., & Walker, I. (2017). Cooperation studies of catastrophe avoidance: implications for climate negotiations. *Climatic Change*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in collective-risk social dilemma games is strongly affected by perceived risk, inequality, uncertainty about thresholds, intergenerational discounting, and the possibility of reputation-based rewards or punishments. High perceived risk, communication (pledges), intermediate targets, and reputation mechanisms can increase cooperation (i.e., the proportion of groups that avert catastrophe). Inequality and uncertainty about the threshold for catastrophe reduce cooperation. The paper discusses the need for credible enforcement mechanisms (punishment) in real-world climate agreements but does not review experiments that directly implement or vary punishment in the game. The main findings are about behavioral cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the importance of enforcement (punishment) mechanisms for sustaining cooperation in public-goods-like threshold games, but does not provide direct experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff. It is useful for understanding moderators of cooperation and the conceptual role of punishment, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that credible punishment mechanisms are likely to be important, especially under uncertainty, but does not provide data or effect sizes.

- source: Jia, C., Zhang, R. X., & Wang, D. (2023). Evolutionary game of cooperative behavior among social capitals in PPP projects: A complex network perspective. *Ain Shams Engineering Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that increasing the default penalty (punishment) leads to higher rates of cooperation among social capitals in a networked prisoner's dilemma setting. Higher network density reduces cooperation, and initial cooperation probability can promote cooperation only when the temptation to defect (betrayal income) is not too high. The results are based on simulation of evolutionary dynamics, not empirical data. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that cooperation is important for the efficiency of PPP projects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that introducing or increasing punishment in a networked prisoner's dilemma can increase cooperation rates, which the authors argue is beneficial for project efficiency. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report results in terms of efficiency ratios. The findings are most relevant for understanding the behavioral mechanism (cooperation) rather than direct prediction of efficiency outcomes. The model's focus on network structure, punishment, and cooperation may inform qualitative expectations about the direction of efficiency effects when punishment is enabled, but does not provide direct predictive data for the downstream task.

- source: Li, H. Y. (2017). Evolutionary Game Analysis of Emergency Management of the Middle Route of South-to-North Water Diversion Project. *Water Resources Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: Theoretical and numerical analysis show that: (1) Local governments are more likely to show strong willingness to implement emergency policies if the profit from doing so exceeds the increased cost; (2) To avoid all local governments selecting weak cooperation, the central authority (MWR) should increase penalties for weak willingness, making the penalty greater than the cost saved by weak cooperation. The model demonstrates how the strength of punishment and the cost/benefit structure affect the evolution of cooperation, but does not directly quantify efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the idea that increasing punishment (penalties) for non-cooperation can increase the willingness to cooperate in multi-agent settings similar to public goods games. However, it does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report empirical results. The findings are most useful for understanding the mechanism by which punishment can shift equilibrium behavior toward greater cooperation, which may be associated with higher efficiency in analogous settings. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that higher punishment effectiveness and costliness of non-cooperation can promote cooperative equilibria, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency change.

- source: Sripada, C. S. (2005). Punishment and the strategic structure of moral systems. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is a more general and empirically plausible mechanism for sustaining compliance with moral norms than reciprocity. Punishment can support cooperation in large groups and can enforce arbitrary rules, not just those with a cooperative payoff structure. The author reviews theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and ethnographic/experimental evidence to support the claim that punishment, including direct, exclusion-based, and reputation-based forms, is universal in human groups. The paper also discusses the problem of stabilizing costly punishment and the implications for understanding moral diversity. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental results on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the importance of punishment mechanisms in sustaining cooperation and compliance in public-goods-like environments, especially in large groups. It suggests that, in theory, enabling punishment should increase compliance and cooperation, and thus could improve efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes. The arguments are useful for understanding why punishment may be effective, but the paper does not supply empirical data or parameterized results that can be directly used for predicting efficiency changes in experimental public goods games. Its value is mainly in supporting the general plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation and norm compliance, especially in large groups.

- source: Nakao, H., & Machery, E. (2012). The evolution of punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is widespread in nature and often evolves for reasons other than inducing behavioral change in the punishee. In many cases (especially among plants and insects), punishment is a loss-cutting or cost-imposing strategy, not a behavior-modification strategy. Even among vertebrates, where behavioral modification is more common, the evolutionary benefits of punishment often do not depend on the punishee changing its behavior. The paper challenges the view that punishment primarily evolved to promote cooperation or efficiency, and instead highlights alternative evolutionary pathways.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative data relevant to predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does, however, offer theoretical context suggesting that the evolutionary function of punishment may not always be to increase cooperation or group efficiency, but could instead be to reduce harm to the punisher or its kin. For the prediction task, this paper suggests caution in assuming that punishment mechanisms will always improve efficiency or cooperation, especially in settings where behavioral modification is unlikely or where punishment serves other evolutionary functions. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental data to calibrate such predictions.

- source: Zhang, Y., & van der Schaar, M. (2013). Robust Reputation Protocol Design for Online Communities: A Stochastic Stability Analysis. *Ieee Journal of Selected Topics in Signal Processing*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that in a repeated random matching game (asymmetric gift-giving, not a standard PGG), a reputation protocol with probabilistic punishment (parameterized by punishment probability beta) can induce full cooperation and achieve maximal efficiency (social welfare = b-c) in the long run, provided the discount factor and punishment strength are within certain bounds. If punishment is too weak or too strong, efficiency is lower. Theoretical analysis is supported by numerical simulations showing that the optimal protocol achieves much higher average social welfare than direct reciprocity (tit-for-tat) or less robust reputation schemes. The protocol's design is robust to stochastic perturbations and ensures convergence to the efficient equilibrium.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated random matching environments with binary contribution (gift-giving), the introduction of a well-calibrated reputation-based punishment protocol can move the system from zero efficiency (no cooperation) to full efficiency (all cooperate, social welfare maximized). The effect is robust to noise and initial conditions, provided the punishment probability is neither too low nor too high. While the game is not a standard PGG, the adjacent structure and explicit efficiency analysis make the results highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar environments. The findings suggest that, when punishment is enabled via a reputation protocol and properly tuned, efficiency can increase dramatically compared to the no-punishment baseline.

- source: Li, J. M., & Jiang, S. S. (2023). How can governance strategies be developed for marine ecological environment pollution caused by sea-using enterprises?-A study based on evolutionary game theory. *Ocean & Coastal Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in the absence of strong governance mechanisms (punishment, compensation, or high ecological performance appraisal), all parties tend to adopt minimal effort strategies, resulting in low efficiency and poor environmental outcomes (prisoner's dilemma). Introducing punishment (penalties for non-compliance, ecological damage compensation), increasing the weight of ecological indicators in local government performance appraisal, and combining these tools can shift the system to a more efficient equilibrium, where local governments and enterprises fully implement and comply with environmental policies, even if central government supervision is weak. The effect of any single tool is limited, but their combination is necessary to achieve high efficiency in governance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and strength of punishment mechanisms (penalties, compensation for damage) and reward mechanisms (performance appraisal) are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from introducing punishment in public-goods-like governance settings. The results suggest that, in adjacent multi-agent settings, punishment alone may not be sufficient, but when combined with other governance tools, it can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction tasks, the presence and magnitude of punishment, reward, and their combination should be considered as key moderators of treatment efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to weak incentives.

- source: Mameli, M. (2013). Meat made us moral: a hypothesis on the nature and evolution of moral judgment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper argues that the evolution of moral judgment is rooted in emotional dispositions that support the enforcement of group-beneficial norms through punishment and meta-punishment. It draws on ethnographic and evolutionary accounts of hunter-gatherer societies, where collective punishment of cheaters and bullies (those who disrupt cooperation and resource sharing) is common and is enforced through both direct and reputational means (e.g., gossip, ostracism, disapproval). The author suggests that these mechanisms enabled stable cooperation in small groups, but the analysis is conceptual and evolutionary rather than empirical or quantitative. The paper does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment and meta-punishment mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in small, egalitarian groups, especially in contexts analogous to public goods dilemmas (e.g., meat sharing after cooperative hunting). However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff outcomes with or without punishment. Its relevance is mainly in supporting the plausibility of punishment as a stabilizer of cooperation, especially when punishment is collective, reputational, and embedded in group norms. For prediction tasks, this paper may inform the expected direction of punishment effects in small, highly interactive, reputation-sensitive groups, but it does not provide quantitative or design-specific guidance.

- source: Brevers, D., Noël, X., Ermer, E., Dabiri, D., Verbanck, P., & Kornreich, C. (2013). Unfairness sensitivity and social decision-making in individuals with alcohol dependence: A preliminary study. *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*.

The Ultimatum Game is adjacent to the target environment, as rejection of unfair offers can be interpreted as a form of costly punishment, but the context is one-shot, dyadic, and does not involve group cooperation or repeated interactions. The findings may inform understanding of individual differences in punishment sensitivity, but do not provide evidence on efficiency or the effect of punishment mechanisms in public goods games.

- source: Sterelny, K. (2016). Cooperation, Culture, and Conflict. *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper argues that human cooperation evolved in two major transitions: first, among Pleistocene foragers, where cooperation was individually advantageous and stabilized by mutualism, reciprocation, and punishment of freeriders (both shirkers and bullies); and second, in the Holocene, where larger, more hierarchical societies required new mechanisms (including group selection and intergroup conflict) to maintain cooperation. Punishment is discussed as a key mechanism for controlling freeriding and maintaining cooperation, but the analysis is conceptual and evolutionary rather than experimental or quantitative. The paper references experimental public goods games and their findings (e.g., punishment stabilizes cooperation), but only as background for theoretical arguments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and norm enforcement may affect cooperation and, by extension, efficiency in public-goods-like environments. It does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence that can be directly used to predict efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions. However, it supports the idea that punishment is more likely to stabilize cooperation (and thus efficiency) in small, transparent, repeated-interaction groups, and that the effectiveness of punishment may depend on social structure, group size, and transparency. For prediction tasks, this paper is best used as background for mechanism hypotheses rather than as a source of direct predictive evidence.

- source: Fagundes, D. (2017). The Social Norms of Waiting in Line. *Law and Social Inquiry-journal of the American Bar Foundation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that the stability of waiting lines among strangers is best explained by the human tendency toward strong reciprocity: people are inclined to cooperate when they see others doing so and to punish norm violators, even at personal cost. It draws on public goods game experiments to support this model, noting that people will punish defectors even when it is costly. The paper describes how informal sanctions (verbal objections, shaming, and sometimes violence) deter line-cutting and maintain order. It also discusses cultural and contextual variation in queue norms, exceptions to the rules, and the role of service providers and technology. However, the paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency or group payoffs, nor does it manipulate or systematically analyze game design dimensions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for the role of punishment and social norms in sustaining cooperation in environments analogous to public goods games, such as physical queues. It supports the idea that informal sanctions can deter defection and promote norm compliance, even among strangers. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar environments. For the downstream prediction task, this paper may inform mechanistic reasoning about why punishment can sustain cooperation, but it does not provide empirical data or parameter estimates relevant to predicting efficiency outcomes as a function of game design dimensions.
