- source: Wolff, I. (2012). Retaliation and the role for punishment in the evolution of cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with punishment and the possibility of retaliation, punishment can help induce partially cooperative equilibria and temporarily increase cooperation and group payoff, especially when mutation is present. However, punishment cannot sustain full cooperation in the presence of even small mutation rates, as the costs of punishment and the threat of retaliation allow non-punishing cooperators and defectors to invade. The efficiency gains from punishment are thus limited and parameter-dependent: punishment is most effective at intermediate continuation probabilities and when retaliation is not too strong. At high continuation probabilities or when retaliation is easy, punishment is driven out and cannot maintain high efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in repeated public goods games is highly sensitive to the possibility of retaliation and the evolutionary dynamics of strategy mutation. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, one should consider whether retaliation is possible, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the continuation probability (analogous to number of rounds or game length), and the presence of mutation or experimentation. Punishment can increase efficiency in some cases, but its effect is undermined by retaliation and high continuation probability. The model suggests that punishment is most likely to improve efficiency when it is effective (high punishment impact per cost), retaliation is costly or rare, and the game is not too long. If retaliation is easy and the game is long, punishment may not improve and may even reduce efficiency.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

- source: Wang, X. J., & Lv, S. J. (2019). The public goods game with shared punishment cost in well-mixed and structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment with shared cost among cooperators in a public goods game can promote and stabilize cooperation, thereby increasing group efficiency and average payoff, provided the penalty imposed on defectors is large enough compared to the punishment cost. In well-mixed populations, this can transform the game from one dominated by defection to one where full cooperation is stable. In structured populations, cooperation can emerge at lower fine thresholds and coexist with defection, but as the penalty increases, well-mixed populations become more favorable for cooperation than structured ones. The effect of punishment is thus generally positive for efficiency, but the benefit depends on the relative size of the fine, the punishment cost, and the group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment (with shared cost among cooperators) increases efficiency and group payoff when the fine is sufficiently large relative to the cost. The effect is modulated by group size (player_count), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech, i.e., fine per cost). Structured populations (networked interactions) can allow cooperation to emerge at lower fine thresholds, but as the fine increases, well-mixed populations yield higher efficiency. The findings support the prediction that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective and not too costly. The model is theoretical, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted as qualitative guidance.

- source: Chen, T., Hu, X. Z., Wang, Y. J., & Wang, L. (2018). The effect of heterogeneous defectors on the evolution of public cooperation. *European Physical Journal B*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment mechanisms in PGGs with heterogeneous defectors can significantly increase the sum of contributions (i.e., promote cooperation), especially when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to punishers. The effect is strongest when the loss to defectors is double the cost to punishers. If the punishment is too costly for punishers relative to the loss inflicted, the mechanism fails to promote cooperation. The network structure also matters: small-world and scale-free networks allow cooperation to emerge more easily with lower punishment costs. The main outcome is the sum of contributions, not efficiency or total group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that punishment can increase group contributions in PGG-like environments, especially when the punishment is effective (defector loss >= punisher cost) and when the network structure facilitates information flow. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or total group payoff, its findings should be used as indirect support for the prediction task. The results suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment with sufficient effectiveness (high punishment impact per cost) is likely to increase group contributions, and thus may increase efficiency, especially in networked settings. The effect is sensitive to the relative cost and impact of punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that in community-structured populations playing a linear PGG with punishment, the presence of group competition (where entire communities can replace each other based on average fitness) or fitness-based migration (where individuals are more likely to migrate if they are fitter) can sustain high levels of cooperation and punishment, suppressing defection in parameter regions where defection would otherwise dominate. Constant (random) migration does not have this effect. The transition between cooperative and defective regimes is sharper with migration, but only group competition and fitness-based migration expand the parameter space where cooperation and punishment are stable. The main reported outcomes are the frequencies of strategies, not efficiency or group payoff, but higher frequencies of cooperation and punishment imply higher group payoffs relative to defection-dominated regimes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task: in PGG-like environments with punishment, the presence of group competition or fitness-based migration mechanisms can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation and punishment, and thus are likely to increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control games without punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is based on the frequencies of cooperative and punishing strategies. The results suggest that, for the same game design dimensions, enabling punishment in a context with group competition or fitness-based migration will likely lead to higher efficiency than in well-mixed or constant migration settings. The findings are most relevant for models with large group sizes, high mutation and migration rates, and community structure.

  findings: The paper finds that contagious punishment (grim trigger strategies) in repeated random-matching social dilemmas can sustain full cooperation (i.e., maximal efficiency) if players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor), the cost of cooperation is not too high, and monitoring is sufficiently broad. The results suggest that, in environments with strong punishment and sufficient monitoring, enabling punishment should increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment), provided the other parameters (patience, cost) are favorable. The paper also provides explicit formulas for the parameter thresholds needed to sustain cooperation, which could be used to predict when punishment will be effective in raising efficiency. However, the results are theoretical and apply to repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-type games, not public goods games per se, though the logic is closely related.
