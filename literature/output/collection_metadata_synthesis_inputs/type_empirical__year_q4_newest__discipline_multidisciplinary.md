- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., Gross, J., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., van Dijk, E., & de Dreu, C. K. W. (2023). Discriminatory punishment undermines the enforcement of group cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

- source: Nakagawa, M., Lefebvre, M., & Stenger, A. (2022). Long-lasting effects of incentives and social preference: A public goods experiment. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that all three incentive mechanisms (monetary punishment, non-monetary reward, recommendation) significantly increase contributions to the public good during the policy phase. Monetary punishment is most effective in raising contributions, but this effect is not persistent: contributions drop sharply after the punishment phase ends. Non-monetary reward and recommendation also increase contributions, with some evidence of longer-lasting effects, especially for recommendation among certain types (e.g., free-riders). However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The study also finds that the effect of incentives varies by social preference type: monetary punishment increases contributions across all types, while non-monetary reward and recommendation mainly affect strong conditional cooperators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling monetary punishment in a repeated linear PGG increases contribution rates during the intervention, but the effect is not persistent after removal. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency outcomes is not possible. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified and match standard PGG lab experiments. The findings support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) during the intervention, but the lack of payoff/efficiency data means the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred. The results are most useful for predicting contribution rate changes, not efficiency per se.

  findings: The study finds no significant difference in PGG contribution or punishment behavior between laboratory and online settings among Japanese university students. The introduction of punishment does not significantly increase average contributions or maintain higher cooperation levels, though it may slow the decay of cooperation over rounds. In the online-worker sample, cooperation is lower and the effect of punishment is even weaker. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), not efficiency or group payoff. The paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely weak or negligible in these settings, especially with a punishment efficiency of 2 (punishment impact per unit cost).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with a punishment efficiency of 2, enabling punishment does not meaningfully increase average group cooperation or, by implication, efficiency, in either laboratory or online settings among Japanese university students. In a Japanese online-worker sample, punishment may even be associated with lower cooperation. The absence of direct efficiency or group payoff data limits quantitative prediction, but the behavioral results suggest that, under these design parameters, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is likely minimal or absent. The study also highlights the importance of sample heterogeneity and the specific punishment parameters (cost and effectiveness) in moderating punishment effects.

- source: Posten, A. C., Ugurlar, P., Kube, S., & Lammers, J. (2025). Maintaining cooperation through vertical communication of trust when removing sanctions. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that when punishment institutions are removed from a public goods game, contributions (i.e., cooperation) typically drop sharply. However, if the authority communicates trust in participants' willingness to cooperate, this drop is substantially reduced, and contribution levels remain similar to those observed when punishment is still in place. This effect is observed for centralized, third-party, and peer punishment mechanisms. The effect of vertical trust communication persists beyond a single round and is mediated by increased trust in coplayers. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined, but the findings strongly suggest that vertical trust communication can substitute for punishment in maintaining cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the removal of punishment institutions in public goods games leads to a sharp decline in cooperation (contributions), but that this decline can be prevented if the authority communicates trust in participants' willingness to cooperate. While the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, the close link between contribution rates and efficiency in standard PGGs suggests that vertical trust communication could maintain efficiency at levels similar to those with punishment, at least in the short term. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence or absence of vertical trust communication is a critical moderator when punishment is removed, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the main outcome is contribution, not efficiency.

Time pressure reduces decision times but does not shift the frequency of punishment, cooperation, or defection.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the behavioral frequency and timing of punishment in repeated network PGGs, but does not report or analyze efficiency or group payoff as a function of punishment. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. It does, however, confirm that punishment is rare and not easily suppressed by time pressure in this design, which may be indirectly relevant for predicting the magnitude of any efficiency effect (i.e., if punishment is rare, its effect on efficiency may be limited).
