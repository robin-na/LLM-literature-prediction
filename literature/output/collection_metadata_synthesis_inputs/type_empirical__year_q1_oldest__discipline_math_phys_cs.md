- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Davis, D. D., & Holt, C. A. (1999). Equilibrium cooperation in two-stage games: Experimental evidence. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the use of contingent punishment/reward strategies in the second stage of a two-stage game is strongly associated with higher rates of cooperation in the first stage. This effect is robust across different treatments, including those where the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium or is Pareto dominated. The structure of the second-stage equilibria does not reliably predict cooperation rates. Instead, initial cohort propensities and first-stage payoff structure are more important. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability and use of punishment/reward strategies can increase cooperation rates in two-stage games, even when the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The findings suggest that behavioral responses to punishment opportunities are important, but the translation to efficiency outcomes is not addressed. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can support cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Falk, A., Fehr, E., & Fischbacher, U. (2005). Driving forces behind informal sanctions. *Econometrica*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a three-player one-shot PD with punishment, cooperators predominantly punish defectors, and this pattern is robust across different punishment effectiveness conditions and elicitation methods. Defectors also punish, but their punishment is more likely to be spiteful and disappears when punishment cannot increase payoff differences. The cost-effectiveness of punishment is manipulated, showing that cooperators are willing to spend more to punish defectors even when punishment is less effective. The results challenge fairness theories that focus only on group-level payoffs or inequality aversion, supporting instead a model where retaliation against unfairness is central. However, the paper does not report on group efficiency or total welfare outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral and motivational aspects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on the behavioral patterns and motives of punishment in a three-player PD with punishment, including how punishment is targeted and how its cost-effectiveness affects behavior. However, it does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that punishment is frequently used by cooperators against defectors, and that the willingness to punish persists even when it is costly, which may imply potential for efficiency improvements in similar games, but this is not tested or quantified here. The design details (player count, one-shot, punishment cost/effectiveness) are well specified and can inform mechanistic expectations, but not direct efficiency predictions.

- source: Chen, M. L. (2012). The Effect of Leader Reward and Punishment Behaviors on Subordinates' Budget Reports. *Engineering Economist*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that hands-off (no reward or punishment) is least effective in reducing budgetary slack. Punishment alone reduces slack more than reward alone, but the combination of reward and punishment (carrot-stick) is most effective. The effect of punishment is stronger when the punishment is large relative to the benefit of slack creation. Rewards alone, regardless of size, have little effect. Requiring a factual assertion (honesty) also reduces slack. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (slack/honesty).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment, especially when combined with reward, reduces dishonest behavior (budgetary slack) in a principal-agent reporting game. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes in public goods games. The design is adjacent to PGGs, as it is a one-superior/one-subordinate budget reporting game, not a multi-player public goods game. The findings are most useful for understanding the behavioral impact of punishment and reward on honesty/slack, but less so for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Goette, L., Huffman, D., Meier, S., & Sutter, M. (2012). Competition Between Organizational Groups: Its Impact on Altruistic and Antisocial Motivations. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a neutral environment, punishment is used prosocially to enforce cooperation, especially when the victim of defection is an in-group member. When competition between groups is introduced, punishment becomes antisocial: out-group members are punished more, regardless of whether they cooperate or defect. Competition increases in-group cooperation but does not reduce out-group cooperation. The paper does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the authors argue that antisocial punishment under competition is wasteful and could reduce overall efficiency, while increased in-group cooperation could enhance it. The net effect on efficiency is not measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of intergroup competition can fundamentally change the function of punishment in social dilemma games: from norm enforcement to antisocial, destructive punishment. While the design is not a standard repeated public goods game, the findings are highly relevant for predicting when punishment will be efficiency-enhancing versus efficiency-reducing. Specifically, in environments with salient group competition, enabling punishment may lead to resource-destroying antisocial punishment, undermining the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data means the findings should be used as qualitative guidance rather than quantitative prediction.

- source: Ashlock, D., Smucker, M. D., Stanley, E. A., & Tesfatsion, L. (1996). Preferential partner selection in an evolutionary study of Prisoner's Dilemma. *Biosystems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that, in dyadic repeated Dictator Game settings, observers rate those who withdraw cooperation from defectors as more cooperative and less vengeful than those who punish. Both withdrawers and punishers are seen as less exploitable than non-sanctioners, but only when the sanctioner ends up with a positive payoff. Punishers are least preferred as future partners. The reputational consequences of punishment are negative compared to withdrawal, and the effect on perceived exploitability depends on whether the sanctioner retains a positive payoff. No actual efficiency or group payoff is measured, so the findings are about perceptions and reputational inferences, not realized payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar environments. Its findings are about reputational perceptions in dyadic games, not about actual payoffs or efficiency. However, it suggests that punishment may have reputational costs compared to withdrawal of cooperation, and that the effect of sanctions on perceived exploitability depends on whether the sanctioner retains a positive payoff. For prediction tasks about efficiency in public goods games, this paper is only weakly informative, as it does not measure efficiency or group payoff, and the game structure is dyadic, not group-based.

- source: Brown, M., Falk, A., & Fehr, E. (2004). Relational contracts and the nature of market interactions. *Econometrica*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The absence of third-party enforcement leads to the endogenous formation of long-term firm-worker relationships, where contract renewal is contingent on high effort. This informal punishment mechanism (firing/termination) disciplines workers and results in higher effort, higher wages, and much higher efficiency and surplus compared to settings where such relationships are not possible. The effect is robust: both firms and workers earn more in long-term relationships, and the distribution of surplus is more equal. When long-term relationships are ruled out (random rematching), effort and efficiency drop sharply, and minimal effort becomes common. When third-party enforcement is available, the market behaves competitively, with low wages, low rent sharing, and little incentive for long-term relationships. The findings show that informal, endogenous punishment (via contract renewal/firing) can substitute for formal enforcement and substantially improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated market-like environments with incomplete contracts, the ability to endogenously form long-term relationships and use contract renewal/firing as a sanction leads to much higher efficiency and surplus than settings where such informal punishment is not possible. For prediction tasks, the presence of repeated interactions, the ability to condition future play on past behavior, and the absence of third-party enforcement are key moderators of punishment's positive effect on efficiency. The findings suggest that, in environments where informal punishment via relationship termination is feasible, efficiency can approach the level seen with formal enforcement, provided that agents can build reputations and relationships. The results are most directly relevant to labor market and relational contract settings, but the underlying mechanism is adjacent to public goods games with endogenous partner choice and repeated play.

- source: Oberholzer-Gee, F., Waldfogel, J., & White, M. W. (2010). FRIEND OR FOE? COOPERATION AND LEARNING IN HIGH-STAKES GAMES. *Review of Economics and Statistics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study documents that, over two seasons of the game show, players shift from unconditional to conditional cooperation based on observable opponent characteristics (e.g., gender, race, age). In the first season, cooperation rates are unrelated to opponent attributes; in the second season, players are more likely to defect (choose 'foe') against opponents whose attributes predict less cooperation. This learning process leads to a substantial decline in overall efficiency: the average share of stakes converted to winnings drops from 39% in season 1 to 30% in season 2. The decline is especially pronounced for players with attributes associated with low cooperation (e.g., men, black players, younger players), whose average efficiency falls sharply. In contrast, players with more cooperative-associated attributes (e.g., women, white players) maintain their efficiency. The findings suggest that conditional cooperation, when based on imperfect signals, can reduce group efficiency in one-shot, high-stakes settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, simultaneous-move games structurally similar to the prisoner's dilemma, the emergence of conditional cooperation based on observable attributes can reduce overall efficiency, especially when players use imperfect signals to predict others' behavior. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling mechanisms that allow players to condition on opponent characteristics (e.g., through history, reputation, or observable traits) may decrease efficiency relative to baseline, particularly if the environment is not anonymous and players can stereotype. The findings are most relevant for games with binary (all-or-nothing) choices, no explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and high stakes. The results caution that learning and conditional strategies do not always improve efficiency and may exacerbate payoff inequality.

- source: Chen, M. K., & Hauser, M. (2005). Modeling reciprocation and cooperation in primates: evidence for a punishing strategy. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that tamarins' cooperative behavior in repeated food-exchange games is best described by 'punish-2' and 'punish-3' strategies, which are more punishing than standard tit-for-tat. These strategies require two or more consecutive cooperative acts to restart cooperation after a breakdown, leading to stable but sometimes brittle cooperation. The analysis uses maximum-likelihood fitting of a suite of behavioral models, showing that these punishing strategies account for over 90% of the posterior model weight. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, and all results are based on behavioral patterns (pulling rates and model fits).
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. However, it does provide indirect evidence that more punishing strategies (requiring multiple acts of cooperation to restore cooperation) can stabilize cooperation in repeated dyadic interactions. The findings may inform mechanistic expectations about how punishment-like strategies affect cooperation dynamics, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency changes due to punishment in PGGs. The game is not a standard PGG, and the outcomes are not payoff-based.
