- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Grund, C., Harbring, C., Thommes, K., & Tilkes, K. R. (2020). Decisions on Extending Group Membership-Evidence from a Public Good Experiment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that temporary group members contribute marginally more when the possibility of group membership extension is decided endogenously by a group member, compared to an exogenous random draw. However, this effect is weak and does not translate into significantly higher overall group contributions. Permanent members' contributions are not significantly affected by the extension mechanism. When a temporary member is replaced (especially exogenously), the newcomer initially contributes more than other group members, but this effect diminishes quickly. The study discusses monetary incentives and payoffs, but the main focus is on contribution behavior rather than efficiency or group payoff. There is no direct analysis of how the extension or replacement mechanisms affect group efficiency relative to the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that mechanisms resembling social sanctions (replacement or exclusion) can have incentive effects on contribution behavior, but these effects are modest and short-lived. The design does not include explicit costly punishment or reward mechanisms, and efficiency is not directly measured. For prediction, the results suggest that changing the group membership mechanism (endogenous vs. exogenous extension) has limited impact on overall group efficiency, and that replacement (as a form of exclusion) may temporarily boost newcomer contributions. However, these effects are primarily on contribution rates, not on efficiency or total group payoff. The study is most informative for understanding how exclusion or replacement (as a weak form of sanction) affects cooperation, rather than for predicting the efficiency impact of explicit punishment mechanisms.

- source: Nax, H. H., Balietti, S., Murphy, R. O., & Helbing, D. (2018). Adding noise to the institution: an experimental welfare investigation of the contribution-based grouping mechanism. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that, in a repeated public goods game with 16 players (4 groups of 4), introducing a contribution-based grouping mechanism (meritocratic matching) with low or moderate noise leads to much higher efficiency (average total payoff) than random matching (no meritocracy). The efficiency gains are robust across different levels of noise, with the highest efficiency observed under perfect meritocracy (no noise), but even substantial gains at moderate noise levels. The control (no meritocracy, i.e., random matching) produces the lowest efficiency, consistent with standard PGG decay. The study also finds that increased meritocracy reduces payoff inequality, contrary to theoretical expectations. The results are robust to different measures of inequality and are observed both in lab and online settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with grouping based on contributions, increasing the precision of the grouping mechanism (i.e., reducing noise in meritocratic matching) leads to large increases in efficiency relative to random matching. For prediction tasks, the presence of a contribution-based grouping mechanism (even if noisy) should be expected to substantially increase efficiency compared to standard PGG controls. The effect is robust across a range of noise levels, and the efficiency gains are not offset by increased inequality. However, the study does not involve punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so its direct applicability to punishment-enabled games is limited. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of sorting/grouping mechanisms, not punishment.

- source: Kanitsar, G. (2021). Self-Governance in Generalized Exchange. A Laboratory Experiment on the Structural Embeddedness of Peer Punishment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Peer punishment increases cooperation and group earnings (efficiency) in public good structures only when punishment is costless; costly punishment does not improve efficiency. In generalized exchange, peer punishment (both costly and costless) does not increase cooperation or group earnings. The effectiveness of punishment is thus highly contingent on the network structure: dense sanctioning networks (public good) allow punishment to deter defection and improve efficiency, while sparse networks (generalized exchange) do not. The cost-impact ratio of punishment is important, but its effect is moderated by the structural environment. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from peer punishment in public goods do not transfer to generalized exchange settings.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the network structure. In standard public good games, costless punishment can substantially increase efficiency, but costly punishment may not. In generalized exchange (circular, sparse punishment network), enabling punishment—even if costless—does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, it is crucial to account for the density of the punishment network and whether the game is a standard public good or a generalized exchange. Control efficiency is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency when moving from public good to generalized exchange structures.

- source: Bolle, F. (2021). Deterrence by Collective Punishment May Work against Criminals but Never against Freedom Fighters. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that collective punishment (CP) is ineffective or counterproductive in threshold public good games where resistance is individually costly but can produce a public good (e.g., protests, strikes). In these cases, increasing the threat or severity of CP does not increase compliance and may even increase resistance. In contrast, in threshold public bad games (e.g., pollution, crime), CP can sometimes increase compliance, but only when the individual benefit from misbehavior is small compared to the social cost. The paper provides formal equilibrium analysis and discusses equilibrium selection, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for predicting the behavioral effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments, especially regarding when punishment is likely to deter undesirable behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on when collective punishment is likely to be effective in public-goods-like games. It suggests that in threshold public good games (where cooperation is costly and produces a group benefit), punishment is unlikely to increase efficiency or compliance, and may even be counterproductive. In threshold public bad games (where defection is individually profitable but socially costly), punishment can increase compliance and, by implication, efficiency, but only when the individual incentive to defect is not too large. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions for efficiency, but its qualitative results can inform predictions about the likely direction of punishment effects given the structure of the game (public good vs. public bad, cost/benefit ratios). However, because the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff directly, its support for efficiency prediction is indirect and should be supplemented with empirical or simulation evidence.

- source: Becchetti, L., Pelligra, V., & Salustri, F. (2018). The impact of redistribution mechanisms in the vote with the wallet game: experimental results. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The introduction of an ex post redistribution mechanism (taxing defectors and subsidizing cooperators) in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game significantly increases the share of cooperative choices compared to the baseline. In the baseline, cooperation decays over time, but with redistribution, cooperation is stabilized at a higher level. Framing the responsible product as socially beneficial also increases cooperation, but the effect of redistribution is robust across frames. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the effect on efficiency cannot be directly determined, but the increase in cooperation rates suggests likely higher group payoffs under redistribution.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that a centralized, ex post redistribution mechanism (akin to a tax/subsidy rather than peer punishment) can substantially increase cooperation rates in a repeated, all-or-nothing public good game with 10 players and no communication. While efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the structure of the payoffs and the increase in cooperation rates imply that group efficiency would also increase under the redistribution mechanism. The findings are most directly relevant for predicting the effect of centralized, automatic sanction/reward mechanisms (not peer punishment) in all-or-nothing, no-communication, 10-player repeated games with known rounds and MPCR of 0.3. The results are less directly applicable to standard peer punishment settings, but suggest that institutionalized redistribution can be highly effective at sustaining cooperation.

- source: Friehe, T., & Tabbach, A. (2018). A comparison of simple action-based and outcome-based policies for emergency-like situations. *Mathematical Social Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis shows that action-based punishment/reward policies are superior to outcome-based policies in generalized volunteer's dilemma settings (where more than one volunteer is needed and costs are private). Action-based policies can uniquely induce the efficient (second-best) outcome and prevent the no-participation equilibrium, while outcome-based policies always allow for a no-participation equilibrium, which is stable and potentially focal. The required level of punishment/reward under action-based policies is lower than under outcome-based policies. Combined policies that include punishment for non-participation can also rule out the no-participation equilibrium. The analysis is grounded in social cost minimization and group welfare, not just participation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that punishment (when implemented as action-based or combined with action-contingency) will have a more positive effect on efficiency (group welfare, social cost minimization) than outcome-based punishment in public-goods-like environments where more than one participant is needed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (action-based vs. outcome-based) is a critical moderator: action-based punishment can uniquely induce efficient outcomes and prevent social traps, while outcome-based punishment may fail to improve efficiency if the no-participation equilibrium is selected. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in settings with binary (all-or-nothing) participation, private costs, and no coordination. However, the results are theoretical and based on a generalized volunteer's dilemma, which is adjacent but not identical to standard PGGs.

- source: Okada, I. (2020). A Review of Theoretical Studies on Indirect Reciprocity. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper synthesizes theoretical advances in indirect reciprocity, showing that cooperation can be maintained in social dilemma settings when reputation mechanisms and justified punishment are present. It details how the refinement of assessment functions (e.g., leading eight norms) resolves issues like the scoring and punishment dilemmas, enabling stable cooperation under certain conditions. However, it notes that these models are not directly applicable to public goods games, as selective cooperation (the core of indirect reciprocity) is not possible in standard PGGs. The review also highlights the importance of the benefit-cost ratio, error rates, and the distinction between public and private reputation systems in determining the stability of cooperation. While punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed, the focus is on their role in norm stability and cooperation, not on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and reputation mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in social dilemmas, but it does not offer direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its models are adjacent to PGGs but differ in key structural ways (e.g., selective cooperation vs. public goods provision). The review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment and norm refinement can support cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment depends on norm structure, reputation information, and error rates, but it does not allow for direct mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency outcomes in PGGs.

- source: Di Guida, S., Han, T. A., Kirchsteiger, G., Lenaerts, T., & Zisis, I. (2021). Repeated Interaction and Its Impact on Cooperation and Surplus Allocation-An Experimental Analysis. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three treatments: (1) Baseline (forced cooperation, exogenous re-matching), (2) Re-match (voluntary cooperation, exogenous re-matching), and (3) Endogenous-match (voluntary cooperation, endogenous group formation). Allowing refusal to cooperate (punishment) increases average contributions and receiver earnings, and decreases distributor earnings, relative to forced cooperation. However, allowing repeated interaction with the same group (endogenous matching) does not further increase average efficiency or payoffs compared to exogenous re-matching. Instead, repeated interaction leads to self-selection: some groups are highly cooperative and efficient, while others are unstable and inefficient. The most efficient groups are composed of 'greedy' distributors (who contribute more to avoid rejection) and 'modest' receivers (who accept lower offers). Overall, the possibility of punishment (refusal to cooperate) increases efficiency, but repeated interaction does not further increase average efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment via refusal to cooperate (i.e., making cooperation voluntary) increases efficiency and average payoffs compared to forced cooperation in a surplus allocation game with asymmetric bargaining power. However, simply allowing repeated interaction and endogenous group formation does not further increase average efficiency or payoffs, though it does create heterogeneity in group outcomes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (even if only via refusal to cooperate) is likely to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment. The effect of repeated interaction/endogenous matching is not to further increase average efficiency, but to increase variance across groups. The findings are directly relevant for settings where punishment is implemented as the ability to refuse cooperation, but less so for costly punishment mechanisms typical in standard PGGs.

- source: Przepiorka, W., & Diekmann, A. (2020). Binding Contracts, Non-Binding Promises and Social Feedback in the Intertemporal Common-Pool Resource Game. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a repeated intertemporal common-pool resource game with five players and ten rounds, the study compares the effects of (1) binding contracts, (2) non-binding promises, (3) private social feedback, and (4) public social feedback, against a no-regulation control. Binding contracts, when unanimously signed, lead to the highest group efficiency (average profit per round close to the cooperative optimum). Non-binding promises increase sustainable investment and efficiency initially, but the effect decays over time. Private feedback (where only the recipient sees ratings) does not improve efficiency over the control. Public feedback (where feedback scores are visible to all) significantly increases sustainable investment and group efficiency, with the effect being more stable over time than promises. The study provides direct evidence that public, but not private, social feedback can substitute for formal sanctions in promoting efficiency in CPR dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated CPR games with continuous contribution choices, public social feedback (where feedback is visible to all) can substantially increase group efficiency, even in the absence of formal punishment or monetary sanctions. Private feedback is ineffective. Binding contracts are most effective but require unanimous agreement. For prediction tasks, the presence of public feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = yes) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, while private feedback (show_other_summaries = yes, show_punishment_id = no) should not. The absence of monetary punishment or reward mechanisms means that the findings are most relevant for settings where social (non-monetary) sanctions are possible. The game design dimensions (player_count = 5, num_rounds = 10, continuous contributions, no chat, known rounds, full feedback on others' choices and payoffs) are well-specified and should be used as reference points for prediction.

- source: Andersson, L. (2020). Cooperation between Emotional Players. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated prisoners' dilemma games, the presence of emotional players—who value others' payoffs positively (friendly) or negatively (hostile) depending on the history of play—can alter the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher efficiency) is sustainable. With Grim Trigger strategies, emotional states (both friendly and hostile) lower the required discount factor for cooperation, making efficient outcomes more attainable. With mutual minmax strategies, emotions can either facilitate or obstruct cooperation: friendly states increase the cost of deviating, but hostile states can make it harder to return to cooperation after punishment, sometimes requiring greater patience than in the standard model. The model also shows that a rational player can manipulate an emotional player into a friendly state to achieve higher payoffs. The analysis is entirely theoretical, with no empirical or experimental data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how emotional dynamics—modeled as state-dependent preferences—can affect the efficiency of repeated social dilemmas. While not a public goods game per se, the repeated prisoners' dilemma is closely related. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, emotional transitions that make punishment more or less credible) on efficiency depends on the interplay between strategy, emotional responsiveness, and patience (discount factor). For prediction tasks, the paper implies that enabling mechanisms that make punishment more credible (e.g., emotional hostility after defection) can increase efficiency under some strategies, but may also backfire if they make return to cooperation difficult. The results are most directly relevant for settings where player preferences or states can change in response to observed actions, and less so for standard PGGs with fixed payoffs and explicit punishment stages.
