- source: Wu, Z. W., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2014). Punishment Mechanism with Self-Adjusting Rules in Spatial Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

- source: Sun, X. P., Liu, X. Z., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2025). The impact of reputation-driven locally variable synergy factors on public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing manipulators—who both punish defectors and reward cooperators—into a spatial public goods game can sustain cooperation and increase average group payoff, even when the synergy factor (r) is low and cooperation would otherwise fail. The model shows that for a range of parameter values (punishment/reward efficiency, manipulation cost), manipulators and cooperators can coexist, and the group achieves higher payoffs than in the absence of punishment/reward. When punishment is sufficiently efficient (beta/gamma high), the system can reach the all-cooperator state, maximizing group payoff. If the cost of manipulation is too high or punishment/reward is inefficient, cooperation collapses and payoffs drop. Reward withdrawal (manipulators reducing rewards when surrounded by cooperators) reduces the average payoff of cooperators and narrows the payoff gap between manipulators and cooperators. The model demonstrates that the combined effect of punishment and reward is robust but sensitive to parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-signal evidence that enabling punishment (and reward) in a spatial public goods game increases group efficiency and average payoff, especially when the control (no punishment/reward) condition would result in low cooperation and low payoff. The effect is moderated by the efficiency of punishment (punishment impact per cost), the cost to punishers/rewarders, and the synergy factor (mpcr equivalent). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment (especially when combined with reward) will generally increase efficiency, provided the punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective. The model also highlights that the presence of defectors and the structure of the population (spatial lattice) are important, but the main prediction is robust across a range of parameter values. This paper should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar spatial PGG designs.

The findings are most directly applicable to settings where punishment/reward is implemented institutionally and funded by mandatory contributions (taxes), and less so to peer-to-peer or voluntary sanctioning. The results also indicate that the effect of punishment is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and the network topology. The paper's detailed mapping of efficiency and strategy frequencies across parameter spaces can be used to inform quantitative predictions of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions.

  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Gao, S. P., & Li, N. (2023). Preference reversal and the evolution of cooperation. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.

Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.
