- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

- source: Baker, S., & Choi, A. H. (2018). Reputation and Litigation: Why Costly Legal Sanctions Can Work Better than Reputational Sanctions. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that legal sanctions (punishment) can be more efficient than reputational sanctions (boycotts) in deterring undesirable behavior, even when both are costly. This is because legal sanctions have an inframarginal effect: increasing damages not only deters more lawsuits at the margin but also increases the penalty for existing lawsuits, improving deterrence at lower cost. Reputational sanctions lack this inframarginal effect, so increasing their severity is less efficient. The efficiency advantage of legal sanctions is greatest when courts are accurate and when litigation outcomes are public. When court error is high or when consumers have independent, reliable information about quality, reputational sanctions become more important. The model predicts that the optimal regime combines both types of sanctions, but leans toward legal sanctions unless litigation is highly error-prone or costly. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide experimental or empirical estimates, but it offers clear comparative statics and policy implications for the efficiency of punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (legal sanctions) in repeated moral hazard environments can increase efficiency relative to relying solely on reputational sanctions, especially when punishment is not too costly or error-prone and when outcomes are public. The inframarginal effect of punishment means that its efficiency benefit can be greater than that of reputational sanctions, which only deter at the margin. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment should increase efficiency, particularly when the punishment mechanism is accurate and visible. However, if punishment is highly error-prone or costly, or if reputational information is highly reliable, the efficiency gain from punishment may be reduced or even reversed. The model is not a direct PGG or lab experiment, so quantitative predictions should be made with caution, but the qualitative comparative statics are clear and relevant.

  findings: The paper finds that peer-policing can sustain high levels of honest reporting in a decentralized community if and only if punishment is opportunistic—i.e., if those who police and catch cheaters are rewarded. When the benefit of catching cheaters and the punishment for being caught are both high (with the benefit exceeding the punishment), the population stabilizes with a majority of honest reporters and a minority of policers and cheaters. If punishment is purely altruistic (costly to the punisher), honesty cannot be sustained and the population converges to all cheating. The model also shows that reducing the cost of policing (e.g., by rewarding replication) or increasing the punishment for cheating can further increase the prevalence of honesty. However, the model does not directly report on group efficiency or total welfare, focusing instead on the behavioral dynamics of honesty, policing, and cheating.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment can sustain prosocial behavior (honest reporting) in social dilemmas, but only if the incentive structure rewards punishers. While the model is not a direct public goods game, it is structurally similar and thus informative for predicting when punishment will be effective in PGG-like environments. The key implication is that punishment will only improve group outcomes (e.g., honesty, cooperation) if the cost-benefit structure makes policing individually rational—i.e., if the reward for punishing exceeds the cost and the punishment for being caught cheating exceeds the benefit of cheating. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency outcomes. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of the incentive structure for the effectiveness of punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that punishment is widespread in nature and often evolves for reasons other than inducing behavioral change in the punishee. In many cases (especially among plants and insects), punishment is a loss-cutting or cost-imposing strategy, not a behavior-modification strategy. Even among vertebrates, where behavioral modification is more common, the evolutionary benefits of punishment often do not depend on the punishee changing its behavior. The paper challenges the view that punishment primarily evolved to promote cooperation or efficiency, and instead highlights alternative evolutionary pathways.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative data relevant to predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does, however, offer theoretical context suggesting that the evolutionary function of punishment may not always be to increase cooperation or group efficiency, but could instead be to reduce harm to the punisher or its kin. For the prediction task, this paper suggests caution in assuming that punishment mechanisms will always improve efficiency or cooperation, especially in settings where behavioral modification is unlikely or where punishment serves other evolutionary functions. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental data to calibrate such predictions.
