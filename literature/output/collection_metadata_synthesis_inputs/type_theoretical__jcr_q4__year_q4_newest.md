- source: Ishikawa, B. Y. S., & Fontanari, J. F. (2025). Revisiting institutional punishment in the N-person prisoner's dilemma. *Theory in Biosciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

- source: Zhang, B. Y., & Pei, S. (2022). Game Theory and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Journal of the Operations Research Society of China*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper reviews theoretical and empirical literature on the evolution of cooperation in public goods games and related social dilemmas. It finds that punishment (both peer and institutional) can promote cooperation, especially in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, but at a cost that can reduce group welfare or average payoff. Peer punishment can curb free-riding but is costly and can lead to second-order free-riding and antisocial punishment, often resulting in lower group payoffs compared to no-punishment conditions. Institutional punishment can overcome some of these issues but is also costly. Reward mechanisms are generally less effective than punishment in moving populations from defection to cooperation, unless the reward is large. The paper emphasizes that combining reward and punishment can have a strong effect on promoting cooperation. Theoretical models predict that the effect of punishment on cooperation and payoff depends on the size and structure of the incentive, and that punishment can eliminate extremely selfish behavior but does not always increase group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based guidance that punishment mechanisms in public goods games can increase cooperation rates, but often at a cost that may reduce group efficiency or average payoff, especially in peer punishment settings. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the game (e.g., peer vs institutional punishment, compulsory vs voluntary participation). For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment is likely to increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, and in some cases may reduce it due to the costs of punishment. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates or new empirical data, so its value is mainly in providing qualitative, mechanism-based expectations and summarizing prior findings.

- source: Gioffré, A., & Tampieri, A. (2025). Community enforcement and the cost of cooperation. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement via grim punishment can sustain efficient cooperation in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with random matching, provided the cost of cooperation is not too low or too high. Specifically, if the cost-to-benefit ratio exceeds a threshold (less than 0.5), punishment is always credible and cooperation is an equilibrium for any population size and discount factor. For any environment, there exists a 'target cost of cooperation' that enables coordination on cooperation and makes punishment effective. When the cost of cooperation is endogenous, players can use it as a coordination device to achieve efficient outcomes. The results are entirely theoretical and provide explicit payoff-based conditions for when punishment increases group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with random matching and private monitoring, the introduction of credible punishment (grim trigger) can increase group efficiency to the cooperative level, provided the cost of cooperation is within a specific range. The key moderators are the cost-to-benefit ratio, population size, and discount factor. The results suggest that, for a wide range of parameter values, enabling punishment will move efficiency from the defection baseline to the cooperative optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the cost of cooperation (analogous to MPCR in PGGs), population size, and the presence of punishment. However, the model is not a literal public goods game but an adjacent repeated PD, so some caution is warranted in direct quantitative transfer.

- source: Uchida, S., Sasaki, T., Yamamoto, H., & Okada, I. (2024). Evolution of Pay-It-Forward in the Presence of the Temptation to Free-Ride. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in a donation game with institutional punishment, the evolution of cooperation and pay-it-forward strategies is strongly affected by the cognitive model of the players. Under EUT, only strong punishment (high fine or high probability) can sustain cooperation, and pay-it-forward strategies are not stable. Under PT, even weak or infrequent punishment is sufficient to deter free-riding, because players overweight small probabilities of being punished. This leads to the emergence and stability of pay-it-forward strategies and higher average payoffs (efficiency) in the population. The model shows bi-stability, with both cooperative and non-cooperative equilibria possible depending on parameters and initial conditions, but overall, punishment increases efficiency more under PT than under EUT.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-like games (here, a donation game) will increase group efficiency, especially when players are subject to prospect-theoretic biases. The effect is more pronounced when punishment is mild or infrequent, as real human players may overweight the risk of being punished. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the payoff-based outcomes are directly analyzed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (even with low probability or low severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, particularly if the control game has low efficiency due to free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of psychological factors (probability weighting) in moderating the effect of punishment.

- source: Zhao, X., & Zou, H. (2025). Research on the Evolutionary Game of Multi-Body Co-Innovation in Green Innovation Ecosystems. *Polish Journal of Environmental Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that government-imposed rewards (subsidies) and punishments (fines) both promote the stability of collaborative innovation among the three actors. There is an optimal benefit-sharing ratio for collaboration, and the effect of government intervention is strongest when both rewards and punishments are used together. The model shows threshold effects for punishment severity, and that the stability of cooperation is sensitive to the distribution of benefits and the magnitude of policy interventions. However, the main outcomes are the evolutionary stability of cooperation, not explicit efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task. It shows that introducing punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases the stability and likelihood of cooperation in a multi-actor game with public-goods-like features. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so it cannot be used to quantitatively predict the change in efficiency from enabling punishment. The results suggest that, in similar multi-actor public-goods-like environments, punishment (especially when combined with reward) is likely to increase cooperation and system stability, which may be associated with higher efficiency, but this is not directly measured.

- source: Kurokawa, S. (2022). Evolution of trustfulness in the case where resources for cooperation are sometimes absent. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated pairwise Prisoner's Dilemma with stochastic absence of resources for cooperation, the evolutionarily stable outcome is often a coexistence of trustful cooperators and trustful defectors, provided the cost-to-benefit ratio, the probability of lacking resources, and the exogenous break-up rate are not too high. Trustful strategies (those that keep interacting with partners who defect due to lack of resources) are robustly favored by natural selection, and this leads to higher equilibrium frequencies of cooperation and higher group payoffs (efficiency). When parameters are unfavorable (high cost, high resource absence, high break-up), defectors dominate and efficiency is low. The model does not include explicit punishment or costly sanctioning, but the 'walk-away' (partner switching) mechanism is functionally adjacent to punishment in that it can enforce cooperation by breaking ties with defectors.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not model a public goods game or explicit punishment, it analyzes a closely related repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with partner choice (walk-away) and stochastic resource constraints. The key design dimensions mapped are the cost-to-benefit ratio (analogous to MPCR), the probability of resource absence (a form of noise or cooperation constraint), and the exogenous break-up rate (analogous to the probability of game continuation or group stability). The main result is that mechanisms allowing players to break ties with defectors (even when defection is due to lack of resources) can support high efficiency, provided the game is not too harsh (low cost, low noise, stable groups). This suggests that in PGG-like environments, enabling partner choice or walk-away (an adjacent form of sanction) can increase efficiency, especially when the baseline efficiency is not too low. However, the absence of explicit punishment means the results are not directly transferable to punishment-enabled PGGs, but the logic of positive assortment and the conditions for efficiency gains are highly relevant.

- source: Lütz, A. F., Amaral, M. A., Braga, I., & Wardil, L. (2023). Invasion of Optimal Social Contracts. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a population playing a stag-hunt game (representing a social contract), the introduction of a moderate diversity incentive (modeled as a snowdrift game in a focal group) allows the population to overcome the invasion barrier and reach the globally optimal equilibrium (all adopting the efficient norm A). This transition increases group efficiency (total payoff). If the diversity incentive is too strong, the system may stabilize at a mixed equilibrium, but turning off the incentive after the transition can still result in full adoption of the optimal norm. The effect is strongest when the number of group connections is moderate; if individuals are too highly connected, the social pressure of the stag-hunt dominates and the incentive is less effective. The findings are supported by analytical and simulation results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that introducing a moderate incentive for diversity (analogous to a reward or tolerance for deviance) can increase group efficiency in coordination games with multiple equilibria, by enabling the population to escape suboptimal equilibria. While the model does not directly study public goods games or punishment, it is adjacent in that it addresses how incentive structures and group connectivity affect the ability to reach efficient outcomes. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that mechanisms which lower the cost or risk of deviating from the majority (even temporarily) can facilitate transitions to higher-efficiency equilibria, especially in moderately connected groups. However, the model does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so direct quantitative predictions for punishment-enabled PGGs are not possible from this paper alone.

- source: Peña, J., Heifetz, A., & Nöldeke, G. (2024). The shirker's dilemma and the prospect of cooperation in large groups. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in the shirker's dilemma, as group size increases, the proportion of volunteers at the stable equilibrium increases (for low enough costs), but the expected payoff (efficiency) decreases. The probability that the public good is provided can either increase or decrease with group size, depending on the cost of cooperation. In the limit of very large groups and low costs, the proportion of volunteers approaches one, and the expected payoff and success probability converge to positive values. However, the basin of attraction for the cooperative equilibrium shrinks with group size, making cooperation less likely to be reached from random initial conditions. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in a binary-action threshold collective action game (shirker's dilemma), increasing group size generally reduces efficiency (expected payoff at equilibrium), even though the proportion of volunteers may increase. The findings suggest that, in the absence of explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, larger groups are less efficient, and the probability of successful collective action may not improve with group size. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar threshold public goods environments without punishment, efficiency will decrease as group size increases. However, because the model does not include punishment, it does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does provide a theoretical baseline for control (no-punishment) conditions in threshold games.

- source: Bellucci, G. (2022). A Model of Trust. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper formalizes trust as a trade-off between vulnerability (potential loss) and positive expectations of a partner's behavior, using a computational model applied to the investment game. Simulations show that trustful investors (high trust attitude) invest more and are more likely to recover trust after breaches, but are also more vulnerable to exploitation. Loss aversion reduces investment and makes trust repair harder, while positive expectations and evidence of partner benevolence or competence increase investment. The model predicts that both trust attitude and learning about the partner's behavior jointly determine investment and, by extension, payoffs. The model is extended to consider both benevolence and competence of the trustee, showing that both traits affect investment and expected payoffs. However, the paper does not address punishment, sanctions, or public goods games, and does not report empirical efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants. It is a theoretical modeling paper focused on the investment game (trust game), which is adjacent but not equivalent to the public goods game. The model and its simulations may inform understanding of how psychological factors (trust, expectations, loss aversion) affect investment and payoffs in dyadic trust settings, but do not address punishment, sanctions, or group-level efficiency in multi-player public goods contexts. Therefore, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games.
