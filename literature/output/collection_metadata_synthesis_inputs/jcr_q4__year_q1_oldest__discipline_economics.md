- source: Carpenter, J., Kariv, S., & Schotter, A. (2012). Network architecture, cooperation and punishment in public good experiments. *Review of Economic Design*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies public goods games with costly punishment under eight different network architectures, varying completeness, connectedness, and directedness. All games have four players, 15 rounds, and fixed MPCR and punishment cost. The main finding is that network structure crucially moderates the effect of punishment on both cooperation and efficiency. Complete and some connected networks achieve high efficiency because they elicit high contributions with relatively low punishment expenditures. In contrast, directed and disconnected networks often see more punishment and lower efficiency, despite sometimes higher contributions. The marginal benefit of adding monitoring edges is non-linear: adding edges to sparse networks increases efficiency, but in denser networks, more edges can actually reduce efficiency due to over-punishment. The paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment is not uniform but depends on the monitoring/punishment network.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that network architecture (who can punish whom) is a critical moderator. In games with complete or well-connected networks, punishment increases contributions and can improve efficiency, especially when punishment expenditures are low. In directed or disconnected networks, punishment is more frequent and severe, often reducing efficiency due to high punishment costs. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and other game parameters are held constant, so the findings isolate the effect of network structure. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one must account for the network's connectedness, completeness, and directedness, as these determine whether punishment will be efficiency-enhancing or not. The results suggest that simply enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the network context is decisive.

- source: Carpenter, J. P., & Matthews, P. H. (2010). Norm Enforcement: The Role of Third Parties. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper develops an evolutionary game-theoretic model of a public goods game with both ingroup and third-party punishment. It finds that when a sufficient proportion of the population is willing to punish norm violators (including as third parties), the system can reach a stable equilibrium with high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency). In this equilibrium, free-riders are nearly eliminated, and the average payoff approaches the cooperative optimum. However, if the initial share of free-riders is too high, the system can instead converge to a no-contribution, low-efficiency equilibrium. The model also shows that the costliness of punishment and aspiration levels have only minor effects on equilibrium shares and efficiency, provided drift (mutation) is low. The results highlight the importance of third-party punishment for sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game (with the modeled parameters) will increase efficiency, provided the initial conditions are favorable (i.e., not too many free-riders). The model is highly informative for settings with four players, binary (all-or-nothing) contribution, MPCR of 0.75, punishment cost of 10, and punishment effectiveness of 20. It suggests that, under these conditions, the introduction of punishment can drive the system toward high efficiency, with average payoffs close to the cooperative maximum. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar structure and parameters, and less so to games with very different group sizes, contribution mechanisms, or punishment technologies. The model also cautions that if the baseline (control) efficiency is very low due to a high initial share of free-riders, punishment may not be sufficient to rescue cooperation.

- source: Fischer, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2007). Ex interim voting:: An experimental study of referendums for public-good provision. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Unanimity voting increases contributions but leads to frequent rejections, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to majority voting or automatic provision. Majority voting does not significantly differ from automatic provision in terms of efficiency or profits. The mechanism (voting rule) is the primary driver of these outcomes, not individual voting behavior. The study provides direct evidence on how institutional design (voting rules) affects efficiency in public good games, but does not test punishment or sanctioning per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly informative for predicting efficiency in public good games with voting mechanisms but does not provide evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that mechanisms that safeguard against exploitation (like unanimity voting) can increase contributions but may reduce efficiency due to coordination failures or excessive vetoes. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Annen, K. (2011). Lies and slander: truth-telling in repeated matching games with private monitoring. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement mechanisms, where players can report on others' behavior and the group can punish defectors, can support efficient cooperation in repeated matching games with private monitoring. When experience-sharing (reporting) is exogenous and truthful, cooperation can be supported for any group size, and the minimal discount factor required does not depend on the number of players. When experience-sharing is endogenous (players choose what to report), personal punishments are needed to incentivize truth-telling, which reduces the severity of community punishment and increases the minimal discount factor required for cooperation. The ability to sustain efficient cooperation thus depends on the number of players and the matching rule in the endogenous case, but for large enough groups, community enforcement remains more effective than personal enforcement alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling community punishment (via experience-sharing/reporting) increases the efficiency of repeated matching games with private monitoring, especially in large groups. However, the efficiency gain is moderated by the need to incentivize truthful reporting, which introduces personal punishments that reduce the overall effectiveness of community enforcement. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is more positive in larger groups and when experience-sharing is exogenous or can be made truthful at low cost. When experience-sharing is endogenous, the efficiency gain from punishment is still present but smaller, and the minimal discount factor required for cooperation is higher. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent, especially on group size and the structure of information sharing.

- source: Davis, D. D., & Holt, C. A. (1999). Equilibrium cooperation in two-stage games: Experimental evidence. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the use of contingent punishment/reward strategies in the second stage of a two-stage game is strongly associated with higher rates of cooperation in the first stage. This effect is robust across different treatments, including those where the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium or is Pareto dominated. The structure of the second-stage equilibria does not reliably predict cooperation rates. Instead, initial cohort propensities and first-stage payoff structure are more important. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability and use of punishment/reward strategies can increase cooperation rates in two-stage games, even when the punishment is not a Nash equilibrium. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The findings suggest that behavioral responses to punishment opportunities are important, but the translation to efficiency outcomes is not addressed. The results are most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms by which punishment can support cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Brosig, J., Weimann, J., & Yang, C. L. (2004). Communication, reputation, and punishment in sequential bargaining experiments. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that, without communication, the efficiency of punishment and the opportunity cost for the first mover significantly affect the likelihood of punishment and the first mover's choices, respectively. However, almost all second movers exploit the first mover's cooperative move, and equal splits are rare. When preplay communication is allowed, especially face-to-face, there is a dramatic increase in the number of equal splits (efficient outcomes), and the influence of punishment efficiency and opportunity cost largely disappears. E-mail communication also increases equal splits, but less so than face-to-face. The effect of communication cannot be fully explained by reputation. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the behavioral shift toward equal splits with communication suggests higher efficiency in those treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that preplay communication (especially face-to-face) is a powerful moderator that increases the likelihood of efficient, equal-split outcomes in sequential bargaining games with punishment and reward options. In the absence of communication, the efficiency of punishment and the opportunity cost for the first mover are important determinants of behavior, but do not lead to efficient outcomes. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence and type of communication should be considered a key dimension, and that enabling communication can substantially increase efficiency, even when punishment is available. However, the results are from a sequential bargaining game, not a standard public goods game, so direct quantitative predictions for PGGs should be made with caution.

- source: Güth, W., Kliemt, H., Levati, M. V., & von Wangenheim, G. (2007). On the coevolution of retribution and trustworthiness:: An (indirect) evolutionary and experimental analysis. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that most participants behave trustworthily (exert effort) and that a small but significant share of punishment (revenge/suing) persists, even among those without monetary inducement. The share of trustworthiness and punishment does not depend on the number of monetarily induced 'moral' players or the order of treatments. There is a weak positive correlation between observed untrustworthiness and subsequent punishment. Monetary inducement increases the likelihood of trustworthiness and punishment among targeted individuals, but not to the level predicted by rational choice. The main outcomes are behavioral; the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated trust game with costly punishment, the availability of punishment leads to persistent (though low) rates of punishment and high rates of trustworthiness, even without material incentives. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. The behavioral findings suggest that punishment can support trustworthiness, but the lack of payoff analysis limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Jones, M. A. (1999). The effect of punishment duration of trigger strategies and quasifinite continuation probabilities for Prisoners' Dilemmas. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that the ability to sustain cooperation in indefinitely repeated Prisoners' Dilemma games depends critically on the duration of punishment (in trigger strategies) and the properties of the continuation probability sequence. Longer punishment durations (including the grim trigger) make it easier to support cooperation in equilibrium. If the continuation probability is 'quasifinite' (i.e., its geometric average tends to zero), then no amount of punishment can sustain cooperation, and only the noncooperative equilibrium exists. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when cooperation is possible, and shows how to compute the minimum punishment duration needed for cooperation given the discount factor and continuation probability. These results are framed in terms of equilibrium existence and structure, not direct efficiency or payoff outcomes, but imply that punishment can enable higher group payoffs when the conditions are met.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how punishment duration and the probability of future interaction (continuation probability) affect the possibility of sustaining cooperation in repeated social dilemmas. For the downstream prediction task, the results suggest that increasing punishment duration (severity) generally increases the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency, provided the continuation probability is not too low. If the effective game horizon is short (quasifinite), punishment will not improve efficiency. While the analysis is for Prisoners' Dilemma rather than public goods games, the logic and mathematical structure are closely related and can inform predictions about when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative predictions of efficiency changes, only conditions for the existence of cooperative equilibria.

- source: Huang, F. (2007). Building social trust: A human-capital approach. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that when punishment institutions are more effective at detecting and punishing defection, more individuals choose to invest in cooperative tendencies, leading to higher social trust (i.e., a higher proportion of cooperative players). However, the average level of intrinsic cooperative tendency among cooperators is lower, since extrinsic incentives substitute for intrinsic motivation. The model predicts multiple equilibria depending on the distribution of investment costs, and highlights the potential for both high- and low-trust equilibria. The relationship between institutional punishment and cooperation is complex: stronger institutions can crowd in more cooperators but may reduce intrinsic motivation. The paper does not provide direct predictions or analysis of group efficiency or total payoff, but the results are relevant for understanding how punishment institutions affect the prevalence of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that increasing the effectiveness of punishment institutions (e.g., higher probability of detection or stronger sanctions) increases the proportion of cooperative players in a prisoners' dilemma-like environment. While it does not directly model public goods games or report efficiency outcomes, it suggests that punishment mechanisms can increase cooperation rates, which may translate into higher efficiency in related environments. However, the paper also warns that reliance on extrinsic punishment may reduce intrinsic motivation over time. For prediction tasks, the main implication is that enabling or strengthening punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency), but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The model is most relevant for settings where the main outcome is the prevalence of cooperation rather than group payoff.

- source: KRAINES, D., & KRAINES, V. (1993). LEARNING TO COOPERATE WITH PAVLOV - AN ADAPTIVE STRATEGY FOR THE ITERATED PRISONERS-DILEMMA WITH NOISE. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that Pavlovian strategies, which adapt their probability of cooperation based on positive and negative payoffs (interpreted as rewards and punishments), can achieve high average payoffs and are robust to noise in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. Pavlov can exploit overly cooperative opponents (like All-C), is not easily exploited except by always-defect strategies, and can recover mutual cooperation after errors more quickly than Tit-for-Tat (TFT). The optimal learning rate (parameter n) balances exploitability and speed of learning, with n=3 or 4 performing best in simulations. The paper provides detailed payoff tables and Markov chain analyses for various strategy matchups, but does not study group interactions, explicit peer punishment, or public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in public goods games, as it does not study group games, peer punishment, or any of the relevant game design dimensions. However, it is informative about how strategies that adapt to positive and negative payoffs (interpreted as endogenous rewards and punishments) can lead to high efficiency and mutual cooperation in repeated dyadic dilemmas. The findings suggest that adaptive learning can be robust to noise and can outperform simple reciprocity (TFT) in some environments. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address the effect of enabling punishment mechanisms or any of the design dimensions in public goods games.
