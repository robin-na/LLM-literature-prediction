- source: Jiang, X., & Wang, X. H. (2024). Gender difference in leaders framed with responsibility or authority: An experimental study. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a leader with the power to punish increases group contributions and efficiency compared to the no-leader baseline. When the leader's role is framed as responsibility (rather than authority), and especially when the leader is selected by voting, female leaders are more effective at increasing group income and efficiency than male leaders. The responsibility framing increases both leader contributions and the effectiveness of punishment, but the positive effect on efficiency is robustly observed only in the voting stage for female-led groups. In other conditions (authority framing, male leaders, or self-selected leaders), the efficiency gains from punishment are smaller or not significant. The study also shows that leader contributions are a more important driver of efficiency than punishment per se, as punishment can offset its own benefits through costs. Overall, the presence of punishment increases efficiency, but the magnitude depends on leader gender, framing, and selection method.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a public good game increases efficiency, especially when the leader is female, the leadership role is framed as responsibility, and the leader is selected by voting. The game design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency gains from punishment are: presence of a leader with punishment power, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (4), number of rounds (5), and information structure (no chat, summary feedback). The control (no-leader) efficiency is reported, and the treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency is higher, particularly in the responsibility/voting/female leader condition. For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when the leader is prosocial (as selected or induced by responsibility framing), and that leader contributions are a key mechanism. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with punishment, especially with comparable group size, round number, and punishment parameters.

- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Fehr, E., Gächter, S., Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2010). Institutions Fostering Public Goods Provision. *Selten School of Behavioral Economics: a Collection of Essays in Honor of Reinhard Selten*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment opportunities in public goods games increase cooperation and contributions, but typically reduce efficiency and group payoffs in standard (short) experimental settings due to the destruction of resources through costly punishment. In most experiments with 10 or fewer rounds, average payoffs are lower with punishment than without. However, in longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when punishment is combined with effective reputation mechanisms, efficiency can be higher with punishment. Endogenous institution choice leads most participants to eventually select punishment institutions, which then sustain high cooperation, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed unless the time horizon is long or reputation is leveraged. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed and highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as number of rounds and the presence of reputation mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive or negative, but depends critically on the number of rounds (time horizon) and the presence of reputation or endogenous institution choice. For standard short games (10 rounds or fewer), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to control. For longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when combined with reputation mechanisms, punishment can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of endogenous institution choice, which can foster high cooperation and, under the right conditions, higher efficiency. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the number of rounds and the presence of reputation or endogenous choice mechanisms are key moderators.

- source: JARUNGRATTANAPONG, R. (2022). PROMOTING COOPERATION IN A PUBLIC GOOD EXPERIMENT THROUGH OSTRACISM AS A PUNISHMENT MECHANISM. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that allowing group members to ostracize (temporarily exclude) others via majority vote in a repeated public goods game increases average contributions by about 45 percentage points and increases average earnings (welfare) by about 23% compared to the control (no punishment) game. The effect is strongest for free-riders but is present for all contributor types. Ostracism is implemented with a small direct cost (1% of endowment per vote), but the main cost is the reduction in group size and thus potential public good returns. Anti-social punishment is observed: non-cooperators who are punished tend to retaliate by voting against others, but this is not seen among high contributors. The positive effect on efficiency is robust, but cooperation and efficiency decline when ostracism is removed (end-game effect).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling ostracism (a form of social punishment) in a repeated public goods game with 4 players, 6 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.625 increases both contribution rates and average group earnings (efficiency) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is large and robust across contributor types. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar lab PGGs. The main caveat is the presence of anti-social punishment and the collapse of cooperation when punishment is removed, suggesting that the positive effect is contingent on the continued availability of the punishment mechanism.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Peng, H. C., & Fan, Y. H. (2023). Incomplete punishment networks, heterogeneity, and cooperation in public good experiments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the structure of the punishment network strongly moderates the effect of peer punishment on both cooperation and efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group payoffs (efficiency) and higher contributions than the complete punishment network. The complete network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower efficiency, lower contributions, and lower punishment severity, likely due to diffusion of responsibility (bystander effect). The effect of punishment is thus not monotonic in network density: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency. The type of MPCR (high or low) also affects individual contributions, with high-MPCR individuals contributing more. The punishment mechanism is costly and binary (1 token cost, 3 token impact), and the experiment uses stranger matching over 15 rounds. No communication or chat is allowed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment network is crucial for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, especially with heterogeneous returns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. When predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, it is not sufficient to know only the presence of punishment; the network structure (complete vs. incomplete, and the type of incomplete network) must be considered. In this design, incomplete networks (circle and homo-pairwise) outperform the complete network in both contributions and efficiency. The findings also show that punishment severity is lower in the complete network, likely due to a bystander effect. The experiment uses a four-player, 15-round, linear PGG with binary punishment, no chat, and random reassignment of MPCRs and groupings each round. These details should be matched when using this evidence for prediction. The results are most relevant for settings with heterogeneous returns and similar punishment mechanics.

Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with five players and MPCR 0.4, the introduction of punishment mechanisms (either formal, with a fixed administrative cost and group-voted sanction rate, or informal, with peer-to-peer costly punishment) leads to large and significant increases in group efficiency and payoffs. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while enabling punishment (either formal or informal) produces large, robust increases in efficiency and group payoff. The results are robust to manipulations of self-regulatory resources and to whether the punishment institution is chosen endogenously or imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, the findings support a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, with formal deterrent punishment achieving near-full efficiency and informal punishment producing substantial, though somewhat lower, efficiency gains.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.

  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.
