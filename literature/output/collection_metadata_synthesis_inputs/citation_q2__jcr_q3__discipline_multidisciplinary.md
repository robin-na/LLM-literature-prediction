- source: Liu, Q., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2018). Evolutionary Public Goods Game on Evolving Random Networks. *Journal of the Korean Physical Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that allowing informed cooperators to rewire their network connections at a cost can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation in public goods games on random networks. Higher rates of network rewiring (by informed cooperators) lower the threshold for cooperation to emerge, especially under adverse conditions (low synergy factor). The system exhibits distinct phases (full cooperation, full defection, coexistence, and segregated sub-networks), and the transition between these phases depends on the synergy factor and rewiring rate. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and does not include explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not implement or analyze punishment or sanctioning mechanisms. Instead, it studies the effect of costly information and network rewiring on cooperation rates. While the findings suggest that network adaptation can promote cooperation, they do not provide evidence about efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor about the effect of punishment. Thus, this paper should not be used as direct evidence for the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency, though it may be contextually relevant for understanding alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms.

- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Zhang, Q. H., Chen, Y. Y., Tao, Y., Farid, T., & Ma, J. H. (2019). How Consistent Contributors Inspire Individuals to Cooperate: The Role of Moral Elevation and Social Value Orientation. *Sustainability*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: This study demonstrates that introducing a consistent contributor (CC) into a step-level public-goods-dilemma game increases both cooperation and group/individual earnings. The effect is robust across both pro-social and pro-self participants, though the underlying psychological mechanisms differ: pro-selfs are influenced via increased moral elevation, while pro-socials respond directly to the CC's behavior. The design does not include punishment or sanction mechanisms, but provides strong evidence that motivational (role model) interventions can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanction mechanisms, introducing a consistent contributor (role model) can increase group efficiency (payoff) in step-level public-goods games. The findings are most relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar motivational interventions, but do not inform the effect of punishment. The design and results can serve as a control or baseline for understanding efficiency in the absence of punishment, and highlight the potential for non-punitive mechanisms to improve outcomes.

- source: Ohnuma, S., Hirose, Y., Karasawa, K., Yorifuji, K., & Sugiura, J. (2005). Why do residents accept a demanding rule?: Fairness and social benefit as determinants of approval of a recycling system. *Japanese Psychological Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that citizens' approval of a demanding, sanction-free recycling system is primarily determined by their perception of social benefit and procedural fairness. Preference for introducing strict rules (penalties, surveillance) is mainly driven by perceived outcome unfairness (i.e., the sense that some people are not complying and are not penalized). The system succeeded in reducing waste, but the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff in a way directly comparable to public goods games. The findings suggest that emphasizing social benefit and procedural fairness can increase approval and cooperation even in the absence of incentives or sanctions, and that perceived unfairness can drive support for introducing punitive measures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. It suggests that in real-world social dilemma settings, approval and willingness to cooperate with demanding rules can be high even without punishment, if social benefit and procedural fairness are perceived as high. Preference for punishment or sanctions arises mainly from perceived unfairness in outcomes. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, and does not manipulate or observe actual punishment regimes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main value is in highlighting the psychological determinants of support for punishment and cooperation in adjacent, real-world collective action problems.

  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=weak | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that strategic management of cooperation between organizations can stimulate the emergence of synergy effects, which include increased competitiveness, innovation, and knowledge sharing. These effects are more likely when organizations set clear goals, establish trust, and manage cooperation dynamically. The research is based on surveys of Slovak enterprises and case studies of global business alliances. While the paper discusses outcomes such as increased economic value and competitiveness, it does not measure efficiency or group payoff in a game-theoretic or experimental sense, nor does it involve punishment or sanction mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or related mechanisms, nor does it use a game-theoretic or experimental design. The outcomes are organizational and strategic, not payoff-based in the sense required for the prediction task. Therefore, this paper should not be used to inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their variants.
