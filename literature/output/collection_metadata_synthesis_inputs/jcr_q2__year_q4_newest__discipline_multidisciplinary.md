- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

  findings: The experiment finds that, in a four-player public goods game with heterogeneous MPCRs (0.3 and 0.7), incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group contributions and higher efficiency (group payoff) than the complete punishment network. The complete punishment network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower contributions and lower efficiency, likely due to a bystander effect that reduces punishment severity. Specifically, enabling punishment by all (complete network) does not maximize efficiency; instead, certain incomplete networks (circle, homo-pairwise) yield higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the 'punishment_tech' and the network structure (not just the presence of punishment) must be considered: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency, and may even reduce it. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative efficiency across punishment network types with punishment enabled is clear. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency as a function of punishment network structure and game design dimensions.

  findings: The study finds that introducing manipulators—who both punish defectors and reward cooperators—into a spatial public goods game can sustain cooperation and increase average group payoff, even when the synergy factor (r) is low and cooperation would otherwise fail. The model shows that for a range of parameter values (punishment/reward efficiency, manipulation cost), manipulators and cooperators can coexist, and the group achieves higher payoffs than in the absence of punishment/reward. The effect is moderated by the efficiency of punishment (punishment impact per cost), the cost to punishers/rewarders, and the synergy factor (mpcr equivalent). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment (especially when combined with reward) will generally increase efficiency, provided the punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective. The model also highlights that the presence of defectors and the structure of the population (spatial lattice) are important, but the main prediction is robust across a range of parameter values. This paper should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar spatial PGG designs.

However, in settings where punishment is cheap, standard punishment (without redistribution) may yield higher efficiency by allowing punishers to eliminate defectors. For prediction, the presence or absence of fine redistribution, the punishment cost, and the fine distribution parameter (alpha) are important moderators. The results suggest that enabling fine redistribution is likely to increase efficiency when punishment is expensive, but may not always do so when punishment is cheap.
