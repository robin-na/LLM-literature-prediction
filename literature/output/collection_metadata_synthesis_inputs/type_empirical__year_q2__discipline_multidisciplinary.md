- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., & Villatoro, D. (2013). Punish and Voice: Punishment Enhances Cooperation when Combined with Norm-Signalling. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with 4-person groups over 30 rounds, the study compares three treatments: (1) punishment only, (2) norm-signaling (message) only, and (3) both punishment and norm-signaling (sanction). In the critical rounds where treatments differ (rounds 11-20), the sanction treatment yields significantly higher average net earnings (31% higher than punishment, 16% higher than message) and higher contributions, with less frequent and less intense punishment. The efficiency gains in the sanction treatment are attributed to the combination of explicit norm communication and the credible threat of punishment, which together increase norm salience and reduce the need for costly punishment. The findings are robust in both human experiments and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with costly punishment, the addition of norm-signaling (explicit communication of normative expectations) to punishment substantially increases group efficiency compared to punishment alone. The efficiency gains are due to both higher cooperation and lower punishment costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of norm-signaling or communication channels that make norms salient can be a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of punishment, the ability to send norm-signaling messages, and the structure of feedback about others' actions. The control efficiency (without punishment or norm-signaling) is lower than in the combined treatment. The results imply that, all else equal, enabling both punishment and norm-signaling will yield higher efficiency than enabling punishment alone.

- source: Wu, J. H., Balliet, D., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2016). Gossip Versus Punishment: The Efficiency of Reputation to Promote and Maintain Cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares the effects of punishment and gossip on cooperation and efficiency in a four-round public goods game. Punishment (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3) did not significantly increase overall cooperation and significantly reduced individual earnings (efficiency) compared to control. Gossip (costless) increased both cooperation and efficiency. When both mechanisms were present, gossip offset the negative efficiency impact of punishment. The study also finds that gossip, but not punishment, has positive spillover effects on trust and trustworthiness in a subsequent trust game. The results suggest that punishment, at moderate cost, is less efficient than gossip for promoting cooperation, and that gossip can mitigate the efficiency losses from punishment.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, four-round PGG with a punishment mechanism (fee-to-fine ratio 1:3, anonymous, no chat, continuous contributions), enabling punishment reduces efficiency (total/individual earnings) compared to control. The negative effect is robust, and the presence of a costless gossip mechanism can counteract this loss. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar PGG designs, especially when punishment is moderately costly and anonymous. The results also suggest that the efficiency cost of punishment is not offset by increased cooperation, except possibly in the final rounds, and that gossip is a more efficient mechanism for promoting cooperation.

- source: Gross, J., Méder, Z. Z., Okamoto-Barth, S., & Riedl, A. (2016). Building the Leviathan - Voluntary centralisation of punishment power sustains cooperation in humans. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study introduces a 'power transfer game'—a repeated public goods game with punishment, where group members can voluntarily transfer their punishment power to others. In groups of five, three conditions were compared: (1) endogenous (voluntary) power transfer, (2) exogenous (mirrored, but not voluntary) power transfer, and (3) fixed (no transfer, standard peer punishment). Only the endogenous condition, where group members could choose whom to empower, led to sustained or increasing cooperation and higher group earnings, approaching the social optimum. In the fixed and exogenous conditions, cooperation and group earnings declined over time. The mechanism works because non-punishing cooperators delegate power to those willing to punish, and centralisation of power in the hands of pro-social punishers deters free riding. The study provides direct evidence that voluntary centralisation of punishment power can efficiently sustain cooperation and group welfare in public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a repeated public goods game with peer punishment, allowing voluntary centralisation of punishment power (i.e., enabling group members to transfer their punishment effectiveness to others) substantially increases group efficiency compared to standard decentralised peer punishment. The effect is not observed when centralisation is imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of a voluntary power transfer mechanism is a key moderator: with it, efficiency is much higher than in standard peer punishment, even when the underlying MPCR, punishment cost, and other parameters are held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in games with and without voluntary centralisation of punishment power.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Chaudhuri, A., & Paichayontvijit, T. (2017). On the long-run efficacy of punishments and recommendations in a laboratory public goods game. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with groups of four, both peer punishment and exhortative recommendations (public messages) increase efficiency compared to a control with no intervention. Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. Punishment starts with lower efficiency due to the cost of punishing but shows an upward trend over time, eventually catching up to or surpassing recommendations in some conditions, particularly with longer time horizons. However, in these experiments, punishment never outperforms the restart recommendation treatment. The effect of punishment is stronger with fixed matching than with random rematching. The cost-effectiveness of punishment depends on the punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) and the length of the game. The study provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, with detailed efficiency data for control, recommendation, and punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear public goods games. It reports efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the social optimum) for control and punishment treatments, with and without recommendations, across different matching protocols and time horizons. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

- source: Hilbe, C., Traulsen, A., Röhl, T., & Milinski, M. (2014). Democratic decisions establish stable authorities that overcome the paradox of second-order punishment. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that second-order punishment institutions (which punish both defectors and those who do not support the punishment institution) result in higher average payoffs and cooperation rates than institutions without second-order punishment. When given a choice, groups using majority voting overwhelmingly select the more efficient second-order punishment institution, while groups using individual migration (foot voting) initially avoid it, resulting in lower payoffs. Over time, some foot-voting groups shift toward the more efficient institution, but not as consistently as with majority voting. The findings highlight the importance of democratic decision-making in establishing efficient punishment institutions that sustain cooperation and maximize group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling second-order punishment in a public goods game increases efficiency (average payoff) compared to a control without such punishment, under otherwise identical game parameters. The effect is robust across both theoretical predictions and experimental results. The findings also show that the method of institutional choice (majority voting vs. individual migration) moderates the realized efficiency, with majority voting more reliably leading to the efficient outcome. For prediction tasks, the presence of second-order punishment, the method of institutional choice, and the group’s prior experience with institutional regimes are important moderators. The paper’s detailed reporting of payoffs under both control and treatment conditions makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

  findings: In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

- source: Muthukrishna, M., Francois, P., Pourahmadi, S., & Henrich, J. (2017). Corrupting cooperation and how anti-corruption strategies may backfire. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). In the standard IPGG (control), stronger leaders (higher punishment multiplier) increase contributions and public good provisioning. However, when bribery is possible (bribery game, BG), stronger leaders actually decrease public good provisioning, as they use their power to extract bribes rather than enforce cooperation. Anti-corruption strategies (partial/full transparency, leader investment) can increase contributions in some contexts (strong leaders or high economic potential), but may have no effect or even reduce contributions further in contexts with weak leaders and poor economic potential. The main outcome is mean contribution to the public good, which is a close proxy for efficiency. The results show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be reversed or undermined by corruption opportunities.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the presence of corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) can reverse the usual positive effect of institutional punishment on efficiency. The effect of punishment depends critically on whether leaders can be corrupted, the strength of the leader's punitive power, and the economic potential (mpcr). In standard IPGGs, stronger punishment increases efficiency, but in bribery-enabled games, stronger punishment can decrease efficiency. Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions. When using this paper for prediction, it is important to consider whether the game design allows for corruption/bribery, the strength of punishment, and the economic context. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported directly, but the relative changes are clear: punishment increases efficiency in standard IPGG, but decreases it when bribery is possible.

The results are most directly applicable to games with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions (in increments), and full feedback on others' contributions and payoffs.
