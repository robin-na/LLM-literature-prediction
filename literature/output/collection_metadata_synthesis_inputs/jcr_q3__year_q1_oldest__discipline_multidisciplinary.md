- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Ohnuma, S., Hirose, Y., Karasawa, K., Yorifuji, K., & Sugiura, J. (2005). Why do residents accept a demanding rule?: Fairness and social benefit as determinants of approval of a recycling system. *Japanese Psychological Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that citizens' approval of a demanding, sanction-free recycling system is primarily determined by their perception of social benefit and procedural fairness. Preference for introducing strict rules (penalties, surveillance) is mainly driven by perceived outcome unfairness (i.e., the sense that some people are not complying and are not penalized). The system succeeded in reducing waste, but the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff in a way directly comparable to public goods games. The findings suggest that emphasizing social benefit and procedural fairness can increase approval and cooperation even in the absence of incentives or sanctions, and that perceived unfairness can drive support for introducing punitive measures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence for the prediction task. It suggests that in real-world social dilemma settings, approval and willingness to cooperate with demanding rules can be high even without punishment, if social benefit and procedural fairness are perceived as high. Preference for punishment or sanctions arises mainly from perceived unfairness in outcomes. However, because the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff, and does not manipulate or observe actual punishment regimes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main value is in highlighting the psychological determinants of support for punishment and cooperation in adjacent, real-world collective action problems.
