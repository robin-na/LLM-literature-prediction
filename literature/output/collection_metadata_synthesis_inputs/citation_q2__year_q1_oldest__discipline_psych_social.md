- source: Brandts, J., & Fatas, E. (2012). THE PUZZLE OF SOCIAL PREFERENCES. *Revista Internacional De Sociologia*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper reviews experimental evidence showing that in public goods games, the introduction of punishment mechanisms typically increases contributions and cooperation. However, it also highlights that punishment is costly and not always optimal from a collective (efficiency) perspective, especially when antisocial punishment or counter-punishment occurs. The effectiveness of punishment is robust to parameter changes (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness), but can be undermined by incomplete information or network structure. Communication and group selection also enhance cooperation. The paper does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral regularities and mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong qualitative support that enabling punishment in public goods games generally increases cooperation and contributions, but warns that the net effect on efficiency depends on the costs of punishment and the prevalence of antisocial punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when punishment is targeted at free riders, costs are not excessive, and antisocial punishment is rare. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes, so its value is mainly in contextualizing and interpreting empirical findings from other sources.

- source: Goette, L., & Huffman, D. (2007). Affect and the motivational foundations of social capital. *Review of General Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, cooperation typically declines over time due to negative affect and conditional cooperation. However, when a punishment option is introduced, cooperation rates remain high, as punishment deters defection. The motivation to punish is linked to affective responses, such as anger at defectors and satisfaction from punishing. The paper notes that punishment can sometimes be misapplied (e.g., punishing cooperators), which can undermine cooperation. The findings are based on a synthesis of prior experimental studies, not new empirical data, and focus on behavioral and affective outcomes rather than direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong qualitative support for the idea that introducing punishment in public goods games can sustain high cooperation rates and prevent the typical decline seen in repeated games without punishment. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, nor does it report new experimental results. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of a punishment mechanism is likely to increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency, provided punishment is targeted at defectors. The review also cautions that punishment can be counterproductive if misapplied. The paper is most useful for understanding the mechanisms and moderators of punishment effects, but less so for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Zschache, J. (2012). Producing public goods in networks: Some effects of social comparison and endogenous network change. *Social Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on networks, the ability of agents to change their social ties in response to social comparison (i.e., to seek out like-minded cooperators and avoid defectors) can sustain or increase cooperation and thus the production of public goods. Low network density and low costs of network change (low cohesion) allow cooperators to persist by forming supportive subgroups, leading to higher efficiency. High initial cooperation and low segregation also promote full cooperation. When network change is costly (high cohesion), cooperation collapses and efficiency is low. The model shows that both forward-looking and backward-looking (adaptive) agents can achieve high efficiency under favorable network conditions, but backward-looking agents require even more favorable starting conditions. Explicit costly punishment is not modeled; instead, social comparison provides a form of endogenous, non-costly social pressure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that endogenous network change and social comparison can substitute for explicit costly punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that game designs allowing for dynamic partner selection or network rewiring (low cohesion/costs) will see higher efficiency, especially when initial cooperation is high and the network is not highly segregated. However, the absence of explicit costly punishment means the results are most applicable to settings where social comparison or endogenous social pressure is the main incentive, not where formal punishment is enabled. The results are directly informative for predicting efficiency in networked PGGs with endogenous tie change, but less so for classic PGGs with explicit punishment options.

- source: Guillen, P., Fatas, E., & Brañas-Garza, P. (2010). Inducing efficient conditional cooperation patterns in public goods games, an experimental investigation. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The introduction of a grim trigger strategy threat (via possible automata) leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (by 40% before a surprise restart and 50% after) compared to a standard repeated public goods game. Contributions in the threat treatment are highly polarized (either full or zero), and the pattern of conditional cooperation changes: in the threat treatment, the minimum contribution in the previous round predicts current contributions, consistent with grim trigger logic. However, contributions still decline over time, and the effect is driven by belief manipulation rather than actual punishment or sanctions. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the higher contributions imply higher group payoffs, net of the absence of punishment costs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that belief in the possibility of strict conditional cooperation (grim trigger) can substantially increase contributions in repeated public goods games, even without actual punishment. However, because there is no real punishment mechanism (no cost, no assignment of punishment, no reduction of others' payoffs), the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to enabling punishment. The results suggest that the mere threat or belief in sanctions can have large effects on cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency impact of actual punishment mechanisms, especially considering the cost of punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, but the absence of real punishment means the findings should be used as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

- source: De Silva, H., & Sigmund, K. (2009). Public Good Games with Incentives: The Role of Reputation. *Games, Groups, and the Global Good*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, cooperation and punishment do not persist in the long run under strict anonymity; the population converges to asocial strategies. Introducing reputation—where players can learn about others' punishment behavior—creates bistability: both full cooperation with punishment and full defection without punishment can be stable. The presence of errors (occasional failures to cooperate) is necessary for reputation to be informative and for punishment to have an effect. The model is primarily theoretical and does not provide explicit efficiency or group payoff results, but the implication is that reputation can enable punishment to support higher cooperation and, by extension, higher efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the importance of reputation and information flow in enabling punishment to sustain cooperation in public goods games. In the absence of reputation, punishment does not improve efficiency; with reputation, punishment can support high cooperation, but outcomes depend on initial conditions and parameters. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of reputation mechanisms (e.g., information about others' punishment behavior) is a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, the paper does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes, so its support is indirect and mechanistic rather than predictive.

- source: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., & De Cremer, D. (2009). When sanctions that can be evaded still work: The role of trust in leaders. *Social Influence*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation depends on both the trustworthiness of the leader who installs the sanction and the general trust participants have in authorities. When an alternative, more harmful defection option is available (i.e., sanctions can be evaded), participants with low general trust in authorities are less likely to cooperate if the leader is untrustworthy, and instead shift to the evasion option. High-trust participants are less sensitive to leader trustworthiness. The main outcomes are behavioral allocations; the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of an evadable punishment option (i.e., a trilemma with an alternative defection route) can undermine the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation, especially among participants with low trust in authorities and when the leader is perceived as untrustworthy. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in designs where punishment can be evaded, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and thus efficiency) may be reduced or even reversed for some subgroups. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of evasion options and trust, but not about the quantitative effect on efficiency.

- source: De Cremer, D., Hoogervorst, N., & Desmet, P. (2012). Procedural Justice and Sanctions in Social Dilemmas: The Moderating Effects of Group Feedback and Identification. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that accurate sanctioning systems (those that monitor all contribution sessions) increase contributions relative to inaccurate systems, but this effect depends on group identification and feedback. For high group identifiers, procedural justice (accuracy) increases contributions only after group failure; for low identifiers, it increases contributions only after group success. The experiment uses a step-level public good game with a threshold and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The findings highlight the importance of procedural justice in sanctioning systems for promoting cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence about effects on efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions depends on the perceived procedural justice (accuracy) of the sanctioning system, and that this effect is moderated by group identification and feedback about group success or failure. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in step-level public good games with punishment, the design of the punishment system (especially its perceived fairness and accuracy) and the social context (identification, feedback) can strongly moderate behavioral responses, which may in turn affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment will be more effective at increasing cooperation (and possibly efficiency) when the system is accurate and procedurally just, but the effect is context-dependent.

- source: Prietula, M. J., & Conway, D. (2009). The evolution of metanorms: quis custodiet ipsos custodes?. *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of a public-goods-like dilemma, the addition of metanorms (punishing those who do not punish defectors) is necessary for the emergence of a behavioral norm against defection. When only first-order punishment is available, defection dominates. When metanorms are present, defection rates drop and punishment rates rise, indicating norm emergence. Decoupling the strategies for punishing defectors and non-punishers does not prevent norm emergence and can reduce the overall level of punishment required. In group-structured games with unequal power, metanorms alone do not produce a norm against defection; only with decoupled sanctioning structures does the strong group enforce a norm against the weak group's defection, leading to group dominance rather than universal cooperation. The outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral strategy values (boldness, vengefulness), not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that the presence of punishment and especially metanorms (second-order punishment) can dramatically reduce defection rates in public-goods-like environments, even when punishment is costly. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (defection, punishment) rather than efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that enabling punishment (especially with metanorms) is likely to increase efficiency in analogous empirical games, but the magnitude and exact efficiency outcomes are not reported. The paper also highlights that group structure and the sophistication of the punishment mechanism (e.g., decoupling punishment types) can moderate the effect, sometimes leading to group dominance rather than overall cooperation.

- source: Engelen, B. (2008). The sources of cooperation - On strong reciprocity and its theoretical implications. *Theory & Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that standard egoistic and instrumental accounts (kin selection, direct/indirect reciprocity, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling) cannot fully explain the high levels of cooperation and costly punishment observed in human experiments such as public goods games, ultimatum games, and trust games. Instead, it posits that 'strong reciprocity'—a norm-driven, non-instrumental willingness to cooperate and punish defectors—better accounts for the empirical findings. The paper highlights that such behavior is observed even in one-shot, anonymous interactions, and is motivated by internalized norms and emotions rather than by material self-interest. The author further argues that cultural group selection and the internalization of social norms are key to understanding the evolutionary origins of strong reciprocity and widespread human cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct quantitative or experimental evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It does, however, provide strong theoretical and interpretive support for the idea that the availability of punishment mechanisms can sustain higher levels of cooperation, even at personal cost, and that this is robust to one-shot and anonymous settings. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when norm internalization is strong) is likely to increase cooperation, but it does not address efficiency or payoff outcomes directly. Therefore, it is not directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, but it does support the general mechanism by which punishment can increase cooperation in public goods environments.

- source: Langlois, C. C., & Langlois, J. P. P. (2004). Provisions for noncompliance and treaty value: A game theoretic perspective. *International Studies Quarterly*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated prisoner's dilemma environments with possible unintentional noncompliance (noise), the design of punishment schemes has a large effect on long-run efficiency (treaty value). Probabilistic trigger schemes, which are analogous to mutual punishment phases after a deviation, are self-punishing and reduce efficiency because both the perpetrator and the victim suffer during punishment. Contrite tit-for-tat (CTFT) schemes, which allow for redress (compensation to the victim) and delay retaliation (generosity), achieve much higher efficiency by minimizing the time spent in mutual defection and ensuring that only the guilty party is punished. The paper provides explicit formulas and comparative statics for how efficiency declines with noise under each scheme, and shows that CTFT dominates trigger schemes in most of the relevant parameter space.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the structure of the punishment scheme (especially whether punishment is self-punishing or allows for redress) is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment in repeated social dilemma games. If the punishment mechanism is costly to the punisher and involves mutual punishment phases (as in standard trigger schemes), efficiency is substantially reduced, especially as noise increases. If the punishment mechanism allows for redress (compensation to the victim) and avoids mutual punishment (as in CTFT), efficiency is much higher. The paper also shows that the speed of retaliation (trigger probability) and the expected duration of punishment phases are important: swifter, shorter punishments are more efficient, but only if they still deter defection. These insights should inform predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment based on the punishment technology, cost structure, and whether redress is possible.

- source: Nakao, K. (2009). CREATION OF SOCIAL ORDER IN ETHNIC CONFLICT. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer monitoring, collective peer punishment, or ethnic conflict) can sustain higher group efficiency (expected payoffs) compared to no punishment, but only if in-group monitoring is sufficiently efficient and not too costly. When monitoring is inefficient or costly, or when noise (misinterpretation of actions) is high, punishment can reduce efficiency due to frequent erroneous punishment or high monitoring costs. The model provides comparative statics showing that efficiency is maximized with efficient peer monitoring and high tolerance, and that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the quality of in-group policing, the length of conflict, and the density of inter-group interactions. The model predicts that groups with high-quality in-group policing achieve higher efficiency and more stable cooperation, while groups with low-quality policing suffer more frequent and longer conflicts, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated social dilemma games depends critically on the efficiency and cost of monitoring and punishment. If the game design allows for efficient, low-cost peer monitoring (low punishment cost, high punishment tech), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. If monitoring is costly or ineffective, punishment can reduce efficiency due to high costs or frequent erroneous punishment. The model also shows that the effect of punishment is moderated by noise (misinterpretation), group size, and the structure of interactions. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency only when monitoring is efficient and not too costly, and that the marginal effect of punishment is lower or negative when monitoring is costly or noise is high. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, so findings should be transferred with caution.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Thomadsen, R., & Bhardwaj, P. (2011). Cooperation in Games with Forgetfulness. *Management Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that the effect of forgetfulness on efficiency in repeated games with punishment is fundamentally ambiguous: it can either reduce or increase the set of discount factors and game parameters under which full cooperation (and thus maximum efficiency) is sustainable. When the stage game is such that the cooperative action always yields nonnegative payoffs, forgetfulness weakens punishment and makes cooperation harder to sustain, reducing efficiency. However, when the stage game allows for the possibility that a forgetful player can be punished below the minimax, forgetfulness can make severe punishments credible and thus expand the range of parameters supporting cooperation, increasing efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas and graphical illustrations for these effects in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and a franchising effort game. The results are theoretical and depend on the structure of the stage game and the probability of forgetfulness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games is not monotonic and can be moderated by cognitive or institutional features like forgetfulness. In environments where punishment is only as severe as the stage minimax, introducing forgetfulness will generally reduce the efficiency gains from punishment. However, in environments where forgetfulness allows for below-minimax punishments, punishment can become more effective at sustaining cooperation, potentially increasing efficiency beyond what is possible with perfect recall. For prediction tasks, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined solely from standard game design dimensions; one must also consider whether the environment allows for forgetfulness and whether the stage game payoffs permit below-minimax punishments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates but gives clear theoretical boundaries and mechanisms.

- source: Heller, W. B., & Sieberg, K. K. (2010). Honor among thieves: Cooperation as a strategic response to functional unpleasantness. *European Journal of Political Economy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation and high group efficiency can emerge and persist in populations where all individuals are fundamentally self-interested and prefer to cheat, as long as some are willing to punish cheaters and some are strategic enough to cooperate when the risk of being punished is high. The presence of 'Strategic Unpleasant' types—who punish cheaters and cooperate only to avoid punishment—can lead to equilibria where most or all players cooperate, resulting in high average payoffs (efficiency). The model provides explicit payoff matrices and equilibrium conditions showing that, under reasonable parameter values (punishment not too costly, punishment more aversive than being cheated, etc.), punishment enables efficient outcomes. The analysis also shows that if punishment is too costly or ineffective, cooperation and efficiency break down.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public-goods-like environment will increase group efficiency, provided that punishment is not excessively costly and is sufficiently effective to deter cheating. The model clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost/impact structure of punishment and the strategic response of players. If the control (no-punishment) game is dominated by cheaters (low efficiency), introducing punishment—even by self-interested types—can shift the equilibrium to high efficiency. The paper's explicit payoff formulas and equilibrium conditions can be used to inform quantitative predictions, especially regarding the importance of punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the strategic incentives for cooperation.

Groups where the reward strategy was used spent more than twice as much time cooperating compared to those using punishment or no strategy.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence about the effects of punishment and reward on cooperation in a setting adjacent to public goods games. The findings suggest that punishment alone is generally ineffective at inducing sustained cooperation, while reward (and especially the combination of reward and communication) is much more effective. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is dyadic, with asymmetric payoffs and the ability for only one player to reward or punish, which differs from standard PGGs. Thus, the main value for prediction is as a caution that punishment may not always increase cooperation or efficiency, especially when reward is also available and more salient.
