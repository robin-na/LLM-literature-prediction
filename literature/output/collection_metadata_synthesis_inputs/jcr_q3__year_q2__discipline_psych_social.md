- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Dorrough, A., Glöckner, A., & Lee, B. (2017). Race for Power in Public Good Games with Unequal, Unstable Punishment Power. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2014). Optimal distribution of incentives for public cooperation in heterogeneous interaction environments. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Irwin, K., Mulder, L., & Simpson, B. (2014). The Detrimental Effects of Sanctions on Intragroup Trust: Comparing Punishments and Rewards. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Both punishment and reward systems, when present, can increase short-term cooperation (especially when sanctions are moderate rather than extreme), but their removal leads to a significant decline in trust, and in some cases, a decline in cooperation. Strong sanctions do not increase cooperation above control, and their removal reduces cooperation. Weaker sanctions increase cooperation while present, but their removal does not immediately reduce cooperation, though trust is still undermined. The negative effects on trust are similar for both punishment and reward. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings must be interpreted through the lens of contribution/cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the presence and removal of punishment (and reward) systems can have complex effects on cooperation, which is a behavioral proxy for efficiency but not a direct measure. Strong punishment does not increase cooperation above control, and its removal reduces cooperation. Weaker punishment increases cooperation while present, but its removal does not immediately reduce cooperation. The undermining of trust by sanctions is robust, regardless of sanction strength. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on the strength of the sanction and the baseline level of cooperation, but direct efficiency outcomes are not reported. Use caution when extrapolating to efficiency, as only contribution rates are measured.

- source: Nelissen, R. M. A., & Mulder, L. B. (2013). What makes a sanction stick? The effects of financial and social sanctions on norm compliance. *Social Influence*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both financial and social sanctions increase cooperation (measured as average contribution) in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-sanction control. Financial sanctions produce higher contributions than social sanctions while the sanctioning opportunity is present. However, after the removal of sanctions, contributions drop much more in the financial sanction condition than in the social sanction or control conditions. Social sanctions thus create more persistent cooperation norms. The study does not report group payoff or efficiency, but the contribution results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency effects in similar game designs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that both financial and social punishment mechanisms increase cooperation in a standard PGG, and that the effect is larger for financial punishment while the mechanism is active. However, the persistence of cooperation after punishment is removed is greater for social sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment will be positive for both financial and social punishment, but the durability of the effect may depend on the type of punishment. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution rates, but the mapping is straightforward in standard PGGs. The paper's design and results are highly informative for settings with 4 players, continuous contributions, no communication, and known MPCR.

- source: Seip, E. C., Van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2014). Anger motivates costly punishment of unfair behavior. *Motivation and Emotion*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across three laboratory experiments, the paper finds that (1) participants assign more costly punishment to less cooperative (unfair) players in a PGG, (2) anger mediates the relationship between perceived unfairness and punishment in both PGG and STG settings, and (3) experimentally induced anger increases costly punishment of unfair players in a subsequent STG. The studies do not report on group efficiency or total payoffs, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment is motivated by anger in PGG and STG settings, and that non-cooperation reliably triggers costly punishment. However, it does not provide any data or analysis on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, which are the primary outcomes for the downstream prediction task. Therefore, while the paper supports the behavioral mechanism by which punishment is enacted, it does not inform whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency under any game design dimension. The design details (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are reported and may be useful for mapping to other studies, but no efficiency outcomes are available.

- source: Park, S. A., Jeong, S., & Jeong, J. (2013). TV programs that denounce unfair advantage impact women's sensitivity to defection in the public goods game. *Social Neuroscience*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in a 10-round, 4-player, binary (all-or-nothing) threshold PGG, women are more cooperative than men in early rounds, but this difference disappears after both genders watch investigative TV programs about free-riding. After media exposure, women become more likely to free-ride in response to others' defection, adopting a tit-for-tat strategy. The main outcomes are cooperation rates and EEG measures; no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported. There is no punishment or reward mechanism in the game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. It does, however, provide detailed information about a binary, threshold PGG with 4 players and 10 rounds, and shows how external context (media exposure) can modulate cooperation rates, especially among women. For the prediction task, this paper is not directly informative about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, but it does confirm that in the absence of punishment, cooperation rates (and thus likely efficiency) can be sensitive to social context and gender. The absence of punishment and the focus on behavioral (not payoff) outcomes limit its direct relevance.

- source: Lierl, M. (2016). Social sanctions and informal accountability: Evidence from a laboratory experiment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment studies a public goods game with two beneficiaries and one provider, where the provider can embezzle contributions. The main manipulation is the structure of costly, mutual sanctioning (punishment and reward) relationships: (1) no sanctioning, (2) only among beneficiaries, (3) between provider and one or both beneficiaries, and (4) combinations thereof. The key finding is that mutual sanctioning among beneficiaries increases public goods provision and efficiency compared to no-sanctioning control, but this effect disappears or reverses when the provider is also part of the sanctioning network. The highest efficiency is observed when only beneficiaries can sanction each other. When both beneficiaries and the provider can mutually sanction, efficiency is not improved and can be lower than control. Actual punishment/reward is rare; the effect is mainly anticipatory. The results challenge the idea that denser or more encompassing sanctioning networks always improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games depends critically on the network structure of sanctioning relationships. If only beneficiaries can punish each other, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to control. If the provider can also punish or be punished, the effect is reduced or negative. For prediction, the presence and direction of punishment effects should be conditioned on who can punish whom. The game is a close variant of a standard PGG, with the addition of a third-party provider who can embezzle, so results are most directly applicable to co-production or agency settings. Control efficiency (no-sanction) is reported, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) varies by network structure. The findings suggest that simply enabling punishment is not always efficiency-improving; the details of the punishment network matter.

- source: Cason, T. N., & Gangadharan, L. (2016). Swords without covenants do not lead to self-governance. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment tested four treatments (baseline, punishment only, communication only, both) in a repeated nonlinear common pool resource game with groups of six. Peer punishment (costly, 3:1 fine-to-fee ratio, up to 5 points per target) did not significantly increase group yield or efficiency relative to baseline. Communication (anonymous chat, twice per session) led to dramatic and statistically significant increases in yield/efficiency, with most groups achieving near the social optimum. When both mechanisms were available, communication made punishment largely redundant and unused. The inefficacy of punishment is attributed to the complexity of the nonlinear environment and insufficient use of strong punishment. The findings challenge the generalizability of positive punishment effects from linear public goods games to more realistic, nonlinear settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in nonlinear common pool resource environments (with concave private benefits and endogenous group costs), enabling peer punishment does not improve efficiency over baseline. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar nonlinear games, one should expect little or no efficiency gain, even with a punishment technology that is effective in linear games. The presence of communication is a much stronger moderator, with large positive effects on efficiency. The results suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the payoff structure (linear vs nonlinear), and that control efficiency alone is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency without considering the game's nonlinearity and the availability of communication.

- source: Cardador, M. T., Northcraft, G. B., Rockmann, K. W., & Grant, B. C. (2016). Characteristics of affected third parties and cooperative behavior in social dilemmas. *Journal of Social Psychology*.

If the third party is seen as unreliable, this effect is diminished, and cooperation returns to baseline (no third party) levels. However, it does provide evidence that non-structural, prosocial motivational interventions (making third-party beneficiaries salient) can increase cooperation in the absence of punishment. The game design is a standard linear public goods game with four players, one-shot, no punishment, no reward, and no communication. - source: Antoci, A., & Zarri, L. (2015). Punish and perish?. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary success and efficiency impact of punishment in a prisoner's dilemma with explicit punishment and reward options depend on the type of strong reciprocity present. Altruistic punishment of defectors (SNR) can support cooperation and high average payoff, but only if punishers are sufficiently numerous; otherwise, defectors dominate and efficiency collapses. Altruistic rewarding (SPR) leads to persistent coexistence of types and fluctuating efficiency. Symmetric strong reciprocity (punishing defectors and rewarding cooperators) is maladaptive: it leads to the extinction of cooperators and punishers, resulting in universal defection and minimal efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (PNP) or both defectors and non-punishing cooperators (HSNR) can robustly enforce cooperation and maintain high efficiency, with the PNP strategy being especially robust. The presence of unconditional cooperators who do not punish can undermine the evolutionary stability of punishment-based cooperation. The model shows that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universally positive and can be negative or fragile depending on the sanctioning structure and population heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments is highly contingent on the details of the punishment and reward mechanisms and the behavioral heterogeneity of the population. In prediction tasks, the presence of explicit reward options, the type of punishment (who is punished), and the initial population composition are critical moderators. Punishment of defectors alone can increase efficiency if enough punishers are present, but this equilibrium is fragile. Adding reward can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (second-order free riders) is more robust in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is essential to consider not just the presence of punishment but also the structure of available sanctions and rewards, and the possibility of second-order free riding. The findings caution against assuming that enabling punishment will always increase efficiency; under some conditions, it can be maladaptive.

- source: Camera, G., & Gioffré, A. (2014). A tractable analysis of contagious equilibria. *Journal of Mathematical Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that contagious punishment (grim trigger strategies) in repeated random-matching social dilemmas can sustain full cooperation (i.e., maximal efficiency) if players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor), the cost of cooperation is not too high, and monitoring is sufficiently broad. The authors derive closed-form expressions for continuation payoffs and provide exact bounds on the discount factor and the cost of cooperation required to make cooperation self-enforcing. They show that increasing the breadth of monitoring (i.e., allowing players to observe more actions outside their own match) makes it easier to sustain cooperation, lowering the required discount factor and cost of cooperation. Conversely, if defection is not absorbing (i.e., if players can revert to cooperation), sustaining cooperation becomes harder. The results are general for random-matching repeated games with contagious punishment, and the framework is intended to inform both theory and laboratory design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the presence and structure of punishment (specifically, contagious, unforgiving punishment) can enable high efficiency in repeated social dilemmas, even with random matching and anonymity. The key moderators are the discount factor, the cost of cooperation, and the breadth of monitoring (how many others' actions are observed). The results suggest that, in environments with strong punishment and sufficient monitoring, enabling punishment should increase efficiency relative to control (no punishment), provided the other parameters (patience, cost) are favorable. The paper also provides explicit formulas for the parameter thresholds needed to sustain cooperation, which could be used to predict when punishment will be effective in raising efficiency. However, the results are theoretical and apply to repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-type games, not public goods games per se, though the logic is closely related.

- source: Myers, C. D. (2016). Participation and punishment. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that when punishment is possible, participation (turnout) increases dramatically (from 60% to 88%). Punishment is overwhelmingly directed at abstainers, and abstainers incur a net loss from being punished. Egoist subjects are more responsive to punishment than pro-social subjects, leading to a more egoist composition among participants when punishment is available. However, because participation is costly and does not increase the value of the prize, increased participation does not improve group efficiency or welfare. The study does not report treatment effects on efficiency or group payoff, and the design makes clear that increased participation is not efficiency-enhancing.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that punishment increases participation in a binary-choice, team-based contest game where participation is individually costly and does not increase group welfare. However, it does not provide evidence that punishment increases efficiency or group payoff; in fact, the design ensures that increased participation is efficiency-reducing. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment depends critically on the structure of the game: in games where participation is not efficiency-enhancing, punishment may increase costly participation without improving (and possibly reducing) efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding behavioral responses (participation/cooperation) to punishment, not for predicting efficiency outcomes in standard public goods games.

- source: Berger, J., & Hevenstone, D. (2016). Norm enforcement in the city revisited: An international field experiment of altruistic punishment, norm maintenance, and broken windows. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that direct and indirect norm enforcement behaviors (including costly punishment) occur in natural, anonymous, one-shot interactions, but at lower rates than in laboratory experiments. Enforcement is more common in Switzerland than in New York City. The presence of disorder (litter) reduces the likelihood of both direct and indirect enforcement.
