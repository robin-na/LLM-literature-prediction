- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Gürerk (2013). Social learning increases the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y. (2013). Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Irwin, K., Mulder, L., & Simpson, B. (2014). The Detrimental Effects of Sanctions on Intragroup Trust: Comparing Punishments and Rewards. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Both punishment and reward systems, when present, can increase short-term cooperation (especially when sanctions are moderate rather than extreme), but their removal leads to a significant decline in trust, and in some cases, a decline in cooperation. Strong sanctions do not increase cooperation above control, and their removal reduces cooperation. Weaker sanctions increase cooperation while present, but their removal does not immediately reduce cooperation, though trust is still undermined. The negative effects on trust are similar for both punishment and reward. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings must be interpreted through the lens of contribution/cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the presence and removal of punishment (and reward) systems can have complex effects on cooperation, which is a behavioral proxy for efficiency but not a direct measure. Strong punishment does not increase cooperation above control, and its removal reduces cooperation. Weaker punishment increases cooperation while present, but its removal does not immediately reduce cooperation. The undermining of trust by sanctions is robust, regardless of sanction strength. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on the strength of the sanction and the baseline level of cooperation, but direct efficiency outcomes are not reported. Use caution when extrapolating to efficiency, as only contribution rates are measured.

- source: dos Santos, M., & Wedekind, C. (2015). Reputation based on punishment rather than generosity allows for evolution of cooperation in sizable groups. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper shows that in simulated public goods games, a reputation system based on punitive actions (punishment scoring) allows for the evolution and maintenance of high cooperation frequencies in large groups, even when errors in reputation assessment are present. In contrast, reputation based on generosity (contributions) or rewards is much less effective: generosity-based reputation rarely leads to cooperation in large groups, and reward-based systems are unstable due to invasion by non-rewarders. The model assumes binary contribution and punishment choices, with payoffs used for evolutionary selection, but does not directly report group efficiency or total payoff as outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment-based reputation mechanisms are more effective than generosity- or reward-based reputation for sustaining cooperation in public goods games, especially as group size increases and errors in reputation assessment become more likely. However, because the main outcome is cooperation frequency rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting the likely positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, especially in large groups and error-prone environments. The model assumes all-or-nothing contribution and punishment choices, no communication, and full observability of actions (except for errors in reputation memory). The results are most informative for predicting the effect of punishment in large, anonymous, repeated PGGs with reputation tracking, but do not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency.

- source: Skatova, A., & Ferguson, E. (2013). Individual differences in behavioural inhibition explain free riding in public good games when punishment is expected but not implemented. *Behavioral and Brain Functions*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both the implementation and the expectation of punishment (even when not actually implemented) increase individual contributions in a one-shot PGG compared to standard PGGs without punishment. Implemented punishment leads to higher contributions than non-implemented punishment. Individual differences in BIS-anxiety (a measure of behavioral inhibition) predict higher contributions and less free riding when punishment is expected but not implemented.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that both implemented and expected (but not implemented) centralized punishment increase contributions in a one-shot PGG, with implemented punishment having a stronger effect. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in contribution rates under different punishment regimes and for understanding the role of individual differences in behavioral inhibition. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling punishment (even if not always implemented) increases cooperation, but the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred from the reported outcomes.

- source: Villatoro, D., Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Nardin, L. G., Sabater-Mir, J., & Conte, R. (2014). The Norm-Signaling Effects of Group Punishment: Combining Agent-Based Simulation and Laboratory Experiments. *Social Science Computer Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when punishment is inflicted by the entire group, human subjects are more likely to cooperate (i.e., contribute to the public good) than when punishment is uncoordinated, even though the material cost to defectors is held constant. The authors attribute this to the norm-signaling function of group punishment, which is hypothesized to activate both instrumental and normative motivations for compliance. Agent-based simulations support the idea that norm salience, as modeled in the EMIL-I-A architecture, can explain the observed human behavior. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so the findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (contribution rates).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of punishment (specifically, whether it is coordinated by the group or uncoordinated) can significantly affect cooperation rates in public goods games, even when the material incentives are held constant. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that group punishment may lead to higher efficiency than uncoordinated punishment, but this is inferred from contribution rates rather than directly measured. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of group punishment structure and norm-signaling on cooperation, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency when combined with other sources.

Financial sanctions produce higher contributions than social sanctions while the sanctioning opportunity is present. However, after the removal of sanctions, contributions drop much more in the financial sanction condition than in the social sanction or control conditions. Social sanctions thus create more persistent cooperation norms. The study does not report group payoff or efficiency, but the contribution results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency effects in similar game designs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that both financial and social punishment mechanisms increase cooperation in a standard PGG, and that the effect is larger for financial punishment while the mechanism is active. However, the persistence of cooperation after punishment is removed is greater for social sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment will be positive for both financial and social punishment, but the durability of the effect may depend on the type of punishment. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff reporting means that predictions must be inferred from contribution rates, but the mapping is straightforward in standard PGGs. The paper's design and results are highly informative for settings with 4 players, continuous contributions, no communication, and known MPCR.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on behavior in PGGs is moderated by group identity: punishment is less frequent and less intense in in-groups, and anger-like emotions drive punishment more in out-groups. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in settings with identity-heterogeneous groups, punishment is more likely to be used, which (given the costliness of punishment) may reduce efficiency relative to in-groups. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms and moderators of punishment, but not about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Thöni, C. (2014). Inequality aversion and antisocial punishment. *Theory and Decision*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that, while inequality aversion can theoretically motivate antisocial punishment under certain conditions, empirical data from several public goods game experiments indicate that most antisocial punishment acts do not fit this explanation. Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The findings suggest that other motives, such as revenge or a desire to harm others, are more likely explanations for antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

- source: Riedel, N., & Schildberg-Hörisch, H. (2013). Asymmetric obligations. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  findings: The experiment shows that both high and low minimum contribution obligations (even when only weakly enforced) increase contributions in the initial periods compared to no obligation, but this effect is not sustained in the long run.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that weakly enforced minimum contribution obligations can temporarily increase contributions in public goods games, regardless of whether obligations are symmetric or asymmetric. The findings suggest that expressive law (weak obligations) can have a short-term effect on cooperation, but do not inform about the efficiency effects of endogenous peer punishment. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of a standard punishment treatment limits direct applicability to the prediction of efficiency under peer punishment.
