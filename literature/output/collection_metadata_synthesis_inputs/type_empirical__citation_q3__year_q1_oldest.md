- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2012). I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

- source: Kocher, M., Martinsson, P., & Visser, M. (2012). Social background, cooperative behavior, and norm enforcement. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper reports on one-shot public goods games with and without punishment, conducted in four South African high schools representing different socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. The introduction of punishment increased contributions significantly only in the white community, and only modestly or insignificantly in the others. Punishment was more frequent and severe in the African communities, leading to lower average earnings in the punishment condition for those groups. The study finds that social background variables (such as trust and household violence) explain much of the variation in cooperation and punishment. Overall, the efficiency impact of punishment is mixed: in some groups, punishment increases contributions but also reduces earnings due to the cost of punishment, sometimes resulting in no net efficiency gain.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in one-shot, three-player public goods games with MPCR=0.5 and a punishment technology of 1:3 (cost:impact) is highly context-dependent. In groups with low baseline contributions, punishment may increase contributions and potentially efficiency, but in groups with already high contributions, punishment may not increase cooperation and can reduce efficiency due to punishment costs. The presence of anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) can further reduce efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is a strong moderator: if baseline contributions are high, punishment may not improve and can even worsen efficiency. Social background and group norms are important moderators, but are not directly encoded in the game design dimensions. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will reliably increase efficiency, especially in one-shot or low-trust settings.

- source: Fatas, E., Morales, A. J., & Ubeda, P. (2010). Blind justice: An experimental analysis of random punishment in team production. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a public goods game with a novel random punishment mechanism: after each round, each group member is excluded from the group benefit with probability 1-R, where R is the normalized group contribution. This 'blind' punishment does not target individual defectors. The experiment compares three conditions: standard VCM (no punishment), random punishment without redistribution (RP), and random punishment with redistribution (RPR). Results show that both RP and RPR significantly increase contributions compared to VCM. However, only RPR increases group earnings (efficiency), as RP's punishment cost outweighs the contribution gains. The effect of random punishment is similar whether introduced after a baseline or from the start. Behavioral analysis shows that high contributors reduce their contributions if punished, but overall, conditional cooperation persists and the net effect is positive for contributions. The mechanism does not fully prevent the decline in contributions over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that random, non-individually-targeted punishment can increase contributions in a standard public goods game, but its effect on efficiency depends on whether the penalty is redistributed. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism that is not individually targeted but is probabilistically linked to group performance will likely increase contributions, but may not increase efficiency unless the punishment cost is recycled within the group. The effect is robust to order and does not depend on communication or knowledge of individual contributions. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5, and the group size is 4. The findings suggest that, for similar designs, enabling random punishment will increase contributions and may increase efficiency if the punishment cost is not lost to the group.

- source: Sell, J., & Wilson, R. K. (1999). The maintenance of cooperation: Expectations of future interaction and the trigger of group punishment. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that requiring a grim trigger punishment rule (where defection locks a player into permanent defection) substantially increases cooperation rates in a repeated public goods game. The effect of the discount parameter (expected number of rounds) on cooperation is only significant when the grim trigger is imposed, and only between the highest and lower values. Without a mandated punishment phase, increasing the discount parameter does not increase cooperation. The main dependent variable is the proportion of cooperative choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that group-level punishment threats can coordinate expectations and sustain cooperation, but the value of future interaction alone is insufficient unless paired with credible punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the imposition of a group-level, long punishment phase (grim trigger) increases cooperation rates in repeated public goods games, especially in the early rounds. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a strong group punishment mechanism (even if not individual-targeted) will increase cooperation and likely improve efficiency, especially when players expect many rounds. The effect of expected future interaction (discount parameter) is only realized when punishment is credible and severe. For prediction tasks, the presence of a mandated, long punishment phase should be treated as a strong positive moderator of efficiency, but the absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative inference.

- source: Messer, K. D., & Zarghamee, H. (2007). New hope for the voluntary contributions mechanism: The effects of context. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that context—specifically, the combination of cheap talk, voting, and a status quo of giving—can dramatically increase and sustain contributions and efficiency in a voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) with a low MPCR. In the baseline (no context), efficiency starts at 82.3% and decays to 72.6% by round 10. In the full-context treatment, efficiency is 99.0% initially and 98.1% in the final round, with no significant decay. Cheap talk and status quo of giving each increase initial contributions, but only their interaction (and with voting) sustains high contributions. Voting alone has no effect. The results show that context can substitute for incentive-compatible mechanisms in achieving high efficiency, but the paper does not test or discuss punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly informative about the effects of communication, voting, and default framing, but not about punishment. For the downstream prediction task—predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency—this paper is not directly relevant. However, it does provide a strong benchmark for how non-punitive contextual factors can achieve high efficiency, which may be useful for comparative purposes or for understanding the limits of punishment's added value in similar settings.

- source: Cadigan, J., Wayland, P. T., Schmitt, P., & Swope, K. (2011). An experimental dynamic public goods game with carryover. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing dynamic carryover (either via endowment or MPCR) can increase first-stage contributions in public goods games, but this does not reliably translate into higher efficiency. Efficiency, defined as the share of maximal cooperative gains captured, is not significantly different from the baseline in most treatments, except when MPCR is increased. The main determinants of contributions and efficiency are endowment and MPCR, consistent with prior VCM literature. There is no evidence on punishment or sanctioning, as these mechanisms are not present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence on how dynamic features like carryover in endowment or MPCR affect efficiency and contributions in public goods games without punishment. It shows that increasing contributions via dynamic incentives does not necessarily increase efficiency, especially if the maximum possible surplus also increases. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency is primarily determined by MPCR and endowment, and that dynamic features alone do not guarantee higher efficiency. The paper does not inform about the effects of punishment, so it should not be used to predict the effect of enabling punishment in similar games.

- source: Pan, X. S., & Houser, D. (2011). Competition for Trophies Triggers Male Generosity. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that in a standard linear public goods game, the introduction of a unique and displayable reward (a 'trophy' mug) leads to significantly higher contributions, especially among males, compared to both a baseline (no reward) and a control reward (ice-cream) that is not unique or displayable. The effect is driven by male competitiveness for the trophy. Female behavior is less affected by the trophy. The paper does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, nor does it report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as outcomes. The main outcomes are behavioral: contribution rates, approval points, and star rewards.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games. It is relevant for understanding the effects of non-pecuniary, status-based rewards (trophies) on cooperation, especially among males, but does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The game design dimensions are well described, but the absence of punishment and lack of efficiency or payoff analysis means this paper should not be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: My, K. B., & Chalvignac, B. (2010). Voluntary participation and cooperation in a collective-good game. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that allowing voluntary participation (exit option) in a repeated public-good game does not increase average contributions compared to the baseline with compulsory participation, unless the exit option is highly attractive. In the VP-16 treatment (exit payoff = 16), the decay in contributions over time is counteracted, and in some groups, contributions even increase in later rounds. However, average contributions are not significantly higher than in the baseline. The less attractive exit option (VP-11) does not have a positive effect. No punishment or sanctioning is present in any treatment. The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-good games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. It does, however, provide informative context about the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency and contribution dynamics in a standard linear PGG with and without voluntary participation. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only useful for understanding the control (no-punishment) condition, not the treatment (punishment-enabled) condition. It does not inform the effect of punishment or moderators of punishment effects.

- source: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., De Cremer, D., & Wilke, H. A. M. (2006). When sanctions fail to increase cooperation in social dilemmas: Considering the presence of an alternative option to defect. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across two lab experiments, the authors find that introducing a sanctioning system (punishment for defection) increases collective outcomes (efficiency) in a standard two-option social dilemma (cooperate or defect). However, when a third option to defect (alternative defection, more harmful to the group) is available, sanctions do not increase efficiency and can even decrease it, particularly when participants have low expectations of others' cooperation. The negative effect is mediated by the failure of sanctions to increase trust or expectations of cooperation in the presence of the third option. Thus, the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is contingent on the decision structure and social expectations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games is not uniform: it is positive in standard two-choice settings but can be null or negative when alternative, more harmful defection options are available and when group members have low expectations of cooperation. For prediction, this means that the presence of alternative defection options (i.e., more than two choices, with at least one being more harmful to the group) and low trust/expectations are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the game allows for alternative defection, enabling punishment may not improve and could even worsen efficiency, especially if group trust is low. In standard two-choice games, punishment is likely to increase efficiency.

- source: Guillen, P., Schwieren, C., & Staffiero, G. (2007). Why feed the Leviathan?. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that a centralized sanctioning mechanism (CSM) in a step-level public good game leads to a dramatic increase in the rate at which groups successfully provide the public good (i.e., reach the threshold). In the baseline (no CSM), groups almost never reach the threshold after a few rounds. With CSM, the threshold is reached in nearly 90% of rounds. After CSM is removed, groups with prior CSM experience continue to reach the threshold at much higher rates than baseline groups (AR: 65%, VR: 48%, BL: 0% in rounds 8-14). The effect is attributed to an 'educational' or coordination-facilitating role of CSM. Voting on CSM removal does not improve post-removal outcomes compared to automatic removal. Failures to reach the threshold during the CSM phase predict lower post-CSM success. The CSM is costly (3 units per player per round), so while it increases the likelihood of public good provision, it also reduces per-round payoffs when active. The paper does not directly report efficiency ratios, but the increased provision rate implies higher group welfare relative to baseline.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a centralized punishment mechanism in a threshold (step-level) public good game increases the rate of successful public good provision, both during and after the punishment phase. The effect persists even after punishment is removed, supporting the idea of an 'educational' or coordination effect. The CSM is costly, so the net efficiency gain depends on the balance between increased public good provision and the cost of the mechanism. For prediction, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to substantially increase efficiency (proxied by provision rate) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially in step-level games with coordination problems. The effect is robust to whether removal is automatic or by vote. However, the paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must use provision rate as a proxy.

- source: Rebers, S., & Koopmans, R. (2012). Altruistic Punishment and Between-Group Competition Evidence from n-person Prisoner's Dilemmas. *Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=other_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The experiment shows that participants spend more resources punishing defectors in n-person prisoner's dilemma games when there is intergroup competition, compared to single-group conditions. However, this increase is explained by participants' expectations about others' punishment behavior, and the direct effect of competition becomes insignificant when controlling for these expectations. There is no effect of intergroup competition on the punishment of cooperators. The study uses a one-shot (single-round) design, so it does not assess the long-term effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency. No direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that intergroup competition increases punishment of defectors, but does not affect punishment of cooperators, in a one-shot n-person prisoner's dilemma with a punishment option. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design is close to a public goods game, but not identical. The findings suggest that in similar one-shot games, punishment may be more frequent under intergroup competition, but the downstream effect on efficiency is not measured. The results are most useful for understanding behavioral mechanisms (punishment behavior) rather than efficiency outcomes.

- source: Hayo, B., & Vollan, B. (2012). Group interaction, heterogeneity, rules, and co-operative behaviour: Evidence from a common-pool resource experiment in South Africa and Namibia. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: This field-lab experiment in rural South Africa and Namibia used a repeated common-pool resource game with 5-person groups over 20 rounds. After 10 rounds, groups voted to implement one of three rules: penalty (punishment), reward, or communication. The introduction of any rule led to a significant reduction in resource extraction (i.e., more cooperation), but there was no significant difference between the effects of penalty, reward, or communication rules on extraction levels after accounting for group dynamics. Group composition (e.g., gender, wealth heterogeneity) and dynamic social interactions had larger effects on cooperation than the specific institutional rule. Payoff-related outcomes (individual and group payoffs) were analyzed as predictors of behavior, but efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum) was not directly reported. The main behavioral outcome is the number of sheep chosen, which is inversely related to cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing a punishment (penalty) rule in a repeated common-pool resource game with 5 players and 20 rounds improves cooperation (reduces extraction), thus likely increasing efficiency relative to the control (no-rule) condition. However, the effect of punishment is not significantly different from reward or communication rules after accounting for group dynamics. The efficiency gain from punishment is thus positive, but the magnitude is similar to that of other rule-based interventions. Group composition and social context are strong moderators. For prediction, the presence of a punishment rule should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no rule, but the effect size may be modest and context-dependent.

- source: Seip, E. C., van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2009). On Hotheads and Dirty Harries The Primacy of Anger in Altruistic Punishment. *Values, Empathy, and Fairness Across Social Barriers*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The main finding is that anger, rather than the perception of unfairness, is the primary driver of altruistic punishment in social dilemma games. Experimental evidence from a one-shot, three-person public goods game with punishment shows that lower contributions from others evoke more anger and lead to harsher punishment, and that the effect of others' contributions on punishment is fully mediated by experienced anger. Priming participants with anger increases their tendency to punish defectors. The paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. However, it does suggest that the presence and intensity of punishment are strongly influenced by emotional responses (especially anger) to defection or low contribution. For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment is likely to be used when players experience anger at norm violations, but the paper does not address whether this increases or decreases group efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment is deployed, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Bracht, J., Figuières, C., & Ratto, M. (2008). Relative performance of two simple incentive mechanisms in a public goods experiment. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares two incentive mechanisms for public goods provision: the Falkinger mechanism (which rewards/penalizes deviations from average contributions) and the compensation mechanism (where players can subsidize each other's contributions). Both mechanisms increase contributions and group efficiency compared to the voluntary contribution control. The Falkinger mechanism, particularly with higher tax-subsidy rates (closer to the efficient value), leads to significantly higher and more stable efficiency gains than the compensation mechanism. The compensation mechanism initially boosts contributions, but this effect decays over time, and average efficiency remains lower than under the Falkinger mechanism. The Falkinger mechanism's performance is robust to parameter changes and predicts average contributions well, while the compensation mechanism suffers from suboptimal subsidy rates and coordination issues.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment-and-reward mechanism (Falkinger) in a two-player, repeated, continuous-contribution public goods game with full information and no communication substantially increases efficiency relative to the control. The effect is stronger and more reliable when the punishment/reward parameter is set closer to the efficient value. The compensation (reward-only) mechanism also increases efficiency, but less so, and the effect is less stable. For prediction, the paper supports a positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is well-calibrated. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed pairs, continuous contribution, no chat, and full feedback settings.

- source: Vyrastekova, J., & van Soest, D. (2008). On the (in)effectiveness of rewards in sustaining cooperation. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: In a repeated CPR game with groups of 5 over 15 rounds, the introduction of net positive rewards (1:3 impact ratio) leads to significantly lower extraction (closer to the social optimum) and higher CPR-stage efficiency compared to both the unregulated baseline and the transfer reward (1:1) treatment. Transfer rewards do not affect extraction or efficiency. However, total group efficiency (including the reward stage) is similar across treatments, as the efficiency gains in the CPR stage with net positive rewards are offset by welfare losses from not using all reward tokens. The study demonstrates that the effectiveness of rewards in sustaining cooperation and improving efficiency depends critically on the reward's impact ratio.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of peer-based incentives in public-goods-like environments depends on the technology of the incentive: net positive rewards (where the recipient's gain exceeds the giver's cost) can increase efficiency, while transfer rewards (1:1) do not. Although the study uses a CPR game (public bad) rather than a standard public goods game, the structure is closely related. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of enabling a reward mechanism, the reward impact ratio (reward_tech) is a critical moderator. The absence of punishment means the results are not directly about punishment, but the logic may transfer to the design of positive incentives. For prediction, the control (no-reward) efficiency is not improved by transfer rewards, but is improved by net positive rewards in the CPR stage. However, total efficiency may not increase if the reward mechanism itself is not fully utilized.

- source: Bolle, F., & Vogel, C. (2011). Power comes with responsibility-or does it?. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Transferring responsibility to a single allocator who determines the contributions of all group members leads to higher average group income (efficiency) than the standard public goods game with voluntary contributions. This effect is observed both when the allocator is randomly assigned and when elected, though the difference between these modes is small. Over repeated phases, allocators become more selfish, reducing their own contributions, but group income remains higher than in the standard PG game. No direct punishment or reward mechanism is present, but the allocator's power to assign contributions functions as a form of enforced cooperation. The study does not find evidence for indirect punishment (allocators do not systematically penalize previous free riders).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a public goods environment, shifting from voluntary contribution to a regime where one player has the power to assign contributions (i.e., a strong form of centralized enforcement) increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to the standard game. While this is not a punishment mechanism per se, it is adjacent to the prediction task because it shows that enforced contributions (even without explicit punishment costs) can improve efficiency. The findings suggest that, in settings where voluntary cooperation is inefficient, introducing a mechanism for enforced contributions (or strong centralized control) can increase group payoff. However, the absence of explicit punishment or cost for enforcement means the results are not directly transferable to standard punishment treatments, but they are informative for understanding the upper bound of efficiency gains from strong enforcement.
