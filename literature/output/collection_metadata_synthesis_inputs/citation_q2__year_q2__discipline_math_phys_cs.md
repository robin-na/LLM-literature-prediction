- source: Cong, R., Li, K., Wang, L., & Zhao, Q. C. (2016). Cooperation induced by wise incentive allocation in spontaneous institution. *Epl*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with optional institution membership, the optimal regime for cooperation and group efficiency is achieved when the institution balances punishment and reward. Pure punishment or pure reward is suboptimal: too much punishment collapses the institution, while too much reward allows institutional defectors to dominate. The efficiency of punishment and reward (i.e., their impact per unit cost) increases the range of conditions under which cooperation prevails. The model provides explicit conditions for when institutional cooperators outcompete defectors, and shows that the stationary distribution of strategies (and thus group efficiency) is maximized at intermediate values of the punishment/reward split.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the balance between punishment and reward, as well as the effectiveness of each. It suggests that enabling punishment alone may not always increase efficiency; the presence and strength of reward, and the cost-effectiveness of both, are crucial moderators. For prediction tasks, the model implies that intermediate punishment (with some reward) is optimal for efficiency, and that both the cost and impact of punishment and reward should be considered. The findings are directly relevant for settings where institutional incentives are endogenous and both punishment and reward are possible.

- source: Yao, Y., & Chen, S. S. (2014). Multiplicative noise enhances spatial reciprocity. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, both strong altruistic punishment and reward are unable to sustain cooperation when implemented in the traditional (non-tax-based) way, due to their high cost. When a tax-based mechanism is introduced—where all players pay a tax that is redistributed to punishers or rewarders—these strategies become viable, leading to stable coexistence of cooperators and punishers/rewarders and the elimination of defectors. This results in higher group efficiency and welfare. Tax-based strong altruistic reward leads to even higher cooperation and efficiency than tax-based strong altruistic punishment under equivalent tax conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the introduction of a tax-based institutional mechanism for funding punishment or reward can transform the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games from negative or neutral to strongly positive. The results suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling tax-based punishment or reward will substantially increase efficiency, with reward being even more effective than punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with strong institutional support and compulsory tax collection, and less so to voluntary or peer-based punishment. The model is deterministic and assumes infinite, well-mixed populations, so predictions should be adjusted for finite or noisy environments.

- source: Sasaki, T. (2014). The Evolution of Cooperation Through Institutional Incentives and Optional Participation. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with optional participation, institutional punishment (penalties) is highly effective at promoting full cooperation and maximizing group welfare, even when the same level of punishment would only produce bi-stability (coordination problem) under compulsory participation. Optional participation allows the population to escape from the inefficient defection equilibrium, making cooperation globally stable for a wide range of punishment strengths. In contrast, institutional rewards combined with optional participation do not robustly promote cooperation or welfare: they often lead to non-participation and only marginally improve group welfare over the baseline. The model provides explicit analytical thresholds for when each regime occurs, and shows that the minimal punishment needed to achieve full cooperation decreases as group size increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with optional participation and institutional punishment, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency (group welfare) compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, rounds, and incentive strengths, provided the punishment is above a threshold that decreases with group size. The model also predicts that institutional rewards are much less effective in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when optional participation and institutional punishment are present. However, the results are theoretical and assume infinite populations and well-mixed groups, so empirical generalizability may be limited.

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2017). Multiple tolerances dilute the second order cooperative dilemma. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper provides a unified theoretical framework for public goods games with multiple tolerant punishment strategies. It shows that: (1) Less tolerant punishers (those who punish with fewer defectors present) are always more abundant and thus more evolutionarily successful than more tolerant ones. (2) The presence of punishment strategies increases group efficiency (measured by the abundance of cooperation and punishers) when the fine for defectors exceeds a threshold determined by group size, enhancement factor, and punishment cost. (3) Larger group sizes can facilitate the evolution of punishment and thus higher group efficiency, but only when the enhancement factor and punishment cost are small. (4) The paper provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment prevails, and these are validated by simulations. (5) The main outcome is the stationary abundance of strategies, which is directly linked to average payoffs and group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games. It provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment increases the abundance of cooperation and group efficiency, as a function of group size, enhancement factor (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency, especially in larger groups when costs are low, and that less tolerant (more responsive) punishment is more effective. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency of games with and without punishment, given the game design parameters. However, the results are derived under evolutionary dynamics with weak selection and large populations, so care should be taken when applying them to small groups or different update rules.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Jacobs, G. S., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2014). Nonequivalence of updating rules in evolutionary games under high mutation rates. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, the choice of updating rule (BP, DP, LD) can dramatically change the long-run frequencies of defectors, cooperators, and punishers. For the same parameter values, the birth-death process leads to a population dominated by defectors, while the death-birth process results in a majority of cooperative strategies (cooperators and punishers). Link dynamics yields intermediate results. These differences arise even in well-mixed populations and are especially pronounced when mutation rates are not negligible and when more than two strategies interact. The paper does not report group efficiency or payoff, but the shift from defector to cooperator/punisher dominance implies a likely increase in group welfare under some updating rules.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the updating rule (a feature of evolutionary dynamics, not typically manipulated in lab experiments) can be a major moderator of the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, on efficiency. In public goods games with punishment, the same game design (including punishment parameters) can yield very different long-run outcomes depending on the updating rule: punishment may be ineffective (defector-dominated) under one rule and highly effective (cooperator/punisher-dominated) under another. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as indicating that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the evolutionary dynamics assumed, especially in theoretical or agent-based models. For empirical prediction, this suggests caution in generalizing across studies with different evolutionary assumptions.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2016). Cooperation and punishment in community-structured populations with migration. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in community-structured populations playing a linear PGG with punishment, the presence of group competition (where entire communities can replace each other based on average fitness) or fitness-based migration (where individuals are more likely to migrate if they are fitter) can sustain high levels of cooperation and punishment, suppressing defection in parameter regions where defection would otherwise dominate. Constant (random) migration does not have this effect. The transition between cooperative and defective regimes is sharper with migration, but only group competition and fitness-based migration expand the parameter space where cooperation and punishment are stable. The main reported outcomes are the frequencies of strategies, not efficiency or group payoff, but higher frequencies of cooperation and punishment imply higher group payoffs relative to defection-dominated regimes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task: in PGG-like environments with punishment, the presence of group competition or fitness-based migration mechanisms can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation and punishment, and thus are likely to increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control games without punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is based on the frequencies of cooperative and punishing strategies. The results suggest that, for the same game design dimensions, enabling punishment in a context with group competition or fitness-based migration will likely lead to higher efficiency than in well-mixed or constant migration settings. The findings are most relevant for models with large group sizes, high mutation and migration rates, and community structure.

This threshold depends negatively on the population size and the public goods multiplier, and positively on the group size. However, it does provide theoretical insight into how reward mechanisms (distinct from punishment) can affect the evolution of cooperation and group payoff. For the downstream prediction task focused on punishment, this paper is not directly relevant, but may be useful for understanding the comparative role of reward-based interventions.

- source: Reeves, T., Ohtsuki, H., & Fukui, S. (2017). Asymmetric public goods game cooperation through pest control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in asymmetric public goods games (modeled as pest control among farmers), the Nash equilibrium investment and resulting efficiency depend strongly on the discount factor and the number of players. There are threshold effects: only farmers close to the risk (frontier) contribute when the discount factor is low, while more distant farmers contribute only if the discount factor is high enough. Increasing the number of players does not always increase efficiency; in some parameter regions, adding more players can paradoxically reduce total investment and group welfare. Asymmetry leads to a disproportionate burden on those at greatest risk, making the system more vulnerable to shocks. The model does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how efficiency in asymmetric public goods games depends on player count, discount factor, and the structure of risk, but it does not address punishment or sanctioning. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is highly informative for baseline (control) efficiency in asymmetric, no-punishment settings, especially regarding the effects of group size and discounting. However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no such mechanism is present or analyzed. Its findings suggest that efficiency can be non-monotonic in group size and is sensitive to asymmetry and time preferences, which should be considered when predicting control efficiency, but it cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment.

- source: Sun, C. H., & Duan, H. B. (2015). Impact of shill intervention on the evolution of cooperation. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The introduction of shill agents with a Fixed-Cost-per-Player contribution rule into a PGG on a scale-free network can substantially increase both cooperation rates and average payoffs (social welfare), particularly when shills are preferentially placed on high-degree nodes and the dilemma is severe (low enhancement factor). The effect is strongest for high-degree-preference shill placement (large alpha) and in harsh environments. As the environment becomes more favorable (higher enhancement factor), local cooperators reappear and can coexist with shill cooperators, and distributing shills on low-degree nodes (small alpha) can maximize social welfare. The paper provides detailed simulation evidence on how these effects depend on the enhancement factor, shill fraction, and shill placement, but does not study punishment or sanctioning.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments with external intervention via shill agents, but not for environments with punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that introducing agents with alternative contribution rules (partial free-riders) can increase efficiency in harsh environments, especially if they are placed on network hubs. The results are parameter-dependent and highlight the importance of network structure and agent placement. However, since the paper does not involve punishment, its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled vs. punishment-disabled efficiency is limited. It is most useful for understanding the effects of external intervention and heterogeneity in contribution rules on efficiency.

Its results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in control games without punishment, or in environments where clustering and spatial structure are the main mechanisms supporting cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how efficiency depends on clustering, internal benefit allocation, and the benefit-to-cost ratio, but offers no evidence about punishment effects.

For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly informative. For tasks considering alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms, the findings suggest that structural and learning modifications can substitute for punishment in promoting cooperation, but the effect on group efficiency is not quantified.

- source: Wang, Y. F., Vasilakos, A. V., & Ma, J. H. (2015). VPEF: A Simple and Effective Incentive Mechanism in Community-Based Autonomous Networks. *Ieee Transactions on Network and Service Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: VPEF, an incentive mechanism based on voluntary participation, round-based entry fees, and voluntary costly punishment, leads to high efficiency in community-based collaborative networks. The system spends most of its time in the punisher state, where all participants contribute and punish defectors, minimizing efficiency loss.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling voluntary costly punishment in a public-goods-like environment with voluntary participation and a small entry fee can dramatically increase efficiency, driving the system to near-full cooperation (punisher state) and minimizing efficiency loss. The efficiency loss formula (4/(8+M)) can be used to predict the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, given the number of users. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values, provided the multiplexing/diversity factor is above 1 and defectors can be reliably detected. The model is most applicable to dynamic, anonymous, and voluntary participation settings, and less so to settings with continuous contribution levels or where punishment/reward is not voluntary.

However, the coercive introduction of a small number of always-defecting agents (the 'social vaccine') stabilizes the punishment system, prevents the drift toward non-punishing cooperators, and maintains a highly cooperative regime.
