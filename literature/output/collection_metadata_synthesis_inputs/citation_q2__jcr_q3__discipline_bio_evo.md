- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Wolff, I. (2012). Retaliation and the role for punishment in the evolution of cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with punishment and the possibility of retaliation, punishment can help induce partially cooperative equilibria and temporarily increase cooperation and group payoff, especially when mutation is present. However, punishment cannot sustain full cooperation in the presence of even small mutation rates, as the costs of punishment and the threat of retaliation allow non-punishing cooperators and defectors to invade. The efficiency gains from punishment are thus limited and parameter-dependent: punishment is most effective at intermediate continuation probabilities and when retaliation is not too strong. At high continuation probabilities or when retaliation is easy, punishment is driven out and cannot maintain high efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in repeated public goods games is highly sensitive to the possibility of retaliation and the evolutionary dynamics of strategy mutation. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, one should consider whether retaliation is possible, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the continuation probability (analogous to number of rounds or game length), and the presence of mutation or experimentation. Punishment can increase efficiency in some cases, but its effect is undermined by retaliation and high continuation probability. The model suggests that punishment is most likely to improve efficiency when it is effective (high punishment impact per cost), retaliation is costly or rare, and the game is not too long. If retaliation is easy and the game is long, punishment may not improve and may even reduce efficiency.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Perc, M., & Xu, H. Y. (2020). The Singaporean model in public goods dilemmas with benevolent leaders and bribery. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that exclusion-based punishment by benevolent leaders, especially when leaders are incentivized by group performance (the Singaporean model), can sustain cooperation and high group efficiency, overcoming the second-order free-rider problem. However, as the cost of exclusion increases, or as bribery becomes more prevalent, the ability of punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency declines, leading to possible collapse into all-defector states. Increasing the performance-based incentive for leaders (parameter alpha) raises the threshold at which punishment remains effective. Bribery undermines the effectiveness of punishment, but its negative impact can be mitigated if the number of bribers is kept low relative to leaders or if the cost of bribery is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the presence of performance-based incentives for punishers, and the prevalence and cost of corruption/bribery. When punishment is low-cost and/or leaders are strongly incentivized by group performance, enabling punishment increases efficiency. When punishment is costly or easily undermined by bribery, enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. For prediction, the model suggests that game designs with low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, and strong performance incentives for punishers will see positive efficiency effects from punishment, while high costs or high corruption will see little or negative effect.

- source: Wang, X. J., & Lv, S. J. (2019). The public goods game with shared punishment cost in well-mixed and structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment with shared cost among cooperators in a public goods game can promote and stabilize cooperation, thereby increasing group efficiency and average payoff, provided the penalty imposed on defectors is large enough compared to the punishment cost. In well-mixed populations, this can transform the game from one dominated by defection to one where full cooperation is stable. In structured populations, cooperation can emerge at lower fine thresholds and coexist with defection, but as the penalty increases, well-mixed populations become more favorable for cooperation than structured ones. The effect of punishment is thus generally positive for efficiency, but the benefit depends on the relative size of the fine, the punishment cost, and the group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment (with shared cost among cooperators) increases efficiency and group payoff when the fine is sufficiently large relative to the cost. The effect is modulated by group size (player_count), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech, i.e., fine per cost). Structured populations (networked interactions) can allow cooperation to emerge at lower fine thresholds, but as the fine increases, well-mixed populations yield higher efficiency. The findings support the prediction that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective and not too costly. The model is theoretical, so quantitative predictions should be interpreted as qualitative guidance.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2016). Cooperation and punishment in community-structured populations with migration. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in community-structured populations playing a linear PGG with punishment, the presence of group competition (where entire communities can replace each other based on average fitness) or fitness-based migration (where individuals are more likely to migrate if they are fitter) can sustain high levels of cooperation and punishment, suppressing defection in parameter regions where defection would otherwise dominate. Constant (random) migration does not have this effect. The transition between cooperative and defective regimes is sharper with migration, but only group competition and fitness-based migration expand the parameter space where cooperation and punishment are stable. The main reported outcomes are the frequencies of strategies, not efficiency or group payoff, but higher frequencies of cooperation and punishment imply higher group payoffs relative to defection-dominated regimes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task: in PGG-like environments with punishment, the presence of group competition or fitness-based migration mechanisms can substantially increase the prevalence of cooperation and punishment, and thus are likely to increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control games without punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is based on the frequencies of cooperative and punishing strategies. The results suggest that, for the same game design dimensions, enabling punishment in a context with group competition or fitness-based migration will likely lead to higher efficiency than in well-mixed or constant migration settings. The findings are most relevant for models with large group sizes, high mutation and migration rates, and community structure.

- source: Akdeniz, A., & van Veelen, M. (2021). The evolution of morality and the role of commitment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that commitment is a central mechanism for the evolution of cooperation, punishment, and other moral behaviors in humans. It reviews evidence and theory suggesting that commitment to punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and prisoners' dilemmas, even when punishment is costly. The authors contend that standard models based on population structure or repetition do not fully explain observed human behavior, and that commitment (and the ability to recognize commitment in others) is necessary for the evolution of costly punishment and conditional cooperation. The paper discusses the implications for efficiency and group welfare in a theoretical sense but does not provide quantitative predictions or empirical results on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for why punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods games and similar environments, emphasizing the role of commitment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the idea that the presence of punishment (and the ability to commit to punishment) can change the strategic environment and potentially increase cooperation, but it does not allow for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes based on game design dimensions. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of commitment and recognizability of commitment for punishment to be effective.

- source: Li, S. L., Du, C. P., Li, X. X., Shen, C., & Shi, L. (2024). Antisocial peer exclusion does not eliminate the effectiveness of prosocial peer exclusion in structured populations. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  findings: The study finds that in structured populations, prosocial peer exclusion can uphold cooperation even in the presence of antisocial peer exclusion, except when exclusion costs are low. Antisocial peer exclusion alone suppresses cooperation, but when both types coexist, prosocial exclusion remains effective at sustaining cooperation through complex coexistence and cyclic dominance patterns. The results are reported in terms of cooperation rates and strategy frequencies, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings are robust across different network sizes and parameter sweeps, but are based on theoretical simulations rather than empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that prosocial peer exclusion can maintain high levels of cooperation in spatial public goods games, even when antisocial exclusion is possible, except at low exclusion costs. However, because the outcomes are reported as cooperation rates and not as efficiency or group payoff, the direct quantitative effect of exclusion on efficiency cannot be extracted. The results suggest that, in structured populations, enabling prosocial exclusion (a form of targeted sanctioning) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that the negative impact of antisocial exclusion does not fully undermine this effect. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect support: higher cooperation rates likely correspond to higher efficiency, but the mapping is not explicit. The findings are most relevant for settings with spatial structure, peer-based exclusion, and variable exclusion costs.

- source: Chen, T., Wu, Z. H., & Wang, L. (2018). Disseminators or silencers: The effect of information diffusion intensity on cooperation in public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  findings: The paper finds that the presence and type of information dissemination strongly affect the speed at which full cooperation is achieved in a spatial public goods game with reputation and conformity. Negative disseminators (who spread information about defectors) most often lead to the fastest convergence to full cooperation, followed by neutral and positive disseminators. However, it is informative about the role of information diffusion and reputation in accelerating the achievement of cooperation. The findings suggest that mechanisms which amplify reputational consequences for defectors (akin to negative gossip or public shaming) can speed up the transition to full cooperation, especially in certain parameter regimes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or payoff, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address whether the final payoffs or efficiency differ between conditions.

- source: Reeves, T., Ohtsuki, H., & Fukui, S. (2017). Asymmetric public goods game cooperation through pest control. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in asymmetric public goods games (modeled as pest control among farmers), the Nash equilibrium investment and resulting efficiency depend strongly on the discount factor and the number of players. There are threshold effects: only farmers close to the risk (frontier) contribute when the discount factor is low, while more distant farmers contribute only if the discount factor is high enough. Increasing the number of players does not always increase efficiency; in some parameter regions, adding more players can paradoxically reduce total investment and group welfare. Asymmetry leads to a disproportionate burden on those at greatest risk, making the system more vulnerable to shocks. The model does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how efficiency in asymmetric public goods games depends on player count, discount factor, and the structure of risk, but it does not address punishment or sanctioning. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is highly informative for baseline (control) efficiency in asymmetric, no-punishment settings, especially regarding the effects of group size and discounting. However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no such mechanism is present or analyzed. Its findings suggest that efficiency can be non-monotonic in group size and is sensitive to asymmetry and time preferences, which should be considered when predicting control efficiency, but it cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment.

- source: Olejarz, J. W., & Nowak, M. A. (2014). Evolution of staying together in the context of diffusible public goods. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in public goods games with diffusible goods can evolve if the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds a threshold determined by the probability of staying together (q) and the internal absorption efficiency (alpha_k). Clustering increases the efficiency of cooperation by allowing cooperators to preferentially benefit from their own public goods.   prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for understanding how structural and spatial factors (such as clustering and internal absorption efficiency) affect the evolution of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games. However, it does not address punishment or sanctioning, so it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in control games without punishment, or in environments where clustering and spatial structure are the main mechanisms supporting cooperation. For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical guidance on how efficiency depends on clustering, internal benefit allocation, and the benefit-to-cost ratio, but offers no evidence about punishment effects.
