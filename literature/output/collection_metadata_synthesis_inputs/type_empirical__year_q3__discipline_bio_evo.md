- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, G. R., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Altered Voluntary Cooperative Norms Compliance Under Equal Decision-Making Power. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rDLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects voluntary cooperative norm compliance (measured as contribution) in a linear public goods game with four players, asymmetric endowments, and no punishment or sanction options. Anodal stimulation increases contributions, cathodal decreases them, and sham is intermediate. The effect is observed for both high and low endowment players. The study also finds that participants' own norm perceptions (norm.own) are more predictive of compliance than their perceptions of others' norms (norm.other), and that anodal stimulation changes the relative influence of these norm perceptions. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported, and the game does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its findings are limited to the effect of neural stimulation on voluntary cooperation (contribution) in a standard linear PGG. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are reported and may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform the downstream prediction task regarding the effect of punishment on efficiency. It may be useful as a control or baseline for voluntary cooperation in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & D'Amico, V. (2021). A preference to learn from successful rather than common behaviours in human social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Participants in a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4, groups of 4, 6 rounds) were more motivated to learn from and copy successful (high-earning) behaviors than to conform to common behaviors. When shown both the average (common) contribution and the average contribution of the highest earners from a previous experiment, participants contributed significantly less than when shown only the common behavior. This decline in cooperation was not explained by simply knowing about free riders, but specifically by the desire to learn from success. Most participants preferred to see information about successful behaviors over common behaviors. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the lower contributions in the 'success' information condition imply lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games without punishment or reward, providing information about successful (high-earning) behaviors leads to lower cooperation and thus likely lower efficiency, compared to providing only information about common behaviors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of success-based social information (even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward) can undermine efficiency. However, the study does not manipulate or analyze punishment directly, and so its direct relevance to predicting the effect of enabling punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of social information and learning in public goods games, and suggest that efficiency may be lower when participants can observe and learn from successful free-riding.

Gossip is especially important when direct observation of others' behavior is limited, as it enables vicarious learning and helps participants adjust their own contributions based on information received from others. Gossip also increases social bonding and alignment of impressions between conversation partners. The study does not include any formal punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling communication (gossip) in public goods games increases contribution rates and sustains cooperation, especially under incomplete information. However, it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on punishment effects or efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that social information transmission (gossip) can substitute for formal sanctions in sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative estimates for efficiency outcomes or for games with punishment enabled. The design dimensions most directly addressed are player count, number of rounds, chat/communication, and information visibility. The absence of punishment and efficiency outcomes limits its direct applicability to the prediction task.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Burum, B., Nowak, M. A., & Hoffman, M. (2020). An evolutionary explanation for ineffective altruism. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that people are generally insensitive to the efficacy (impact) of their charitable giving, in contrast to their sensitivity when making decisions for themselves or family. Social rewards (reputation, praise, monetary reward from third parties) are given for the act of giving, not for giving effectively or giving more. This insensitivity is not due to cognitive or emotional limitations, but rather to the evolutionary logic that social rewards are easier to coordinate around observable, well-defined actions (like giving) than around less observable or harder-to-define features (like efficacy or amount). The studies do not use public goods games or punishment, but the reward mechanism is conceptually adjacent to sanctioning. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. However, it offers indirect evidence that social incentives (rewards) are most effective when they are based on observable, well-defined actions, and that people are not motivated by efficiency or impact when giving is socially rewarded. The findings suggest that, in environments where social rewards or punishments are based on easily observed behaviors (like contributing at all), these incentives may not improve efficiency if they do not target effective or high-impact contributions. The lack of sensitivity to efficacy in social rewards may limit the effectiveness of punishment or reward mechanisms in increasing group efficiency, unless the mechanisms are designed to be sensitive to impact. The paper is most relevant for understanding the limitations of social incentives in promoting efficient cooperation, rather than for direct prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGGs with punishment.
