  findings: The paper finds that enabling punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency and sustain cooperation, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defectors. However, the effect is not universally positive: high punishment costs, the presence of antisocial punishment, or poorly tuned parameters can lead to lower efficiency or even the collapse of cooperation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, mpcr, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. If punishment is costly or antisocial punishment is possible, efficiency gains may be lost or reversed. The paper's phase diagrams and model descriptions can be used to map specific game designs to expected efficiency outcomes, given the control efficiency. However, the results are based on theoretical models and simulations, not direct empirical measurement, so predictions should be interpreted with caution and in the context of the model's assumptions.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, cooperation and group efficiency decline over time when punishment is not available, as self-regarding individuals free ride and conditional cooperators reduce their contributions in response. When a costly punishment mechanism is introduced, cooperation and group efficiency increase dramatically, approaching full cooperation and maximum group earnings. This effect is robust even when punishment is costly and when group composition changes to prevent repeated interactions. The review also highlights that a minority of strong reciprocators can sustain high efficiency when punishment is possible, but even a majority cannot do so without punishment. The findings are supported by experimental evidence from Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and others.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games substantially increases group efficiency, even when punishment is costly to the punisher and when group composition is randomized. The effect is robust across different group sizes and settings. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency from low or declining levels (in the control) to near-maximal levels (in the treatment), provided that punishment is sufficiently effective and visible. The review also suggests that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are important moderators, but the main qualitative result is that punishment enables high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with reasonable cost and effectiveness) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the marginal benefit of also enabling reward is likely negligible except under harsh conditions (low MPCR, low cost). The results are most directly applicable to spatial (networked) PGGs with group size 5, continuous contributions, and symmetric cost/impact for punishment and reward.

  findings: The paper finds that in finite-population PGGs, the expected total cost to an institution of achieving a minimum cooperation level via punishment or reward can be exactly calculated and optimized. The cost efficiency of punishment versus reward depends on the required cooperation threshold: for low cooperation targets, reward is more cost-efficient; for high targets, punishment is more cost-efficient, with the threshold typically around 50% cooperation. The cost functions exhibit phase transitions as the intensity of selection changes. The analysis provides explicit formulas and algorithms for determining the optimal incentive size and minimal institutional cost, given the game parameters, population size, and selection intensity.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, theoretically grounded formulas for predicting the efficiency (cost-effectiveness) of institutional punishment (and reward) in finite-population PGGs, as a function of player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, selection intensity, and the required cooperation level. It shows that the efficiency of punishment depends critically on the required cooperation threshold and the selection intensity. For prediction tasks, the paper enables calculation of the minimal institutional cost (and thus efficiency) for any specified game design, provided the relevant parameters are known. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the institution can set the punishment/reward level to achieve a target cooperation rate, and where the main outcome of interest is the total cost to the institution (i.e., efficiency in terms of budget spent per unit of cooperation achieved). The findings are less directly applicable to settings where individual payoffs or group welfare (rather than institutional cost) are the main outcome, but the cost formulas can be mapped to group efficiency if the institutional cost is subtracted from total group payoffs.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. This policy outperforms pure reward or pure punishment in both effectiveness (range of conditions for full cooperation) and efficiency (cumulative cost to reach/restore cooperation). The findings hold for both well-mixed and spatial populations, and are robust to changes in group size, incentive leverage, spatial structure, and other model details. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, allowing optional participation (the 'take-it-or-leave-it' option) enables even small penalties to establish a globally stable cooperative norm, maximizing group welfare. In contrast, compulsory participation requires much larger penalties to achieve the same effect, and can leave the group trapped in full defection if initial cooperation is low. The efficiency gains from punishment with optional participation are robust across several model variants, including user-pays and capped punishment. The key outcome is that punishment, when combined with voluntary participation, is a highly efficient mechanism for overcoming social dilemmas in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling optional participation (voluntary entry) dramatically increases the efficiency of institutional punishment: much smaller penalties suffice to achieve full cooperation and maximize group welfare. The model is directly relevant to settings where the game design allows players to opt out, and where punishment is administered by an institution rather than peers. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing institutional punishment with optional participation will yield a much larger increase in efficiency than with compulsory participation, and that the cost required to achieve this is much lower than for reward-based incentives. The results are robust to variations in group size, cost structure, and punishment allocation, making them broadly applicable to prediction tasks involving these design dimensions.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary public goods games with large populations, pool punishment cannot emerge from rare initial conditions due to the second-order free rider problem. However, if a voluntary reward fund is available and especially if it rewards those who contribute to pool punishment, then pool punishment can emerge and become stable, leading to high group efficiency (i.e., the population converges to all-punisher state). The reward mechanism acts as a mediator, facilitating the transition from free riding to stable punishment-supported cooperation. Once established, pool punishment is self-sustaining and the reward fund is no longer needed. The findings are robust to variations in group size, benefit multipliers, and cost parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of a reward mechanism, especially one that rewards contributors to punishment, is a key moderator for the emergence and stability of efficient outcomes in public goods games with punishment. In the absence of such a reward, punishment alone cannot overcome the second-order free rider problem in large populations, and efficiency remains low. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the presence or absence of a reward mechanism (and its design) is critical. Other dimensions such as group size, MPCR, and cost parameters are explored and found not to qualitatively change the main result: without reward, punishment does not increase efficiency from a free-rider baseline; with reward, punishment can emerge and efficiency increases dramatically.

  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.

  findings: The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with pool punishment and probabilistic corruption (bribery), the evolutionary outcome for cooperation and group efficiency depends on the balance between the expected loss to defectors from punishment and the expected bribe received by punishers. If the expected loss from punishment is less than the bribe, a stable coexistence of cooperators, defectors, and punishers is possible, maintaining cooperation and higher efficiency. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when punishment is strong and corruption is weak, but can be neutral or negative when corruption undermines punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the effectiveness of punishment relative to the possibility and attractiveness of corruption (bribery). For prediction, if the game design ensures that punishment is costly and effective (high fine, low bribe, low probability of successful bribery), enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency. If the design allows for easy or attractive bribery (high bribe, high probability of bribery, low punishment cost), enabling punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model is most relevant for infinite, well-mixed populations and pool punishment settings.
