- source: Ishikawa, B. Y. S., & Fontanari, J. F. (2025). Revisiting institutional punishment in the N-person prisoner's dilemma. *Theory in Biosciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutional punishment in public goods games can stabilize high-efficiency (all-punishers) equilibria, but only if the cost to punishers is sufficiently low relative to the fines imposed on non-punishers and defectors. Cost sharing among punishers (i.e., dividing the maintenance cost of the institution) expands the parameter region where high efficiency is possible and increases the basin of attraction for the all-punishers equilibrium. However, the all-defectors equilibrium (zero efficiency) is always stable, so initial conditions and parameter settings are crucial. The group size N and the minimum number of punishers required to establish the institution (K_m) both affect the threshold frequency of punishers needed for high efficiency. In finite populations, demographic noise can sometimes allow cooperators to fixate, but this does not generally lead to robust high-efficiency outcomes. The model does not include optional participation, peer punishment, or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for how institutional punishment, cost sharing, group size, and punishment cost parameters affect the efficiency of public goods games. For the prediction task, it shows that enabling institutional punishment can increase efficiency dramatically, but only if the punishment cost is not too high relative to the fines and the group can reach a critical mass of punishers. Cost sharing (lower per-punisher cost as more punishers join) makes high efficiency more likely. Larger groups require a higher absolute number of punishers, but the threshold frequency can vary non-monotonically with group size. If the control game (no punishment) is at low efficiency (all-defectors), enabling punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to high efficiency, but only if the cost/benefit ratios and initial conditions are favorable. The paper does not address peer punishment, rewards, or communication, so its findings are most applicable to institutional punishment in large, well-mixed groups.

- source: Powers, S. T., Perret, C., & Currie, T. E. (2023). Playing the political game: the coevolution of institutions with group size and political inequality. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that institutionally arranged punishment in public goods games allows groups to sustain high levels of cooperation and achieve much larger group sizes (higher efficiency/welfare) than groups without such institutions. As groups grow, the cost of forming consensus over rules increases, but the evolution of hierarchy (a skewed distribution of influence) allows large groups to maintain efficient cooperation-promoting institutions by reducing the cost of consensus formation. Groups that invest just enough in punishment to deter defection, while allocating the rest to technology (increasing carrying capacity), achieve the highest efficiency. If hierarchy cannot evolve, large groups cannot sustain cooperation and remain small and inefficient. The model also shows that if the political game (consensus formation) is too difficult, groups may fail to maintain cooperation-promoting institutions, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that institutional rules are set optimally (not over- or under-investing in punishment). The model also highlights that the cost of consensus formation (political game) can limit the efficiency gains from punishment in large groups unless hierarchy evolves to reduce these costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by group size, the cost of institutional rule formation, and the ability to evolve or implement hierarchy. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is institutionally arranged and the costs of monitoring/sanctioning are covered by the group, rather than being individually altruistic.

- source: Odouard, V. V., Smirnova, D., & Edelman, S. (2023). Polarize, catalyze, and stabilize: how a minority of norm internalizers amplify group selection and punishment. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The presence of norm internalizers in the strategy space (even as a small minority, around 10% of the population) leads to higher mean cooperation rates, longer and more stable periods of high cooperation, and fewer long periods of low cooperation, compared to conditions with only group selection and punishment. This effect is most pronounced at intermediate benefit-to-cost ratios, where cooperation is possible but not guaranteed. The mechanism is indirect: norm internalizers polarize groups (increasing inter-group variance), catalyze spikes in cooperation when global cooperation is low, and stabilize high-cooperation states. The effect is not due to norm internalizers themselves cooperating more, but to their influence on the behavior of other agents. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, so the direct effect on efficiency is not established.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in agent-based models of public goods games with group selection and imperfect punishment, adding a minority of norm internalizers can substantially increase mean cooperation rates, especially at intermediate benefit-to-cost ratios. However, because the reported outcomes are cooperation rates and not efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used as indirect evidence for predicting efficiency: higher cooperation rates likely imply higher efficiency, but the mapping is not quantified here. The model assumes punishment is imperfect (not all defections are observed), and punishment is implemented as withholding benefits rather than costly punishment. The results are robust across two different model structures (abstract and spatial/naturalistic). For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of norm internalization mechanisms can amplify the effect of punishment and group selection on cooperation, but does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that the expected total cost to the institution for achieving a target cooperation level in finite-population PGGs depends strongly on the threshold for incentive application (t), the strength of selection (β), mutation rate (μ), and the type of incentive (reward, punishment, hybrid). For small thresholds (t=1), the cost function is always non-decreasing in the per-capita incentive cost, and explicit formulas are derived. For larger thresholds, the cost function can exhibit non-monotonic behavior and phase transitions, especially at high selection strength. Numerical results show that both reward and punishment incentives can be optimized for cost, but the optimal cost and its behavior depend on the game parameters and incentive scheme. The paper provides a framework for predicting the institutional cost required to achieve a desired cooperation level, given the game design and population parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and numerical evidence on how the cost of institutional incentives (reward, punishment, hybrid) required to achieve a target cooperation level in PGG-like environments depends on game design dimensions such as player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, and the structure of the incentive scheme (threshold t, cost per unit, effectiveness). While the main outcome is the institutional cost rather than group efficiency, the results are directly relevant for predicting how changes in punishment or reward design (e.g., cost, threshold, effectiveness) will affect the efficiency of institutional intervention. The paper is most informative for settings where the goal is to optimize the cost of achieving cooperation, rather than to maximize group payoff per se. It provides explicit formulas and numerical results that can be used to inform predictions about the efficiency of punishment or reward interventions, conditional on game design and baseline cooperation levels.

  findings: The paper finds that kin selection (homophily) can facilitate the initial invasion of coordinated cooperation in public goods games with nonlinear (sigmoidal) benefits, even when such cooperation cannot invade in a well-mixed population due to cognitive or coordination costs. Once established, coordinated cooperation can persist as homophily declines, provided that the benefit function is sufficiently nonlinear and the switching gain from non-contribution to contribution at the coordination point exceeds the cost. If this condition is not met, some level of homophily must be maintained to prevent the invasion of defectors or 'liars' (who commit to contribute but do not follow through). The framework provides analytical and numerical tools for predicting evolutionary outcomes (including efficiency and group payoff) as a function of group size, relatedness, benefit function nonlinearity, and strategy set. The results are directly relevant to predicting efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but do not address punishment or sanctions directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a general theoretical framework for predicting efficiency and group payoff in public goods games with arbitrary group size, number of strategies, and non-additive (e.g., sigmoidal) benefit functions, under varying levels of kin structure (homophily). It is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency of control games (without punishment) as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR (via the benefit function), and group structure. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The framework could, in principle, be extended to include punishment as a discrete strategy, but this is not done in the present paper. Therefore, the paper is highly informative for predicting baseline efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but provides only indirect or contextual support for predicting the effect of punishment.

For low growth rates, punishment does not improve efficiency; for moderate/high growth rates, punishment can improve efficiency, but outcomes may be bistable. The results are most applicable to settings where resource dynamics and centralized sanctioning are key features.   findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player games with an opting-out option, spiteful (costly harming) strategies can sometimes persist, but only under restrictive conditions: low cost-to-harm ratio, small group size, and specific opting-out rules. As group size increases, it becomes harder for spiteful strategies to achieve higher payoffs than nonspiteful strategies, and the basin of attraction for all-nonspiteful populations grows. The opting-out rule (how easily groups disband) is a key moderator: when homogeneous groups (all spiteful or all nonspiteful) are forced to disband, spite can persist more easily, but this is a somewhat artificial rule. The analysis is directly about payoff and efficiency, not just behavior.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public-goods-like n-player games with an opting-out mechanism, the introduction of costly punishment (here, spiteful harming) generally reduces group efficiency unless the cost-to-harm ratio is extremely low and the group size is small. As group size increases, the conditions for punishment/spite to persist become more restrictive, and group efficiency is more likely to be maximized when punishment/spite is absent. The details of the opting-out rule (e.g., whether homogeneous groups are forced to disband) can moderate this effect, but in most realistic settings, punishment/spite is not efficiency-enhancing. The model is not an exact PGG with standard punishment, but the payoff structure and evolutionary logic are closely related, and the results are directly about payoff/efficiency. Thus, the paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment/spite in large groups with standard cost structures will not increase efficiency, and may reduce it.

  findings: The paper finds that in the 'others-only' n-person prisoner's dilemma (a strong altruism scenario), cooperation cannot evolve under asexual reproduction or sexual reproduction with selfing, but can evolve and become stable under biparental sexual reproduction if the group benefit multiplier (r) is sufficiently large relative to group size (n) and the cost of cooperation (c) is not too high.   prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments that lack punishment or reward mechanisms, especially when the game structure is the 'others-only' variant of the n-person prisoner's dilemma. The results suggest that, without punishment, efficiency is generally low unless these conditions are met. Its main value is in establishing a baseline: in the absence of punishment, efficiency is generally low except under the specified genetic/demographic conditions. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not manipulate or analyze punishment.
