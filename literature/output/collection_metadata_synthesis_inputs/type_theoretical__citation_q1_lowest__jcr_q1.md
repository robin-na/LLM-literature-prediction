- source: Bühren, C., Haarde, J., Hirschmann, C., & Kesten-Kühne, J. (2023). Social preferences in the public goods game-An Agent-Based simulation with EconSim. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The agent-based model, using reinforcement learning and a social preference framework, closely replicates the experimental findings of [20] in a public goods game with endogenous punishment. For low cooperators, severe punishment increases contributions and can raise payoffs toward the social optimum, but mild punishment is ineffective and reduces payoffs due to its cost. For high cooperators, punishment (both mild and severe) increases contributions only slightly but reduces payoffs because the cost of punishment outweighs the marginal gain in cooperation. Middle cooperators benefit from mild punishment, which increases both contributions and payoffs. The model demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the underlying social preference structure of the group. Endogenous institution choice in the simulation leads agents to select the institution that maximizes their own payoffs, mirroring the learning observed in the experimental Sorted-Info treatment but not in the Sorted treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with known design parameters. The simulation results, validated against experimental data, show that the impact of punishment on efficiency depends on the group’s baseline cooperativeness: severe punishment can increase efficiency for low cooperators but is costly and can reduce efficiency for high cooperators. The model’s detailed reporting of payoffs under different punishment regimes, player types, and institution selection mechanisms makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings highlight the importance of considering both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the social preference composition of the group.

The results are most applicable to settings with continuous contribution, no communication, and fixed group size and structure. The study is simulation-based, so real-world generalizability may be limited, but the mechanism-level insight is strong for theoretical prediction.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Zefferman, M. R. (2023). Constraints on cooperation shape hierarchical versus distributed structure in human groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the efficiency of punishment and monitoring technologies fundamentally shapes the optimal structure of cooperation in public goods games. When punishment is efficient (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the impact on free-riders), hierarchical (tree-like) organizations are optimal and can support universal cooperation (full efficiency) even in large groups. When punishment is inefficient (costly for punishers), distributed (collective) monitoring and punishment are optimal, but these structures are limited in scale by monitoring costs. The model provides explicit analytic conditions for when universal contribution (full cooperation) is a Nash equilibrium and socially efficient, and when universal free-riding is the only equilibrium. The model also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment networks (hierarchical vs. distributed) is an endogenous outcome of the costs and effectiveness of these institutions. Thus, the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency depends critically on the cost-effectiveness of punishment and monitoring, as well as group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that enabling punishment can increase group efficiency (by supporting universal cooperation) if punishment is sufficiently efficient (low cost, high impact), and if monitoring is not prohibitively costly. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds at which universal cooperation is efficient and stable, depending on group size, public goods multiplier, monitoring efficiency, and punishment efficiency. It also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment (hierarchical vs. distributed) is determined by these parameters, which in turn affects the scalability of cooperation. For prediction, the model implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is cheap and effective, monitoring is efficient, and group size is not too large for the given monitoring technology. If punishment is costly or monitoring is inefficient, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency, especially in larger groups.

- source: Gao, S. P., & Li, N. (2023). Preference reversal and the evolution of cooperation. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that consensual peer punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation and group efficiency, but only if the level of cooperators' preference for punishment is above a threshold and the consensus required to implement punishment is not too high. When preference reversal is introduced (i.e., cooperators may change their mind about punishing), the effect on efficiency and cooperation depends on the structure of reversal probabilities and thresholds. If those who initially do not want to punish are likely to reverse and join in punishment, cooperation and efficiency are promoted. If those who initially want to punish are likely to reverse and abstain, the effect is negative. When reversal probabilities are uniform, increasing reversal undermines the positive effect of punishment, and high reversal can eliminate cooperation and reduce efficiency. Thus, the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the details of collective decision-making and preference instability.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games depends on the interplay between group size, consensus thresholds, punishment cost and effectiveness, and especially the stability of individuals' preferences for punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment will not always increase efficiency: the effect is positive only when the group is likely to reach consensus to punish and when cooperators' willingness to punish is stable or when preference reversal tends to increase the number of punishers. If preference reversal is common and leads to fewer punishers, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The model provides explicit conditions (in terms of group size, punishment parameters, and preference dynamics) for when punishment will or will not increase efficiency, which can be mapped to the prediction dimensions.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a compulsory, fully monitored PGG, it is possible to achieve full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) by punishing only a fraction of defectors, provided the punishment is sufficiently severe. The model formalizes the trade-off between the fraction of defectors punished (scope) and the severity of punishment (magnitude), showing that these can be tuned to reach stable full cooperation. The results indicate that the threat of punishment, even if not universally applied, can discipline the population and sustain cooperation, thus maximizing group payoff. The findings are entirely theoretical, based on replicator dynamics and equilibrium analysis.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in a compulsory and fully observable PGG, enabling punishment (even if not universal) can substantially increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline. The model gives explicit conditions (in terms of player count, MPCR, punishment severity, and punishment scope) under which full cooperation is stable. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that introducing a sufficiently strong and well-targeted punishment mechanism will likely move efficiency close to 1, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. However, the model assumes perfect monitoring, compulsory participation, and centralized punishment, so predictions should be adjusted if these assumptions do not hold in the target environment.

- source: Powers, S. T., Perret, C., & Currie, T. E. (2023). Playing the political game: the coevolution of institutions with group size and political inequality. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that institutionally arranged punishment in public goods games allows groups to sustain high levels of cooperation and achieve much larger group sizes (higher efficiency/welfare) than groups without such institutions. As groups grow, the cost of forming consensus over rules increases, but the evolution of hierarchy (a skewed distribution of influence) allows large groups to maintain efficient cooperation-promoting institutions by reducing the cost of consensus formation. Groups that invest just enough in punishment to deter defection, while allocating the rest to technology (increasing carrying capacity), achieve the highest efficiency. If hierarchy cannot evolve, large groups cannot sustain cooperation and remain small and inefficient. The model also shows that if the political game (consensus formation) is too difficult, groups may fail to maintain cooperation-promoting institutions, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that institutional rules are set optimally (not over- or under-investing in punishment). The model also highlights that the cost of consensus formation (political game) can limit the efficiency gains from punishment in large groups unless hierarchy evolves to reduce these costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by group size, the cost of institutional rule formation, and the ability to evolve or implement hierarchy. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is institutionally arranged and the costs of monitoring/sanctioning are covered by the group, rather than being individually altruistic.

- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

For prediction tasks, this paper informs the expected direction and possible moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency.

For prediction tasks, the results suggest that enabling punishment can increase efficiency (by supporting all-cooperation) when the control game is below the cooperation threshold, but may have little or even negative effect when the control game already achieves high cooperation due to network reciprocity.
