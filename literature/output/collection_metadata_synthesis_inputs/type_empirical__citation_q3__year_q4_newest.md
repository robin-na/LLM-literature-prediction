- source: Zhou, X. Y., Alysandratos, T., & Naef, M. (2023). Rice Farming and the Origins of Cooperative Behaviour. *Economic Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) participants from wetland rice regions contribute more in both the no-punishment and punishment conditions of a repeated PGG, and (2) participants from rice regions assign more punishment points to free riders than those from non-rice regions. The difference in contribution is larger in the punishment condition. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. The results are robust to a wide range of controls and IV strategies, and are interpreted as evidence of persistent cultural norms of cooperation and punishment rooted in historical rice farming.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a punishment option increases contribution rates in a repeated PGG, and that the magnitude of this effect is moderated by cultural background (rice vs. non-rice regions). However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are useful for predicting contribution behavior under different cultural backgrounds, but not for direct efficiency prediction as required by the downstream task.

- source: Zhou, Y. X., Chen, S. W., Chen, Y. F., & Vollane, B. (2022). Does parental migration impede the development of the cooperative preferences in their left-behind children? Evidence from a large-scale field experiment in China. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) children's cooperation (measured by contribution in a public goods game) increases with age; (2) paternal migration significantly reduces children's cooperation, while maternal or both-parent migration does not; (3) both exogenous and endogenous punishment mechanisms significantly increase cooperation, with exogenous punishment being more effective; (4) exogenous punishment can offset the negative effect of paternal migration on cooperation. The main outcome is contribution level, not efficiency or group payoff, so the findings are most directly about behavioral cooperation rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing punishment mechanisms (especially exogenous punishment) increases cooperation (contributions) in one-shot public goods games with children, and can offset negative effects of paternal absence. However, because the outcome is contribution level rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be mapped to efficiency outcomes with caution. The game design dimensions are well specified (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), and the effect of punishment is robustly positive on contributions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect but informative evidence that punishment is likely to increase efficiency in similar settings, especially when baseline cooperation is low due to social factors.

- source: Spadaro, G., Graf, C., Jin, S. X., Arai, S., Inoue, Y., Lieberman, E., Rinderu, M. I., Yuan, M. L., Van Lissa, C. J., & Balliet, D. (2022). Cross-Cultural Variation in Cooperation: A Meta-Analysis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The meta-analysis finds that the presence of sanctions (punishment and/or reward) in social dilemma experiments is associated with significantly higher cooperation rates. Other situational features, such as communication and lower conflict of interest, also increase cooperation. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff. No significant cross-societal or cross-cultural differences in cooperation are found after controlling for study characteristics. The effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff is not directly analyzed or reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment or reward mechanisms increases cooperation rates in PGG and PD experiments, across a wide range of societies and experimental designs. However, because the outcome is cooperation rate rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The paper does not address whether increased cooperation from punishment leads to higher or lower efficiency, as it does not analyze payoff-based outcomes. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence about efficiency effects.

- source: Capraro, V. (2024). The Dual-Process Approach to Human Sociality: Meta-Analytic Evidence for a Theory of Internalized Heuristics for Self-Preservation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that promoting intuitive processing (via time pressure, ego depletion, conceptual primes, etc.) increases negative reciprocity (i.e., punishment of norm violations) in economic games, including public goods games with punishment phases and ultimatum games. This is supported by a meta-analysis showing a small but significant increase in punishment/rejection rates when intuition is promoted. However, the review does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not addressed; the focus is on the cognitive basis of the decision to punish, not on the downstream consequences for group outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that intuitive processing increases the likelihood of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, but it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this review is not directly informative, as it does not report or synthesize efficiency or welfare outcomes. It is useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior (e.g., cognitive manipulation, individual differences), but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. Use this paper to inform models of punishment frequency or behavioral response, not efficiency.

- source: Wang, X. M., Couto, M. C., Wang, N. Y., An, X. M., Chen, B., Dong, Y. L., Hilbe, C., & Zhang, B. Y. (2023). Cooperation and coordination in heterogeneous populations. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endowment inequality is a significant obstacle to successful coordination in threshold public goods games, reducing the proportion of groups that reach the threshold and thus receive the group reward. Productivity inequality does not have a similar negative effect. Participants tend to coordinate on equal relative contributions, even when this is not payoff-maximizing. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or cooperation. The findings highlight the importance of focal points and fairness heuristics in equilibrium selection, and show that evolutionary game theory models may mis-predict outcomes unless they account for participants' initial expectations and heuristics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design. However, it provides informative context on how inequality (in endowment or productivity) affects coordination and group success in threshold public goods games. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it suggests that baseline efficiency (without punishment) is lower under endowment inequality. This could be relevant for understanding the baseline to which punishment effects would be compared in similar asymmetric games.

- source: Otten, K., Frey, U. J., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N. (2022). Human cooperation in changing groups in a large-scale public goods game. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that the number of newcomers in a group is negatively associated with the average percentage of resources contributed to the public good. This effect is strongest in small groups and at later time periods. The lower group contribution is primarily due to newcomers contributing less than incumbents, though their contributions increase as they spend more time in the group. The difference between newcomers and incumbents is partially explained by differences in private good levels and public good levels, but remains significant even after controlling for these factors. The study does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or the effect of punishment, and there is no evidence of sanctioning mechanisms being central to the game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide informative context about how group composition changes (newcomer entry) can reduce cooperation (as measured by contribution rates), especially in the absence of punishment or exclusion. For prediction tasks involving efficiency, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not report payoff-based outcomes or manipulate punishment. It may be useful for understanding baseline cooperation dynamics in large, dynamic groups without punishment.

- source: Köster, R., Hadfield-Menell, D., Everett, R., Weidinger, L., Hadfield, G. K., & Leibo, J. Z. (2022). Spurious normativity enhances learning of compliance and enforcement behavior in artificial agents. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=other_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: Introducing a punishment regime for norm violations (e.g., eating a poisonous berry) enables agents to learn to avoid harmful actions more quickly, resulting in higher group efficiency (collective return) than in the absence of punishment. Adding a second, arbitrary 'silly' rule (punishment for eating a harmless berry) further increases group efficiency during the learning process, as it provides more opportunities for agents to practice enforcement and compliance, leading to faster and more robust avoidance of genuinely harmful actions. The effect is strongest during the intermediate learning phase, with the silly rule condition yielding the highest group payoffs. The study demonstrates that third-party punishment, even when applied to arbitrary rules, can enhance the emergence of efficient, norm-compliant behavior in artificial agent societies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a multiagent environment with norm violations increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust even when punishment is costly and decentralized. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the core mechanism—costly third-party punishment of norm violations—closely parallels PGG punishment treatments. The results suggest that, for similar environments, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially when the environment presents a credit assignment problem (delayed or opaque consequences of harmful actions). The effect is further enhanced if the environment provides more opportunities for agents to practice enforcement (e.g., via additional, even arbitrary, punishable actions). The findings are most directly applicable to settings where learning and credit assignment are challenging, and where punishment is visible and incentivized. The results are less directly transferable to standard PGGs with immediate, transparent payoffs, but the qualitative direction (punishment increases efficiency) is likely to hold.

- source: Makovi, K., Sargsyan, A., Li, W. D., Bonnefon, J. F., & Rahwan, T. (2023). Trust within human-machine collectives depends on the perceived consensus about cooperative norms. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a third-party punishment game followed by a trust game, humans earn less trust by sharing with or punishing bots than with humans. Trust-gain is attenuated but not eliminated in mixed human-bot collectives. Perceived consensus about cooperative norms predicts trust-gain, and experimentally making norm consensus salient increases trust-gain in some conditions. The main outcomes are trust and norm perception, not group efficiency or total payoff. The design is adjacent to public goods games, but the outcome focus is on trust and norm-following rather than efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. Its design is adjacent (third-party punishment game with binary sharing), and the main outcomes are trust-gain and norm perception, not efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment can increase trust in norm-followers, especially when norm consensus is salient, but do not quantify effects on group efficiency. The paper may inform mechanism design around norm salience and trust, but is not directly informative for efficiency prediction tasks.

- source: Guo, Z. Y., Guo, R., Xu, C. Y., & Wu, Z. (2022). Reflexive or reflective? Group bias in third-party punishment in Chinese and Western cultures. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both Chinese and Western participants show ingroup favoritism in third-party punishment under reflexive (fast, high cognitive load) conditions, punishing ingroup norm violators less than outgroup violators. Under reflective (slow, low cognitive load) conditions, Western participants—especially those with high group identity—reduce ingroup favoritism and may even punish ingroup violators more, while Chinese participants continue to show strong ingroup favoritism regardless of decision mode. Group identity amplifies these effects. The main outcome is the amount of punishment assigned, not efficiency or group payoff. The game is a third-party punishment mini-dictator game, not a standard public goods game, and does not report on group efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence about how group identity, culture, and decision-making mode moderate the behavioral use of costly punishment in a third-party punishment context. However, it does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The design is adjacent to PGGs (mini-dictator/TPP), and the outcome is punishment behavior, not payoff. The findings are useful for understanding when and how punishment is used, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect of TPP corruption on efficiency is not measured. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.

- source: Gummerum, M., López-Pérez, B., Van Dijk, E., & Van Dillen, L. F. (2022). Ire and punishment: Incidental anger and costly punishment in children, adolescents, and adults. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.

In third-party punishment games, incidental anger increases punishment severity for unfair offers in adults and adolescents, but not in children. The effect of anger is specific to unfair (unequal) offers and does not increase punishment of fair (equal) offers. Its main contribution is to show that emotional state (incidental anger) can increase costly punishment of unfairness, especially when the violation is self-relevant (second-party) or, for adults, when it violates fairness norms (third-party). For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment rates and severity can be modulated by emotional and developmental factors, but it does not inform how these changes in punishment behavior translate into changes in efficiency or group payoff. The game designs used (ultimatum and third-party punishment games) are adjacent to, but not the same as, standard public goods games, and no efficiency or welfare outcomes are reported.

- source: Sun, K. T., Liu, Y., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2022). Evolution of trust in a hierarchical population with punishing investors. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.

- source: Fitouchi, L., & Singh, M. (2023). Punitive justice serves to restore reciprocal cooperation in three small-scale societies. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: Across three small-scale societies, punitive justice is primarily restorative and dyadic, not group-norm-enforcing. Third-party punishment is rare, and most interventions are aimed at mediating disputes and restoring relationships. Compensation to victims is common and often proportional to harm, especially among the Mentawai and Nuer. Kinship and interdependence reduce the likelihood or magnitude of punishment/compensation. When costs are not imposed on offenders, cooperation often breaks down and feuding may result. The paper does not use public goods games or measure efficiency, but provides evidence that punishment and compensation are more about restoring dyadic cooperation than increasing group-level efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the function of punishment in environments that are adjacent to public goods games: real-world small-scale societies with reciprocal obligations and norm violations. It suggests that, in such contexts, punishment is not primarily about increasing group efficiency or enforcing group-level cooperation, but about restoring dyadic relationships and preventing escalation. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment may depend on whether the context is more about dyadic reciprocity or group-level cooperation. However, because the paper does not use experimental PGGs or report efficiency or payoff outcomes, its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes in PGGs is limited.

- source: Dimant, E., & Gesche, T. (2023). Nudging enforcers: how norm perceptions and motives for lying shape sanctions. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment increases with the size of the lie and is harsher when the lie increases inequity or is for personal gain, compared to when it restores equity. Norm-nudges (empirical or normative information about others' behavior or approval) increase punishment, especially when preexisting norms are ambiguous.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on how punishment behavior is shaped by norm-nudges and the context of norm violations, but it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it shows that punishment is sensitive to context and can be increased by norm-nudges, especially when norms are ambiguous. However, it does not provide evidence on whether or how punishment affects group efficiency or welfare, nor does it use a public goods game structure. Thus, it should be used as indirect evidence about the determinants of punishment behavior, not about efficiency effects.

- source: Nunney, S. J., van der Schalk, J., & Manstead, A. S. R. (2022). Emotion and intergroup cooperation: How verbal expressions of guilt, shame, and pride influence behavior in a social dilemma. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab experiments using an intergroup centipede game, the authors find that when a group expresses shame (or guilt) after behaving uncooperatively, the opposing group is more likely to cooperate in subsequent rounds, resulting in higher group payoffs (tickets won) and more prosocial behavior. In contrast, when a group expresses pride after uncooperative behavior, the opposing group is less likely to cooperate, resulting in lower payoffs and more costly punishment. The effect of emotion expression is causal (Study 2) and operates above and beyond the effect of prior behavior. The study does not use a standard public goods game or implement a formal punishment stage as in PGGs, but does include a costly punishment measure in Study 2.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that communication—specifically, the expression of self-conscious emotions—can significantly moderate the effect of prior uncooperative behavior on subsequent group efficiency in repeated, sequential social dilemmas. While not a standard PGG, the centipede game shares key features (sequential cooperation, group payoffs, potential for exploitation). The findings suggest that, in environments where communication is possible, expressions of shame or guilt can substitute for or augment formal punishment mechanisms in restoring cooperation and efficiency, while pride expressions can undermine them. However, the study does not manipulate punishment cost, technology, or other standard PGG design dimensions, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled PGG efficiency is limited. It does, however, highlight the importance of communication and emotional signaling as moderators of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Singh, M., & Garfield, Z. H. (2022). Evidence for third-party mediation but not punishment in Mentawai justice. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that, in the Mentawai context, third-party punishment is absent: no cases were found where third parties (outside the victim, their close kin, or spouse) directly punished or demanded penalties from transgressors. Indirect or second-order sanctions by third parties are also not observed. Instead, penalties (tulou) are negotiated and paid primarily to restore dyadic cooperation, with kinship playing a major role in the likelihood and size of payments. Mediation by third parties (especially shamans and elders) is common, particularly in more severe or between-clan disputes, but the mediator's role is to facilitate negotiation, not to enforce punishment. The study suggests that, in this small-scale society, institutionalized penalties serve to repair relationships rather than to enforce group-level norms through punishment. The findings are based on retrospective interviews and ethnographic observation of 302 reliable cases of transgression and response.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The studied context is not a PGG or close variant, and the outcomes are not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that, in small-scale, kin-based societies, third-party punishment is rare or absent, and penalties serve to restore dyadic cooperation rather than to enforce group norms or increase group efficiency. If extrapolating to PGG-like settings, the results would suggest that the presence or absence of third-party punishment may depend on the social structure and the nature of cooperation (private vs. public goods), but the paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes. Thus, it is of limited use for the downstream prediction task, except as a caution that punishment mechanisms may not always function as assumed in small-scale, kin-based societies.
