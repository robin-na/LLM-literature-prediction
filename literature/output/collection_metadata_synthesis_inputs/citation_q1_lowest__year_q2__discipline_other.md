- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

- source: Andrews, J., & Davidson, D. J. (2013). Cell-Gazing Into the Future: What Genes, Homo heidelbergensis, and Punishment Tell Us About Our Adaptive Capacity. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that punishment is a fundamental mechanism for stabilizing cooperation in large groups, enabling the evolution and maintenance of pro-social norms and group-level adaptation. It synthesizes evidence from evolutionary theory, ethnography, and game theory to claim that humans' unique adaptive capacity is rooted in our ability to cooperate, which is supported by both formal and informal punishment of defectors. The paper does not present new empirical findings or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes, but it does highlight the importance of punishment for sustaining cooperation and adaptive capacity in complex social systems.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment mechanisms are important for sustaining cooperation in group settings, which is relevant to public goods games and their variants. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in experimental games. The arguments may inform the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase cooperation and potentially efficiency, but the paper does not address how specific game design dimensions (such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost) moderate these effects. Thus, its value for quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency is limited to high-level theoretical justification for including punishment as a positive moderator of cooperation.

- source: Heimola, M. (2014). Threats of Hellfire and Jumping for Heavenly Joy Manifest Emotionalism as a Solution to the Problem of Machiavellian Hypocrites in Religious Revivals. *Method & Theory in the Study of Religion*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that manifest emotional displays in religious revivals function as hard-to-fake signals of genuine commitment, which help groups identify and exclude hypocrites who might otherwise free-ride on group cooperation. Emotional signaling is proposed as a solution to the problem of Machiavellian opportunists in cooperative settings, supplementing or replacing costly ritual signals. The historical case of 19th-century Finnish religious revivals is used to illustrate how emotionalism emerged in response to perceived hypocrisy and was associated with group formation and norm enforcement. However, the paper does not present empirical data or experimental results on efficiency, payoff, or punishment effects in public goods game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for the role of emotional signaling and commitment in solving cooperation problems, which may be relevant for understanding mechanisms underlying punishment and norm enforcement in public goods games. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze the game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task. Its value is primarily in framing the importance of reliable signals (emotional or costly) for group cohesion and cooperation, rather than in providing quantitative or predictive evidence for punishment effects on efficiency.

- source: Sthel, M., Tostes, J. G., & Tavares, J. (2013). Sustainable Complex Triangular Cells for the Evaluation of CO2 Emissions by Individuals instead of Nations in a Scenario for 2030. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper proposes that intense human cooperation and resource sharing, conceptualized as a 'regular hexagonal pyramid' of interconnected individuals, is the most effective way to reduce CO2 emissions and achieve sustainability. It applies this model to scenarios for 2030, suggesting that targeting individual high emitters and allowing low emitters to increase emissions is a fair and effective approach. The paper is entirely theoretical and does not present data or experimental results. There is no analysis of efficiency, payoff, or the effects of punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical or experimental evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not discuss punishment, sanctions, or game design dimensions such as player count, rounds, or payoff structures. Its conceptual arguments about cooperation and resource sharing may be contextually relevant to the broader literature on cooperation, but it does not inform quantitative prediction tasks involving efficiency or payoff outcomes in controlled game settings.
