- source: Kocher, M. G., & Matzat, D. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that, although punishment increases contributions more than reward or the standard VCM, it reduces group efficiency because the costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. The reward institution, despite a lower maximum leverage, is both the most preferred and the most efficient, yielding the highest average profits. The standard VCM yields intermediate efficiency. The effectiveness (leverage) of both punishment and reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency. There is no significant difference in efficiency or institutional choice between partner and stranger matching. Gender differences exist in preferences for institutions, but not in efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency (average group profit) compared to both the control (standard VCM) and the reward institution. The efficiency loss is due to the cost of punishment actions. The reward institution, even with lower maximum leverage, is more efficient than both punishment and the standard VCM. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency, unless the punishment technology is extremely effective and/or punishment is used sparingly. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across partner and stranger matching, and is not moderated by self-selection or communication (which is absent). The paper also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment or reward increases contributions, but only reward increases efficiency.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., Masclet, D., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Norm enforcement in social dilemmas: An experiment with police commissioners. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

- source: Page, T., Putterman, L., & Garcia, B. (2013). Voluntary contributions with redistribution: The effect of costly sanctions when one person's punishment is another's reward. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, the authors compare standard costly punishment (where fines are burned) to a redistributive punishment treatment (where fines are transferred to other group members as rewards). Both treatments use the same marginal per capita return (MPCR = 0.4), punishment cost (0.25 per unit), and group structure. The redistributive punishment treatment leads to significantly higher average contributions (9.03 vs. 7.09), higher average earnings (15.18 vs. 12.88), and higher efficiency, even after controlling for the resource-conserving effect of redistribution (earnings net of transfers: 14.20 vs. 12.88). The effect on efficiency is marginally statistically significant after netting out transfers. Redistributive punishment also reduces the share of punishment targeted at high contributors and increases the monetary return to contributing (from negative to positive). The study provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism can moderate the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no communication, and fixed partners, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency due to the costliness and mis-targeting of punishment. However, if the punishment mechanism is modified so that fines are redistributed as rewards to other group members (rather than burned), both contributions and efficiency increase, and the return to contributing becomes positive. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the structure of the punishment mechanism (specifically, whether punishment is redistributive or not) is a key moderator of the efficiency effect. The control (punishment-off) efficiency is not directly reported, but the paper references prior work showing that standard punishment does not increase efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, while redistributive punishment does. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the nature of the punishment and reward mechanisms.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that subjects are willing to pay to establish a norm enforcement mechanism that punishes or rewards deviations from the group average contribution. With the mechanism, average group contributions to the public good are substantially higher than in the baseline, and average earnings are also higher. The mechanism is almost always implemented, and its strength (enforcement parameter) is positively associated with higher public good contributions. Over time, groups learn to reduce over-investment in the mechanism. The mechanism treatment results in more stable and higher efficiency (measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling an endogenous, average-oriented punishment/reward mechanism in a repeated public good game with three players and ten rounds increases both group efficiency and average earnings compared to a control without such a mechanism. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency improvement is robust. The findings support predicting a more positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is endogenously funded and targets deviations from the group average. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed groups with repeated interaction and endogenous institution formation.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Kingsley, D. C. (2016). Endowment heterogeneity and peer punishment in a public good experiment: Cooperation and normative conflict. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that peer punishment increases contributions and group earnings in public goods games with homogeneous endowments, but not in games with heterogeneous endowments. In homogeneous groups, punishment mitigates the decay of cooperation and raises average contributions and pre-punishment earnings, though post-punishment earnings are lower due to the cost of punishment. In heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase contributions or earnings, and punishment costs further reduce efficiency. The results suggest that normative conflict arising from endowment heterogeneity undermines the effectiveness of peer punishment as a self-governance mechanism for promoting efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on endowment homogeneity. When endowments are homogeneous, enabling punishment increases contributions and can increase efficiency (though punishment costs may offset gains). When endowments are heterogeneous and endowment information is not available in the punishment stage, enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency, and punishment costs reduce group earnings. For prediction, the presence of endowment heterogeneity (without endowment information in punishment) should be expected to moderate or eliminate the positive effect of punishment on efficiency, even if control efficiency is known. The game is a continuous-contribution, interior-solution PGG with 4 players, 15 rounds, no chat, and punishment cost/impact of 1:3.

The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Boosey, L., & Isaac, R. M. (2016). Asymmetric network monitoring and punishment in public goods experiments. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment compares three network structures (Complete, Circle, Asymmetric) in a repeated linear public goods game with decentralized punishment. In all treatments, punishment is possible, but the network structure determines who can punish whom. The Asymmetric network features one under-monitored player (D) who can only be punished by one other player (C), while C is responsible for punishing all others. The main findings are: (1) Average contributions and efficiency are significantly lower in the Asymmetric network than in the Complete or Circle networks; (2) The drop in efficiency is driven entirely by the under-monitored player D, who contributes less and retaliates more against punishment; (3) There is no evidence that players with sole punishment responsibility (C) punish their unique target (D) more harshly; (4) Retaliatory (anti-social) punishment is more frequent from the under-monitored player, deterring effective punishment and reducing efficiency. The study provides direct evidence that network asymmetry can undermine the positive effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment network is a critical moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency in public goods games. When punishment is possible but monitoring/punishment is asymmetric (i.e., some players are under-monitored), efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or eliminated, primarily due to lower contributions and increased retaliation by under-monitored players. In symmetric networks (Complete, Circle), punishment supports higher contributions and efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends not only on the presence of punishment but also on the symmetry and coverage of the punishment network. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is known, the expected efficiency gain from punishment will be lower or even negative in asymmetric/patched networks compared to symmetric ones, even with identical punishment parameters.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Godoy, S., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2016). An individualistic approach to institution formation in public good games. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with four players and 20 rounds, the study compares four treatments: (1) standard VCM (no punishment), (2) exogenous universal punishment (StdPun), (3) endogenous punishment with zero cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP0), and (4) endogenous punishment with a small or large cost to acquire the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5). When the right to punish is costless (CTP0), participation is high enough that group contributions and efficiency are as high as in the exogenous punishment treatment (StdPun), and both are significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline (VCM). When there is any cost to acquiring the right to punish (CTP1, CTP5), participation drops, antisocial punishment increases, and group contributions and efficiency fall to levels not significantly different from the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust to the size of the cost: even a negligible cost eliminates the efficiency gains from punishment. The number of persistent punishers is a key moderator: at least two are needed for punishment to be effective. The study provides direct evidence that the institutional details of how punishment rights are provided (costless vs. costly, endogenous vs. exogenous) are critical for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend critically on whether the right to punish is costless and universally available, or must be acquired at a cost. When punishment is exogenously provided or costlessly available to all, efficiency increases substantially relative to the no-punishment baseline. When even a small cost is required to acquire the right to punish, participation drops, antisocial punishment rises, and efficiency gains disappear. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence and cost of acquiring punishment rights are key moderators: if the game design includes a cost to acquire punishment rights, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. If punishment is costless and universally available, expect large efficiency gains. The number of persistent punishers is also important: at least two are needed for effectiveness in this four-player design.

Restricting punishment to only allow higher contributors to punish lower contributors (legitimate punishment) increases both cooperation and efficiency compared to unrestricted punishment. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling unrestricted punishment may not increase efficiency due to antisocial punishment, but enabling legitimate punishment with full feedback is likely to yield a substantial efficiency gain. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the structure of punishment and information feedback.

- source: Leibbrandt, A., Ramalingam, A., Sääksvuori, L., & Walker, J. M. (2015). Incomplete punishment networks in public goods games: experimental evidence. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study experimentally manipulates punishment network structure and punishment capacity in a repeated linear public goods game with four players per group. Treatments include no-punishment, complete punishment, pairwise punishment, and untouchable punishment networks, as well as variants with increased punishment capacity. The main findings are: (1) Complete punishment networks lead to higher contributions than no-punishment or pairwise networks, but not necessarily higher group earnings after accounting for punishment costs. (2) Pairwise punishment networks are less effective at increasing contributions and do not improve efficiency. (3) Increasing punishment capacity in incomplete networks does not significantly improve contributions or efficiency. Enabling punishment in a complete network increases contributions but does not necessarily increase efficiency due to the cost of punishment. Incomplete networks (e.g., pairwise) do not yield efficiency gains and may even reduce efficiency. Increasing punishment capacity without changing network structure does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, it is crucial to account for both the network structure and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless network structure and punishment costs are considered.

- source: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y. (2013). Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy. *Economic Record*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).

It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A. (2016). Non-binding agreements in public goods experiments. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., & Masclet, D. (2015). Emotion venting and punishment in public good experiments. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard 4-player, 10-round public goods game with punishment (costly to punisher, more costly to target), the authors introduce several treatments allowing for different levels of emotion venting before punishment decisions: (1) Baseline (no venting), (2) Waiting (5-min cooling-off), (3) Waiting & Emotion (cooling-off plus self-reported emotions and unsent messages), and (4) Virtual Punishment (all previous plus non-binding virtual punishment). All treatments have punishment enabled. The main findings are: (a) All venting treatments reduce the amount and frequency of costly punishment compared to baseline; (b) The simple cooling-off period (Waiting) increases final payoffs (efficiency) relative to baseline, as reduced excessive punishment outweighs any loss in contributions; (c) More extensive venting (Virtual Punishment) reduces both punishment and contributions, and only increases efficiency in the early rounds, not in the long run; (d) The relationship between emotion venting and efficiency is non-linear (inverted-U): moderate venting is optimal for efficiency, while too little or too much venting is suboptimal.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard lab PGG with punishment, the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the emotional context of punishment decisions. If punishment is enabled without any venting, excessive punishment can reduce efficiency. Introducing a simple cooling-off period (moderate venting) reduces excessive punishment and increases efficiency. However, if venting is too extensive (e.g., virtual punishment), punishment is reduced too much, contributions fall, and efficiency gains are not sustained.
