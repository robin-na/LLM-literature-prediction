- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Prétôt, L., Taylor, Q., & Mcauliffe, K. (2024). Children cooperate more with in-group members than with out-group members in an iterated face-to-face Prisoner's Dilemma Game. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (CPR settings), cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained if and only if the group collectively invests a sufficient fraction of its resources into monitoring and sanctioning rule violations. When monitoring is funded unilaterally (peer punishment), self-interested agents will not invest enough, leading to a second-order free-rider problem and low efficiency. When monitoring is funded collectively from the common-pool resource (institutional punishment), there exists a threshold investment (beta) above which full cooperation and high efficiency are stable equilibria. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum investment required and shows that the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment technology), the cost of monitoring, and the severity of sanctions all moderate the efficiency gains from punishment. The model also predicts that initial 'free' monitoring (by some agents ignoring costs) may be needed to escape the no-cooperation equilibrium, but once cooperation is established, monitoring can be fully incentivized and efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on game design dimensions. It shows that the presence and structure of punishment (especially whether it is institutionally organized and collectively funded) are critical moderators of efficiency. The explicit inequalities derived in the paper allow one to predict whether enabling punishment will increase efficiency, given the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the fraction of resources invested in monitoring. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The results are most directly applicable to settings where monitoring and sanctioning are collective, and less so to purely peer-based punishment. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

  findings: Third-party punishment and the combination of third-party punishment and reward both significantly increase the average level of cooperation in a public goods game compared to a baseline with no sanctions. Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.

The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., & van Dijk, E. (2019). The effect of decision timing on the willingness to costly reward cooperation and punish noncooperation: Sanctioning the past, the present, or the future. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The main finding is that people are less willing to assign costly rewards or punishments when they must decide before observing others' (non)cooperation, compared to after. This effect is robust across both dichotomous and continuous public goods game variants, and for both reward and punishment, though the effect is stronger for punishment. The effect is not explained by differences in emotional reactions. The results are interpreted as evidence for nonconsequential reasoning: uncertainty about future behavior reduces decisiveness and willingness to sanction.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the willingness to use costly punishment (and reward) in public goods games is lower when sanctions must be decided before observing others' behavior, compared to after. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it cannot directly inform predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding when punishment is likely to be used, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Morsky, B., & Akçay, E. (2021). False beliefs can bootstrap cooperative communities through social norms. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that inaccurate, overly optimistic beliefs about the level of cooperation can bootstrap and sustain high cooperation in communities governed by conditional cooperation norms, even in anonymous settings. The model shows that the dynamics of joining, learning, and leaving the community can create bistability, abrupt transitions, or cycles in cooperation rates and community size. High cooperation can be achieved and maintained if naive (optimistic) individuals are present in sufficient numbers, and if learning the true (lower) level of cooperation is slow or if disillusioned individuals can easily leave. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward, but discusses how mechanisms that increase transparency or allow for punishment may interact with these dynamics. The findings are relevant for understanding how social norms and belief management can foster cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how belief management and social norm dynamics can affect cooperation rates in public-goods-game-like environments, especially in the absence of explicit punishment or reward. It suggests that mechanisms which slow learning about true cooperation levels or allow for easy exit can paradoxically increase cooperation by maintaining a pool of optimistic, conditionally cooperative individuals. However, because the model does not include punishment, reward, or explicit efficiency/payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The discussion of punishment is conceptual and highlights possible trade-offs between transparency (which may reduce cooperation by revealing low compliance) and incentive mechanisms (which may increase or decrease cooperation depending on beliefs). For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of belief dynamics and social norms as moderators, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: van Klingeren, F., & Buskens, V. (2024). Graduated sanctioning, endogenous institutions and sustainable cooperation in common-pool resources: An experimental test. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a dynamic common-pool resource (CPR) game with four players and repeated rounds, the introduction of sanctioning (punishment) mechanisms—either strict or graduated—leads to higher cooperation and resource sustainability compared to no sanctioning. Strict sanctioning (fixed, high-cost punishment) initially results in higher resource size and profit, but graduated sanctioning (punishment severity increases with repeated defection) leads to greater cooperation and lower appropriation over time, eventually closing the gap in resource size and profit. When groups can endogenously choose their sanctioning mechanism (via majority vote every five rounds), cooperation and resource outcomes improve further, especially in the long run. However, the differences in group and individual profit between sanctioning types are small and diminish over time. The study provides partial support for the effectiveness of graduated and endogenously chosen sanctioning, especially for long-term cooperation, but strict sanctioning may be more effective in short games or early rounds.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, dynamic CPR games with endogenous resource dynamics and mutual monitoring. The results suggest that both strict and graduated punishment mechanisms increase efficiency (resource sustainability and group profit) relative to no punishment. Strict punishment is more effective in the short term, but graduated punishment becomes equally or more effective over longer time horizons. Endogenously chosen punishment mechanisms (via group voting) further enhance cooperation and resource outcomes, though the effect on profit is small. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude and time course depending on the punishment regime and whether the institution is endogenously chosen. The findings are most applicable to settings with 4 players, 40 rounds, continuous contribution/appropriation, no chat, and mutual monitoring.

- source: Antoci, A., & Zarri, L. (2015). Punish and perish?. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary success and efficiency impact of punishment in a prisoner's dilemma with explicit punishment and reward options depend on the type of strong reciprocity present. Altruistic punishment of defectors (SNR) can support cooperation and high average payoff, but only if punishers are sufficiently numerous; otherwise, defectors dominate and efficiency collapses. Altruistic rewarding (SPR) leads to persistent coexistence of types and fluctuating efficiency. Symmetric strong reciprocity (punishing defectors and rewarding cooperators) is maladaptive: it leads to the extinction of cooperators and punishers, resulting in universal defection and minimal efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (PNP) or both defectors and non-punishing cooperators (HSNR) can robustly enforce cooperation and maintain high efficiency, with the PNP strategy being especially robust. The presence of unconditional cooperators who do not punish can undermine the evolutionary stability of punishment-based cooperation. The model shows that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universally positive and can be negative or fragile depending on the sanctioning structure and population heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-like environments is highly contingent on the details of the punishment and reward mechanisms and the behavioral heterogeneity of the population. In prediction tasks, the presence of explicit reward options, the type of punishment (who is punished), and the initial population composition are critical moderators. Punishment of defectors alone can increase efficiency if enough punishers are present, but this equilibrium is fragile. Adding reward can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce efficiency. Punishing non-punishing cooperators (second-order free riders) is more robust in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is essential to consider not just the presence of punishment but also the structure of available sanctions and rewards, and the possibility of second-order free riding. The findings caution against assuming that enabling punishment will always increase efficiency; under some conditions, it can be maladaptive.
