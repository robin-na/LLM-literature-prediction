  findings: The paper finds that enabling punishment in public goods games can increase group efficiency and sustain cooperation, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defectors. However, the effect is not universally positive: high punishment costs, the presence of antisocial punishment, or poorly tuned parameters can lead to lower efficiency or even the collapse of cooperation. If punishment is costly or antisocial punishment is possible, efficiency gains may be lost or reversed. The paper's phase diagrams and model descriptions can be used to map specific game designs to expected efficiency outcomes, given the control efficiency. However, the results are based on theoretical models and simulations, not direct empirical measurement, so predictions should be interpreted with caution and in the context of the model's assumptions.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, an adaptive hybrid policy that rewards when cooperation is rare and punishes when cooperation is common ('first carrot, then stick') minimizes the cost of establishing and maintaining cooperation. This policy outperforms pure reward or pure punishment in both effectiveness (range of conditions for full cooperation) and efficiency (cumulative cost to reach/restore cooperation). The findings hold for both well-mixed and spatial populations, and are robust to changes in group size, incentive leverage, spatial structure, and other model details. The hybrid policy is especially efficient at converting a population of defectors to cooperators, requiring less total incentive expenditure than either reward or punishment alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, for a given public goods game structure, the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on how punishment is combined with reward. Pure punishment is effective at recovering cooperation but costly for establishing it, while pure reward is the opposite. The adaptive hybrid policy achieves both at lower cost. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is maximized when punishment is used adaptively after cooperation is established, and that the efficiency benefit is greater when the punishment technology is strong relative to reward. The results are robust across a range of game design dimensions, including player count, group size, and spatial structure. However, the results are theoretical and assume institutional (not peer-to-peer) sanctioning, with rational agents and no implementation errors.

The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: Sasaki, T., Uchida, S., & Chen, X. J. (2015). Voluntary rewards mediate the evolution of pool punishment for maintaining public goods in large populations. *Scientific Reports*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary public goods games with large populations, pool punishment cannot emerge from rare initial conditions due to the second-order free rider problem. However, if a voluntary reward fund is available and especially if it rewards those who contribute to pool punishment, then pool punishment can emerge and become stable, leading to high group efficiency (i.e., the population converges to all-punisher state). The reward mechanism acts as a mediator, facilitating the transition from free riding to stable punishment-supported cooperation. Once established, pool punishment is self-sustaining and the reward fund is no longer needed. The findings are robust to variations in group size, benefit multipliers, and cost parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence of a reward mechanism, especially one that rewards contributors to punishment, is a key moderator for the emergence and stability of efficient outcomes in public goods games with punishment. In the absence of such a reward, punishment alone cannot overcome the second-order free rider problem in large populations, and efficiency remains low. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the presence or absence of a reward mechanism (and its design) is critical. Other dimensions such as group size, MPCR, and cost parameters are explored and found not to qualitatively change the main result: without reward, punishment does not increase efficiency from a free-rider baseline; with reward, punishment can emerge and efficiency increases dramatically.

  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible. When the synergy factor is low, prosocial punishment can still restore cooperation and efficiency, because nonpunishing defectors (second-order free-riders) undermine antisocial punishers, allowing punishing cooperators to dominate. The result is that, across a wide range of parameters, the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency, even in the presence of antisocial punishment. The paper also identifies parameter regions with stable coexistence of strategies, but the main result is that prosocial punishment is robustly effective in structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2013). The evolution of cooperation by social exclusion. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

  findings: The paper finds that social exclusion is a more effective and robust mechanism than costly punishment for sustaining cooperation and high efficiency in public goods games. When excluders can effectively and cheaply exclude defectors, the group can reach and maintain the efficient outcome where all contribute and defectors are eliminated. Costly punishment, even when effective, is vulnerable to invasion by non-punishing cooperators (second-order freeriders), leading to a decline in punishers and eventual dominance by defectors, which collapses efficiency. The model demonstrates that social exclusion can solve both the emergence and maintenance problems of cooperation, provided the cost of exclusion is not too high. The results are robust to changes in group size and to stochastic effects. The paper also notes that if monitoring costs are high, or if exclusion is not self-serving, the advantage of social exclusion disappears.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling a punishment mechanism that takes the form of social exclusion (where defectors are excluded from benefit sharing and the cost of exclusion is not too high) will lead to a large increase in group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across group sizes and does not require repeated interaction or reputation. In contrast, enabling costly punishment (where punishers pay to reduce defectors' payoffs) is less likely to increase efficiency, especially in the long run, due to vulnerability to second-order freeriders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social exclusion mechanism with low cost and high effectiveness should be treated as a strong positive moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment. The specific model parameters (group size, multiplication factor, cost of exclusion/punishment, effectiveness) are all explicitly analyzed, allowing for quantitative predictions if mapped to empirical settings.

  findings: The paper finds that, in finite-population public goods games, exclusion strategies (both peer and pool) consistently outperform punishment strategies (peer and pool) in terms of evolutionary advantage, which is measured by stationary frequencies and average payoffs. Peer exclusion dominates in the absence of second-order sanctioning, while pool exclusion dominates when second-order sanctioning is present. The results hold for both optional and compulsory PGGs and are robust to variations in group size and punishment fine (unless the fine is extremely high). The findings imply that, for a given control efficiency, enabling exclusion (especially peer exclusion) will yield higher group efficiency than enabling punishment, and that the presence of second-order sanctioning shifts the advantage to pool exclusion.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in standard PGGs, enabling exclusion (especially peer exclusion) will increase group efficiency more than enabling punishment, across a wide range of parameter settings. The effect is robust to group size, number of rounds (since the model is evolutionary and not round-based), and the relative costs of punishment and exclusion (as long as they are not extremely imbalanced). The presence of second-order sanctioning is a key moderator: without it, peer exclusion is optimal; with it, pool exclusion is optimal. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment (vs. exclusion), one should expect a positive but smaller efficiency gain than with exclusion, and that the details of sanctioning (peer vs pool, second-order) are critical.

For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.
