- source: Drouvelis, M., & Grosskopf, B. (2016). The effects of induced emotions on pro-social behaviour. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This study uses a one-shot voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) public goods game with and without punishment, manipulating both the announcement of punishment and participants' emotional state (anger vs. happiness). The main findings are: (1) Announcing the punishment stage increases contributions and average net earnings, confirming that punishment can improve efficiency in the short run. (2) Induced anger reduces contributions and increases the severity of punishment, leading to lower average net earnings (i.e., lower efficiency) compared to the happy condition. (3) The efficiency benefit of punishment is present but is diminished when subjects are angry, as harsher punishment increases costs and reduces net group payoffs. (4) The effect of punishment on efficiency is measured directly via average net earnings, and the design allows for clean causal inference due to the one-shot structure and between-subjects manipulation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a one-shot, three-player VCM with punishment, the introduction of a punishment stage (when announced) increases group efficiency (average net earnings) compared to the same game without punishment. The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends on the emotional state of participants: it is larger when subjects are happy and smaller (but still positive) when subjects are angry, due to increased punishment costs. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, controlling for baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the effect can be moderated by factors (such as mood) that affect the severity and frequency of punishment.

- source: Dickinson, D. L., & Masclet, D. (2015). Emotion venting and punishment in public good experiments. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard 4-player, 10-round public goods game with punishment (costly to punisher, more costly to target), the authors introduce several treatments allowing for different levels of emotion venting before punishment decisions: (1) Baseline (no venting), (2) Waiting (5-min cooling-off), (3) Waiting & Emotion (cooling-off plus self-reported emotions and unsent messages), and (4) Virtual Punishment (all previous plus non-binding virtual punishment). All treatments have punishment enabled. The main findings are: (a) All venting treatments reduce the amount and frequency of costly punishment compared to baseline; (b) The simple cooling-off period (Waiting) increases final payoffs (efficiency) relative to baseline, as reduced excessive punishment outweighs any loss in contributions; (c) More extensive venting (Virtual Punishment) reduces both punishment and contributions, and only increases efficiency in the early rounds, not in the long run; (d) The relationship between emotion venting and efficiency is non-linear (inverted-U): moderate venting is optimal for efficiency, while too little or too much venting is suboptimal. The study does not include a no-punishment control, so all results are relative to punishment-enabled baselines.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard lab PGG with punishment, the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the emotional context of punishment decisions. If punishment is enabled without any venting, excessive punishment can reduce efficiency. Introducing a simple cooling-off period (moderate venting) reduces excessive punishment and increases efficiency. However, if venting is too extensive (e.g., virtual punishment), punishment is reduced too much, contributions fall, and efficiency gains are not sustained. For prediction, this suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is maximized when the game design includes a moderate opportunity for emotional cooling-off before punishment, but not when venting is so extensive that it undermines the deterrence function of punishment. The results are most relevant for standard 4-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGGs with punishment enabled and no communication.

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2013). Enforcement of contribution norms in public good games with heterogeneous populations. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in the absence of punishment, public good groups (both homogeneous and heterogeneous) rapidly converge to low contributions and low efficiency, regardless of the normative appeal of efficiency or fairness rules. When punishment is enabled, contributions and efficiency increase sharply in all group types. About 40% of groups achieve full efficiency (maximal contributions by all) with punishment, while the rest enforce different relative contribution norms depending on group heterogeneity (e.g., equal contributions, proportional to endowment). The increase in efficiency from punishment is substantial and robust, though the exact efficiency level and norm enforced depend on the group structure. The paper provides direct evidence that punishment enables groups to overcome the collective action problem and coordinate on efficient or near-efficient outcomes, even in the presence of heterogeneity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in linear public good games with three players and ten rounds leads to a large increase in group efficiency, regardless of whether the group is homogeneous or heterogeneous in endowments or marginal benefits. The effect is robust: without punishment, efficiency is low due to free-riding; with punishment, efficiency rises substantially, with many groups achieving full efficiency. The specific norm enforced (equal contributions, proportionality, etc.) depends on the group’s heterogeneity, but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, in similar lab PGG designs, enabling punishment will significantly increase efficiency relative to the control, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by group heterogeneity and the specific norm that emerges.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2014). Self-Organization for Collective Action: An Experimental Study of Voting on Sanction Regimes. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, groups could vote between no sanctions (NS), informal sanctions (IS: peer punishment), and formal sanctions (FS: centralized punishment with fixed and variable costs, deterrent or non-deterrent). Both IS and deterrent FS substantially increased group efficiency and earnings relative to NS. IS were highly cost-effective, yielding higher returns per point spent on sanctions than FS in most conditions. IS were especially effective when chosen by vote, with group earnings and efficiency about 30% higher than when IS were imposed exogenously. Non-deterrent FS also increased efficiency when chosen by vote, but less so than IS. High fixed costs for FS reduced both their efficiency and their popularity. Reducing the effectiveness of IS (increasing cost per unit punishment) decreased their efficiency somewhat, but IS remained popular and relatively effective. Allowing IS and FS to coexist did not substantially change these patterns. Overall, IS can be as or more effective than FS in promoting efficiency, especially when adopted democratically.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with 5 players, enabling informal peer punishment (IS) can increase group efficiency to levels comparable to or exceeding those achieved by formal sanctions, especially when IS are chosen by vote. The efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost-effectiveness of the sanction regime, the fixed and variable costs of punishment, and whether the regime is adopted endogenously. High fixed costs for FS reduce their efficiency advantage. IS are most effective when punishment is relatively cheap and well-targeted. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling IS is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, and that the effect is robust to moderate changes in punishment technology and institutional coexistence. The control (no-sanction) efficiency is typically well below full cooperation, while IS and deterrent FS can approach or reach high efficiency, with IS often being more cost-effective.

- source: Kölle, F. (2015). Heterogeneity and cooperation: The role of capability and valuation on public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: homogeneous groups, groups with one high-valuation member (valuation heterogeneity), and groups with one high-capability member (capability heterogeneity). Each group plays 20 rounds, with punishment enabled in rounds 11-20. Efficiency (group payoff as % of social optimum) is measured with and without punishment. In homogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly change efficiency. In valuation-heterogeneous groups, punishment does not significantly increase efficiency and increases inequality. In capability-heterogeneous groups, punishment significantly increases efficiency (from 86% to 93% of optimum) and reduces inequality. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus conditional on the type of heterogeneity, being most effective when group members differ in capability but not in valuation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect is not uniform but depends on the type of group heterogeneity. If the game is homogeneous or has only capability heterogeneity (where some players' contributions are more productive but all value the public good equally), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially in the capability-heterogeneous case. If the group has valuation heterogeneity (where some players value the public good more), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency and increases inequality. The control efficiency (without punishment) is also higher in capability-heterogeneous groups than in valuation-heterogeneous groups. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, the type of heterogeneity (capability vs. valuation) is a critical moderator. Other design features (e.g., punishment cost, group size, feedback) are held constant in this study.

- source: Wolitzky, A. (2013). Cooperation with Network Monitoring. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games with network monitoring, the maximum sustainable level of cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved by grim trigger (punishment) strategies. The effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation depends on the monitoring network's structure (who observes whom), group size, and the type of public good. For pure public goods, efficiency is typically increasing in group size; for divisible public goods, it is typically decreasing. More connected and less uncertain monitoring networks support higher efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas for the maximum sustainable efficiency as a function of these design dimensions. Rewards are not necessary for sustaining maximum cooperation; punishment alone suffices.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public goods games with punishment, as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that, with punishment enabled (grim trigger strategies), the maximum sustainable efficiency is determined by the network structure, group size, monitoring technology, and the marginal per-capita return (mpcr). The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions: enabling punishment (grim trigger) increases efficiency up to the robust maximum, which is explicitly characterized. The paper also provides comparative statics for how efficiency changes with group size, network connectivity, and monitoring certainty, all of which are key design dimensions.

- source: Dal Bó, E., & Dal Bó, P. (2014). Do the right thing: The effects of moral suasion on cooperation. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a series of lab experiments using a two-player linear public goods game, the authors show that (1) moral suasion (exposure to a moral message) increases contributions and group payoffs, but the effect is short-lived when punishment is not available; (2) when a punishment stage is added (where players can pay to reduce their partner's payoff), the effect of moral suasion becomes persistent, with higher and sustained contributions and higher group payoffs; (3) punishment alone increases contributions compared to no-punishment, but does not prevent a decline over time; (4) the combination of punishment and moral suasion produces the highest and most persistent cooperation and payoffs; (5) the mechanisms include both changes in expectations about others and changes in preferences. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, but does report group payoffs and their changes, which are closely related.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player linear public goods game with random rematching, adding a punishment stage (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases group payoffs and sustains higher cooperation, especially when combined with moral suasion. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, and the effect is amplified when moral suasion is present. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are well specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with and without punishment. The paper is less informative about settings with more players, field contexts, or different punishment technologies, but is highly informative for standard lab PGGs.

- source: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K. (2013). Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). In the stranger condition (random rematching), punishment sustains higher contributions (11.38) than reward (9.21). The effect of punishment on cooperation is robust to group reshuffling, while reward is much less effective when strategic reciprocity is not possible. The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.

  findings: The experiment demonstrates that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions, with groups converging to near-full cooperation. Emotional responses (measured by SCR and self-reported valence) are closely linked to both cooperation and punishment: positive emotions are associated with higher contributions, and negative emotions in response to free riding predict punishment behavior. Receiving punishment leads to negative emotions and increased subsequent contributions by free riders. While efficiency is not directly reported, the near-maximal contributions in the punishment condition imply that group efficiency approaches the social optimum. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no rewards, anonymous punishment, full feedback) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar lab PGG designs. - source: Weng, Q., & Carlsson, F. (2015). Cooperation in teams: The role of identity, punishment, and endowment distribution. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that (1) strong identity (via human knot) increases contribution rates in heterogeneous teams without punishment, but not in homogeneous teams; (2) strong identity (via online chat) increases contribution rates in homogeneous teams; (3) punishment increases contribution rates when punishment is effective (1:3), even under strong identity, but not when punishment is less effective (1:2) and identity is strong; (4) strong identity does not further enhance contributions or punishment when punishment is available; (5) punishment of deviations from the team average does not depend on the target's endowment. The main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (as measured by contribution rate) depends on the effectiveness of punishment and the strength of identity-building. When punishment is effective (punishment impact per unit cost is high), it increases contributions even under strong identity. When punishment is less effective and identity is strong, punishment does not increase contributions. However, the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, for similar public good games, enabling effective punishment is likely to increase efficiency (by increasing contributions), but the effect may be moderated by identity-building and punishment effectiveness. The absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative prediction.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: Charness, G., & Yang, C. L. (2014). Starting small toward voluntary formation of efficient large groups in public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that endogenous group formation with exit, exclusion, and merger options produces very high contribution rates and group efficiency in public goods games. In the main treatment, where the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) increases with group size, groups tend to grow large and stable, and efficiency is maximized. When the efficiency gain from larger groups is capped, groups remain smaller and less stable, and efficiency is lower, but still substantially higher than in fixed-group baselines. Exclusion (a form of sanction) is used to remove low contributors, which protects cooperators and sustains high efficiency. Redemption (the ability for excluded individuals to rejoin) further boosts efficiency. The results suggest that voluntary group formation with exclusion and merger mechanisms can solve the free-rider problem and achieve high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games, mechanisms allowing voluntary group formation, exit, exclusion (as a sanction), and merger can dramatically increase efficiency compared to fixed groups. The effect is especially strong when economies of scale are present (i.e., when MPCR increases with group size). The exclusion mechanism functions as a sanction, but is not a direct costly punishment; rather, it is a group-level sanction that removes low contributors. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of exclusion/exit/merger mechanisms and increasing MPCR with group size are strong positive moderators of the efficiency effect of sanction-like mechanisms. However, the effect is not directly about costly punishment, but about exclusion as a sanction. The results are less informative for standard costly punishment, but highly informative for exclusion-based sanctions and endogenous group formation. Control efficiency (fixed group) is much lower than in the endogenous group treatments.

- source: Aimone, J. A., Iannaccone, L. R., Makowsky, M. D., & Rubin, J. (2013). Endogenous Group Formation via Unproductive Costs. *Review of Economic Studies*.

  findings: Allowing subjects to self-select into groups with lower private returns (sacrifice) leads to higher contributions and, crucially, higher group and individual payoffs in high-sacrifice groups compared to no-sacrifice groups.   prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games with endogenous group formation and self-imposed unproductive costs, but it does not inform about punishment or sanction mechanisms. If a game design allows for endogenous sorting into groups with different private returns (sacrifice), expect higher efficiency in high-sacrifice groups due to screening and substitution effects. The control (random assignment) condition shows that endogenous sorting is key to the efficiency gains.
