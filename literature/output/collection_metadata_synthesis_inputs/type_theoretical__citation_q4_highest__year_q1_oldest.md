- source: Cressman, R., Song, J. W., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2012). Cooperation and evolutionary dynamics in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, cooperation and group efficiency decline over time when punishment is not available, as self-regarding individuals free ride and conditional cooperators reduce their contributions in response. When a costly punishment mechanism is introduced, cooperation and group efficiency increase dramatically, approaching full cooperation and maximum group earnings. This effect is robust even when punishment is costly and when group composition changes to prevent repeated interactions. The review also highlights that a minority of strong reciprocators can sustain high efficiency when punishment is possible, but even a majority cannot do so without punishment. The findings are supported by experimental evidence from Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and others.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games substantially increases group efficiency, even when punishment is costly to the punisher and when group composition is randomized. The effect is robust across different group sizes and settings. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency from low or declining levels (in the control) to near-maximal levels (in the treatment), provided that punishment is sufficiently effective and visible. The review also suggests that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are important moderators, but the main qualitative result is that punishment enables high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Sasaki, T., Brännström, Dieckmann, U., & Sigmund, K. (2012). The take-it-or-leave-it option allows small penalties to overcome social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, allowing optional participation (the 'take-it-or-leave-it' option) enables even small penalties to establish a globally stable cooperative norm, maximizing group welfare. In contrast, compulsory participation requires much larger penalties to achieve the same effect, and can leave the group trapped in full defection if initial cooperation is low. Rewarding also promotes cooperation, but requires much higher costs to achieve full cooperation and does not eliminate the possibility of stable coexistence between cooperators and defectors. The efficiency gains from punishment with optional participation are robust across several model variants, including user-pays and capped punishment. The key outcome is that punishment, when combined with voluntary participation, is a highly efficient mechanism for overcoming social dilemmas in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling optional participation (voluntary entry) dramatically increases the efficiency of institutional punishment: much smaller penalties suffice to achieve full cooperation and maximize group welfare. The model is directly relevant to settings where the game design allows players to opt out, and where punishment is administered by an institution rather than peers. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), introducing institutional punishment with optional participation will yield a much larger increase in efficiency than with compulsory participation, and that the cost required to achieve this is much lower than for reward-based incentives. The results are robust to variations in group size, cost structure, and punishment allocation, making them broadly applicable to prediction tasks involving these design dimensions.

The reviewed experimental evidence (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000) shows that punishment prevents the decline of cooperation and can even increase it to near full cooperation, implying a positive effect on efficiency. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that punishment will have a more positive effect on efficiency when the punishment technology is strong and the cost is low, and that group size and the structure of repeated interaction also matter.

- source: Rand, D. G., Armao, J. J., Nakamaru, M., & Ohtsuki, H. (2010). Anti-social punishment can prevent the co-evolution of punishment and cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both well-mixed and spatially structured populations, allowing anti-social punishment (punishment of cooperators) prevents the evolution of cooperation in public goods games with costly punishment. In models where only pro-social punishment is possible, cooperation and punishment can co-evolve, leading to high group efficiency. However, when anti-social punishment is included, defecting strategies that punish others become dominant, and cooperation is not stable. This result holds across a wide range of parameter values and is robust to small behavioral errors. The findings highlight that the efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the absence of anti-social punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly contingent on the type of punishment allowed. If anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, as cooperation is not stable and defectors who punish others dominate. In contrast, if only pro-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment can increase efficiency by supporting cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of anti-social punishment as a design dimension is critical: if anti-social punishment is allowed, do not expect efficiency gains from punishment; if it is not allowed, efficiency gains are possible under the right parameter regimes.

The model demonstrates that, for a finite number of punishment stages, an arbitrarily small amount of conformist transmission is sufficient to stabilize cooperation and punishment, leading to higher group payoffs. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation and punishment are stable and when prosocial genetic traits may invade.

The model clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the costs of cooperation and punishment, the error rate, and the strength of conformist transmission. It also highlights that, in the absence of punishment, cooperation is not stable and efficiency is low. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of social learning mechanisms and the cost structure are critical. The model is most informative for settings where group size is large, punishment is costly but effective, and social learning is plausible.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: The simulations show that the introduction of social control (punishment/sanctions) in a public goods environment can move a group from a low-efficiency, noncooperative equilibrium to a high-efficiency, cooperative equilibrium. Material sanctions (with moderate cost and linear production) are most effective at achieving and sustaining high efficiency, while moral sanctions are vulnerable to start-up problems unless the cost structure is highly favorable. Internalized (self-imposed) fairness norms can lead to even higher efficiency but risk overcooperation (wasteful excess contribution). The model demonstrates that sanctioning systems can be self-limiting: once high cooperation is achieved, the need for enforcement diminishes. The efficiency gains from punishment depend on the sanctioning regime's cost, production function, and the group's learning rate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game can increase group efficiency, especially when the punishment system is not too costly and is effective at inducing compliance. The effect is moderated by the type of sanction (material vs. moral vs. internalized), the cost structure of enforcement, and the learning dynamics of the group. For prediction, the findings suggest that, all else equal, adding a reasonably efficient punishment mechanism to a PGG-like environment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude and sustainability of the effect depend on the details of the punishment technology and cost. The model also highlights the risk of overcooperation with internalized norms and the importance of self-limiting enforcement. The results are most directly applicable to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games with group sizes around 100, moderate jointness of supply, and no communication.

- source: Perc, M. (2012). Sustainable institutionalized punishment requires elimination of second-order free-riders. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a spatial public goods game with pool-punishment, sustainable institutionalized punishment (i.e., a non-negative punishment pool balance and thus maintained group payoff) is only possible if second-order free-riders are eliminated. This elimination occurs via a discontinuous phase transition as the punishment fine increases. If second-order free-riders are not punished sufficiently, the punishment institution cannot be sustained, and efficiency remains low. Notably, even with a significant fraction of defectors, efficiency can be maintained if second-order free-riders are absent. The critical parameters for this transition are the punishment fine (γ), the relative punishment of second-order free-riders (δ), and the synergy factor (r). The model provides phase diagrams showing regions of full, conditional, and unsustainable punishment, directly mapping to efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with institutionalized punishment is highly sensitive to whether second-order free-riders are sanctioned. If the game design allows for second-order free-riders and does not punish them sufficiently, enabling punishment will not improve efficiency and may even be unsustainable. If the design ensures that second-order free-riders are eliminated (e.g., by punishing them at a sufficient rate), enabling punishment can lead to a discontinuous jump in efficiency, even with a nontrivial fraction of defectors. Thus, for prediction, the presence and treatment of second-order free-riders, the punishment fine, and the synergy factor are critical moderators. The model is most directly relevant to spatial, structured populations with pool-punishment, but the qualitative insight about the necessity of eliminating second-order free-riders for sustainable efficiency is likely to generalize to other institutional punishment settings.

  findings: The main finding is that in optional public goods games, allowing all forms of punishment (including antisocial punishment) leads to the breakdown of the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group efficiency. Punishment does not increase average payoffs or cooperation rates; instead, it is used by all strategy types to protect against invasion, resulting in a 'tragedy of the commons' where costly punishment is widespread and destructive. Only when punishment is weak (low impact per cost) and the public good is highly efficient (high multiplier) can high cooperation and efficiency be sustained. Theoretical results are supported by large-scale simulations and are robust to parameter variation. Preliminary behavioral experiments are consistent with the model's predictions, but the main results are theoretical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the set of available punishment strategies. If only pro-social punishment (cooperators punishing defectors) is allowed, punishment can greatly increase efficiency. However, if antisocial punishment is possible, punishment does not improve efficiency and can even reduce it. The effect is robust across player count, group size, and a wide range of parameters. The efficiency impact of punishment is more positive when the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is high and punishment is weak (low punishment impact per cost). For prediction, the presence of antisocial punishment options and the effectiveness of punishment are key moderators.
