- source: Zhang, Z. Y., Elvis, N. T., Wang, J. W., & Hou, G. L. (2024). Can Leading by Example Alone Improve Cooperation?. *Behavioral Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use. The group average payoff in the punishment and reward conditions is higher than in the control and leader-only conditions, indicating a clear positive effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a 4-player, 32-round linear PGG with a 0.4 MPCR and no communication, enabling leader-administered punishment (with a 1:3 cost/impact ratio) increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to both a control (no leader, no punishment) and a leader-only (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

- source: JARUNGRATTANAPONG, R. (2022). PROMOTING COOPERATION IN A PUBLIC GOOD EXPERIMENT THROUGH OSTRACISM AS A PUNISHMENT MECHANISM. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that allowing group members to ostracize (temporarily exclude) others via majority vote in a repeated public goods game increases average contributions by about 45 percentage points and increases average earnings (welfare) by about 23% compared to the control (no punishment) game. The effect is strongest for free-riders but is present for all contributor types. Ostracism is implemented with a small direct cost (1% of endowment per vote), but the main cost is the reduction in group size and thus potential public good returns. Anti-social punishment is observed: non-cooperators who are punished tend to retaliate by voting against others, but this is not seen among high contributors. The positive effect on efficiency is robust, but cooperation and efficiency decline when ostracism is removed (end-game effect).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling ostracism (a form of social punishment) in a repeated public goods game with 4 players, 6 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.625 increases both contribution rates and average group earnings (efficiency) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is large and robust across contributor types. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost, etc.) are clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in similar lab PGGs. The main caveat is the presence of anti-social punishment and the collapse of cooperation when punishment is removed, suggesting that the positive effect is contingent on the continued availability of the punishment mechanism.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Peng, H. C., & Fan, Y. H. (2023). Incomplete punishment networks, heterogeneity, and cooperation in public good experiments. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the structure of the punishment network strongly moderates the effect of peer punishment on both cooperation and efficiency in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. Incomplete punishment networks (circle and homo-pairwise) lead to higher group payoffs (efficiency) and higher contributions than the complete punishment network. The complete network, despite more potential punishers, results in lower efficiency, lower contributions, and lower punishment severity, likely due to diffusion of responsibility (bystander effect). The effect of punishment is thus not monotonic in network density: more potential punishers do not always increase efficiency. The type of MPCR (high or low) also affects individual contributions, with high-MPCR individuals contributing more. The punishment mechanism is costly and binary (1 token cost, 3 token impact), and the experiment uses stranger matching over 15 rounds. No communication or chat is allowed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment network is crucial for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games, especially with heterogeneous returns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the structure of the punishment network (i.e., who can punish whom) is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs. When predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, it is not sufficient to know only the presence of punishment; the network structure (complete vs. incomplete, and the type of incomplete network) must be considered. In this design, incomplete networks (circle and homo-pairwise) outperform the complete network in both contributions and efficiency. The findings also show that punishment severity is lower in the complete network, likely due to a bystander effect. The experiment uses a four-player, 15-round, linear PGG with binary punishment, no chat, and random reassignment of MPCRs and groupings each round. These details should be matched when using this evidence for prediction. The results are most relevant for settings with heterogeneous returns and similar punishment mechanics.

- source: Kamei, K. (2024). Self-regulatory resources and institutional formation: An experiment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with five players and MPCR 0.4, the introduction of punishment mechanisms (either formal, with a fixed administrative cost and group-voted sanction rate, or informal, with peer-to-peer costly punishment) leads to large and significant increases in group efficiency and payoffs. Without punishment, free riding is prevalent and efficiency is low. With punishment enabled, average group payoffs rise significantly, and a much higher proportion of groups achieve high payoff thresholds. The effect is present regardless of whether participants’ self-regulatory resources are depleted. Formal punishment, when implemented with a deterrent sanction rate, achieves almost full efficiency. Informal punishment also increases efficiency, though not as much as formal deterrent punishment. The act of voting on institutions or sanction rates does not itself account for the efficiency gains; the presence of the punishment mechanism is the key driver.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard repeated public goods game. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no reward, etc.) are all clearly specified and match the canonical PGG setup. The control (no-punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while enabling punishment (either formal or informal) produces large, robust increases in efficiency and group payoff. The results are robust to manipulations of self-regulatory resources and to whether the punishment institution is chosen endogenously or imposed exogenously. For prediction tasks, the findings support a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency in similar game designs, with formal deterrent punishment achieving near-full efficiency and informal punishment producing substantial, though somewhat lower, efficiency gains.

- source: Joseph, G., Miquel-Florensa, J., Pahuja, S., Hoo, Y. R., & Tebekew, T. (2025). Motivating Public Sector Employees: Public Good Contributions in Addis Ababa Water and Sewerage Authority. *Economic Development and Cultural Change*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compared three public good game variants: (1) standard, (2) threshold (group must reach a minimum contribution or all is lost), and (3) monitor (one randomly chosen, anonymous group member can punish others at a cost). Both the threshold and monitor games increased individual contributions compared to the standard game, with the threshold game increasing contributions the most. However, because about one-quarter of groups failed to meet the threshold and lost all contributions, the monitor game produced the highest average group payoff (efficiency). The punishment mechanism was effective: monitors punished in about 73% of groups, and punishment was targeted at low contributors. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to order effects and individual characteristics. The study provides direct evidence that introducing costly punishment in a one-shot, anonymous, five-player public good game increases group efficiency relative to the control.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a five-player, one-shot public good game with continuous contributions, no communication, and a known MPCR of 0.4. The monitor (punishment) treatment increased group efficiency/payoff compared to the control. The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

- source: Botelho, A., Harrison, G. W., Pinto, L. M. C., Ross, D., & Rutström, E. E. (2022). Endogenous choice of institutional punishment mechanisms to promote social cooperation. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study replicates and extends classic public goods experiments by allowing subjects to experience both a punishment-enabled and a no-punishment institution, then vote on which to use for a high-stakes final round. Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. Only one session (out of 13) saw a majority vote for the punishment institution, and this was in a small cohort with high returns and high initial cooperation. Statistical analysis shows that subjects' votes are overwhelmingly determined by which institution gave them higher profits, not by which supported higher cooperation. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in matching protocol, order of experience, and marginal per-capita return. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard public goods games with costly voluntary punishment, enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, even though it increases contributions. The effect is robust across a range of game parameters (player count, MPCR, matching protocol, order, and returns). Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.

- source: Kim, H., Iris, D., Lee, J., & Tavoni, A. (2025). Representation, Peer Pressure and Punishment in a Public Goods Game. *Environmental & Resource Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment opportunities increase contributions in all treatments, but the efficiency gains depend on the decision structure. In the control (NoR, self-representation), punishment increases contributions to 75% of the endowment and does not reduce group payoffs, so efficiency is maintained or improved. In representation treatments (RnoM and RM), punishment increases contributions but also increases antisocial punishment, leading to lower group payoffs and reduced efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline. Communication (RM) between team members and representatives slightly improves outcomes compared to RnoM but does not fully restore efficiency. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive in self-representation but negative or neutral under representation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the decision structure: punishment is most effective (and efficient) when individuals decide for themselves, but can reduce efficiency when representatives decide for groups, especially if communication is limited. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency in self-representation settings, but may decrease or only modestly increase efficiency in representation settings, particularly when antisocial punishment is prevalent. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for direct mapping to prediction models. Communication channels (chat/messages) can moderate but not fully reverse negative efficiency effects under representation.

- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Punishment mechanisms and cooperation in public goods games: Experimental evidence. *Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics*.

  findings: In a lab public goods game with four players per group, the introduction of either individual or majority-voting punishment mechanisms increases average contributions relative to the no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether MPCRs are homogeneous or heterogeneous. However, group payoffs (efficiency) do not significantly differ between the no-punishment, individual punishment, and majority-voting punishment conditions.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds per condition, and either homogeneous (MPCR=0.5) or heterogeneous (MPCR=0.3/0.5/0.7) returns, enabling punishment (either individual or majority-voting) increases contributions but does not increase efficiency relative to the control. The cost of punishment absorbs the surplus from higher cooperation. Thus, when predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency unless the cost structure or punishment technology is more favorable. The majority-voting mechanism may reduce antisocial punishment but does not improve efficiency over individual punishment. The findings are most applicable to small, anonymous, stranger-matched groups with no communication and known round structure.

For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that adding punishment to a repeated linear PGG with 4 players, 10 rounds, and MPCR=0.4 will increase efficiency, even if punishment is not immediately observable. The magnitude of the effect may vary by group, but the direction is reliably positive. The paper also provides parameter values for key design dimensions.

- source: Bühren, C., Dannenberg, A., & Händel, P. (2025). The demand for complete and incomplete punishment institutions to promote cooperation. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with endogenous, costless, formal punishment institutions, complete punishment institutions (where all group members are covered) reliably lead to the implementation of deterrent punishment when cooperation is profitable, resulting in high contributions and, by implication, high efficiency. In contrast, incomplete punishment institutions (where only a subgroup is covered, or where subgroups can independently implement punishment) lead to less consistent implementation of deterrent punishment, lower average punishment rates, and lower contributions, even when cooperation is profitable. The marginal group return (MGR) is a strong predictor of punishment demand and contributions in complete institutions, but much less so in incomplete institutions, where individual cooperativeness and subgroup coordination become more important. Coordination failures between subgroups often prevent the realization of efficient outcomes in incomplete institutions.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution (complete vs. incomplete) is a critical moderator. When punishment is costless and covers all players, enabling punishment leads to near-maximal efficiency if cooperation is profitable. When punishment is incomplete (applies only to a subgroup or is implemented separately by subgroups), enabling punishment leads to less reliable efficiency gains, with outcomes depending more on group composition and coordination. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is a strong predictor of efficiency gains from punishment in complete institutions, but much weaker in incomplete ones. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the design implies low efficiency in the absence of punishment when cooperation is not profitable. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the presence of a complete, costless, formal punishment institution is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, while incomplete institutions introduce substantial uncertainty and lower average efficiency.
