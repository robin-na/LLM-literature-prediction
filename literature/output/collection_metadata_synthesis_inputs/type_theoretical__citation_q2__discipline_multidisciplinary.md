- source: Hetzer, M., & Sornette, D. (2013). An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, evolutionary dynamics lead to two regimes: (1) a defection regime with low cooperation and low group payoff when punishment is weak or absent, and (2) a coordination regime with higher cooperation and higher group payoff when punishment is sufficiently strong and driven by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model predicts the empirically observed propensity to punish in lab experiments. The presence of punishment transforms the social dilemma into a coordination problem, enabling higher efficiency. Disadvantageous inequity aversion is shown to be evolutionarily dominant, and populations with this trait invade and outcompete purely self-regarding populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency (group payoff) when punishment is sufficiently strong and is motivated by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model gives explicit formulas for the threshold propensity to punish and shows that, above this threshold, populations coordinate on higher contributions and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar design parameters (e.g., n=4, r=3, MPCR=0.4), enabling punishment will shift the group from low to high efficiency, provided the punishment regime is effective. The paper also predicts how efficiency and punishment propensity depend on group size and punishment effectiveness, which can inform predictions for other parameterizations. However, the paper does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency change, so predictions should be interpreted as strong theoretical expectations rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: Roberts, G. (2013). When Punishment Pays. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, punishment can be self-interested and increase group efficiency when the cost to punishers is outweighed by the benefits of increased cooperation. The key condition is that the cost to the punisher (p) is less than the product of the punishment impact (q), the benefit per cooperative act (b), divided by the net cost of cooperation (c): p < q*b/c. Simulations confirm that with typical lab parameters (e.g., group size 3, k=2, p=1, q=3), punishment leads to high stable cooperation and high group payoffs. The effect is robust to group size and number of rounds, provided the parameter condition holds. The paper also explains why punishment sometimes fails to increase payoffs (e.g., when p is too high or q is too low), and why punishment is more effective in repeated than one-shot games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for predicting that, in repeated public goods games with punishment enabled and with favorable punishment parameters (low p, high q), efficiency will be substantially higher than in control games without punishment. The key moderators are the punishment cost (p), punishment effectiveness (q), group size (g), and the number of rounds (m). The paper gives explicit formulas for when punishment will increase efficiency, and shows that the effect is robust across a range of typical lab parameters. If the control game has low efficiency and the punishment parameters satisfy p < q*b/c, enabling punishment should lead to a large increase in efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Bodnar, T., & Salathé, M. (2012). Governing the Global Commons with Local Institutions. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the ability of punishment to maintain high efficiency in a public goods game depends critically on the spatial reach of the sanctioning institution relative to the interaction range of the commons. Both peer and pool punishment can maintain the commons (i.e., prevent the tragedy of the commons and sustain high group payoff/efficiency) if the coverage coefficient (probability that two players can punish each other) is above a threshold. There are sharp phase transitions: small increases in the reach of punishment can lead to large increases in efficiency. Pool punishment can solve the second-order free rider problem and maintain the commons even when peer punishment cannot, but only if the cost of contributing to the punishment pool is low and the punishment imposed on defectors is high. The model also shows that in some parameter regimes, peer punishment can be more effective than pool punishment, especially when the reach of the sanctioning system is intermediate.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase efficiency, provided that the punishment system has sufficient reach (coverage) and the cost/impact parameters are favorable. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not linear but can exhibit threshold effects: if the punishment system is too local or weak, it may have little effect, but once a critical coverage is reached, efficiency can increase dramatically. The model also distinguishes between peer and pool punishment, showing that institutionalized (pool) punishment can maintain efficiency even in the face of second-order free riding, but only if the cost structure is appropriate. For prediction tasks, this paper indicates that the spatial reach and cost-effectiveness of punishment are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment, and that the absence of sufficient reach can nullify the effect of punishment.

- source: Ye, H., Chen, S., Luo, J., Tan, F., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2016). Increasing returns to scale: The solution to the second-order social dilemma. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a PGG with increasing returns to scale, the presence of punishment leads to a dramatic increase in group efficiency and average payoff, provided the IRS parameter (alpha) is above a threshold (about 1.2–1.3). In standard PGGs (alpha=1), punishment does not stably increase efficiency, as defectors can invade and dominate. With strong IRS (alpha > 1.3), punishers can resist invasion by both defectors and second-order free-riders, leading to stable high cooperation and high group payoff. The effect of punishment is thus highly contingent on the returns-to-scale structure of the public good. The results are supported by both analytical evolutionary models and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment in a PGG depends critically on the degree of increasing returns to scale in the production function. In standard linear PGGs, punishment may not improve efficiency, but in games with strong IRS, punishment can lead to near-maximal efficiency by stabilizing cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence and strength of IRS (or analogous nonlinear production functions) should be considered a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Other game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are also discussed, but the IRS effect is dominant.

- source: Hintze, A., Staudacher, J., Gelhar, K., Pothmann, A., Rasch, J., & Wildegger, D. (2020). Inclusive groups can avoid the tragedy of the commons. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment in public goods games lowers the critical synergy threshold required for cooperation to emerge, thereby increasing the likelihood of cooperation and improving group-level payoff (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. However, the cost of punishment reduces the total group payoff, so the efficiency gain from increased cooperation is partially offset by punishment costs. The effect is stronger when punishment is more efficient (higher fine per unit cost). The paper also shows that inclusiveness (redistribution of payoffs) is an even more effective way to promote cooperation and efficiency, often making punishment unnecessary if inclusiveness is high enough.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and computational evidence that enabling punishment in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is efficient (high fine, low cost). The effect is moderated by the costliness of punishment: higher punishment costs reduce the efficiency benefit. The findings also highlight that the structure of payoff redistribution (inclusiveness) can be a more powerful moderator than punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that, all else equal, adding punishment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the degree of payoff redistribution.

- source: Schnell, E., & Muthukrishna, M. (2024). Indirect reciprocity undermines indirect reciprocity destabilizing large- scale cooperation. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a nested PGG environment with both local and global public goods, indirect reciprocity (reputation-based conditional aid) can stabilize cooperation at a single scale, but when multiple scales are present, local cooperation is more stable and can invade global cooperation. This occurs even when the global PGG has a higher theoretical maximum payoff. The model demonstrates that the presence of multiple, potentially conflicting reputations leads to a situation where local cooperation undermines global cooperation, reducing overall efficiency at the larger scale. The more fragmented the population (more local groups), the harder it is for global cooperation to be stable. Thus, indirect reciprocity, as typically modeled, cannot by itself sustain efficient, large-scale cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the structure of group membership and the scale at which reputational incentives operate are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from indirect reciprocity-based 'punishment' (here, conditional aid/reward) in PGG-like environments. Specifically, if the game design allows for multiple, nested groups and reputations can be built at the local level, enabling local cooperation, then enabling indirect reciprocity (punishment/reward) may not increase, and can even decrease, efficiency at the global (large-group) level. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of multiple group scales and the ability to build local reputations are key negative moderators of the efficiency effect of punishment/reward mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the control (no punishment/reward) game is inefficient, but the addition of indirect reciprocity does not guarantee higher efficiency at the largest scale.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of cooperation in costly institutions exhibits Red Queen and Black Queen dynamics in heterogeneous public goods. *Communications Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The model demonstrates that introducing a costly institution (with a participation fee) alongside a free institution in a public goods game can increase cooperation and group efficiency. For low enhancement factors (low profitability), only the costly institution supports cooperation, and average payoffs remain close to the entrance cost (i.e., not much higher than the baseline). For higher enhancement factors, cooperation emerges in both institutions, and group payoffs increase above zero. The costly institution is more likely to be populated by cooperators, as defectors avoid the entrance cost unless there are enough cooperators to exploit. The model also shows that the effect of the entrance cost is context-dependent: it is beneficial for cooperation and efficiency at low profitability, but can be detrimental at high profitability if the cost is too high, as it reduces participation. The results hold in both well-mixed and structured populations, though spatial structure removes some bistability and further supports cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that introducing a costly institution (entrance fee) in a public goods game can increase efficiency and group payoff, especially when the baseline profitability (mpcr/enhancement factor) is low. The entrance cost acts as a filter, keeping defectors out and allowing cooperators to achieve higher payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly institution (or, by analogy, a punishment mechanism with a cost to participate) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such cost, particularly when the marginal per-capita return is low. However, if the cost is too high and the game is already highly profitable, the effect may reverse. The model does not include explicit punishment, but the costly institution's effect is adjacent to punishment in that it deters free-riding through a cost barrier. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where institutional participation costs or costly precommitment mechanisms are present, rather than direct peer punishment.
