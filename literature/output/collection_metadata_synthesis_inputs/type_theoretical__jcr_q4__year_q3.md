- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Eldakar, O. T., Kammeyer, J. O., Nagabandi, N., & Gallup, A. C. (2018). Hypocrisy and Corruption: How Disparities in Power Shape the Evolution of Social Control. *Evolutionary Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that punishment, whether selfish or altruistic, can sustain altruism in public goods games, but the efficiency of this outcome is contingent on power dynamics. Selfish punishment by a powerful minority can prevent the collapse into pure selfishness when power is highly asymmetric, but this comes at the cost of reduced group productivity compared to scenarios where altruistic punishment prevails. When power is more evenly distributed, altruistic punishment dominates, leading to higher levels of altruism and group efficiency. The model also shows that if selfish punishment is not possible, populations are more vulnerable to a collapse into selfishness under power asymmetry. Thus, the presence of punishment generally increases group-level cooperation and productivity, but the efficiency gain is maximized when punishment is altruistic and power is symmetric.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the symmetry and distribution of power. In environments with high power asymmetry, punishment may maintain some cooperation but at a lower efficiency due to the persistence of selfish punishers. In more egalitarian settings, punishment is more likely to be altruistic, leading to higher efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that game designs with symmetric power and distributed punishment opportunities will see greater efficiency gains from punishment, while those with concentrated or asymmetric power may see only partial gains or even efficiency losses if selfish punishment dominates. The model also highlights the importance of the severity of punishment consequences and the ability to exclude selfish punishers in determining efficiency outcomes.

- source: Bolle, F. (2021). Deterrence by Collective Punishment May Work against Criminals but Never against Freedom Fighters. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that collective punishment (CP) is ineffective or counterproductive in threshold public good games where resistance is individually costly but can produce a public good (e.g., protests, strikes). In these cases, increasing the threat or severity of CP does not increase compliance and may even increase resistance. In contrast, in threshold public bad games (e.g., pollution, crime), CP can sometimes increase compliance, but only when the individual benefit from misbehavior is small compared to the social cost. The paper provides formal equilibrium analysis and discusses equilibrium selection, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for predicting the behavioral effects of punishment in public-goods-like environments, especially regarding when punishment is likely to deter undesirable behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on when collective punishment is likely to be effective in public-goods-like games. It suggests that in threshold public good games (where cooperation is costly and produces a group benefit), punishment is unlikely to increase efficiency or compliance, and may even be counterproductive. In threshold public bad games (where defection is individually profitable but socially costly), punishment can increase compliance and, by implication, efficiency, but only when the individual incentive to defect is not too large. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions for efficiency, but its qualitative results can inform predictions about the likely direction of punishment effects given the structure of the game (public good vs. public bad, cost/benefit ratios). However, because the paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff directly, its support for efficiency prediction is indirect and should be supplemented with empirical or simulation evidence.

- source: Skarzhinskaya, Y. M., & Tsurikov, V. I. (2021). Organizational change: from community to firm. *Rossiiskii Zhurnal Menedzhmenta-russian Management Journal*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper develops mathematical models of a self-governed collective where individual effort generates aggregate income, distributed by pre-negotiated shares. In the baseline, self-interested behavior leads to an inefficient Nash equilibrium. When a coalition forms and coordinates effort, the coalition's and its members' payoffs increase. If the coalition offers contracts to non-members (who then forego their share in exchange for a reward per effort), the organization transforms into a hierarchical structure, and under certain conditions, all agents' payoffs increase, reaching the communal optimum. The paper provides theorems showing that expanding the coalition always increases the sum of payoffs for coalition members and the new joiner, and can also increase payoffs for non-coalition members. The analysis is entirely theoretical, with no empirical or experimental results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that mechanisms enabling coordination, selective incentives, or contracts (analogous to institutionalized punishment or reward) can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments. While it does not model explicit punishment, it analyzes how selective incentives and contracts can overcome inefficiency. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that introducing mechanisms that align individual and group incentives (such as contracts or selective rewards) can increase group efficiency, even in the absence of explicit punishment. The findings are most relevant for settings where coalition formation or contracting is possible, and less directly informative for standard PGGs with simple punishment options.

- source: Friehe, T., & Tabbach, A. (2018). A comparison of simple action-based and outcome-based policies for emergency-like situations. *Mathematical Social Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis shows that action-based punishment/reward policies are superior to outcome-based policies in generalized volunteer's dilemma settings (where more than one volunteer is needed and costs are private). Action-based policies can uniquely induce the efficient (second-best) outcome and prevent the no-participation equilibrium, while outcome-based policies always allow for a no-participation equilibrium, which is stable and potentially focal. The required level of punishment/reward under action-based policies is lower than under outcome-based policies. Combined policies that include punishment for non-participation can also rule out the no-participation equilibrium. The analysis is grounded in social cost minimization and group welfare, not just participation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that punishment (when implemented as action-based or combined with action-contingency) will have a more positive effect on efficiency (group welfare, social cost minimization) than outcome-based punishment in public-goods-like environments where more than one participant is needed. The results suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (action-based vs. outcome-based) is a critical moderator: action-based punishment can uniquely induce efficient outcomes and prevent social traps, while outcome-based punishment may fail to improve efficiency if the no-participation equilibrium is selected. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in settings with binary (all-or-nothing) participation, private costs, and no coordination. However, the results are theoretical and based on a generalized volunteer's dilemma, which is adjacent but not identical to standard PGGs.

- source: Okada, I. (2020). A Review of Theoretical Studies on Indirect Reciprocity. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper synthesizes theoretical advances in indirect reciprocity, showing that cooperation can be maintained in social dilemma settings when reputation mechanisms and justified punishment are present. It details how the refinement of assessment functions (e.g., leading eight norms) resolves issues like the scoring and punishment dilemmas, enabling stable cooperation under certain conditions. However, it notes that these models are not directly applicable to public goods games, as selective cooperation (the core of indirect reciprocity) is not possible in standard PGGs. The review also highlights the importance of the benefit-cost ratio, error rates, and the distinction between public and private reputation systems in determining the stability of cooperation. While punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed, the focus is on their role in norm stability and cooperation, not on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and reputation mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in social dilemmas, but it does not offer direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its models are adjacent to PGGs but differ in key structural ways (e.g., selective cooperation vs. public goods provision). The review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment and norm refinement can support cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment depends on norm structure, reputation information, and error rates, but it does not allow for direct mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency outcomes in PGGs.

- source: Andersson, L. (2020). Cooperation between Emotional Players. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated prisoners' dilemma games, the presence of emotional players—who value others' payoffs positively (friendly) or negatively (hostile) depending on the history of play—can alter the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher efficiency) is sustainable. With Grim Trigger strategies, emotional states (both friendly and hostile) lower the required discount factor for cooperation, making efficient outcomes more attainable. With mutual minmax strategies, emotions can either facilitate or obstruct cooperation: friendly states increase the cost of deviating, but hostile states can make it harder to return to cooperation after punishment, sometimes requiring greater patience than in the standard model. The model also shows that a rational player can manipulate an emotional player into a friendly state to achieve higher payoffs. The analysis is entirely theoretical, with no empirical or experimental data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how emotional dynamics—modeled as state-dependent preferences—can affect the efficiency of repeated social dilemmas. While not a public goods game per se, the repeated prisoners' dilemma is closely related. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment-like mechanisms (here, emotional transitions that make punishment more or less credible) on efficiency depends on the interplay between strategy, emotional responsiveness, and patience (discount factor). For prediction tasks, the paper implies that enabling mechanisms that make punishment more credible (e.g., emotional hostility after defection) can increase efficiency under some strategies, but may also backfire if they make return to cooperation difficult. The results are most directly relevant for settings where player preferences or states can change in response to observed actions, and less so for standard PGGs with fixed payoffs and explicit punishment stages.

- source: Madeo, D., & Mocenni, C. (2021). Consensus towards Partially Cooperative Strategies in Self-Regulated Evolutionary Games on Networks. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in Stag-Hunt and Chicken games on networks, consensus on partial cooperation can be globally stable and more rewarding (in terms of average payoff) than full cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma. The stability of full cooperation, full defection, or partial cooperation depends on the self-regulation parameter relative to network degree. The model does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms; instead, it focuses on endogenous self-regulation. Payoff at equilibrium is analytically and numerically compared across game types and equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in networked games with self-regulation (but without explicit punishment or reward), partial cooperation can be globally stable and yield higher payoffs than full cooperation in the PD. However, because the model does not include punishment, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to enabling punishment in public goods games. The results are most relevant for understanding how internalized social preferences (modeled as self-regulation) affect efficiency, rather than the effect of exogenous punishment mechanisms.

- source: McCannon, B. C. (2018). Social norms and cooperation. *International Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that incorporating a preference for social norm compliance into a one-shot prisoner's dilemma can generate cooperative behavior as an equilibrium outcome, even without repeated interaction, communication, or punishment. The likelihood of cooperation depends on the strength of the disutility from norm violation. However, the non-cooperative outcome is always an equilibrium as well. The model is presented as a theoretical explanation for observed cooperation in experiments, but does not itself report empirical results or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not include punishment, sanctions, or payoff-based outcomes, and is focused on a two-player, one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Its main contribution is to show that social norm preferences can support cooperation in the absence of punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative, except as a reminder that cooperation can arise from norm compliance alone, and that the absence of punishment does not necessarily imply zero cooperation. It does not inform how punishment affects efficiency or how game design dimensions moderate that effect.
