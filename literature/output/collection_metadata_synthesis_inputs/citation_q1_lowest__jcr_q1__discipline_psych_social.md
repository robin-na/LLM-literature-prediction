- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

- source: Duell, D., Mengel, F., Mohlin, E., & Weidenholzer, S. (2024). Cooperation through collective punishment and participation. *Political Science Research and Methods*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring (noisy signals about individual contributions, but perfect information about total group contribution), the authors experimentally compare five regimes: no punishment, peer-to-peer punishment, collective punishment, peer-to-peer punishment with commitment, and collective punishment with commitment. The key finding is that only collective punishment with commitment (where group members can opt in to a regime that automatically punishes the whole group if total contributions fall short) leads to significantly higher group payoffs and cooperation rates compared to both no punishment and peer-to-peer punishment. Peer-to-peer punishment does not improve payoffs or cooperation relative to no punishment in this noisy environment. The positive effect of collective punishment with commitment is especially pronounced in the later rounds, suggesting a learning or coordination effect. The study also shows that the ability to participate in the decision to implement collective punishment (i.e., voting/commitment) is crucial for its effectiveness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling collective punishment with commitment in a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling collective punishment with commitment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff) substantially, especially over time, provided the punishment is collective, pre-committed, and participation in the institution is possible. The effect is not observed for peer-to-peer punishment or for collective punishment without commitment. The experimental design and reporting allow for direct mapping to prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, given the game design dimensions.

- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

- source: Chen, J. C., D'Attoma, J., Fonseca, M. A., & Malezieux, A. (2025). Promoting Cooperation in an Unequal World: Experimental Evidence on the Role of Transparency and Punishment. *Journal of Business Ethics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across two lab experiments using a four-player linear public goods game with unequal endowments (determined by merit and luck), the paper finds that: (1) Peer punishment increases contributions and group payoffs, but only when endowments are transparent. (2) When endowments are secret, punishment has a limited effect on cooperation and does not reduce inequality; advantaged participants contribute a lower fraction of their endowment and earn much higher payoffs. (3) When endowments are made public, punishment becomes much more effective at increasing contributions and reducing inequality, especially among the advantaged. (4) Willingness to pay for secrecy is higher among advantaged participants, especially when their advantage is due to luck. (5) Overall, transparency is necessary for punishment to improve efficiency and mitigate the negative effects of inequality.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on whether endowments (wealth) are transparent. In games with secret endowments, punishment has little effect on group payoff or efficiency; in games with public endowments, punishment significantly increases contributions and group payoffs, especially among the advantaged. For prediction, the presence of transparency (showing endowments or relevant summaries) is a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when transparency is high. The game uses continuous contributions, no chat, and a fixed MPCR of 0.4. Punishment is costly (cost 1, impact 3), and there is no reward option. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar design features.

  findings: Centralized punishment substantially increases the frequency of cooperation in both public good and generalized exchange games. However, the main focus is on how punishment affects solidarity, not efficiency. Solidarity (measured affectively, behaviorally, and via beliefs) increases with punishment in generalized exchange but not in the public good setting. The positive effect of punishment on solidarity is largely mediated by increased cooperation frequency. The experiment does not report group efficiency or total payoff, and the only payoff-related outcomes are cooperation rates and within-group payoff variation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that centralized punishment increases cooperation rates in both public good and generalized exchange games with four players and 16 rounds. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, binary choice, etc.) are well specified, and the manipulation of punishment is clear (centralized, probabilistic, fixed fine). The main value for efficiency prediction is the robust increase in cooperation frequency with punishment, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in standard PGGs, but the absence of direct efficiency or payoff analysis limits the strength of inference.

- source: Eisenkopf, G., & Walter, C. (2022). Leadership with Imperfect Monitoring. *Leadership Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a three-player, 20-round public goods game, punishment by a leader only increases contributions when the leader can observe individual follower contributions (individual monitoring). Under group monitoring (only aggregate info), punishment does not increase contributions compared to a simple recommendation. Leading-by-example increases coordination (alignment of contributions) but not aggregate contributions relative to recommendations. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined. However, since contributions are higher with punishment under individual monitoring, it is likely that efficiency would also be higher in that condition, assuming standard PGG payoff structure. The effect of punishment is thus strongly moderated by the monitoring technology.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether the punisher can observe individual contributions (i.e., the 'punishment_tech' and monitoring dimension). If only aggregate (group-level) monitoring is available, punishment does not increase cooperation or likely efficiency. If individual-level monitoring is available, punishment increases cooperation and likely efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency or payoff data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution rates. Other design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, etc.) are clearly specified and match standard PGG setups. The absence of communication and rewards, and the use of continuous contribution choices, are also informative for prediction.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is contingent on the probability of enforcement. The main moderators (payment scheme, transparency) do not interact with the symbol to affect efficiency. Thus, symbolic authority should not be expected to improve efficiency in similar lab PGG environments.

- source: Odouard, V. V., Smirnova, D., & Edelman, S. (2023). Polarize, catalyze, and stabilize: how a minority of norm internalizers amplify group selection and punishment. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The presence of norm internalizers in the strategy space (even as a small minority, around 10% of the population) leads to higher mean cooperation rates, longer and more stable periods of high cooperation, and fewer long periods of low cooperation, compared to conditions with only group selection and punishment. This effect is most pronounced at intermediate benefit-to-cost ratios, where cooperation is possible but not guaranteed. The mechanism is indirect: norm internalizers polarize groups (increasing inter-group variance), catalyze spikes in cooperation when global cooperation is low, and stabilize high-cooperation states. The effect is not due to norm internalizers themselves cooperating more, but to their influence on the behavior of other agents. The paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, so the direct effect on efficiency is not established.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in agent-based models of public goods games with group selection and imperfect punishment, adding a minority of norm internalizers can substantially increase mean cooperation rates, especially at intermediate benefit-to-cost ratios. However, because the reported outcomes are cooperation rates and not efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used as indirect evidence for predicting efficiency: higher cooperation rates likely imply higher efficiency, but the mapping is not quantified here. The model assumes punishment is imperfect (not all defections are observed), and punishment is implemented as withholding benefits rather than costly punishment. The results are robust across two different model structures (abstract and spatial/naturalistic). For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of norm internalization mechanisms can amplify the effect of punishment and group selection on cooperation, but does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency.
