- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Micheli, L., Stallen, M., & Sanfey, A. G. (2021). The Effect of Centralized Financial and Social Incentives on Cooperative Behavior and Its Underlying Neural Mechanisms. *Brain Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: Across two lab experiments (one with fMRI), the prospect of centralized, probabilistic financial or social incentives (either rewards or punishments) substantially increases the percentage of tokens contributed to the group pot in a public goods game, compared to a no-incentive baseline. Financial incentives have a stronger effect than social incentives, but both are effective. There is no significant difference between the effect of rewards and punishments on contribution rates. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome, so the findings are most directly about contribution behavior rather than payoff-based efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized, probabilistic punishment (and/or reward) mechanisms increases contribution rates in a standard public goods game, with financial incentives being more effective than social incentives. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings must be interpreted as indirect evidence for efficiency effects. In typical PGGs, higher contribution rates are associated with higher efficiency, so the results suggest that enabling punishment (or reward) would likely increase efficiency relative to a no-incentive baseline, especially when the incentives are financial and administered centrally. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, probabilistic centralized punishment, no chat, continuous contribution) are well specified, making the study informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Liu, X. L., Li, J. B., Wang, G. R., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., & Fu, X. G. (2020). Transcranial direct current stimulation of the rLPFC shifts normative judgments in voluntary cooperation. *Neuroscience Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study demonstrates that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects both participants' normative judgments about appropriate contributions and their actual contributions in a one-shot, linear, asymmetric public goods game. Anodal stimulation (increasing rLPFC excitability) leads to higher normative judgments and higher contributions, while cathodal stimulation (decreasing rLPFC excitability) leads to lower judgments and contributions, relative to sham. The effect is robust across both 'rich' and 'poor' player types. No punishment, sanction, or reward mechanisms are present in the game, and no efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. Its findings are relevant to understanding the neural basis of normative judgment and voluntary cooperation, but not to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design dimensions reported (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds) may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this study should not be used as direct evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

- source: Park, S. A., Jeong, S., & Jeong, J. (2013). TV programs that denounce unfair advantage impact women's sensitivity to defection in the public goods game. *Social Neuroscience*.

  findings: The study finds that in a 10-round, 4-player, binary (all-or-nothing) threshold PGG, women are more cooperative than men in early rounds, but this difference disappears after both genders watch investigative TV programs about free-riding. After media exposure, women become more likely to free-ride in response to others' defection, adopting a tit-for-tat strategy. It does, however, provide detailed information about a binary, threshold PGG with 4 players and 10 rounds, and shows how external context (media exposure) can modulate cooperation rates, especially among women. For the prediction task, this paper is not directly informative about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, but it does confirm that in the absence of punishment, cooperation rates (and thus likely efficiency) can be sensitive to social context and gender. The absence of punishment and the focus on behavioral (not payoff) outcomes limit its direct relevance.

- source: Ashlock, D., Smucker, M. D., Stanley, E. A., & Tesfatsion, L. (1996). Preferential partner selection in an evolutionary study of Prisoner's Dilemma. *Biosystems*.

  findings: The paper finds that, in dyadic repeated Dictator Game settings, observers rate those who withdraw cooperation from defectors as more cooperative and less vengeful than those who punish. For prediction tasks about efficiency in public goods games, this paper is only weakly informative, as it does not measure efficiency or group payoff, and the game structure is dyadic, not group-based.

- source: Watson, R., Morgan, T. J. H., Kendal, R. L., van de Vyver, J., & Kendal, J. (2024). Investigating the effects of social information on spite in an online game. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.

  findings: Participants overwhelmingly chose altruism over spite when given a costly choice to increase or decrease an anonymous partner's payoff. Exposure to social information indicating others had behaved spitefully (vs. altruistically) reduced the degree of altruism, but did not substantially increase spiteful acts. The source of social information (majority vs. most successful) had no effect. The study does not report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff, and the design is a one-shot, two-player, non-iterated game with a continuous choice from costly spite to costly altruism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar repeated group games. The design is a one-shot, two-player, continuous-choice game where participants can pay to harm or help an anonymous partner, and the main outcome is a behavioral slider, not group efficiency or payoff. There is no repeated interaction, no group context, and no explicit public goods structure. The findings suggest that when both costly spite and costly altruism are available, spite is rare and social information can reduce altruism, but this is not directly transferable to the efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments.

- source: Rilling, J. K., Goldsmith, D. R., Glenn, A. L., Jairam, M. R., Elfenbein, H. A., Dagenais, J. E., Murdock, C. D., & Pagnoni, G. (2008). The neural correlates of the affective response to unreciprocated cooperation. *Neuropsychologia*.
