- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Gürdal, M. Y., Gürerk, & Yahsi, M. (2021). Culture and prevalence of sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

- source: Glöckner, A., Kube, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2018). The joint benefits of observed and unobserved social sanctions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players over ten rounds, the authors compare four conditions: no punishment, observed punishment only, unobserved (delayed) punishment only, and both punishment channels available. Both observed and unobserved punishment alone increase contributions but do not improve efficiency (average payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline, due to the costliness of punishment. However, when both punishment channels are available, group efficiency is significantly higher than in all other conditions. This is because the threat of unobserved punishment increases the effectiveness of observed punishment, leading to higher cooperation with less punishment expenditure. The combination of punishment channels thus overcomes the typical efficiency losses seen in standard lab PGG punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the punishment institution. Standard punishment (observed or unobserved alone) does not improve efficiency over the control, but the combination of both channels leads to a large positive effect. For prediction, this means that the presence of multiple punishment channels (observed and unobserved) is a key moderator: if only one is present, efficiency is not improved; if both are present, efficiency is substantially increased. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the punishment technology (timing/observability), cost, and whether multiple channels are available. Control efficiency (no punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless the institutional details of punishment are considered.

- source: Zhang, B. J., Cui, Z. G., & Yue, X. H. (2019). Cluster evolution in public goods game with fairness mechanism. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a well-mixed PGG, adding a fairness mechanism and peer punishment (with costs shared among cooperators) can achieve full cooperation and maximum efficiency in small clusters. In larger clusters, full cooperation is difficult, but increasing the fairness factor (weight on fairness utility) and decreasing the cost of punishment both increase the equilibrium number of cooperators and group utility. The model provides explicit formulas for the utilities of cooperators and defectors with and without punishment, and shows that punishment is only effective (i.e., leads to higher group efficiency) when the number of cooperators exceeds a threshold. Simulation results confirm that lower punishment cost and higher fairness factor lead to higher group efficiency and larger stable clusters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in PGGs with peer punishment, efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) increases when punishment is enabled, especially when punishment cost is low and the fairness factor is high. The model allows explicit calculation of group efficiency under different parameter settings, including player count, punishment cost, and fairness factor. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, with the effect size moderated by punishment cost and the fairness factor. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, continuous-contribution PGGs with no communication, and where punishment costs are shared among cooperators.

- source: Chugunova, M., Luhan, W. J., & Nicklisch, A. (2020). When to leave carrots for sticks: On the evolution of sanctioning institutions in open communities. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Participants initially choose reward-based public goods communities, which provide higher payoffs and are preferred by most. Over time, as rewards decline and inequality increases in the reward community, some participants migrate to the punishment community. The punishment community achieves higher contribution rates, but lower average payoffs compared to the reward community. Importantly, when punishment and reward communities compete, the punishment community requires less actual punishment to sustain cooperation, reducing welfare losses typically associated with punishment. The threat of punishment, rather than its frequent use, is sufficient to maintain high contributions. However, the reward community still retains a majority of participants and maintains higher average payoffs, though the difference with the punishment community narrows over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the institutional context. In isolation, punishment communities sustain high contributions but at a welfare cost due to punishment expenditures. When a reward community is available, migration dynamics mean that the punishment community can sustain cooperation with less actual punishment, reducing welfare losses. Thus, the negative efficiency impact of punishment is mitigated when there is institutional competition. For prediction, this implies that the efficiency of punishment-enabled games may be higher when players can choose between institutions, and that the mere threat of punishment can be effective if migration is possible. However, in direct comparison, reward communities yield higher payoffs than punishment communities, though the gap narrows over time.

  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a weak, automatic punishment institution (where the lowest contributor in a group loses 1 yuan per round) does not significantly increase contributions compared to a control PGG or to a reward institution. There is no significant effect of matching or mismatching subjects' institutional preferences (punishment vs reward) with the institution they are assigned to.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a repeated linear PGG with weak, automatic, centralized punishment (punishment cost = 1, punishment tech = 1, imposed on lowest contributor), enabling punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency compared to a control. The punishment is non-deterrent and strictly efficiency-reducing by design. Therefore, in similar game designs (weak, automatic, centralized punishment), one should not expect punishment to improve efficiency over the control. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so the evidence is indirect and based on contribution rates and the institutional design. The findings are most relevant for settings with weak, automatic, non-peer punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment or stronger punishment regimes.

- source: Liu, J., Riyanto, Y. E., & Zhang, R. K. (2020). Firing the right bullets: Exploring the effectiveness of the hired-gun mechanism in the provision of public goods. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The hired-gun mechanism, a centralized punishment system targeting the lowest contributor(s), is highly effective at increasing contributions in public goods games when both unilateral and tie punishments are positive. The effect is robust to increasing the choice set (larger endowment). However, if the unilateral punishment is removed (set to zero), contributions decay over time, and the mechanism fails to sustain high cooperation, even if the tie punishment remains. Theoretical predictions suggest a range of punishment parameters should work, but empirically, zero unilateral punishment is insufficient. The main outcome is contribution rate, not direct efficiency or group payoff, but high contributions imply high efficiency in this context. The study also finds that punishment can be softened (reduced in size) to a degree without loss of effectiveness, but not eliminated.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks relating to efficiency in public goods games with centralized punishment, this paper provides strong evidence that the hired-gun mechanism (centralized, automatic punishment of the lowest contributor) is highly effective at increasing contributions (and thus likely efficiency) when both unilateral and tie punishments are positive. The effect is robust to larger choice sets and group size of 4. However, if the unilateral punishment is zero, the mechanism is ineffective, and contributions decay. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the near-full-contribution rates in the treatment with punishment strongly suggest near-maximal efficiency. Thus, when mapping from game design to expected efficiency, the presence and size of both punishment components are critical moderators. The absence of unilateral punishment is a strong negative moderator. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with centralized, automatic punishment and group size 4, with full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Yang, L. H., Xu, Z. J., Zhang, L. Z., & Yang, D. X. (2018). Benefits of intervention in spatial public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.

The effect is non-monotonic: weak or excessive intervention can reduce efficiency.
