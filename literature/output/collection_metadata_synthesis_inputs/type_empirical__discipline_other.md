  findings: This study finds that in a one-shot voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM) with four players, the introduction of costly opportunities to sanction or reward other group members does not significantly increase group account contributions or allocative efficiency compared to a baseline VCM without such opportunities. The mean allocative efficiency is highest in the baseline (77.37%), and is similar or slightly lower in the sanction and reward treatments, with no statistically significant differences. Subjects do use sanctions and rewards, and these are directed as expected (punishing low contributors, rewarding high contributors), but the costs of these actions offset any potential gains in cooperation, resulting in no net efficiency improvement. Making the imposition of sanctions or rewards uncertain (vs. certain) also has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in one-shot, four-player, continuous-contribution VCMs with a marginal per-capita return of 0.5, the introduction of costly punishment or reward opportunities does not increase efficiency relative to the control. The costs of punishment and reward offset any behavioral increases in cooperation, resulting in similar or slightly lower efficiency. The results suggest that, in one-shot settings, enabling punishment or reward is unlikely to improve group efficiency, and may even reduce it slightly due to the direct costs incurred. The findings are directly relevant for settings with no repeated interaction, no communication, and anonymous groupings.

- source: Grechenig, K., Nicklisch, A., & Thöni, C. (2010). Punishment Despite Reasonable Doubt-A Public Goods Experiment with Sanctions Under Uncertainty. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard repeated public goods game with and without punishment, and with varying levels of noise in information about others' contributions, the paper finds that: (1) Punishment increases efficiency and cooperation only when information is accurate; (2) With moderate or high noise, punishment does not improve cooperation and reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline; (3) Subjects continue to punish under noise, but punishment is less targeted and more frequent, leading to more antisocial punishment and welfare loss; (4) The efficiency loss from punishment under noise is substantial and persistent, even though subjects could choose not to punish. The results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing mechanism is highly contingent on the accuracy of information about others' behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency benefit of enabling punishment in public goods games depends critically on the accuracy of information about others' contributions. When information is accurate, punishment increases efficiency; when information is noisy, punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring is a key moderator: if the game design includes noisy or imperfect information about contributions, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency. If information is accurate, punishment is likely to increase efficiency. Other design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable moderator is information accuracy.

- source: 
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a within-subjects lab experiment with 76 participants, five public goods game variants were tested: no punishment, peer punishment (individual/shared costs), and democratic punishment (individual/shared costs). All punishment systems increased cooperation rates relative to no-punishment. However, peer punishment (both cost structures) led to lower total payoffs than no-punishment, due to high punishment costs and antisocial punishment. Democratic punishment systems (both cost structures) achieved higher total payoffs than peer punishment, and were not significantly different from the no-punishment system in total payoff. Democratic punishment also reduced antisocial punishment and increased psychological benefits. Thus, punishment can increase efficiency relative to peer punishment, but not relative to no-punishment; only democratic punishment avoids the efficiency loss seen with peer punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends strongly on the institutional design. Peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to punishment costs and antisocial punishment. Democratic punishment systems (with voting) mitigate these costs, resulting in efficiency that matches the no-punishment baseline and exceeds peer punishment. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of voting are all critical moderators. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is high, enabling peer punishment may reduce efficiency, but enabling democratic punishment is likely to maintain efficiency at the control level. The presence of voting and the structure of punishment costs are key design dimensions.

For prediction, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially if chosen by vote and with low or no fixed cost) should be expected to increase efficiency substantially, often approaching the full-cooperation benchmark. The findings also suggest that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and whether punishment is formal or informal are key moderators.   type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  dimensions: num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: This survey synthesizes experimental evidence on the effects of punishment in linear public goods games. It finds that costly punishment opportunities generally increase contributions and cooperation, but do not always increase efficiency or group earnings. Efficiency improvements are only robust when punishment is highly effective (i.e., each unit of punishment inflicts a much larger cost on the target than on the punisher) or when the game is played for many rounds, allowing cooperation to stabilize and punishment costs to fall. In many standard settings, punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to the costliness of punishment. The presence of anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) and counter-punishment can further undermine efficiency. The effect of punishment is also moderated by group composition, information, and cultural context.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this survey provides strong evidence that the effect is not universally positive. The efficiency gain from punishment depends crucially on the punishment technology (cost and impact), the number of rounds (longer games allow efficiency to recover as punishment use declines), and the presence or absence of anti-social punishment. If punishment is low-cost and high-impact, and/or the game is long, efficiency is likely to increase. If punishment is costly relative to its impact, or if anti-social punishment is prevalent, efficiency may decrease even as cooperation rises. Control efficiency (without punishment) is a necessary baseline, but the effect of punishment is highly sensitive to these design dimensions. The survey also highlights that communication, sorting, and non-monetary sanctions can sometimes achieve similar or better efficiency gains than monetary punishment.

- source: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C. (2014). Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.
