- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Waichman, I., & Stenzel, L. (2019). When Punishment Strikes Late: The Effect of a Delay in Punishment and Punishment Feedback on Cooperation and Efficiency. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public good game compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect holds even when punishment is delayed by five periods, as long as participants receive salient feedback linking punishment to their past contributions. If this feedback is absent (i.e., participants are punished after a delay and are not reminded of their past behavior), punishment still increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, due to increased punishment costs and less targeted norm enforcement. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency thus depends on the informational environment, specifically whether punished individuals can clearly associate punishment with their own prior actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with four players and 20 rounds, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, provided that feedback links punishment to past contributions. The effect is robust to a five-period delay in punishment, as long as feedback is salient. If the design omits such feedback, punishment may still increase cooperation but not efficiency, due to higher punishment costs and less effective norm targeting. For prediction, the presence or absence of feedback linking punishment to behavior is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is lower than treatment efficiency (with punishment and feedback), but not when feedback is absent. Other design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are held constant in this study, so the main moderator evidenced is the feedback/identification of norm violation.

- source: van Aaken, A. (2019). Experimental Insights for International Legal Theory. *European Journal of International Law*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that experimental evidence from public goods games challenges the strong free-rider and sanctioners' dilemma assumptions of rationalist theory. It highlights that individuals often cooperate and punish defectors even at a cost to themselves, and that mechanisms such as communication, framing, trust, and legitimacy can enhance cooperation. Punishment (including decentralized, third-party, and symbolic forms) is shown to be effective in sustaining cooperation, especially when perceived as fair. Outcasting (exclusion) and endogenously chosen rules (consent) are also identified as effective mechanisms. However, the paper does not report new experimental results or provide direct measures of efficiency; rather, it draws on the literature to inform theoretical debates in international law.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-level theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment and related mechanisms (communication, framing, trust, legitimacy) can increase cooperation and, by implication, efficiency in public goods games and similar environments. It is useful for understanding which design dimensions (e.g., presence of punishment, communication, framing, endogenous rule choice) are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. However, it does not provide quantitative estimates or direct empirical evidence on efficiency outcomes, so it should be used as a source of mechanism-based priors and qualitative guidance rather than for direct prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Harrell, A. (2018). Competition for Leadership Promotes Contributions to Collective Action. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that the opportunity to compete for an elected leadership position (with punishment power) increases contributions to the public good before the leader is chosen, but only when voters can observe and use prior contribution behavior in their decision. After the leader is installed, groups with elected leaders maintain higher contributions than those with appointed leaders. The leader's use of punishment does not differ significantly between elected and appointed conditions.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the process of leader selection (election vs. appointment) and the visibility of contribution history can significantly affect contribution rates in public goods games with punishment. However, because the paper does not report group payoff, efficiency, or welfare outcomes, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. Its findings are most useful for predicting how leader selection mechanisms and information about contributions affect cooperation, which may indirectly inform efficiency predictions if contribution rates are assumed to map closely to efficiency in similar designs.

  findings: Normative disagreement between newcomers and incumbents in a public goods game with peer punishment does not reduce average contributions to the public good. While group identification and the emergence of a shared social norm are negatively affected by normative disagreement, cooperation (as measured by contribution rate) is maintained. Punishment is used as a norm enforcement tool, especially targeting low-contributing newcomers, who subsequently increase their contributions to match incumbent standards. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a 3-player repeated public goods game with peer punishment, normative disagreement does not reduce average contributions, and punishment is effective at norm enforcement. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and findings are relevant for understanding behavioral mechanisms, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper can inform the mapping from design to expected contribution rates, but not to efficiency.

- source: Schlösser, T., Berger, S., & Fetchenhauer, D. (2018). Justice Sensitivity and Cooperation Dynamics in Repeated Public Good Games. *Social Justice Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public good game leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (from 9.18 to 18.34 tokens per round out of 20). This replicates the classic result that punishment options strongly promote cooperation. The effect of individual justice sensitivity on cooperation is strong in the absence of punishment but disappears when punishment is available, as cooperation becomes strategic to avoid sanctions. Punishment is used infrequently, but its mere availability is sufficient to sustain high cooperation. Although efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the high contribution rates in the punishment condition imply much higher group efficiency compared to the no-punishment condition.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a standard repeated public good game with four players, ten rounds, and a punishment technology similar to Fehr & Gächter (2002) leads to a large increase in group efficiency, as measured by average contributions. The control (no-punishment) condition yields about 46% of maximum possible contributions, while the punishment condition yields about 92%. This suggests that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will approximately double efficiency relative to the control. The paper's design and results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated linear PGGs with similar parameters.

  findings: In a 20-round PGG (10 rounds without punishment, 10 with punishment), groups of four played with either all members having eaten breakfast or all having skipped breakfast. In the no-punishment phase, hungry participants contributed more initially and were more likely to match their contributions to those of their groupmates in subsequent rounds. In the punishment phase, hungry participants punished less, especially when group contributions were high, making punishment less effective at boosting contributions. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined from the reported results.

However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment is less effective at increasing contributions when participants are hungry, which may imply a smaller efficiency gain from punishment in such contexts, but this is not quantified in payoff terms. The game design dimensions are well specified and match standard PGG with punishment, so the behavioral patterns may inform models that link contribution and punishment behavior to efficiency, but direct efficiency data are lacking.

- source: Morgan, S. N., Mason, N. M., & Shupp, R. S. (2019). The effects of voice with(out) punishment: Public goods provision and rule compliance. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that: (1) Comments alone do not significantly increase contributions or compliance with the MCR compared to control. (2) Enforcement (punishment) alone increases contributions and compliance, but the effect is significantly larger when comments are combined with enforcement. (3) The comment with enforcement treatment leads to the highest and most sustained contributions and compliance. (4) Rule-makers exposed to comments set higher MCRs, which in turn increases contributions. (5) The effect of comments on contributions is partly mediated by the higher MCRs set by rule-makers. (6) The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the increased contributions and compliance in the comment with enforcement treatment imply higher efficiency in that condition.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that in a linear public goods game with a probabilistic punishment mechanism, adding a comment-based participation mechanism (private, one-way comments to a rule-maker who sets a minimum contribution rule) increases contributions and compliance above punishment alone. While efficiency is not directly measured, the structure of the game (MPCR = 0.4, group size 5, continuous contributions, no chat, no rewards) means that higher contributions and compliance will translate into higher efficiency. The effect of punishment alone is positive but smaller than the effect of punishment plus comments. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency, so predictions should be based on the observed contribution and compliance patterns. The findings are most relevant for games with third-party rule-making, probabilistic exogenous punishment, and no peer-to-peer punishment or reward.
