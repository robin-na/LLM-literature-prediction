- source: Gürdal, M. Y., Gürerk, & Yahsi, M. (2021). Culture and prevalence of sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game where participants can choose between a standard VCM (no punishment) and a PUN (punishment) institution, the PUN institution is strongly preferred over time in both Turkey and Germany. Contributions and payoffs are much higher in the PUN institution than in the VCM institution, and this holds across both cultures. The average payoff in the PUN institution exceeds that in the VCM institution by about 4 tokens (Turkey: 45.33 vs 41.34; Germany: 45.64 vs 41.75). The efficiency effect of punishment is thus strongly positive and robust to cultural context. The design allows for endogenous migration between institutions, and the results show that the efficiency advantage of punishment is not limited to exogenous assignment. Punishment is used primarily to discipline free-riders, and anti-social punishment is rare and similar across cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling punishment leads to a substantial and robust increase in group efficiency (payoff) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is consistent across two culturally distinct countries (Turkey and Germany), and the game design is a close match to standard PGGs. The results suggest that, for similar game parameters (group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure), enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, even when the control efficiency is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments.

- source: Ramalingam, A., Morales, A. J., & Walker, J. M. (2019). Peer punishment of acts of omission versus acts of commission in give and take social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds (20 with/without punishment), the opportunity for peer punishment increases group contributions in both Give and Take frames. However, only in the Take frame does punishment significantly increase group earnings (efficiency) relative to the no-punishment baseline. In the Give frame, the increase in contributions does not translate into a significant increase in group earnings, as the costs of punishment offset the gains. The effect is driven by more effective targeting of low contributors in the Take game, where acts of commission are punished more harshly and by more group members. The magnitude of punishment per se is not higher in the Take game, but the likelihood of being punished for low contributions is greater. Thus, the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment is context-dependent, being stronger in environments where non-cooperation is framed as an act of commission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the framing of the social dilemma (Give vs. Take). For games isomorphic to the Take frame (where non-cooperation is an act of commission), enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial increase in efficiency, as measured by group earnings relative to the social optimum. For standard Give frame public goods games, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not reliably increase efficiency, due to the costliness of punishment. The game design dimensions—player count (4), number of rounds (30), no chat, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.5, punishment cost 1, punishment impact 3, no rewards, full information on round number and others' contributions, but no punishment source identification—are all precisely reported and should be used for prediction. The control efficiency (group earnings in the no-punishment condition) is a strong predictor of the potential for efficiency gains from punishment, especially in Take games where baseline efficiency is lower and punishment is more effective.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Lim, W., & Zhang, J. P. (2020). Endogenous Authority and Enforcement in Public Goods Games. *B E Journal of Theoretical Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing an endogenous authority with punishment power in a linear public goods game significantly increases group efficiency and average earnings compared to a no-authority baseline. In the experiment, with N=4, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical prediction) or 12.6% (empirical, due to positive baseline contributions) to 40.9% with authority; with N=8, efficiency gain rises from 0% (theoretical) or 16.6% (empirical) to 47.7%. The social cost of contesting for authority is lower in larger groups (18.95% for N=8 vs 32.2% for N=4). Authorities tend to punish the lowest contributor and exercise most of their punishment power. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across treatment orders. Authorities contribute less than others but still contribute positively. The mechanism is effective at raising efficiency, especially as group size increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing a punishment-enabled authority in a linear public goods game. The design dimensions (player_count, mpcr, punishment_tech, etc.) are clearly specified and manipulated. The efficiency gains from punishment are substantial and increase with group size, while the social cost of authority selection decreases with group size. The control (no authority) efficiency is empirically measured, and the treatment (authority/punishment enabled) efficiency is reported, allowing for direct mapping to the prediction task. The findings suggest that, for similar game parameters, enabling punishment via an endogenous authority will significantly increase efficiency, with the effect size moderated by group size and the cost structure of authority selection.

- source: Glöckner, A., Kube, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2018). The joint benefits of observed and unobserved social sanctions. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players over ten rounds, the authors compare four conditions: no punishment, observed punishment only, unobserved (delayed) punishment only, and both punishment channels available. Both observed and unobserved punishment alone increase contributions but do not improve efficiency (average payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline, due to the costliness of punishment. However, when both punishment channels are available, group efficiency is significantly higher than in all other conditions. This is because the threat of unobserved punishment increases the effectiveness of observed punishment, leading to higher cooperation with less punishment expenditure. The combination of punishment channels thus overcomes the typical efficiency losses seen in standard lab PGG punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the punishment institution. Standard punishment (observed or unobserved alone) does not improve efficiency over the control, but the combination of both channels leads to a large positive effect. For prediction, this means that the presence of multiple punishment channels (observed and unobserved) is a key moderator: if only one is present, efficiency is not improved; if both are present, efficiency is substantially increased. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the punishment technology (timing/observability), cost, and whether multiple channels are available. Control efficiency (no punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency unless the institutional details of punishment are considered.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Putterman, L., & Thöni, C. (2021). Trigger-happy or precisionist? On demand for monitoring in peer-based public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the ability to monitor others' contributions at a modest cost enables peer punishment to sustain high cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, even when information is otherwise imperfect. When monitoring is cheap, groups achieve high efficiency, with average earnings close to the efficient benchmark. When monitoring is expensive, efficiency drops significantly. Publicly revealing monitoring decisions does not significantly affect efficiency compared to private monitoring. The main moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency is the cost of monitoring, not the publicness of monitoring. Groups with higher average monitoring achieve substantially higher efficiency. Punishment is most effective when informed by accurate monitoring, and the threat of punishment (signaled by monitoring) can deter free riding, reducing the need for actual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games depend critically on the cost and availability of accurate information about others' contributions. If monitoring is cheap, punishment opportunities lead to high efficiency; if monitoring is expensive, efficiency gains are much smaller. The publicness of monitoring (whether others know who is monitoring) has little effect on efficiency. For prediction, the key design dimensions are the cost of monitoring (a proxy for information quality), the presence and effectiveness of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's results show that punishment with affordable monitoring can raise efficiency close to the social optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings where information about contributions is imperfect but can be improved at a cost.

- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Chugunova, M., Luhan, W. J., & Nicklisch, A. (2020). When to leave carrots for sticks: On the evolution of sanctioning institutions in open communities. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Participants initially choose reward-based public goods communities, which provide higher payoffs and are preferred by most. Over time, as rewards decline and inequality increases in the reward community, some participants migrate to the punishment community. The punishment community achieves higher contribution rates, but lower average payoffs compared to the reward community. Importantly, when punishment and reward communities compete, the punishment community requires less actual punishment to sustain cooperation, reducing welfare losses typically associated with punishment. The threat of punishment, rather than its frequent use, is sufficient to maintain high contributions. However, the reward community still retains a majority of participants and maintains higher average payoffs, though the difference with the punishment community narrows over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the institutional context. In isolation, punishment communities sustain high contributions but at a welfare cost due to punishment expenditures. When a reward community is available, migration dynamics mean that the punishment community can sustain cooperation with less actual punishment, reducing welfare losses. Thus, the negative efficiency impact of punishment is mitigated when there is institutional competition. For prediction, this implies that the efficiency of punishment-enabled games may be higher when players can choose between institutions, and that the mere threat of punishment can be effective if migration is possible. However, in direct comparison, reward communities yield higher payoffs than punishment communities, though the gap narrows over time.

- source: Langenbach, P., & Tausch, F. (2019). Inherited Institutions: Cooperation in the Light of Democratic Legitimacy. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot, three-player linear public goods game with or without a centralized, non-deterrent punishment (automatic fine for less-than-full contribution). The institutional setting (punishment or not) is determined either by majority vote (Endo) or random draw (Exo) in the first generation, and this setting is inherited by a matched group in the second generation. In the first generation, contributions are higher when punishment is democratically adopted and lower when it is democratically rejected, compared to random assignment. In the second generation, the positive effect of democratic adoption disappears, but the negative effect of democratic rejection persists (marginally). The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or total earnings as a share of the social optimum, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible from this paper alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a centralized, non-deterrent punishment mechanism can increase contributions if it is democratically adopted, but only in the generation that participates in the decision. The effect does not persist to subsequent generations who inherit the institution. The negative effect of democratic rejection may persist. However, since the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, only contribution rates, its direct value for predicting efficiency is limited. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is informative for mapping design to expected contribution behavior, but not for direct efficiency prediction. If contribution is used as a proxy for efficiency (assuming linearity and no punishment cost to the group), the results suggest that democratic adoption of punishment increases efficiency in the first generation, but not in the second.

- source: Liu, J., Riyanto, Y. E., & Zhang, R. K. (2020). Firing the right bullets: Exploring the effectiveness of the hired-gun mechanism in the provision of public goods. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The hired-gun mechanism, a centralized punishment system targeting the lowest contributor(s), is highly effective at increasing contributions in public goods games when both unilateral and tie punishments are positive. The effect is robust to increasing the choice set (larger endowment). However, if the unilateral punishment is removed (set to zero), contributions decay over time, and the mechanism fails to sustain high cooperation, even if the tie punishment remains. Theoretical predictions suggest a range of punishment parameters should work, but empirically, zero unilateral punishment is insufficient. The main outcome is contribution rate, not direct efficiency or group payoff, but high contributions imply high efficiency in this context. The study also finds that punishment can be softened (reduced in size) to a degree without loss of effectiveness, but not eliminated.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks relating to efficiency in public goods games with centralized punishment, this paper provides strong evidence that the hired-gun mechanism (centralized, automatic punishment of the lowest contributor) is highly effective at increasing contributions (and thus likely efficiency) when both unilateral and tie punishments are positive. The effect is robust to larger choice sets and group size of 4. However, if the unilateral punishment is zero, the mechanism is ineffective, and contributions decay. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the near-full-contribution rates in the treatment with punishment strongly suggest near-maximal efficiency. Thus, when mapping from game design to expected efficiency, the presence and size of both punishment components are critical moderators. The absence of unilateral punishment is a strong negative moderator. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with centralized, automatic punishment and group size 4, with full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Lippert, S., & Tremewan, J. (2021). Pledge-and-review in the laboratory. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment compares a standard two-player public goods game (PG) to three pledge-and-review (P&R) variants: fixed disagreement payoffs (F), heterogeneous/certainty (C), and uncertainty (U). In all P&R treatments, after pledges are made, both players must vote to accept the pledges for them to be implemented; otherwise, both receive a disagreement payoff. Efficiency (average payoffs as a fraction of the full cooperation benchmark) is significantly higher in all P&R treatments than in the control PG game. The efficiency gain is persistent in the fixed disagreement payoff treatment, with no decline over 20 rounds, while some decline is observed in the other P&R treatments. There is no significant difference in efficiency between the three P&R variants. The mechanism allows conditional cooperators to avoid the typical decline in cooperation seen in repeated public goods games. The P&R institution functions as a form of second-party punishment (via voting down low pledges), but with endogenous costs and without explicit costly punishment tokens.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that adding a pledge-and-review (P&R) mechanism—where players can veto low contributions after seeing pledges—substantially increases efficiency in a two-player, 20-round public goods game with continuous contributions and no communication. The effect is robust to variations in disagreement payoffs (fixed, heterogeneous, uncertain). For prediction, if the control (PG) efficiency is known, enabling a P&R-style review stage is likely to increase efficiency by about 10% of the full cooperation benchmark, with the effect persisting over repeated rounds (at least in the fixed disagreement payoff case). The P&R mechanism is not identical to standard costly punishment (no explicit punishment tokens), but functions similarly by allowing players to block low contributions at a cost. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, repeated, continuous-contribution games without chat, with full feedback, and a review/veto stage after pledges.

- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2020). The impact of peer ratings on cooperation: The role of information and cost of rating. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, adding a mandatory, costless peer rating stage (where players rate each other's contributions on a 0-10 scale, with no monetary consequences) increases group contributions and efficiency relative to a baseline with no rating. This effect is robust to whether rating information is private (only the recipient sees their ratings) or public (all see everyone's ratings).   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costless, non-monetary peer approval mechanism (rating) in a standard repeated public goods game increases efficiency, even when ratings have no direct payoff consequences and are anonymous. The effect is robust to whether rating information is private or public. However, even a small cost to rating eliminates the effect. For prediction, the presence of a costless, mandatory, non-monetary peer approval mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control. If rating is costly, no efficiency gain should be expected. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the only sanction/reward is social approval, not monetary punishment or reward.
