- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

- source: Shinada, M., & Yamagishi, T. (2008). Bringing Back Leviathan into Social Dilemmas. *New Issues and Paradigms in Research on Social Dilemmas*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The chapter synthesizes evidence that punishment opportunities in public goods games reliably increase cooperation and contributions, but the net effect on group efficiency or total payoff is not always positive. The net benefit of sanctions depends on game parameters such as MPCR and the punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When the MPCR is high and/or the punishment is efficient (high fine-to-fee ratio), the net benefit of punishment is more likely to be positive. However, in many experiments, the total costs of punishment (including both the cost to punishers and the fines paid by punished) exceed the extra benefits from increased cooperation, resulting in a net loss in group welfare. The chapter also reviews evidence that sanctions can have negative psychological effects, such as reducing intrinsic motivation and trust, and that the indirect effect of sanctions (changing expectations about others' behavior) can be as important as the direct deterrent effect.
  prediction_guidance: This chapter provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on structural game parameters, especially MPCR and punishment technology (fine-to-fee ratio). When predicting the efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, it is crucial to consider whether the increased cooperation from punishment will be sufficient to offset the costs of administering punishment. If the control game (without punishment) already has high efficiency, or if the punishment is costly and/or the MPCR is low, enabling punishment may reduce overall efficiency. Conversely, with high MPCR and efficient punishment, enabling punishment may increase efficiency. The chapter also highlights the importance of considering psychological and indirect effects, such as changes in trust and expectations, which may moderate the long-term impact of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Jaffe, K., & Zaballa, L. (2010). Co-Operative Punishment Cements Social Cohesion. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that co-operative punishment (where the cost of punishing free-riders is shared among group members) is much more effective at maintaining a high proportion of contributors in a simulated public goods game than either no punishment or altruistic punishment (where punishers pay the cost individually). The effectiveness of punishment depends on the ratio of cost to punished versus cost to punisher, and on the efficiency of detecting free-riders. Co-operative punishment allows pro-social behavior to invade and persist in populations of free-riders, while altruistic punishment does not. However, the primary outcome is the proportion of contributors, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the structure of punishment (co-operative vs. individual) is a critical moderator of cooperation in public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, the maintenance of high contribution rates under co-operative punishment suggests that efficiency would likely be higher in such settings, especially when the cost of punishment is shared and detection of free-riders is effective. For prediction tasks, the results imply that enabling co-operative punishment mechanisms (where costs are distributed) should be expected to yield higher efficiency than individual punishment, all else equal. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of quantitative predictions.

For prediction tasks, the paper supports the general expectation that enabling punishment will increase cooperation and likely improve group outcomes, but it does not provide the detailed, dimension-specific evidence needed for precise prediction of efficiency changes.

- source: Fatas, E., Morales, A. J., & Ubeda, P. (2010). Blind justice: An experimental analysis of random punishment in team production. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a public goods game with a novel random punishment mechanism: after each round, each group member is excluded from the group benefit with probability 1-R, where R is the normalized group contribution. This 'blind' punishment does not target individual defectors. The experiment compares three conditions: standard VCM (no punishment), random punishment without redistribution (RP), and random punishment with redistribution (RPR). Results show that both RP and RPR significantly increase contributions compared to VCM. However, only RPR increases group earnings (efficiency), as RP's punishment cost outweighs the contribution gains. The effect of random punishment is similar whether introduced after a baseline or from the start. Behavioral analysis shows that high contributors reduce their contributions if punished, but overall, conditional cooperation persists and the net effect is positive for contributions. The mechanism does not fully prevent the decline in contributions over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that random, non-individually-targeted punishment can increase contributions in a standard public goods game, but its effect on efficiency depends on whether the penalty is redistributed. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism that is not individually targeted but is probabilistically linked to group performance will likely increase contributions, but may not increase efficiency unless the punishment cost is recycled within the group. The effect is robust to order and does not depend on communication or knowledge of individual contributions. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5, and the group size is 4. The findings suggest that, for similar designs, enabling random punishment will increase contributions and may increase efficiency if the punishment cost is not lost to the group.

The results suggest that group-level punishment threats can coordinate expectations and sustain cooperation, but the value of future interaction alone is insufficient unless paired with credible punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the imposition of a group-level, long punishment phase (grim trigger) increases cooperation rates in repeated public goods games, especially in the early rounds. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a strong group punishment mechanism (even if not individual-targeted) will increase cooperation and likely improve efficiency, especially when players expect many rounds. The effect of expected future interaction (discount parameter) is only realized when punishment is credible and severe. For prediction tasks, the presence of a mandated, long punishment phase should be treated as a strong positive moderator of efficiency, but the absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative inference.

- source: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., De Cremer, D., & Wilke, H. A. M. (2006). When sanctions fail to increase cooperation in social dilemmas: Considering the presence of an alternative option to defect. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.

  findings: Across two lab experiments, the authors find that introducing a sanctioning system (punishment for defection) increases collective outcomes (efficiency) in a standard two-option social dilemma (cooperate or defect). However, when a third option to defect (alternative defection, more harmful to the group) is available, sanctions do not increase efficiency and can even decrease it, particularly when participants have low expectations of others' cooperation. The negative effect is mediated by the failure of sanctions to increase trust or expectations of cooperation in the presence of the third option. Thus, the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is contingent on the decision structure and social expectations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games is not uniform: it is positive in standard two-choice settings but can be null or negative when alternative, more harmful defection options are available and when group members have low expectations of cooperation. For prediction, this means that the presence of alternative defection options (i.e., more than two choices, with at least one being more harmful to the group) and low trust/expectations are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the game allows for alternative defection, enabling punishment may not improve and could even worsen efficiency, especially if group trust is low. In standard two-choice games, punishment is likely to increase efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

For prediction, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to substantially increase efficiency (proxied by provision rate) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially in step-level games with coordination problems.
