- source: Cong, R., Li, K., Wang, L., & Zhao, Q. C. (2016). Cooperation induced by wise incentive allocation in spontaneous institution. *Epl*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with optional institution membership, the optimal regime for cooperation and group efficiency is achieved when the institution balances punishment and reward. Pure punishment or pure reward is suboptimal: too much punishment collapses the institution, while too much reward allows institutional defectors to dominate. The efficiency of punishment and reward (i.e., their impact per unit cost) increases the range of conditions under which cooperation prevails. The model provides explicit conditions for when institutional cooperators outcompete defectors, and shows that the stationary distribution of strategies (and thus group efficiency) is maximized at intermediate values of the punishment/reward split.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the balance between punishment and reward, as well as the effectiveness of each. It suggests that enabling punishment alone may not always increase efficiency; the presence and strength of reward, and the cost-effectiveness of both, are crucial moderators. For prediction tasks, the model implies that intermediate punishment (with some reward) is optimal for efficiency, and that both the cost and impact of punishment and reward should be considered. The findings are directly relevant for settings where institutional incentives are endogenous and both punishment and reward are possible.

- source: Yao, Y., & Chen, S. S. (2014). Multiplicative noise enhances spatial reciprocity. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, both strong altruistic punishment and reward are unable to sustain cooperation when implemented in the traditional (non-tax-based) way, due to their high cost. When a tax-based mechanism is introduced—where all players pay a tax that is redistributed to punishers or rewarders—these strategies become viable, leading to stable coexistence of cooperators and punishers/rewarders and the elimination of defectors. This results in higher group efficiency and welfare. Tax-based strong altruistic reward leads to even higher cooperation and efficiency than tax-based strong altruistic punishment under equivalent tax conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the introduction of a tax-based institutional mechanism for funding punishment or reward can transform the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games from negative or neutral to strongly positive. The results suggest that, for a given control efficiency (without punishment), enabling tax-based punishment or reward will substantially increase efficiency, with reward being even more effective than punishment. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with strong institutional support and compulsory tax collection, and less so to voluntary or peer-based punishment. The model is deterministic and assumes infinite, well-mixed populations, so predictions should be adjusted for finite or noisy environments.

- source: Sasaki, T. (2014). The Evolution of Cooperation Through Institutional Incentives and Optional Participation. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with optional participation, institutional punishment (penalties) is highly effective at promoting full cooperation and maximizing group welfare, even when the same level of punishment would only produce bi-stability (coordination problem) under compulsory participation. Optional participation allows the population to escape from the inefficient defection equilibrium, making cooperation globally stable for a wide range of punishment strengths. In contrast, institutional rewards combined with optional participation do not robustly promote cooperation or welfare: they often lead to non-participation and only marginally improve group welfare over the baseline. The model provides explicit analytical thresholds for when each regime occurs, and shows that the minimal punishment needed to achieve full cooperation decreases as group size increases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with optional participation and institutional punishment, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency (group welfare) compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, rounds, and incentive strengths, provided the punishment is above a threshold that decreases with group size. The model also predicts that institutional rewards are much less effective in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when optional participation and institutional punishment are present. However, the results are theoretical and assume infinite populations and well-mixed groups, so empirical generalizability may be limited.

- source: Hetzer, M., & Sornette, D. (2013). An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with punishment, evolutionary dynamics lead to two regimes: (1) a defection regime with low cooperation and low group payoff when punishment is weak or absent, and (2) a coordination regime with higher cooperation and higher group payoff when punishment is sufficiently strong and driven by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model predicts the empirically observed propensity to punish in lab experiments. The presence of punishment transforms the social dilemma into a coordination problem, enabling higher efficiency. Disadvantageous inequity aversion is shown to be evolutionarily dominant, and populations with this trait invade and outcompete purely self-regarding populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency (group payoff) when punishment is sufficiently strong and is motivated by disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model gives explicit formulas for the threshold propensity to punish and shows that, above this threshold, populations coordinate on higher contributions and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, for games with similar design parameters (e.g., n=4, r=3, MPCR=0.4), enabling punishment will shift the group from low to high efficiency, provided the punishment regime is effective. The paper also predicts how efficiency and punishment propensity depend on group size and punishment effectiveness, which can inform predictions for other parameterizations. However, the paper does not provide direct empirical estimates of efficiency change, so predictions should be interpreted as strong theoretical expectations rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: Roberts, G. (2013). When Punishment Pays. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods games, punishment can be self-interested and increase group efficiency when the cost to punishers is outweighed by the benefits of increased cooperation. The key condition is that the cost to the punisher (p) is less than the product of the punishment impact (q), the benefit per cooperative act (b), divided by the net cost of cooperation (c): p < q*b/c. Simulations confirm that with typical lab parameters (e.g., group size 3, k=2, p=1, q=3), punishment leads to high stable cooperation and high group payoffs. The effect is robust to group size and number of rounds, provided the parameter condition holds. The paper also explains why punishment sometimes fails to increase payoffs (e.g., when p is too high or q is too low), and why punishment is more effective in repeated than one-shot games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based support for predicting that, in repeated public goods games with punishment enabled and with favorable punishment parameters (low p, high q), efficiency will be substantially higher than in control games without punishment. The key moderators are the punishment cost (p), punishment effectiveness (q), group size (g), and the number of rounds (m). The paper gives explicit formulas for when punishment will increase efficiency, and shows that the effect is robust across a range of typical lab parameters. If the control game has low efficiency and the punishment parameters satisfy p < q*b/c, enabling punishment should lead to a large increase in efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Hwang, S. H. (2017). Conflict technology in cooperation: The group size paradox revisited. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that coordinated punishment in public goods games can lead to a concentration effect, making it easier to sustain cooperation and achieve high group efficiency as group size increases. When punishers are even slightly better coordinated than defectors, the set of states in which it is advantageous to be a punisher expands with group size, and the all-punisher (fully cooperative) equilibrium becomes stochastically stable in the long run. This result is robust to several variations, including the presence of unconditional punishment costs and some decay in coordination as group size increases. The model provides a theoretical explanation for empirical findings that larger groups can sometimes achieve higher cooperation and efficiency, contrary to standard free-rider logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with coordinated punishment, increasing group size can increase the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, even when the control (no-punishment) game would predict lower efficiency in larger groups. The key moderator is the degree of coordination among punishers relative to defectors: as long as punishers are at least slightly better coordinated, the efficiency benefit of punishment increases with group size. The model also shows that this result is robust to moderate punishment costs and some decay in coordination. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not necessarily diminished in larger groups, and may in fact be enhanced, provided coordinated punishment is feasible.

- source: Ye, H., Chen, S., Luo, J., Tan, F., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2016). Increasing returns to scale: The solution to the second-order social dilemma. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a PGG with increasing returns to scale, the presence of punishment leads to a dramatic increase in group efficiency and average payoff, provided the IRS parameter (alpha) is above a threshold (about 1.2–1.3). In standard PGGs (alpha=1), punishment does not stably increase efficiency, as defectors can invade and dominate. With strong IRS (alpha > 1.3), punishers can resist invasion by both defectors and second-order free-riders, leading to stable high cooperation and high group payoff. The effect of punishment is thus highly contingent on the returns-to-scale structure of the public good. The results are supported by both analytical evolutionary models and agent-based simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment in a PGG depends critically on the degree of increasing returns to scale in the production function. In standard linear PGGs, punishment may not improve efficiency, but in games with strong IRS, punishment can lead to near-maximal efficiency by stabilizing cooperation. For prediction tasks, the presence and strength of IRS (or analogous nonlinear production functions) should be considered a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Other game design dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are also discussed, but the IRS effect is dominant.

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2017). Multiple tolerances dilute the second order cooperative dilemma. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper provides a unified theoretical framework for public goods games with multiple tolerant punishment strategies. It shows that: (1) Less tolerant punishers (those who punish with fewer defectors present) are always more abundant and thus more evolutionarily successful than more tolerant ones. (2) The presence of punishment strategies increases group efficiency (measured by the abundance of cooperation and punishers) when the fine for defectors exceeds a threshold determined by group size, enhancement factor, and punishment cost. (3) Larger group sizes can facilitate the evolution of punishment and thus higher group efficiency, but only when the enhancement factor and punishment cost are small. (4) The paper provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment prevails, and these are validated by simulations. (5) The main outcome is the stationary abundance of strategies, which is directly linked to average payoffs and group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games. It provides explicit analytical conditions for when punishment increases the abundance of cooperation and group efficiency, as a function of group size, enhancement factor (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency, especially in larger groups when costs are low, and that less tolerant (more responsive) punishment is more effective. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency of games with and without punishment, given the game design parameters. However, the results are derived under evolutionary dynamics with weak selection and large populations, so care should be taken when applying them to small groups or different update rules.

- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: Noussair, C. N., & van Soest, D. P. (2014). Economic Experiments and Environmental Policy. *Annual Review of Resource Economics, Vol 6*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: chat, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews laboratory experimental evidence on public goods games and related social dilemmas, focusing on mechanisms to promote cooperation and efficiency. It finds that allowing peer punishment and/or rewards generally increases cooperation and can improve group payoffs, but the effect on efficiency is not universal and depends on implementation details (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural context). Communication between players robustly increases efficiency. Top-down interventions (such as fines or minimum contributions) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, sometimes reducing overall welfare. In market-based resource allocation, auction and trading mechanisms can achieve high efficiency, but are affected by strategic behavior, market power, and the presence or absence of endowment effects. The review highlights that the design of the game and institutions (e.g., whether players can undo others' contributions, whether the game is framed as giving or taking) strongly moderates the effect of punishment and other interventions on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides broad, high-level evidence that punishment can increase cooperation and sometimes efficiency in public goods games, but the effect is highly context-dependent. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when (a) group composition and identity are stable, (b) counter-punishment is not possible, and (c) the cultural context supports prosocial punishment. However, the review also warns that punishment can fail to improve efficiency or even reduce it if retaliation is possible or if the strategic environment allows for undoing others' contributions. Communication is a more robust positive moderator of efficiency. The review does not provide quantitative effect sizes but offers qualitative guidance on when punishment is likely to be effective. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; moderators such as communication, game framing, and the possibility of counter-punishment must be considered.

- source: Kaiping, G. A., Jacobs, G. S., Cox, S. J., & Sluckin, T. J. (2014). Nonequivalence of updating rules in evolutionary games under high mutation rates. *Physical Review E*.

  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, the choice of updating rule (BP, DP, LD) can dramatically change the long-run frequencies of defectors, cooperators, and punishers. For the same parameter values, the birth-death process leads to a population dominated by defectors, while the death-birth process results in a majority of cooperative strategies (cooperators and punishers). These differences arise even in well-mixed populations and are especially pronounced when mutation rates are not negligible and when more than two strategies interact.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the updating rule (a feature of evolutionary dynamics, not typically manipulated in lab experiments) can be a major moderator of the effect of punishment on cooperation and, by implication, on efficiency. In public goods games with punishment, the same game design (including punishment parameters) can yield very different long-run outcomes depending on the updating rule: punishment may be ineffective (defector-dominated) under one rule and highly effective (cooperator/punisher-dominated) under another. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its findings should be interpreted as indicating that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the evolutionary dynamics assumed, especially in theoretical or agent-based models. For empirical prediction, this suggests caution in generalizing across studies with different evolutionary assumptions.
