# Paper Set Evidence Digest

Each item below is a compact paper-level analysis digest. Use only this digest.

- source: Arechar, A. A., Gächter, S., & Molleman, L. (2018). Conducting interactive experiments online. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that enabling peer punishment in a repeated four-person public goods game (20 rounds: 10 without, 10 with punishment) substantially increases average contributions and group efficiency in both laboratory and online (MTurk) samples. In the no-punishment condition, average contributions and earnings are lower and decay over time. When punishment is introduced, contributions jump and are sustained at higher levels, and average earnings per participant per period increase (lab: 22.64 to 26.91; online: 22.64 to 26.91, as reported). Punishment is mostly prosocial (cooperators punish defectors), and the effect is robust across both environments. The paper also finds that attrition (dropout) is not related to experimental condition or behavior, supporting the internal validity of the results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with peer punishment (cost 1, impact 3), enabling punishment increases group efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust across both lab and online samples, and the design matches canonical PGG-with-punishment studies. The paper's detailed reporting of game parameters, outcome measures, and the direct comparison of control and punishment conditions make it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A., & Gallier, C. (2020). The choice of institutions to solve cooperation problems: a survey of experimental research. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The review finds that (1) punishment institutions, when adopted, reliably increase both cooperation and group efficiency/payoff in public goods and related games; (2) the effect is strongest when the institution eliminates free-riding incentives and is not too costly; (3) the popularity and effectiveness of punishment institutions increase with repeated choice and learning; (4) endogenously chosen punishment institutions often yield higher efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, especially for weaker institutions, but the aggregate benefit of endogenous choice is ambiguous because some groups fail to implement the institution; (5) institutional cost and incomplete elimination of free-riding are key barriers to adoption and effectiveness; (6) most studies report both behavioral and payoff outcomes, with efficiency and group payoff being central dependent variables.
  prediction_guidance: This review provides strong evidence that, in public goods game-like environments, enabling punishment mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and payoffs, especially when the punishment is effective (eliminates free-riding) and not too costly. The effect is robust across a variety of game designs, player counts, and institutional choice mechanisms. For prediction, the presence of a punishment institution should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, with the magnitude moderated by punishment cost, effectiveness, and whether the institution is adopted. The review also highlights that repeated exposure, information, and learning opportunities increase the likelihood of institution adoption and thus efficiency gains. However, if the institution is costly or does not fully deter free-riding, efficiency gains may be limited or absent. The review's synthesis of many studies with detailed design features makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Okada, I. (2020). A Review of Theoretical Studies on Indirect Reciprocity. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper synthesizes theoretical advances in indirect reciprocity, showing that cooperation can be maintained in social dilemma settings when reputation mechanisms and justified punishment are present. It details how the refinement of assessment functions (e.g., leading eight norms) resolves issues like the scoring and punishment dilemmas, enabling stable cooperation under certain conditions. However, it notes that these models are not directly applicable to public goods games, as selective cooperation (the core of indirect reciprocity) is not possible in standard PGGs. The review also highlights the importance of the benefit-cost ratio, error rates, and the distinction between public and private reputation systems in determining the stability of cooperation. While punishment and reward mechanisms are discussed, the focus is on their role in norm stability and cooperation, not on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and reputation mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in social dilemmas, but it does not offer direct evidence for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its models are adjacent to PGGs but differ in key structural ways (e.g., selective cooperation vs. public goods provision). The review is useful for understanding the mechanisms by which punishment and norm refinement can support cooperation, but it does not provide quantitative or comparative data on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that the effectiveness of punishment depends on norm structure, reputation information, and error rates, but it does not allow for direct mapping from game design dimensions to efficiency outcomes in PGGs.

- source: Enke, B. (2019). KINSHIP, COOPERATION, AND THE EVOLUTION OF MORAL SYSTEMS. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that kinship structure predicts the type of moral system a society develops: tight kinship societies rely on communal values, shame, revenge, and local institutions, while loose kinship societies develop universal values, guilt, impersonal (altruistic/third-party) punishment, and large-scale institutions. These systems are internally consistent and mediate cooperation patterns, such as in-group favoritism and trust. Theoretical modeling suggests that universalizing moral systems in loose kinship societies enable more efficient cooperation with strangers, which becomes advantageous with economic development. However, empirical analyses focus on psychological, cultural, and institutional variables, not direct measures of efficiency or group payoff in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence about the role of punishment and moral systems in supporting cooperation, especially in relation to kinship structure and the scope of cooperation. It does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games or similar environments. However, it suggests that impersonal (altruistic/third-party) punishment is more prevalent in societies with loose kinship ties and universal values, which are theoretically linked to higher efficiency in broad cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper is most useful for understanding how cultural and institutional context may moderate the effectiveness of punishment, but it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency effects in controlled game settings.

- source: Fonseca, M. A., & Peters, K. (2018). Will any gossip do? Gossip does not need to be perfectly accurate to promote trust. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that enabling gossip (reputational information transmission) in a repeated trust game increases both trust and trustworthiness, leading to higher efficiency (joint payoffs) compared to a no-message baseline. Even when gossip is substantially inaccurate (either endogenously or exogenously), efficiency remains higher than in the absence of gossip, though it is lower than with accurate information. The effect is robust across treatments, with the largest efficiency gains observed when information is accurate, but meaningful gains persist even with high inaccuracy. The mechanism is reputational concern and discrimination, not direct costly punishment. The study provides direct evidence that non-monetary, reputation-based social sanctions (gossip) can substitute for formal punishment in promoting efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a reputational mechanism (gossip) in repeated trust-based interactions increases efficiency, even when the information is not perfectly accurate. For prediction tasks, the presence of a gossip/reputation channel should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a no-communication baseline, with the effect size depending on the accuracy of the information. However, the mechanism is not direct costly punishment, but rather indirect social sanctioning via reputation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games and adjacent to public goods games, especially in settings where partner choice or discrimination is possible. The results suggest that even imperfect reputation systems can yield substantial efficiency gains, and that the absence of any reputational channel is particularly detrimental to efficiency.

- source: Falk, A., Becker, A., Dohmen, T., Enke, B., Huffman, D., & Sunde, U. (2018). GLOBAL EVIDENCE ON ECONOMIC PREFERENCES. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds substantial heterogeneity in economic preferences across and within countries. Preferences such as patience, risk tolerance, altruism, reciprocity, and trust are systematically related to individual characteristics (age, gender, cognitive ability) and to country-level geographic and cultural variables. Patience is strongly correlated with economic development (GDP per capita), while negative reciprocity is correlated with the frequency of armed conflicts. Social preferences (altruism, positive reciprocity, trust) are associated with prosocial behaviors and charitable activities. However, the study does not involve public goods games, group efficiency, or experimental manipulation of punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. While it measures negative reciprocity (willingness to punish unfairness) as a preference and relates it to macro-level outcomes (e.g., conflict), it does not analyze the effect of punishment opportunities on group payoff, efficiency, or cooperation in controlled game settings. Therefore, it offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task, except perhaps as very indirect context on the prevalence of punishment motives across societies.

- source: Sutter, M., Angerer, S., Glätzle-Rützler, D., & Lergetporer, P. (2018). Language group differences in time preferences: Evidence from primary school children in a bilingual city. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The main finding is that German-speaking children are significantly more patient (more likely to delay gratification) than Italian-speaking children, even after controlling for age, IQ, risk attitudes, and family background. This difference is robust across different elicitation methods and is also reflected in self-reported saving behavior. Children from bilingual households show intermediate levels of patience. The study does not involve public goods games, punishment, or efficiency/welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not study group cooperation, sanctions, or payoff-based outcomes. Its findings on time preferences and cultural/language group differences in patience are not transferable to the prediction task focused on punishment and efficiency in public goods games.
