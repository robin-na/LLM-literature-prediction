- source: Rand, D. G., Dreber, A., Ellingsen, T., Fudenberg, D., & Nowak, M. A. (2009). Positive Interactions Promote Public Cooperation. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, both punishment and reward mechanisms can maintain high levels of cooperation and efficiency compared to a control with no targeted interactions. However, reward leads to higher total payoffs than punishment, as punishment is costly and does not increase efficiency beyond what is achieved by reward. When both reward and punishment are available, groups that use reward more achieve higher payoffs, while groups that use punishment more achieve lower payoffs. The results suggest that positive reciprocity (reward) is more effective than negative reciprocity (punishment) for promoting efficiency in repeated public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with persistent group identity and known group membership, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to a control, but enabling reward is equally or more effective and leads to higher group payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment alone should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no-punishment controls, but not as much as reward or combined reward/punishment treatments. The effect of punishment is less positive for efficiency than reward, and when both are available, punishment may even reduce efficiency due to its cost. The findings are most applicable to repeated, fixed-group, non-anonymous settings with continuous contribution choices and no communication.

- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2006). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with endogenous institution choice, participants could choose between a sanctioning institution (with both positive and negative sanctions) and a sanction-free institution. Initially, most participants preferred the sanction-free institution, but over time, nearly all migrated to the sanctioning institution. In the sanctioning institution, cooperation and efficiency increased steadily, reaching over 95% of the maximum possible group payoff in the final period, while the sanction-free institution's efficiency fell to zero. The presence of punishment (negative sanctions) was the key driver of sustained high cooperation and efficiency. The study demonstrates that sanctioning institutions have a strong competitive advantage and can resolve the social dilemma by attracting and converting even initially noncooperative individuals.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game dramatically increases efficiency compared to a control condition without punishment, even when participants can freely choose between institutions. The design closely matches canonical PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is explicitly measured. The findings support strong positive predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in repeated games with migration or institution choice. The specific punishment cost and effectiveness parameters, as well as the presence of both positive and negative sanctions, are informative for parameterizing prediction models.

- source: Rockenbach, B., & Milinski, M. (2006). The efficient interaction of indirect reciprocity and costly punishment. *Nature*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that when participants can choose between groups with or without costly punishment, and with or without an indirect reciprocity (reputation) mechanism, most eventually prefer the group with both punishment and reputation. The combination (PUN&IR) leads to higher contributions, more efficient use of punishment (less frequent, more targeted), and significantly higher efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) than punishment alone (PUN). The destructive effect of punishment on efficiency is largely mitigated by the presence of reputation, as punishment is used less but more effectively. Full contributors in the combined treatment achieve payoffs close to the social optimum, and even the least cooperative subjects are more cooperative in the combined treatment. The results suggest that punishment and reputation are complementary, not substitutes, and their interaction provides a low-cost, high-efficiency solution to social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with costly punishment, adding a reputation/indirect reciprocity mechanism increases efficiency beyond what is achieved by punishment alone. For prediction tasks, the presence of both punishment and reputation should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, especially when punishment is costly and group members can observe each other's contributions and reputational history. The efficiency boost is due to reduced punishment frequency, more targeted punishment, and increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when both punishment and reputation mechanisms are present.

- source: Fehr, E., & Gächter, S. (2002). Altruistic punishment in humans. *Nature*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with random group assignment and no reputation or repeated interaction, the opportunity for costly punishment (1 MU cost to punisher, 3 MU cost to target per point) leads to a large and sustained increase in average contributions compared to a no-punishment baseline. Most punishment is directed at below-average contributors by above-average contributors. The threat and execution of punishment both increase cooperation. Individual income is maximized when contributions are close to the group average, and both positive and negative deviations reduce income. The emotional mechanism underlying punishment is negative affect toward free riders. The paper does not report group efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the data and payoff structure imply that efficiency is much higher with punishment enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds, no communication, continuous contribution choice, and no reputation or repeated interaction. The control (no-punishment) condition shows declining contributions and low efficiency, while the punishment condition shows increasing contributions and much higher efficiency. The punishment mechanism is costly (1:3 cost:impact), and punishment is anonymous. The findings support the prediction that, in similar designs, enabling punishment will substantially increase group efficiency relative to the control. The paper does not manipulate other design dimensions, so its predictive value is strongest for games matching its structure.

- source: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2012). I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

- source: Herrmann, B., Thöni, C., & Gächter, S. (2008). Antisocial punishment across societies. *Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that introducing a punishment option in a repeated public goods game increases cooperation and group earnings in some societies, but not in others. In societies with high levels of antisocial punishment, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation and group earnings is diminished or absent. The variation in outcomes is strongly associated with societal-level factors such as norms of civic cooperation and the strength of the rule of law. The study demonstrates that punishment is not universally beneficial for efficiency or cooperation; its effect depends on the prevalence of antisocial punishment, which varies widely across societies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly context-dependent. When predicting the efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, it is crucial to consider not only the game design dimensions but also the likelihood of antisocial punishment, which may be influenced by societal or group-level norms. In environments similar to those with low antisocial punishment (e.g., Western Europe, US), punishment is likely to increase efficiency. In environments with high antisocial punishment, punishment may have little or even negative effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions in this study (e.g., player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, anonymity) are held constant, so the main moderator is the social context, not the game mechanics. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment) unless antisocial punishment is low.

- source: Baldassarri, D., & Grossman, G. (2011). Centralized sanctioning and legitimate authority promote cooperation in humans. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Centralized sanctioning, where a single monitor can punish, increases contributions to the public good compared to a baseline with no punishment. When the monitor is elected (legitimate authority), contributions increase even more than when the monitor is randomly assigned. The effect is attributed to the legitimacy of the authority, not to differences in the monitors' intrinsic characteristics or sanctioning behavior. The study is conducted in the field with Ugandan farmer cooperatives, using a repeated PGG with 8-12 players per group. The main outcome is contribution, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that centralized punishment (by a single monitor) increases cooperation (contributions) in public goods games, and that legitimacy of the authority (election vs. random assignment) further enhances this effect. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting the effect of centralized punishment and authority legitimacy on contribution rates, and suggest that similar effects may be expected for efficiency if contributions translate directly to group payoff in the game design. The study is highly relevant for understanding the impact of centralized punishment and authority legitimacy, but less so for direct efficiency prediction.

- source: Fowler, J. H., & Christakis, N. A. (2010). Cooperative behavior cascades in human social networks. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in both standard and punishment-enabled public goods games, individuals' contributions are causally influenced by the contributions of others in their group, and this influence spreads to others in subsequent rounds and groups, up to three degrees of separation. The presence of punishment increases contributions (as established in the original Fehr & Gachter 2002 study), but in this analysis, punishment does not fundamentally change the pattern or extent of behavioral cascades. Punishment can trigger cooperative cascades, but punishment behavior itself does not spread. The main outcome is the spread and persistence of cooperative behavior, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence about the spread of cooperative behavior in public goods games with and without punishment, but it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. Therefore, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. However, it does confirm that punishment increases contributions (as in the original Fehr & Gachter 2002), and that the presence of punishment does not fundamentally alter the network dynamics of cooperation spread. For efficiency prediction, this paper is only indirectly informative, as it does not quantify payoff or efficiency effects.

- source: Alexander, M., & Christia, F. (2011). Context Modularity of Human Altruism. *Science*.

  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment (sanctions) increases contributions to the public good, but only in institutional contexts characterized by integration (i.e., integrated schools). In segregated institutional contexts, the availability of punishment does not increase contributions. Group diversity (ethnic heterogeneity) reduces contributions in segregated contexts but not in integrated ones. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the results imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency would be positive in integrated contexts and negligible in segregated contexts, assuming higher contributions translate to higher group payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (proxied by contributions) is highly context-dependent: punishment increases contributions only when institutional integration is present. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (4), number of rounds (20), continuous contribution, no chat, and the presence/absence of punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so predictions must infer from contribution rates. The findings suggest that, in similar field settings, enabling punishment will only improve efficiency if the institutional context supports integration among participants.

- source: Milinski, M., Semmann, D., & Krambeck, H. J. (2002). Reputation helps solve the 'tragedy of the commons'. *Nature*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when public goods games are alternated with indirect reciprocity games (where players' reputations are visible and affect future help), cooperation in the public goods game remains high and group payoffs are significantly higher than in treatments where public goods games are played in blocks without reputation incentives. When the possibility of future indirect reciprocity is removed (by announcing only public goods rounds remain), cooperation and efficiency quickly decline. Thus, reputation-based incentives can substitute for direct punishment in maintaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms which link reputation to future rewards (indirect reciprocity) can sustain high efficiency in public goods games, even in the absence of explicit punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that the presence of reputation/reward mechanisms (even without punishment) can lead to high efficiency, especially when players expect future interactions where their reputation matters. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation is salient and can affect future payoffs, and less directly informative for games with explicit costly punishment but no reputation/reward linkage.

- source: Kümmerli, R., Burton-Chellew, M. N., Ross-Gillespie, A., & West, S. A. (2010). Resistance to extreme strategies, rather than prosocial preferences, can explain human cooperation in public goods games. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: Across four lab experiments, when the public goods game is modified so that full cooperation is individually optimal (e.g., by setting the multiplier above the group size or by adding group rewards), mean contributions and group efficiency increase significantly compared to the standard PGG. However, contributions remain well below 100% and full efficiency is not achieved. The proportion of subjects contributing fully in the last round of the modified games ranges from 35% to 72%, and the mean group contribution is always significantly less than the maximum possible. The results challenge the interpretation that high cooperation in PGGs is due to prosocial preferences, and instead point to psychological resistance to extreme strategies (full defection or full cooperation) as a key driver. The study does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that increasing the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) or otherwise aligning individual and group incentives increases group efficiency in public goods games. However, even when efficiency-maximizing behavior is straightforward, groups do not reach full efficiency, suggesting that psychological factors limit the effect. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency as a function of mpcr and group reward structures, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning is present in any treatment. Thus, the paper is highly informative for baseline/control efficiency prediction, but does not support prediction of the effect of enabling punishment.
