- source: Wang, Y. F., Nakao, A., Vasilakos, A. V., & Ma, J. H. (2011). On the effectiveness of service differentiation based resource-provision incentive mechanisms in dynamic and autonomous P2P networks. *Computer Networks*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: In dynamic, anonymous P2P environments modeled as public goods games, traditional punishment- and reward-based service differentiation mechanisms fail to sustain cooperation or high efficiency due to the presence of 'cooperator' mutants who contribute but do not punish or reward. Introducing a small entry fee and allowing voluntary participation enables the punishment-based scheme to achieve high average payoff and system efficiency, with punishers dominating the population most of the time. Reward-based schemes, even with entry fees, are less effective and more unstable, with defectors occupying the system more frequently. The effect is robust to group size, total population, and punishment cost. The average payoff in the punishment-based scheme approaches the ideal (but unfeasible) case of a reward-based scheme where rewarders can selectively reward only other rewarders and incur zero cost, but such selective rewarding is not feasible in anonymous, dynamic settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods game environments with anonymous, dynamic, and voluntary participation, punishment-based mechanisms with a small entry fee are much more effective at sustaining high efficiency (average payoff) than reward-based mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the control (no-punishment) condition is dominated by defectors. The results suggest that, if the game allows for voluntary entry and a small entry fee, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of group size, population size, or moderate changes in punishment cost. However, if there is no entry fee, neither punishment nor reward mechanisms are effective. The paper's results are most applicable to settings with anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, and may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.

- source: Helbing, D., Szolnoki, A., Perc, M., & Szabo, G. (2010). Evolutionary Establishment of Moral and Double Moral Standards through Spatial Interactions. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that costly punishment in spatial public goods games enables the spread and dominance of moralists (cooperators who punish), eliminating both defectors and non-punishing cooperators over time. This resolves the second-order free-rider problem and leads to higher levels of cooperation than would be expected in well-mixed populations, where defectors dominate. The effect depends on the punishment fine-to-cost ratio and the synergy factor (MPCR analog), with higher synergy and/or higher fine-to-cost ratios favoring the spread of moralists. The presence of a small number of defectors can accelerate the spread of moralists. The model shows that spatial structure is crucial: in well-mixed populations, punishment does not prevent the tragedy of the commons, but in spatial settings, punishment can establish and maintain cooperation. While the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, the dominance of moralists and elimination of defectors implies a more efficient outcome than the baseline.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in spatially structured public goods games can substantially increase group efficiency by promoting the dominance of punishing cooperators (moralists) and eliminating defectors and non-punishing cooperators. The effect is sensitive to the punishment fine-to-cost ratio and the synergy factor (MPCR), but is robust to group size and network structure as long as spatial clustering exists. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (with sufficient effectiveness and not too high cost) in a spatial or networked setting should be expected to increase efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the control is dominated by defectors. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency, so predictions should be qualitative or based on the expected dominance of cooperative strategies.

- source: ABREU, D., MILGROM, P., & PEARCE, D. (1991). INFORMATION AND TIMING IN REPEATED PARTNERSHIPS. *Econometrica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated partnership games with imperfect monitoring, the ability to sustain efficient cooperation through punishment is fundamentally limited by the informativeness of the monitoring technology and the timing of information. When monitoring is imperfect, reducing the interest rate (making players more patient) increases the efficiency of equilibrium outcomes, potentially allowing near-first-best payoffs. However, shortening the period of fixed action or reducing the lag with which information is reported can actually decrease efficiency, sometimes eliminating the possibility of cooperation altogether. Delaying information release can, counterintuitively, increase efficiency by reducing the cost of deterring deviations. The paper provides generalizations to n-player symmetric games and shows that these results are robust to more complex action and signal spaces.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency of repeated public-goods-like games with punishment under imperfect monitoring. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the informativeness of monitoring, the timing of information, and the patience of players. In particular, enabling punishment does not guarantee high efficiency unless monitoring is sufficiently informative and the timing of information and action periods is chosen carefully. For prediction, the paper suggests that increasing patience (lower interest rate) is reliably associated with higher efficiency, but increasing the frequency of action or information updates can reduce efficiency when monitoring is imperfect. The explicit formulas and comparative statics in the paper can be used to estimate efficiency given the relevant game design dimensions.

For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that punishment is only effective in structured (non-well-mixed) populations, and that the effectiveness of punishment depends on network density and punishment strength.

- source: Thomadsen, R., & Bhardwaj, P. (2011). Cooperation in Games with Forgetfulness. *Management Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that the effect of forgetfulness on efficiency in repeated games with punishment is fundamentally ambiguous: it can either reduce or increase the set of discount factors and game parameters under which full cooperation (and thus maximum efficiency) is sustainable. When the stage game is such that the cooperative action always yields nonnegative payoffs, forgetfulness weakens punishment and makes cooperation harder to sustain, reducing efficiency. However, when the stage game allows for the possibility that a forgetful player can be punished below the minimax, forgetfulness can make severe punishments credible and thus expand the range of parameters supporting cooperation, increasing efficiency. The paper provides explicit formulas and graphical illustrations for these effects in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and a franchising effort game. The results are theoretical and depend on the structure of the stage game and the probability of forgetfulness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated games is not monotonic and can be moderated by cognitive or institutional features like forgetfulness. In environments where punishment is only as severe as the stage minimax, introducing forgetfulness will generally reduce the efficiency gains from punishment. However, in environments where forgetfulness allows for below-minimax punishments, punishment can become more effective at sustaining cooperation, potentially increasing efficiency beyond what is possible with perfect recall. For prediction tasks, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined solely from standard game design dimensions; one must also consider whether the environment allows for forgetfulness and whether the stage game payoffs permit below-minimax punishments. The paper does not provide empirical estimates but gives clear theoretical boundaries and mechanisms.

- source: Evans, R., & Thomas, J. P. (2001). Cooperation and punishment. *Econometrica*.

  findings: The paper proves that in repeated common interest games (with no discounting), the mere presence of cooperative (nondraconian) strategies in the set of possible strategies does not guarantee efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcomes in equilibrium. However, if the set of possible strategies includes at least one 'draconian' strategy (i.e., a strategy that is willing to punish noncooperation to the maximum extent, such as min-maxing the opponent forever after a deviation), then all Nash equilibria are efficient. The paper also shows that this result is robust to certain computability restrictions and discusses the limits of these results under discounting and other technical assumptions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated games with a structure similar to public goods or common interest games, the availability of sufficiently severe punishment (i.e., the ability to impose maximal punishment at low or no cost) is both necessary and sufficient for punishment to induce efficient outcomes in equilibrium. If the game design allows for draconian punishment strategies, then enabling punishment should lead to high efficiency, regardless of the control efficiency. If only mild or 'cooperative' punishment is possible, efficiency gains are not guaranteed. The results are most directly applicable to two-player, infinite-horizon, undiscounted games, but the logic is relevant for understanding the role of punishment severity and credibility in other settings.
