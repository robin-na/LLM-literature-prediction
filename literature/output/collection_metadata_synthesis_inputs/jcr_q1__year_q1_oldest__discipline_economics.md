- source: Sutter, M., Haigner, S., & Kocher, M. G. (2010). Choosing the Carrot or the Stick? Endogenous Institutional Choice in Social Dilemma Situations. *Review of Economic Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that enabling groups to endogenously choose their institution (via voting) leads to higher contributions and higher group profits (efficiency) than exogenously imposed institutions, holding the institution constant. Both punishment and reward options increase contributions and profits compared to the standard VCM, but the effect is much stronger when the punishment/reward technology is highly effective (high leverage). However, punishment is rarely chosen when groups can choose, likely due to its costliness; groups prefer reward when it is effective. In exogenous treatments, high-leverage reward yields the highest profits, followed by high-leverage punishment, with the standard VCM lowest. The efficiency gains from punishment are offset by the cost of punishing, while reward can be efficiency-enhancing if its leverage is high. The positive effect of endogenous choice is not explained by selection of more cooperative types into certain institutions, but rather by the act of participation itself.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players and 10 rounds, enabling punishment (especially with high effectiveness) increases group efficiency (average profit) compared to the standard VCM, but the efficiency gain is limited by the cost of punishment. Reward, when highly effective, yields even higher efficiency than punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive but smaller than the effect on contributions, due to the costliness of punishment. Endogenous institutional choice (voting) further increases efficiency for any given institution. For prediction, the presence and effectiveness of punishment/reward, the cost/impact ratio, and whether the institution is chosen endogenously or exogenously are all important moderators. Control efficiency (standard VCM) is always lower than with effective punishment or reward, but the size of the efficiency gain depends on the punishment/reward technology and institutional choice procedure.

- source: Fehr, E., & Gächter, S. (2000). Cooperation and punishment in public goods experiments. *American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment option in a repeated public goods game with groups of four, a known 10-round horizon, and no communication or identification, leads to a dramatic increase in average contributions and, after an initial period of adjustment, to higher group efficiency (payoff) compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the no-punishment condition, contributions and efficiency decline toward full free-riding. In the punishment condition, contributions are sustained at high levels (often near full cooperation), and group payoffs in the final periods are 10-20% higher than in the control. Punishment is used primarily against those who contribute less than the group average, and punished individuals tend to increase their contributions in subsequent rounds. The effect is robust across both stranger (random rematching) and partner (fixed group) treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling costly punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and anonymous interactions. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low (due to declining contributions), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency by 10-20% in the final rounds, with the effect being stronger in fixed groups (partner) than in random groups (stranger). The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, known horizon, full feedback, anonymous punishment) closely match the canonical prediction task, making this paper highly informative for forecasting efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled.

- source: Ambrus, A., & Greiner, B. (2012). Imperfect Public Monitoring with Costly Punishment: An Experimental Study. *American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In 50-round, three-player public goods games with all-or-nothing contributions, the introduction of costly punishment options has different effects depending on monitoring and punishment severity. With perfect monitoring, both regular and strong punishment increase average net payoffs, with stronger punishment yielding higher efficiency. With imperfect (noisy) monitoring, regular punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline, while strong punishment increases cooperation but only restores efficiency to the no-punishment level (i.e., the gains from increased cooperation are offset by the costs of punishment). The relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is U-shaped under noise: intermediate punishment is worst for efficiency, while strong punishment is neutral. The paper provides direct, quantitative evidence on efficiency outcomes for each treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, especially as a function of monitoring quality and punishment severity. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive: under imperfect monitoring, standard punishment can reduce efficiency, and only very strong punishment can (at best) restore efficiency to the no-punishment baseline. The results are directly applicable to settings with all-or-nothing contributions, 3 players, 50 rounds, and known MPCR and punishment parameters. The findings highlight the importance of monitoring quality and punishment effectiveness in predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Fudenberg, D., & Pathak, P. A. (2010). Unobserved punishment supports cooperation. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares two versions of a public goods game with punishment: one where punishment is revealed after each round (Observed), and one where punishment is only revealed at the end of 10 rounds (Unobserved). Both treatments use random rematching, groups of 4, 10 rounds per set, and a standard VCM payoff structure (endowment 20, MPCR 0.4). Punishment costs 1 token per point assigned and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) average contributions are significantly higher in the Unobserved treatment than in the Observed treatment; (2) average income (efficiency) is also higher in the Unobserved treatment, though confidence intervals overlap in some periods; (3) the fraction of participants who punish and punishment expenditure are at least as high, or higher, in the Unobserved treatment; (4) punishment is more sensitive to undercontribution in the Unobserved treatment. The results show that costly punishment supports cooperation and efficiency even when it cannot have immediate strategic or reputational effects, suggesting an intrinsic motivation for punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with punishment, the presence of a punishment option increases efficiency (average income) relative to the baseline with no punishment, and that this effect is robust to whether punishment is immediately observed or only revealed at the end. The game design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds per set, no chat, continuous contribution, no default, MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 1, punishment tech 3, no reward, random rematching, and full feedback on others' contributions. The results suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the effect is not dependent on immediate feedback about punishment. The paper is directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in standard PGGs with punishment.

- source: Nikiforakis, N. (2008). Punishment and counter-punishment in public good games: Can we really govern ourselves?. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three treatments: (1) standard public good game (VCM, no punishment), (2) public good game with one-sided punishment (P), and (3) public good game with two-sided punishment/counter-punishment (PCP). In the P treatment, punishment increases contributions but not enough to offset the cost of punishment, so average earnings are lower than in the control. In the PCP treatment, the threat of counter-punishment reduces the willingness to punish free riders, leading to lower contributions and even lower earnings than in both the control and the P treatment. The presence of counter-punishment opportunities causes a breakdown of cooperation and group welfare. The results are robust to matching protocol (fixed vs random) and sequencing effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard linear public goods games with 4 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and standard punishment technology, enabling punishment reduces efficiency compared to the control (no punishment), and enabling counter-punishment reduces efficiency even further. The effect is robust across matching protocols. For prediction, if the control efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, and enabling counter-punishment will reduce it further. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the punishment cost and technology, which can inform predictions for similar designs.

- source: Gächter, S., & Herrmann, B. (2011). The limits of self-governance when cooperators get punished: Experimental evidence from urban and rural Russia. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that, contrary to much of the previous literature, introducing a punishment option in one-shot public goods games in Russia did not increase cooperation or group efficiency. In fact, average earnings (a direct measure of efficiency) were substantially lower in the punishment condition than in the no-punishment condition across all subject pools. This is attributed to high levels of punishment directed at cooperators as well as free riders, leading to large payoff losses. The effect is robust across urban/rural and young/mature subject pools. The findings are supported by both within-subject and between-subject comparisons, and are further corroborated by similar results in repeated-game settings in Russia.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot public goods games with a punishment option, the effect of punishment on efficiency can be negative if misdirected punishment is prevalent. The game design here (n=3, one-shot, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=1, punishment impact=3, no chat, no reward, continuous contributions) is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar settings. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment in a similar Russian context is likely to reduce efficiency, not increase it. The findings highlight the importance of considering the prevalence of antisocial punishment and the social context when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Masclet, D., Noussair, C., Tucker, S., & Villeval, M. C. (2003). Monetary and nonmonetary punishment in the voluntary contributions mechanism. *American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that introducing either monetary or nonmonetary punishment in a standard voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no punishment. Nonmonetary (costless, expressive) punishment increases contributions and earnings immediately, while monetary punishment initially reduces earnings due to punishment costs but eventually leads to higher contributions and similar earnings as nonmonetary punishment. The effect of nonmonetary punishment is stronger when groups are stable (Partner matching) than when they are rematched (Stranger matching). Both types of punishment are most effective at increasing contributions among those who contributed less than the group average. The study provides direct evidence that both formal (monetary) and informal (nonmonetary) sanctions can increase efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (either monetary or nonmonetary) in a standard PGG/VCM increases group efficiency (average earnings) compared to a no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across two forms of punishment, but monetary punishment is more effective at sustaining high contributions over time, though it incurs costs that initially reduce efficiency. The design dimensions—such as group size (4), number of rounds (10 per segment), no chat, continuous contributions, known MPCR (0.4), and anonymous punishment—are all specified, making the findings directly applicable to prediction tasks involving similar game structures. The results also highlight that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the cost structure and the matching protocol (Partner vs Stranger), with stable groups supporting more effective informal sanctions. For prediction, the presence of punishment (especially monetary) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude and time profile may depend on punishment cost and group stability.

- source: Andreoni, J., & Gee, L. K. (2012). Gun for hire: Delegated enforcement and peer punishment in public goods provision. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with four players, the introduction of a delegated punishment mechanism (either imposed or endogenously hired) leads to a substantial and immediate increase in both contributions and net earnings (welfare), compared to both the no-punishment baseline and the peer-to-peer punishment regime. When both delegated and peer-to-peer punishment are available, subjects overwhelmingly choose to implement the delegated mechanism, which then crowds out peer punishment and further increases efficiency. The delegated mechanism is low-cost, targets only the largest free rider, and is effective at deterring free riding. The efficiency gains are robust across different baseline conditions (with or without peer punishment as the initial regime).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players and repeated rounds, enabling a delegated punishment mechanism (with a low per-person cost and a punishment that targets only the largest free rider) increases group efficiency (net earnings) substantially compared to both no-punishment and peer-to-peer punishment regimes. The efficiency gains are primarily due to a reduction in costly peer-to-peer punishment, not just increased contributions. The findings are highly informative for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in similar game designs, especially when the punishment is centralized, low-cost, and targets the worst free rider. The results are less directly informative for other forms of punishment (e.g., peer-to-peer only, or high-cost/low-impact punishment), but the contrast with peer-to-peer punishment is explicitly tested and shows delegated punishment is superior for efficiency.

- source: Nikiforakis, N., Noussair, C. N., & Wilkening, T. (2012). Normative conflict and feuds: The limits of self-enforcement. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game, groups of four played 10 rounds with or without punishment. In the 'Symmetric' treatment (all players have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment opportunities led to higher contributions and significantly higher group earnings (efficiency) compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the 'Asymmetric' treatment (two players have high, two have low returns), punishment also increased contributions, but the frequency and severity of counter-punishment and feuds rose sharply, leading to high punishment costs. As a result, group earnings in the Asymmetric treatment with punishment were not significantly higher than in the no-punishment baseline. The study demonstrates that the efficiency benefits of punishment depend critically on the absence of normative conflict: when such conflict is present, punishment can fail to improve, or even reduce, efficiency due to costly feuding.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games depends on group homogeneity and the presence of normative conflict. In symmetric (homogeneous) groups, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. In asymmetric (heterogeneous) groups with normative conflict, enabling punishment increases cooperation but not efficiency, as punishment costs (especially from feuds) offset the gains. For prediction, the presence of heterogeneity in returns (or other sources of normative conflict) is a key moderator: control efficiency alone is not sufficient—one must also consider whether the game design induces normative conflict. Other design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, technology, player count) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable moderator is the symmetry/asymmetry of returns.

  findings: The paper finds that when groups in a linear public goods game are allowed to vote on the parameters of a formal sanction scheme (who is penalized, maximum penalty, and exemption level), most groups quickly learn to select efficient penalty rules that induce full cooperation and maximize group earnings. In the baseline (no sanctions), contributions and earnings decline over time as expected. In the penalty treatment, after voting, contributions and earnings rise and are sustained at high levels, with the median group achieving full cooperation and maximum efficiency. The effect is attributed to the ability of groups to endogenously select strong, well-targeted penalties for free-riding. Individual differences (cooperative orientation, IQ, political conservatism, gender) predict voting behavior, but do not prevent most groups from converging to efficient outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, direct evidence that enabling groups to endogenously design formal sanction schemes in a standard linear public goods game can transform a low-efficiency baseline into a high-efficiency outcome. The effect is robust to group heterogeneity and persists over repeated rounds. For prediction, the presence of a formal, group-voted punishment mechanism with high penalty rates and full exemption for full contributors is highly likely to increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, provided the group can select the parameters. The findings are most applicable to settings with 5 players, 24 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about group outcomes.

- source: Ertan, A., Page, T., & Putterman, L. (2009). Who to punish? Individual decisions and majority rule in mitigating the free rider problem. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when groups can vote on punishment rules, they overwhelmingly prohibit punishment of high contributors and converge toward allowing punishment of low contributors only. This rule structure leads to higher contributions and, crucially, higher efficiency (group earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to both no punishment and unrestricted punishment. The cost of punishment under unrestricted rules reduces efficiency, while targeted punishment of low contributors effectively deters free riding and increases group welfare. The findings are robust across two experimental designs (3-vote and 5-vote), and the efficiency gains are statistically significant. The study provides direct evidence that the structure and targeting of punishment, not just its presence, are critical for achieving efficiency gains in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency only if punishment is restricted to low contributors (i.e., free riders) and not allowed against high contributors. Unrestricted punishment can reduce efficiency due to costly 'perverse punishment.' The experimental design and results allow direct mapping to prediction tasks: for a 4-player, 30-round, no-chat, continuous-contribution VCM with MPCR 0.4, punishment cost 0.25 per 1 unit impact, and anonymous punishment, efficiency is highest when punishment is allowed only for low contributors. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is known, enabling targeted punishment (low-only) is likely to increase efficiency substantially; enabling unrestricted punishment may not. The findings are directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the targeting and scope of punishment.

- source: Kosfeld, M., Okada, A., & Riedl, A. (2009). Institution Formation in Public Goods Games. *American Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when groups can endogenously form a centralized sanctioning institution (which punishes non-contributors among its members), they do so in a large majority of cases, and most often as a 'grand organization' including all players. This leads to much higher and more stable contributions and efficiency compared to control groups without the institution option. In the lower MPCR treatment (a=0.4), efficiency rises from 25% (control) to 51% (institution formation); in the higher MPCR treatment (a=0.65), efficiency rises from 62% (control) to 70% (institution formation), though the latter difference is not statistically significant. The institution is only implemented if all players join, reflecting strong fairness concerns. When institution formation fails, efficiency is not lower than in the control. The results show that endogenous punishment institutions can solve the public goods dilemma and raise efficiency, but their success depends on fairness-driven willingness to include all group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling centralized punishment institutions in linear public goods games. When the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling institution formation can more than double efficiency, especially at lower MPCR. The effect is robust to group size (n=4), number of rounds (20), and the specific centralized punishment structure used here. The main moderator is the MPCR: the efficiency gain is larger when MPCR is lower. The institution is only effective if all players join, so settings that facilitate or require universal participation are more likely to see large efficiency gains. The cost of the institution (here, 2 points per round shared among members) is low relative to the efficiency gains. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment options.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed

  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.
