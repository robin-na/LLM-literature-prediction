- source: Sripada, C. S. (2005). Punishment and the strategic structure of moral systems. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is a more general and empirically plausible mechanism for sustaining compliance with moral norms than reciprocity. Punishment can support cooperation in large groups and can enforce arbitrary rules, not just those with a cooperative payoff structure. The author reviews theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and ethnographic/experimental evidence to support the claim that punishment, including direct, exclusion-based, and reputation-based forms, is universal in human groups. The paper also discusses the problem of stabilizing costly punishment and the implications for understanding moral diversity. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental results on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the importance of punishment mechanisms in sustaining cooperation and compliance in public-goods-like environments, especially in large groups. It suggests that, in theory, enabling punishment should increase compliance and cooperation, and thus could improve efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes. The arguments are useful for understanding why punishment may be effective, but the paper does not supply empirical data or parameterized results that can be directly used for predicting efficiency changes in experimental public goods games. Its value is mainly in supporting the general plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation and norm compliance, especially in large groups.

- source: Nakao, H., & Machery, E. (2012). The evolution of punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is widespread in nature and often evolves for reasons other than inducing behavioral change in the punishee. In many cases (especially among plants and insects), punishment is a loss-cutting or cost-imposing strategy, not a behavior-modification strategy. Even among vertebrates, where behavioral modification is more common, the evolutionary benefits of punishment often do not depend on the punishee changing its behavior. The paper challenges the view that punishment primarily evolved to promote cooperation or efficiency, and instead highlights alternative evolutionary pathways.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative data relevant to predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does, however, offer theoretical context suggesting that the evolutionary function of punishment may not always be to increase cooperation or group efficiency, but could instead be to reduce harm to the punisher or its kin. For the prediction task, this paper suggests caution in assuming that punishment mechanisms will always improve efficiency or cooperation, especially in settings where behavioral modification is unlikely or where punishment serves other evolutionary functions. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental data to calibrate such predictions.

- source: CAMPBELL, D. T. (1991). A NATURALISTIC THEORY OF ARCHAIC MORAL ORDERS. *Zygon*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists
  findings: The paper theorizes that the universal presence of supernatural sanction systems (rewarding and punishing afterlives) in archaic civilizations functioned to curb individual selfishness and support group-level cooperation, overcoming the problem of genetic competition among cooperators. It suggests that such beliefs and associated costly rituals (e.g., wasteful royal funerals) reinforced the credibility of these sanction systems, thereby enhancing group cohesion and effectiveness. The argument is that these group-level cultural adaptations, including moral norms and invisible sanction systems, were selected because they increased the survival and success of social groups, even if they imposed costs on individuals. However, the paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that sanction systems (punishment and reward, especially supernatural or invisible ones) can enhance group-level cooperation and cohesion, potentially increasing group effectiveness or efficiency in a broad sense. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. Its relevance is mainly conceptual, supporting the plausibility of positive effects of sanction systems on group outcomes, but it does not inform specific game design dimensions or control efficiency values.
