  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that gossip can promote cooperation in public goods game environments, even under heterogeneous interpersonal relationships. Among the three mechanisms for dealing with gossip (punishment only, punishment with reputation compensation, and punishment with monetary compensation), punishment with monetary compensation is most effective at increasing cooperation rates and total contributions. The model also identifies optimal parameter ranges for tolerance, penalty time, and rebirth coefficient to maximize cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (cooperation speed, total contributions) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that monetary compensation for those wrongly affected by gossip, combined with punishment for false gossipers, can best promote cooperative behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing punishment (especially with monetary compensation for those wrongly affected) can increase cooperation rates and total contributions in public-goods-game-like environments. However, because the outcomes are not reported as efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting a more positive effect of punishment on cooperation and contribution behavior, rather than on efficiency per se. The model's design and parameter exploration may inform predictions about the likely direction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require caution. The paper is most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms and the relative effectiveness of different punishment/compensation schemes.

- source: Pedroso, M. (2021). Blind Cooperation: The Evolution of Redundancy via Ignorance. *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that redundancy in group cooperation can evolve without targeted punishment or coercion if individuals are ignorant of others' strategies and/or face harsh environments. In a threshold public goods game, when players do not know what others will do, they cooperate with an intermediate probability, leading to more cooperators than strictly necessary (redundancy). Harsher environments (higher group-level cost of failure) increase the probability of cooperation and thus redundancy. In contrast, if individuals are well-informed about others' strategies, redundancy does not evolve without punishment. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or group payoff, but discusses these concepts in relation to the model.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how information availability and environmental harshness affect cooperation and redundancy in threshold public goods games. It suggests that punishment is not strictly necessary for redundancy or intermediate cooperation rates if players are ignorant of others' actions. However, it does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff with or without punishment, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment parameters. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of information and environmental context as moderators, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.

- source: Rubin, H. (2022). When it pays to punish in the evolution of honesty and cooperation. *Synthese*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper uses evolutionary game theory and simulations to analyze how different forms of costly punishment (punishing lying vs. punishing defection) affect the evolution of honesty and cooperation in a repeated signaling/donation game. It finds that: (1) Punishment of lying increases honesty but often reduces the prevalence of cooperation, because the costs of punishing and being punished can outweigh the benefits of increased honesty. (2) Punishment of defection (as in standard strong reciprocity models) increases cooperation and group success, consistent with prior findings in stag hunt and repeated PD games. (3) The effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency depends on game parameters (number of rounds, benefit of donation, lie detection probability, punishment cost and effectiveness). (4) When both types of punishment are available, the results are intermediate. The paper highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and can be negative when punishment targets lying rather than defection.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important cautionary evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in games with multi-stage cooperation (e.g., signaling plus donation). If punishment targets lying (dishonest signaling), it may increase honesty but can reduce overall cooperation and thus efficiency, especially when punishment is costly and lie detection is imperfect. If punishment targets defection (failure to cooperate), it is more likely to increase cooperation and efficiency, as in standard public goods or stag hunt models. For prediction tasks, the design dimension 'punishment target' (lying vs. defection) is critical. The paper also shows that the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the number of rounds, and the benefit of cooperation all moderate the effect. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not always a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency (with punishment), especially when punishment targets lying.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that under a static reward and punishment mechanism (SRPM), the system does not reach a stable equilibrium; strategies of all parties fluctuate periodically. Under a dynamic reward and punishment mechanism (DRPM), the system converges to a stable mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE), interpreted as an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Increasing the upper limit of punishments for illegal emissions by village enterprises significantly increases the probability of legal emissions and reduces the need for strict government regulation and active supervision by villagers. Increasing the upper limit of rewards for villagers' active supervision increases their supervision probability but may reduce legal compliance by enterprises and government regulatory effort. The DRPM is recommended for more effective and stable governance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that dynamic, behavior-dependent punishment mechanisms (analogous to more flexible or responsive punishment rules in public goods games) can stabilize cooperative behavior (legal emissions) and reduce the need for costly monitoring and enforcement. However, the model is not a standard public goods game and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The main outcomes are strategy probabilities, not payoffs. The findings suggest that increasing punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech) and using dynamic punishment rules may improve stability and compliance, but the effect on efficiency is not directly quantified. The results are most relevant for understanding how punishment design (cost, tech, dynamic vs static) may affect behavioral outcomes in multi-agent governance games.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment may not always translate to higher efficiency, especially if the game design allows for undetected defection. However, the models are dyadic PDs, not public goods games, and do not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct quantitative prediction is not possible from this paper.
