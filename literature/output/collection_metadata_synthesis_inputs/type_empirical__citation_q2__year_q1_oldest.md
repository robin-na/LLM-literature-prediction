- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Nicklisch, A., & Wolff, I. (2011). COOPERATION NORMS IN MULTIPLE-STAGE PUNISHMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment implements a linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, and multiple endogenous punishment stages per round. Players can punish at a convex cost, and punishment reduces the target's payoff by 10% per point (up to 10 points). Two treatments are compared: BASIC (standard punishment) and OPINION (bystanders can approve/disapprove of punishment, but this has no direct payoff effect). The main findings are: (1) Average payoffs and efficiency remain above the Nash equilibrium and do not differ significantly between treatments or over time; (2) The opportunity for counter-punishment does not lead to a collapse in cooperation or efficiency; (3) Punishment is guided by an absolute full-contribution norm, and both punishers and bystanders apply similar standards; (4) Retaliation occurs but does not undermine overall efficiency; (5) The presence of bystander approval does not materially change efficiency or cooperation patterns. The results suggest that multi-stage punishment with changing groups can sustain cooperation and efficiency, even when counter-punishment is possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a four-player, ten-round linear PGG with continuous contributions, full information, and multi-stage punishment (with convex costs and 10% effectiveness per point), the introduction of punishment sustains or increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment Nash equilibrium. Efficiency remains stable and above the Nash level, and does not collapse even when counter-punishment is possible. The presence of bystander approval (OPINION treatment) does not significantly alter efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that in similar designs (with multi-stage punishment, convex costs, and changing groups), enabling punishment is likely to have a more positive effect on efficiency, and the risk of efficiency collapse due to counter-punishment is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in this class of games.

- source: Guillen, P., Fatas, E., & Brañas-Garza, P. (2010). Inducing efficient conditional cooperation patterns in public goods games, an experimental investigation. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The introduction of a grim trigger strategy threat (via possible automata) leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (by 40% before a surprise restart and 50% after) compared to a standard repeated public goods game. Contributions in the threat treatment are highly polarized (either full or zero), and the pattern of conditional cooperation changes: in the threat treatment, the minimum contribution in the previous round predicts current contributions, consistent with grim trigger logic. However, contributions still decline over time, and the effect is driven by belief manipulation rather than actual punishment or sanctions. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the higher contributions imply higher group payoffs, net of the absence of punishment costs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that belief in the possibility of strict conditional cooperation (grim trigger) can substantially increase contributions in repeated public goods games, even without actual punishment. However, because there is no real punishment mechanism (no cost, no assignment of punishment, no reduction of others' payoffs), the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task of estimating efficiency changes due to enabling punishment. The results suggest that the mere threat or belief in sanctions can have large effects on cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the efficiency impact of actual punishment mechanisms, especially considering the cost of punishment. The design dimensions are well specified, but the absence of real punishment means the findings should be used as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

- source: Chung, D., Yun, K., Kim, J. H., Jang, B., & Jeong, J. (2011). Different Gain/Loss Sensitivity and Social Adaptation Ability in Gifted Adolescents during a Public Goods Game. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that gifted adolescents are more cooperative and earn higher total payoffs than average adolescents in a repeated binary public goods game with five players and no punishment or reward mechanisms. Three incentive conditions are tested: (1) standard PGG, (2) no-loss guarantee for cooperators, and (3) equalized bonus for all in successful rounds. Gifted adolescents show higher gain sensitivity and lower loss sensitivity, and are more strategic in maximizing payoffs. Average adolescents are more loss-averse, less strategic, and more influenced by social adaptation behaviors. The study provides detailed evidence on how group composition (gifted vs. average) and incentive structure affect efficiency and cooperation, but does not manipulate or analyze punishment or sanctioning.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on efficiency and payoff outcomes in repeated binary PGGs without punishment or reward. It is directly informative for predicting efficiency in control (no-punishment) conditions with known game design parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.). However, it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or discussed in the experimental design. Thus, it is useful for establishing baseline efficiency but not for predicting the effect of punishment.

- source: Noussair, C., & Soo, C. (2008). Voluntary contributions to a dynamic public good: Experimental evidence. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that in a dynamic public goods game where the MPCR increases or decreases based on group contributions, many groups are able to sustain high contributions and achieve high efficiency, even in the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms. The average efficiency is .671 in the high initial MPCR treatment and .632 in the low initial MPCR treatment, both substantially above the efficiency that would result from full free-riding. There is substantial heterogeneity: some groups converge to high contributions and high MPCR, while others decline to low contributions. The initial MPCR does not significantly affect long-run outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a baseline for the efficiency of dynamic public goods games without punishment or reward. It shows that, in such settings, efficiency can be substantially higher than in standard static public goods games, due to the dynamic feedback of contributions on the MPCR. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is useful for estimating control (no-punishment) efficiency in dynamic PGGs, but it does not provide any evidence about the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present or discussed experimentally. Thus, it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency impact of enabling punishment, but it does suggest that dynamic feedback mechanisms can themselves sustain high efficiency in some groups.

- source: Denant-Boemont, L., Masclet, D., & Noussair, C. (2011). ANNOUNCEMENT, OBSERVATION AND HONESTY IN THE VOLUNTARY CONTRIBUTIONS GAME. *Pacific Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that requiring subjects to make non-binding announcements of intended contributions, combined with making actual contributions publicly observable and attributable, significantly increases average contribution levels in a repeated public goods game. However, neither announcements alone nor observation alone have a significant effect. The increase in contributions is interpreted as arising from increased social pressure (guilt and shame) and greater honesty when actions are observable. The study does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, and does not report efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on behavioral outcomes (contribution, announcement, honesty).
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. However, it does provide informative evidence about the effects of communication (announcements) and observability (public attribution of actions) on contribution behavior. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly relevant, but it may be useful for understanding the baseline effects of non-punitive social mechanisms (communication, transparency) on cooperation. It also clarifies that, in the absence of punishment, such mechanisms can increase contributions, but do not always do so unless combined.

- source: Colombier, N., Masclet, D., Mirza, D., & Montmarquette, C. (2011). GLOBAL SECURITY POLICIES AGAINST TERRORISM AND THE FREE RIDING PROBLEM: AN EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a laboratory experiment simulating international counterterrorism cooperation as a public good with a stochastic loss, the authors find that, absent institutional incentives, most participants defect and invest in private protection rather than the collective good. Introducing punishment (sanction) or reward institutions significantly increases investment in the collective account. However, while both punishment and reward reduce the probability of a loss event and increase first-stage payoffs, only reward institutions lead to higher overall welfare. Punishment increases cooperation but the direct costs of punishing (borne by both punishers and targets) more than offset the efficiency gains from increased cooperation, resulting in lower final payoffs than the baseline. Reward institutions, by contrast, increase both cooperation and final payoffs. Thus, punishment is not efficiency-enhancing in this environment, while reward is.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public-goods-like environment with costly punishment, enabling punishment increases cooperation but reduces overall efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The costs of punishment (to both punisher and target) outweigh the benefits from increased cooperation. Therefore, when predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, one should expect a less positive or even negative effect on group payoff/welfare, especially when punishment is costly and the structure is similar to this experiment (n=4, 40 rounds, costly punishment, no identification of punishers, no chat, continuous contributions). The paper also shows that reward institutions can increase both cooperation and efficiency, in contrast to punishment.

- source: Fatas, E., Jimenez-Jimenez, F., & Morales, A. J. (2010). Blind Fines in Cooperatives. *Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that a blind, probabilistic punishment mechanism based on group performance can significantly increase average group quality (success ratio) in a cooperative production setting, but only when the cost of contributing high quality is not too high. In the LC treatment, average group quality rises from about 40% to about 70% of the maximum after punishment is introduced, while in the HC treatment, there is no significant improvement. The mechanism does not require individual monitoring, and its effectiveness depends on the underlying cost structure. The main outcome is group quality, which is closely related to efficiency, though not reported as explicit group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a group-level, blind punishment mechanism can substantially increase group performance (as measured by average quality/success ratio) in public-goods-like environments, but only when the cost of cooperation is not too high. For prediction tasks, the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive when the marginal cost of cooperation is low relative to the group benefit, and negligible when costs are high. The design features—no chat, fixed group size (n=4), 20 rounds per block, no individual monitoring, and punishment based on group outcome—are all relevant for mapping to prediction settings. The main limitation is that the outcome is group quality, not direct group payoff, but the two are tightly linked in this design.

- source: Akpalu, W., & Martinsson, P. (2012). Ostracism and Common Pool Resource Management in a Developing Country: Young Fishers in the Laboratory. *Journal of African Economies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Allowing group members to ostracize (permanently exclude) over-extractors via majority vote, even at a cost to the remaining group, sharply reduced overfishing compared to a baseline with no ostracism. In the baseline, extraction effort increased over time toward the Nash equilibrium (over-exploitation), while with ostracism, effort dropped toward the social optimum. The effect was immediate and strong after ostracism was introduced. Voting to ostracize was targeted at over-extractors, and the number of votes received was the main determinant of reduced extraction. The costliness of ostracism did not prevent its use. The study does not report group payoffs or efficiency ratios, but the reduction in extraction effort implies improved efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a credible, group-administered punishment mechanism (ostracism) in a CPR environment can substantially reduce over-extraction and move behavior toward the social optimum, even when punishment is costly to the group. While efficiency is not directly measured, the reduction in extraction effort is a strong proxy for increased efficiency in this context. For prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, enforceable punishment mechanism (ostracism) is likely to increase efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, especially in settings with no communication, fixed group membership, and a known time horizon. The specific design (majority vote, permanent exclusion, cost to punishers) is important for generalization.

- source: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., & De Cremer, D. (2009). When sanctions that can be evaded still work: The role of trust in leaders. *Social Influence*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation depends on both the trustworthiness of the leader who installs the sanction and the general trust participants have in authorities. When an alternative, more harmful defection option is available (i.e., sanctions can be evaded), participants with low general trust in authorities are less likely to cooperate if the leader is untrustworthy, and instead shift to the evasion option. High-trust participants are less sensitive to leader trustworthiness. The main outcomes are behavioral allocations; the paper does not report group payoff or efficiency, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the presence of an evadable punishment option (i.e., a trilemma with an alternative defection route) can undermine the effectiveness of sanctions in promoting cooperation, especially among participants with low trust in authorities and when the leader is perceived as untrustworthy. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in designs where punishment can be evaded, the positive effect of punishment on cooperation (and thus efficiency) may be reduced or even reversed for some subgroups. For prediction, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of evasion options and trust, but not about the quantitative effect on efficiency.

- source: De Cremer, D., Hoogervorst, N., & Desmet, P. (2012). Procedural Justice and Sanctions in Social Dilemmas: The Moderating Effects of Group Feedback and Identification. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that accurate sanctioning systems (those that monitor all contribution sessions) increase contributions relative to inaccurate systems, but this effect depends on group identification and feedback. For high group identifiers, procedural justice (accuracy) increases contributions only after group failure; for low identifiers, it increases contributions only after group success. The experiment uses a step-level public good game with a threshold and a punishment mechanism for the lowest contributor, but does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings. The findings highlight the importance of procedural justice in sanctioning systems for promoting cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence about effects on efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing contributions depends on the perceived procedural justice (accuracy) of the sanctioning system, and that this effect is moderated by group identification and feedback about group success or failure. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results suggest that, in step-level public good games with punishment, the design of the punishment system (especially its perceived fairness and accuracy) and the social context (identification, feedback) can strongly moderate behavioral responses, which may in turn affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that punishment will be more effective at increasing cooperation (and possibly efficiency) when the system is accurate and procedurally just, but the effect is context-dependent.

- source: Brick, K., & Visser, M. (2010). Meeting a national emission reduction target in an experimental setting. *Climate Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Voluntary cooperation alone is insufficient to meet a group emission reduction target in a public good game with heterogeneous players. Allowing communication increases average contributions and the proportion of groups meeting the target (from 35% to 50%), but also increases polarization between full cooperation and free-riding. Introducing a tax-based sanction (automatic fine for under-contribution) leads to near-universal compliance with the target (88–91% of groups meet the target), but also crowds out voluntary contributions above the minimum required. The effect of taxation on contributions above the minimum differs by player type: for capital, taxation reduces excess contributions; for labour, taxation can encourage excess contributions relative to baseline. The study does not directly analyze efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the compliance rates and average earnings suggest that tax-based sanctions are highly effective at achieving the group target.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that automatic, rule-based sanctions (taxes/fines for under-contribution) are highly effective at increasing compliance with group targets in public good games, even with heterogeneous players. Communication alone improves cooperation but is less reliable than sanctions. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of institutional (not peer) punishment on group compliance and contributions, especially in settings with asymmetric player types and marginal returns. However, because the study does not report efficiency in the standard sense (group payoff as a share of maximum possible), its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. Still, the high compliance rates under tax-based sanctions suggest that efficiency would be much higher with punishment enabled than in the voluntary or communication-only conditions.

- source: Corgnet, B. (2012). PEER EVALUATIONS AND TEAM PERFORMANCE: WHEN FRIENDS DO WORSE THAN STRANGERS. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that using peer evaluations to allocate team profits in a real-effort team task leads to lower team performance (measured as a percentage of maximum possible profits) than an equal split rule. This effect is statistically significant and is stronger in teams where members are acquaintances or friends. The mechanism appears to involve a crowding-out of intrinsic motivation and increased negative reciprocity when peer evaluations are used among friends. The majority of peer assessments follow equality norms, but a significant minority use equity (contribution-based) norms. The results suggest that peer evaluation mechanisms may not always improve efficiency, especially in contexts with strong interpersonal relationships.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in small real-effort team settings with face-to-face communication and high social proximity, introducing a peer evaluation mechanism for allocating payoffs (a form of endogenous sanction/reward) can reduce efficiency compared to a fixed equal split. This is contrary to standard public goods game findings where punishment or reward options often increase efficiency. The negative effect is especially pronounced when team members are friends or classmates. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of introducing peer-based sanction/reward mechanisms on efficiency is context-dependent: in environments with high social ties and communication, such mechanisms may reduce efficiency relative to control. The findings are most relevant for small teams with real-effort tasks, face-to-face interaction, and endogenous allocation of payoffs via peer assessment.

- source: Grimm, V., & Mengel, F. (2011). Matching technology and the choice of punishment institutions in a prisoner's dilemma game. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that a local, exogenous punishment institution can sustain high cooperation and high average payoffs (efficiency) when the matching structure (population viscosity) is high, i.e., when group members mostly interact with each other. As viscosity decreases (more random mixing), both cooperation and average payoffs decline. In the most separated case (x=0), average profits are 373 (out of a possible 400), while in the most mixed case (x=1), average profits drop to 230 (the mutual defection payoff is 200). A control with internalized punishment (punishment always applies) at low viscosity (x=2/3) yields higher average profits (243) than local punishment (230). Thus, punishment institutions can increase efficiency, but their effectiveness depends strongly on the matching structure and the scope of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment depends on the matching structure (population viscosity) and the scope of punishment (local vs internalized). When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the presence of a punishment institution will increase efficiency only if the environment allows for sufficient group separation (high viscosity) or if punishment is internalized (applies in all matches). In low viscosity or random matching, local punishment is much less effective. The control efficiency (without punishment) is the mutual defection payoff (200), and the treatment efficiency with punishment can approach the cooperative payoff (400) under high viscosity, but remains low under random matching. The paper also shows that the technical details of punishment (local vs internalized) matter for efficiency outcomes.

- source: Abbink, K., Sadrieh, A., & Zamir, S. (2004). Fairness, public good, and emotional aspects of punishment behavior. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that making punishment (rejection) visible to proposers increases the frequency of punishment and changes proposer behavior (more equal offers over time), but this does not translate into higher efficiency. Instead, efficiency is lower in the open (punishment-enabled) treatment compared to the covered (punishment-disabled) treatment, because the cost of punishment (lost payoffs from rejections) outweighs any gains from increased fairness. Only when rejection rates are either very high (deterring almost all unfair offers) or very low (almost no punishment) does efficiency approach the maximum. The study provides direct evidence that punishment can reduce efficiency in bargaining environments, especially when it is used at intermediate rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (making it visible and actionable) in a repeated ultimatum minigame reduces efficiency compared to a control where punishment is hidden/disabled. The effect is driven by increased costly punishment (rejections), which reduces total group payoffs. The design is not a standard public goods game, but the punishment mechanism and efficiency analysis are directly relevant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar two-player bargaining games, enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency unless punishment is either very rare or so frequent that it almost eliminates unfair offers. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will improve efficiency in environments where its cost is high and its deterrence effect is partial.

- source: Bolle, F., Breitmoser, Y., & Schlächter, S. (2011). Extortion in the laboratory. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that when punishment is cheap and effective, efficiency is likely to be lower than in a fully cooperative scenario, and the presence of punishment can lead to persistent efficiency losses. The results are most directly relevant to asymmetric, dyadic games rather than standard symmetric public goods games, but the mechanisms (punishment cost, effectiveness, behavioral response) are informative for predicting efficiency in related environments.

- source: Tan, F. F., & Xiao, E. T. (2012). Peer punishment with third-party approval in a social dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment compares two treatments in a repeated prisoner's dilemma: (1) baseline, where players can directly punish each other, and (2) TPA (third-party approval), where a third party must approve any punishment before it is implemented. The TPA treatment results in significantly lower cooperation rates (0.47 vs. 0.70) and marginally significantly lower average earnings (23.52 vs. 25.30 tokens per period) compared to the baseline. Third-party approval reduces antisocial punishment but also reduces punishment of defectors, which appears to undermine the positive effect of punishment on cooperation. The study suggests that, in settings where antisocial punishment is rare, requiring third-party approval for punishment can reduce both cooperation and group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with peer punishment, introducing a third-party approval step for punishment reduces both cooperation and average earnings compared to direct peer punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that adding procedural hurdles or external approval to punishment mechanisms may decrease the efficiency gains typically associated with peer punishment, especially when antisocial punishment is infrequent. The findings are most directly relevant to adjacent settings (prisoner's dilemma rather than public goods), but the mechanism—peer punishment with and without third-party approval—maps closely to PGG-like environments. The control (baseline) efficiency is higher than in the treatment with third-party approval, indicating a less positive effect of punishment on efficiency when third-party approval is required.
