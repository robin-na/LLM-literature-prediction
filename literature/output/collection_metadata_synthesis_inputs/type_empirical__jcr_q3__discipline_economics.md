- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Kube, S., & Traxler, C. (2011). THE INTERACTION OF LEGAL AND SOCIAL NORM ENFORCEMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares two treatments: BASE (only social punishment) and LAW (social plus mild legal punishment). In LAW, legal sanctions are nondeterrent in isolation but, when combined with social punishment, they increase cooperation (mean contribution rises from 11.8 to 15.1 out of 20) and total efficiency (mean payoff rises from 18.59 to 24.65, a 33% increase). Social punishment is partially crowded out (mean punishment points drop by 57%), but the overall cost of norm enforcement falls and group welfare rises. The marginal incentive to cooperate is higher in LAW due to the additive effect of legal and social sanctions. The results are robust to both between- and within-subject comparisons.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, continuous contributions, and no communication, adding a mild legal punishment mechanism to existing social punishment increases group efficiency substantially. The effect is driven by higher contributions and lower social punishment costs, even though legal sanctions partially substitute for social ones. The design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, etc.) are all clearly specified, making this study highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar game designs.

- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2013). Legitimate punishment, feedback, and the enforcement of cooperation. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (unrestricted punishment, full feedback), (2) Full R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, full feedback), and (3) Partial R. (restricted/legitimate punishment, partial feedback). Restricting punishment to only allow higher contributors to punish lower contributors (legitimate punishment) increases both cooperation and efficiency compared to unrestricted punishment. The highest efficiency and earnings are observed in the Full R. treatment, where both legitimacy and full feedback are present. In the Baseline, a significant share of punishment is antisocial, which reduces efficiency. When feedback about high contributors is removed (Partial R.), the positive effect of legitimate punishment on efficiency and cooperation is greatly reduced. Thus, both legitimacy and feedback are necessary for punishment to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, restricting punishment to legitimate cases (i.e., only higher contributors can punish lower contributors) and providing full feedback about all group members' contributions both increase efficiency and group earnings compared to unrestricted punishment. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling unrestricted punishment may not increase efficiency due to antisocial punishment, but enabling legitimate punishment with full feedback is likely to yield a substantial efficiency gain. The effect is much weaker or absent if feedback about high contributors is not provided. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the structure of punishment and information feedback.

- source: Nikiforakis, N. (2010). Feedback, punishment and cooperation in public good experiments. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard public good game with peer punishment, the format of feedback (contribution feedback, earnings feedback, or both) after each round has a large effect on group efficiency and cooperation. Contribution feedback leads to the highest efficiency and contributions, earnings feedback leads to lower efficiency and contributions, and providing both types of feedback leads to the lowest efficiency due to increased punishment and lack of convergence. The punishment mechanism itself (cost and effectiveness) is held constant across treatments. The results show that the same punishment institution can have very different efficiency outcomes depending on how information is presented to participants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is not only a function of the punishment parameters, but also of the information environment—specifically, the feedback format. For prediction tasks, this means that even with identical game design dimensions and control efficiency, the realized efficiency with punishment can vary dramatically depending on whether feedback is about contributions, earnings, or both. Contribution feedback supports higher efficiency, while earnings feedback undermines it. If both are shown, efficiency can be even lower than with earnings feedback alone. Thus, the 'show_other_summaries' dimension (and its content) is a critical moderator for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Nicklisch, A., & Wolff, I. (2011). COOPERATION NORMS IN MULTIPLE-STAGE PUNISHMENT. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment implements a linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, and multiple endogenous punishment stages per round. Players can punish at a convex cost, and punishment reduces the target's payoff by 10% per point (up to 10 points). Two treatments are compared: BASIC (standard punishment) and OPINION (bystanders can approve/disapprove of punishment, but this has no direct payoff effect). The main findings are: (1) Average payoffs and efficiency remain above the Nash equilibrium and do not differ significantly between treatments or over time; (2) The opportunity for counter-punishment does not lead to a collapse in cooperation or efficiency; (3) Punishment is guided by an absolute full-contribution norm, and both punishers and bystanders apply similar standards; (4) Retaliation occurs but does not undermine overall efficiency; (5) The presence of bystander approval does not materially change efficiency or cooperation patterns. The results suggest that multi-stage punishment with changing groups can sustain cooperation and efficiency, even when counter-punishment is possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a four-player, ten-round linear PGG with continuous contributions, full information, and multi-stage punishment (with convex costs and 10% effectiveness per point), the introduction of punishment sustains or increases efficiency relative to the no-punishment Nash equilibrium. Efficiency remains stable and above the Nash level, and does not collapse even when counter-punishment is possible. The presence of bystander approval (OPINION treatment) does not significantly alter efficiency. For prediction, this suggests that in similar designs (with multi-stage punishment, convex costs, and changing groups), enabling punishment is likely to have a more positive effect on efficiency, and the risk of efficiency collapse due to counter-punishment is low. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design dimensions in this class of games.

- source: Eckel, C. C., Fatas, E., & Wilson, R. (2010). Cooperation and Status in Organizations. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with a star network (one central, three peripheral players), the introduction of costly punishment increases contributions and efficiency only when the central player is low-status (assigned by lowest trivia quiz score). In high-status groups (central player is highest scorer), punishment does not increase contributions or efficiency. Peripheral players are more likely to mimic high-status central players, but high-status central players do not set higher contribution levels. Punishment is used more in low-status groups, and low-status central players are punished more and earn less. Efficiency, measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible, increases with punishment only in low-status groups. The number of rounds does not affect efficiency trends, which remain flat over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by the status of a central player in a star network. If the central player is low-status, enabling punishment increases efficiency; if high-status, punishment has little or no effect on efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, network structure, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) are all clearly specified, making the findings directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar lab settings. The results suggest that, in games with a central leader and status manipulation, the status of the leader is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across treatments, but the efficiency gain from punishment is present only in low-status central player groups.

- source: Tyran, J. R., & Feld, L. P. (2006). Achieving compliance when legal sanctions are non-deterrent. *Scandinavian Journal of Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a one-shot, linear public goods game with groups of three, the authors compare no law, exogenous mild law (non-deterrent sanction), exogenous severe law (deterrent sanction), and endogenous (voted) versions of mild and severe law. Exogenous mild law does not significantly increase efficiency or compliance compared to no law (net efficiency: 33% vs. 30%). Exogenous severe law achieves near-maximal efficiency (89%). When mild law is enacted by group vote, net efficiency rises to 62%, nearly double the exogenous mild law and about three times the no law condition. Endogenous severe law achieves 96% net efficiency. The increase in efficiency under endogenous mild law is not due to selection effects but to the causal effect of the law: voting for mild law signals commitment, raises expectations of cooperation, and activates conditional cooperation. If mild law is rejected, efficiency is even lower than in the no law condition. The results show that the effectiveness of mild punishment depends critically on whether it is self-imposed or externally imposed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends not only on the severity of the sanction but also on whether the punishment regime is endogenously chosen (via group voting) or exogenously imposed. For prediction tasks, the presence of endogenous (voted) mild punishment should be expected to yield much higher efficiency than exogenous mild punishment, even when the punishment is non-deterrent. Severe punishment (deterrent) is highly effective regardless of endogeneity. The game is one-shot, with no communication, group size of 3, and a marginal per-capita return of 0.5. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar lab PGGs with and without punishment, especially when considering the mode of law enactment (voted vs. imposed).

- source: Zheng, X. T., & Nie, P. Y. (2013). Effective Punishment Needs Legitimacy. *Economic Record*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally compares three public goods game treatments: (1) standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM, no punishment), (2) VCM with anonymous punishment (AP), and (3) VCM with public punisher ID to the punished (PPID). Group sizes are 4, 8, and 12. In all treatments, subjects choose continuous contributions from 0 to 10, and in punishment treatments, each subject can punish one other group member per round, with a cost of 1 EMU to reduce the target's payoff by 2 EMUs. Communication is not allowed. Efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is reported for each treatment and group size. Key findings: (a) In small groups (n=4), AP yields the highest efficiency (85.55%), followed by PPID (78.25%) and VCM (76.60%). (b) In larger groups (n=8, 12), PPID yields the highest efficiency (80.60% and 78.05%), followed by AP (76.10% and 61.35%), with VCM lowest (68.60% and 63.30%). (c) In large groups, AP can reduce efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. (d) The number of punishers and the perceived legitimacy of punishment are critical for increasing cooperation and efficiency. (e) Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when it is seen as legitimate (e.g., when multiple group members punish a free-rider, or when punisher identity is known and retaliation is possible).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, conditional on group size and punishment institution. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment is not always positive: in small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency more than public punishment, but in larger groups, public punishment (with punisher ID revealed) is more effective and can prevent the efficiency losses seen with anonymous punishment. The marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all clearly specified, and the control (VCM) efficiency is reported for each group size. The results support the importance of group size, punishment institution (anonymity vs. identifiability), and the legitimacy of punishment as moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency. For prediction, the paper provides a table of efficiency outcomes for each treatment and group size, allowing direct mapping from design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A. (2016). Non-binding agreements in public goods experiments. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that in a 4-player, 10-round linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4), the introduction of a costly peer punishment stage substantially increases group efficiency only when paired with a universal, non-binding agreement requiring full cooperation. In the absence of punishment, agreements requiring only a minimum contribution (second-best) are more effective than full-cooperation agreements, as they are more likely to be formed and kept, leading to higher payoffs. When punishment is available, first-best agreements are more likely to be formed and complied with, and groups achieve payoffs very close to the social optimum. Punishment is used less frequently and more effectively in these settings, with little anti-social punishment. Punishment alone (without agreement) or with second-best agreements increases contributions but not payoffs, as punishment costs offset the gains. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is conditional on the presence of a clear, ambitious agreement.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the presence and type of agreement. In a standard linear PGG (n=4, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.4), adding punishment to a baseline game increases contributions but not payoffs (efficiency), unless a first-best (full cooperation) agreement is also present. In that case, efficiency increases dramatically, approaching the social optimum. Punishment is most effective when it can enforce a clear, ambitious group standard. If only a minimum contribution agreement is present, or if there is no agreement, punishment increases contributions but not efficiency. Thus, the predicted efficiency with punishment should be adjusted upward only if the game design includes both a punishment stage and a clear, universal agreement on full cooperation. Otherwise, the efficiency gain is likely to be small or negligible.

  findings: This study experimentally compares two centralized punishment institutions in a linear public goods game: absolute punishment (all violators punished) and relative punishment (only the worst violators punished). With deterrent sanctions (where the penalty is strong enough), both institutions achieve similar, high efficiency (group profit). With non-deterrent sanctions (penalty not strong enough to make compliance a dominant strategy), the relative punishment institution achieves significantly higher efficiency and contributions than the absolute institution. The theoretically optimal threshold for punishment does not always yield the highest observed profit, suggesting behavioral factors affect outcomes. Overall, relative punishment is at least as effective, and often more so, than absolute punishment for sustaining group efficiency, especially when sanctions are weak.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution matters: relative punishment (punishing only the worst violators) can yield higher efficiency than absolute punishment (punishing all violators) when sanctions are not strong enough to be fully deterrent. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and the punishment is non-deterrent, enabling relative punishment is likely to increase efficiency more than enabling absolute punishment. When sanctions are deterrent, both institutions perform similarly well. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 15 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, and fixed punishment parameters as in the experiment.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment used a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with three players per group, five rounds, and random rematching (perfect stranger design). After each contribution stage, subjects could punish others at randomly assigned costs per unit of punishment. The main findings are: (1) punishment is used even when costly and even when there is no strategic benefit; (2) the amount of punishment is strongly negatively related to its cost (law of demand); (3) punishment is mainly directed at free riders, but a significant amount of punishment is also directed at high contributors by low contributors; (4) higher contributions are observed when punishment is available and especially when it is cheap, but efficiency (group earnings as a percentage of the maximum possible) is actually lower when punishment is cheap, due to the direct costs of punishment.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the cost of punishment. When punishment is cheap, it is used more, leading to higher contributions but lower efficiency due to the costs incurred by both punishers and targets. When punishment is expensive, it is used less, contributions are somewhat lower, but efficiency is higher. For prediction tasks, this means that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are key moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment. The control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the paper's design and results allow inference about the relative efficiency across different punishment cost regimes. The findings suggest that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the net effect depends on the balance between increased cooperation and the direct costs of punishment. Game designs with low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment are likely to see the largest increases in contribution but also the largest efficiency losses.

  findings: Joint decision-making in pairs, where members can communicate, leads to higher and more sustained efficiency (measured by average group payoff/contributions) than individual decision-making in finitely repeated two-player public goods games. This effect is strongest under partner matching (fixed pairs), where efficiency remains high until the end-game.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player finitely repeated public goods games, enabling informal punishment (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases efficiency to a level comparable to that achieved by joint decision-making in pairs with communication. The paper's direct comparison of control (no punishment), punishment, and joint decision-making treatments makes it highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions and control efficiency.
