- source: Zhang, Z. Y., Elvis, N. T., Wang, J. W., & Hou, G. L. (2024). Can Leading by Example Alone Improve Cooperation?. *Behavioral Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that (1) introducing a leader who acts first increases both cooperation and group payoffs compared to a standard PGG; (2) empowering the leader with the ability to punish or reward followers further increases both cooperation and group payoffs, with punishment being more effective than reward in sustaining high cooperation; (3) both leader and follower payoffs are higher in the punishment and reward conditions than in the control or leader-only conditions, though the difference between punishment and reward in final payoffs is not statistically significant; (4) leaders tend to use rewards more frequently than punishments, but punishment is more effective per use. The group average payoff in the punishment and reward conditions is higher than in the control and leader-only conditions, indicating a clear positive effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that, in a 4-player, 32-round linear PGG with a 0.4 MPCR and no communication, enabling leader-administered punishment (with a 1:3 cost/impact ratio) increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to both a control (no leader, no punishment) and a leader-only (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The design matches the prediction task closely, and the reported payoffs allow for direct estimation of efficiency gains from punishment. The evidence is most applicable to settings with similar group size, round count, and punishment technology.

- source: Ye, H., Zhang, Y. Y., & Lee, C. R. (2023). Dual Effects of Cognitive Ability in Public Goods Dilemmas. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper experimentally studies the effects of punishment (none, small, large) on efficiency (collective payoff) in both one-shot and repeated public goods games with groups of four. It finds that: (1) In one-shot PGGs, both small and large punishment increase collective payoff compared to no punishment, with large punishment having a stronger effect. Cognitive ability does not significantly affect efficiency except under large punishment, where high cognitive ability groups achieve higher payoffs. (2) In repeated PGGs, the effect of punishment is more complex: small punishment can reduce efficiency for high cognitive ability groups (who contribute less than in the no-punishment baseline), while large punishment increases efficiency for both high and low cognitive ability groups. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive and depends on both the magnitude of punishment and group cognitive composition.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs depends on the punishment magnitude and the cognitive ability of participants. Small punishment may not always increase efficiency and can even reduce it in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups. Large punishment reliably increases efficiency. The game design dimensions most relevant here are player_count (4), num_rounds (1 for one-shot, 10 for repeated), mpcr (0.5), punishment_cost (institutional, fixed, not paid by peers), and punishment_tech (penalty for under-contribution, with two levels: small and large). There is no chat, no reward, and players receive feedback about others' investments. The findings suggest that, for similar lab PGGs with institutional punishment, enabling large punishment will increase efficiency, but enabling only small punishment may not, especially in repeated settings with high cognitive ability participants.

- source: Joseph, G., Miquel-Florensa, J., Pahuja, S., Hoo, Y. R., & Tebekew, T. (2025). Motivating Public Sector Employees: Public Good Contributions in Addis Ababa Water and Sewerage Authority. *Economic Development and Cultural Change*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compared three public good game variants: (1) standard, (2) threshold (group must reach a minimum contribution or all is lost), and (3) monitor (one randomly chosen, anonymous group member can punish others at a cost). Both the threshold and monitor games increased individual contributions compared to the standard game, with the threshold game increasing contributions the most. However, because about one-quarter of groups failed to meet the threshold and lost all contributions, the monitor game produced the highest average group payoff (efficiency). The punishment mechanism was effective: monitors punished in about 73% of groups, and punishment was targeted at low contributors. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to order effects and individual characteristics. The study provides direct evidence that introducing costly punishment in a one-shot, anonymous, five-player public good game increases group efficiency relative to the control.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a five-player, one-shot public good game with continuous contributions, no communication, and a known MPCR of 0.4. The monitor (punishment) treatment increased group efficiency/payoff compared to the control. The punishment mechanism involved a cost of 1 token to the punisher and a loss of 3 tokens to the punished. The design closely matches standard PGGs, and the efficiency outcome is directly reported. The findings support the prediction that, in similar settings, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control. The paper also provides comparative evidence on a threshold (common goal) mechanism, which increased contributions but did not increase group efficiency due to the risk of losing all contributions if the threshold was not met.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

- source: Botelho, A., Harrison, G. W., Pinto, L. M. C., Ross, D., & Rutström, E. E. (2022). Endogenous choice of institutional punishment mechanisms to promote social cooperation. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study replicates and extends classic public goods experiments by allowing subjects to experience both a punishment-enabled and a no-punishment institution, then vote on which to use for a high-stakes final round. Across 13 sessions, punishment increased contributions but almost always reduced average profits due to the direct costs of punishing. Only one session (out of 13) saw a majority vote for the punishment institution, and this was in a small cohort with high returns and high initial cooperation. Statistical analysis shows that subjects' votes are overwhelmingly determined by which institution gave them higher profits, not by which supported higher cooperation. The negative effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to variations in matching protocol, order of experience, and marginal per-capita return. The paper also reviews prior studies and finds similar patterns: punishment often fails to improve, and sometimes reduces, group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard public goods games with costly voluntary punishment, enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, even though it increases contributions. The effect is robust across a range of game parameters (player count, MPCR, matching protocol, order, and returns). Exceptions are rare and occur mainly when punishment is used very sparingly and cooperation is already high. For prediction tasks, the presence of costly punishment should generally be expected to lower efficiency relative to control, unless the design or context strongly favors efficient, low-use punishment. The paper also highlights that the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) and matching protocol (reputation effects) can moderate the effect, but the main driver is the costliness and frequency of punishment.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Duell, D., Mengel, F., Mohlin, E., & Weidenholzer, S. (2024). Cooperation through collective punishment and participation. *Political Science Research and Methods*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring (noisy signals about individual contributions, but perfect information about total group contribution), the authors experimentally compare five regimes: no punishment, peer-to-peer punishment, collective punishment, peer-to-peer punishment with commitment, and collective punishment with commitment. The key finding is that only collective punishment with commitment (where group members can opt in to a regime that automatically punishes the whole group if total contributions fall short) leads to significantly higher group payoffs and cooperation rates compared to both no punishment and peer-to-peer punishment. Peer-to-peer punishment does not improve payoffs or cooperation relative to no punishment in this noisy environment. The positive effect of collective punishment with commitment is especially pronounced in the later rounds, suggesting a learning or coordination effect. The study also shows that the ability to participate in the decision to implement collective punishment (i.e., voting/commitment) is crucial for its effectiveness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling collective punishment with commitment in a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling collective punishment with commitment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff) substantially, especially over time, provided the punishment is collective, pre-committed, and participation in the institution is possible. The effect is not observed for peer-to-peer punishment or for collective punishment without commitment. The experimental design and reporting allow for direct mapping to prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, given the game design dimensions.

- source: Prétôt, L., Taylor, Q., & Mcauliffe, K. (2024). Children cooperate more with in-group members than with out-group members in an iterated face-to-face Prisoner's Dilemma Game. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (CPR settings), cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained if and only if the group collectively invests a sufficient fraction of its resources into monitoring and sanctioning rule violations. When monitoring is funded unilaterally (peer punishment), self-interested agents will not invest enough, leading to a second-order free-rider problem and low efficiency. When monitoring is funded collectively from the common-pool resource (institutional punishment), there exists a threshold investment (beta) above which full cooperation and high efficiency are stable equilibria. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum investment required and shows that the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment technology), the cost of monitoring, and the severity of sanctions all moderate the efficiency gains from punishment. The model also predicts that initial 'free' monitoring (by some agents ignoring costs) may be needed to escape the no-cooperation equilibrium, but once cooperation is established, monitoring can be fully incentivized and efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on game design dimensions. It shows that the presence and structure of punishment (especially whether it is institutionally organized and collectively funded) are critical moderators of efficiency. The explicit inequalities derived in the paper allow one to predict whether enabling punishment will increase efficiency, given the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the fraction of resources invested in monitoring. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The results are most directly applicable to settings where monitoring and sanctioning are collective, and less so to purely peer-based punishment. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Chen, J. C., D'Attoma, J., Fonseca, M. A., & Malezieux, A. (2025). Promoting Cooperation in an Unequal World: Experimental Evidence on the Role of Transparency and Punishment. *Journal of Business Ethics*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across two lab experiments using a four-player linear public goods game with unequal endowments (determined by merit and luck), the paper finds that: (1) Peer punishment increases contributions and group payoffs, but only when endowments are transparent. (2) When endowments are secret, punishment has a limited effect on cooperation and does not reduce inequality; advantaged participants contribute a lower fraction of their endowment and earn much higher payoffs. (3) When endowments are made public, punishment becomes much more effective at increasing contributions and reducing inequality, especially among the advantaged. (4) Willingness to pay for secrecy is higher among advantaged participants, especially when their advantage is due to luck.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on whether endowments (wealth) are transparent. In games with secret endowments, punishment has little effect on group payoff or efficiency; in games with public endowments, punishment significantly increases contributions and group payoffs, especially among the advantaged. For prediction, the presence of transparency (showing endowments or relevant summaries) is a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when transparency is high. The game uses continuous contributions, no chat, and a fixed MPCR of 0.4. Punishment is costly (cost 1, impact 3), and there is no reward option. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar design features.
