- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Villatoro, D., Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Nardin, L. G., Sabater-Mir, J., & Conte, R. (2014). The Norm-Signaling Effects of Group Punishment: Combining Agent-Based Simulation and Laboratory Experiments. *Social Science Computer Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when punishment is inflicted by the entire group, human subjects are more likely to cooperate (i.e., contribute to the public good) than when punishment is uncoordinated, even though the material cost to defectors is held constant. The authors attribute this to the norm-signaling function of group punishment, which is hypothesized to activate both instrumental and normative motivations for compliance. Agent-based simulations support the idea that norm salience, as modeled in the EMIL-I-A architecture, can explain the observed human behavior. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, so the findings are limited to behavioral outcomes (contribution rates).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of punishment (specifically, whether it is coordinated by the group or uncoordinated) can significantly affect cooperation rates in public goods games, even when the material incentives are held constant. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the paper suggests that group punishment may lead to higher efficiency than uncoordinated punishment, but this is inferred from contribution rates rather than directly measured. The absence of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency gains from punishment. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of group punishment structure and norm-signaling on cooperation, which may be relevant for predicting efficiency when combined with other sources.

- source: Ren, T. Y., & Zheng, J. J. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics in the spatial public goods game with tolerance-based expulsion and cooperation. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that in a standard linear public goods game (VCM) with four players and 20 rounds per part, groups that start with unequal endowments (two rich, two poor) and then experience redistribution to equality do not increase their group contributions or efficiency after redistribution. Group contributions in the post-redistribution phase are indistinguishable from those in groups that remain unequal, and both are significantly lower than in always-equal groups. The experience of initial inequality creates a persistent negative spillover, locking groups into low cooperation and efficiency even after equality is restored. The mechanism is traced to the former poor mimicking the low-contribution behavior of the former rich after redistribution. No punishment or reward mechanisms are present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or reward mechanisms, simply reducing inequality via redistribution does not increase efficiency in public goods games if groups have previously experienced inequality. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, for games with similar design (no punishment, no reward, linear PGG, 4 players, 20+20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, no identification, no summary of individual contributions), the efficiency in the treatment with redistribution will not exceed that of the control with persistent inequality, and both will be lower than in always-equal groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGGs without punishment or reward, but do not inform about the effect of punishment.

- source: Drouvelis, M., Metcalfe, R., & Powdthavee, N. (2015). Can priming cooperation increase public good contributions?. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that priming participants with cooperative concepts (via a word search) leads to significantly higher contributions in a one-shot public goods game compared to a neutrally primed control group. The effect is robust to controls for gender, nationality, and field of study, and does not appear to be mediated by beliefs about others' contributions. The main outcome is contribution level; average earnings are reported but not analyzed in detail. No punishment, reward, or sanction mechanisms are present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism. Its findings are not directly transferable to the prediction of efficiency with or without punishment. However, it does provide evidence that subtle environmental interventions (like priming) can increase contributions in a standard linear PGG, which may be contextually relevant for understanding other non-incentive-based interventions. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not informative about punishment effects.

- source: Martinsson, P., Villegas-Palacio, C., & Wollbrant, C. (2015). Cooperation and social classes: evidence from Colombia. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that both high and medium-low social class groups contribute about 40% of their endowment in a one-shot public goods game, with no significant difference in unconditional contributions or beliefs about others' contributions. The distribution of contributor types (conditional cooperators, free riders, etc.) is also similar between groups. However, medium-low social class members are more likely to contribute when others contribute nothing (i.e., higher baseline conditional cooperation). The study does not include punishment, reward, or repeated interaction, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide a clear description of a standard one-shot linear public goods game with four players, continuous contributions, and MPCR of 0.4, which may be useful as a control or baseline for comparison with studies that do include punishment. The findings on contribution behavior by social class may inform expectations about baseline cooperation in similar environments, but cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Hashim, M. J., Kannan, K. N., Maximiano, S., & Ulmer, J. R. (2014). Digital Piracy, Teens, and the Source of Advice: An Experimental Study. *Journal of Management Information Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Advice from parents, especially when they are punished for their child's piracy, increases purchasing and group profit and reduces piracy compared to other advice sources. Advice from record labels is the least effective and can even reduce group profit. Advice from a regulator is largely ignored. The effect of advice is strongest when the adviser has both a social tie and a stake in the outcome. The study does not include standard peer punishment or reward mechanisms; the only 'punishment' is an exogenous penalty to parents in one treatment. The game is a close variant of a public goods game, but not a standard PGG. Efficiency-related outcomes (group profit, purchasing) are reported, but not as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that exogenous punishment of a third party (parents) for a player's free-riding can increase public good provision and group profit, especially when combined with a social tie. However, it does not test endogenous peer punishment or standard PGG punishment institutions. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms may be more effective when they are socially salient and affect someone with a close tie to the player, but direct transfer to standard PGG with peer punishment is limited. Control efficiency (group profit without punishment) is reported, and the effect of introducing a punishment-like mechanism (parent penalty) can be inferred, but the mechanism differs from standard punishment. The absence of peer punishment, reward, or communication means the results are only adjacent for direct prediction of PGG punishment effects.

- source: Xu, Z. J., Li, R. Y., & Zhang, L. Z. (2019). The role of memory in human strategy updating in optional public goods game. *Chaos*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that in a spatial optional public goods game, human players update their strategies primarily based on their own past experiences (memory), not by imitating neighbors. The probability of switching to a strategy is higher if it performed well for the individual in the past, regardless of its performance among neighbors. Imitation and mutation dynamics do not adequately explain observed behavior. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, and does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it analyze efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome. Its main contribution is to the understanding of human strategy updating (memory vs. imitation) in repeated spatial PGGs with voluntary participation. It is not informative for the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects.

- source: Sun, K. T., Liu, Y., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2022). Evolution of trust in a hierarchical population with punishing investors. *Chaos Solitons & Fractals*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment finds that exogenously imposed collective punishment in a binary trust game significantly increases trustworthiness (from 38.5% to 82.3%) and somewhat increases trust (from 28.1% to 39.6%). When the adoption of the punishment mechanism is determined by majority vote, most subjects vote against it, and the endogenous adoption does not further increase trust or trustworthiness compared to exogenous imposition. Cognitive ability predicts voting for punishment. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff outcomes, and the main focus is on behavioral measures (trust, trustworthiness, voting).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that collective punishment can increase trustworthiness and trust in a binary trust game, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The design is adjacent to public goods games, as it is a binary trust game rather than a multi-player PGG. The punishment mechanism is collective and endogenous in some treatments, but the main outcomes are behavioral. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides indirect evidence that punishment can improve cooperative behavior, but does not allow direct inference about efficiency effects. The absence of efficiency or payoff data limits its direct utility for the prediction task.

- source: Sun, C. B., & Luo, C. (2020). Co-evolution of influence-based preferential selection and limited resource with multi-games on interdependent networks. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that female water point leaders (WPLs) in rural Namibia are younger and better educated than male WPLs, but are often related to traditional elites. In a trust game with third-party punishment, female WPLs punish less frequently (not statistically significant) and significantly less intensively than male WPLs. However, these differences in punishment behavior do not translate into differences in villager satisfaction with leaders. The experiment does not report on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence on gender differences in third-party punishment in a trust game, which is adjacent to public goods games. It shows that female leaders punish less, but this does not affect satisfaction. However, because the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The design and findings may be useful for understanding how leader characteristics affect norm enforcement, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Bolton, G., Greiner, B., & Ockenfels, A. (2018). Dispute Resolution or Escalation? The Strategic Gaming of Feedback Withdrawal Options in Online Markets. *Management Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In laboratory markets with two-sided moral hazard (both buyer and seller can defect), the introduction of a mutual feedback withdrawal option leads to more strategic negative feedback, less honest feedback, and lower efficiency (measured as realized gains from trade divided by maximum possible gains). The feedback system becomes less informative, and trust/trustworthiness are reduced. In one-sided markets (only seller can defect), the feedback withdrawal option does not significantly affect efficiency or the informativeness of feedback. The negative effect of FBW on efficiency is thus conditional on the presence of two-sided moral hazard and mutual withdrawal. The paper also finds that some buyers engage in 'altruistic punishment' by refusing to withdraw negative feedback even at a cost to themselves, partially mitigating the detrimental effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments structurally similar to public goods games with two-sided moral hazard and mutual punishment/retaliation options, introducing a mutual feedback withdrawal (punishment-like) mechanism reduces efficiency relative to a control without such a mechanism. The effect is not observed in one-sided environments. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of mutual, bilateral punishment/retaliation options in two-sided games is likely to reduce efficiency, even if the control game is already inefficient. The effect is mediated by strategic retaliation and the breakdown of reputation as an incentive for cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar two-sided, bilateral punishment/retaliation structures.

- source: Oberholzer-Gee, F., Waldfogel, J., & White, M. W. (2010). FRIEND OR FOE? COOPERATION AND LEARNING IN HIGH-STAKES GAMES. *Review of Economics and Statistics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study documents that, over two seasons of the game show, players shift from unconditional to conditional cooperation based on observable opponent characteristics (e.g., gender, race, age). In the first season, cooperation rates are unrelated to opponent attributes; in the second season, players are more likely to defect (choose 'foe') against opponents whose attributes predict less cooperation. This learning process leads to a substantial decline in overall efficiency: the average share of stakes converted to winnings drops from 39% in season 1 to 30% in season 2. The decline is especially pronounced for players with attributes associated with low cooperation (e.g., men, black players, younger players), whose average efficiency falls sharply. In contrast, players with more cooperative-associated attributes (e.g., women, white players) maintain their efficiency. The findings suggest that conditional cooperation, when based on imperfect signals, can reduce group efficiency in one-shot, high-stakes settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, simultaneous-move games structurally similar to the prisoner's dilemma, the emergence of conditional cooperation based on observable attributes can reduce overall efficiency, especially when players use imperfect signals to predict others' behavior. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling mechanisms that allow players to condition on opponent characteristics (e.g., through history, reputation, or observable traits) may decrease efficiency relative to baseline, particularly if the environment is not anonymous and players can stereotype. The findings are most relevant for games with binary (all-or-nothing) choices, no explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, and high stakes. The results caution that learning and conditional strategies do not always improve efficiency and may exacerbate payoff inequality.

- source: Chen, M. K., & Hauser, M. (2005). Modeling reciprocation and cooperation in primates: evidence for a punishing strategy. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that tamarins' cooperative behavior in repeated food-exchange games is best described by 'punish-2' and 'punish-3' strategies, which are more punishing than standard tit-for-tat. These strategies require two or more consecutive cooperative acts to restart cooperation after a breakdown, leading to stable but sometimes brittle cooperation. The analysis uses maximum-likelihood fitting of a suite of behavioral models, showing that these punishing strategies account for over 90% of the posterior model weight. The paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, and all results are based on behavioral patterns (pulling rates and model fits).
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes. However, it does provide indirect evidence that more punishing strategies (requiring multiple acts of cooperation to restore cooperation) can stabilize cooperation in repeated dyadic interactions. The findings may inform mechanistic expectations about how punishment-like strategies affect cooperation dynamics, but cannot be used to quantitatively predict efficiency changes due to punishment in PGGs. The game is not a standard PGG, and the outcomes are not payoff-based.
