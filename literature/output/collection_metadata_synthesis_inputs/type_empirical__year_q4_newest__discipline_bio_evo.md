- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Nhim, T., Schuch, E., & Richter, A. (2023). Water scarcity and support for costly institutions in public goods: Experimental evidence from Cambodia. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that costly institutions (minimum contribution/tax and strict enforcement with punishment) are broadly supported by participants, especially those with prior experience of water scarcity. Both types of costly institutions increase average contributions to the public good compared to costless regimes. However, only the minimum contribution (tax) regime leads to higher payoffs and welfare, while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but does not improve payoffs due to the cost of enforcement. The efficiency of the group (measured as payoff relative to the social optimum) is thus higher under the tax regime but lower under strict enforcement compared to their respective costless counterparts.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing costly punishment or enforcement institutions in public goods games depends on the specific design. Minimum contribution (tax) regimes can increase both contributions and efficiency (payoff), while strict enforcement with costly punishment increases contributions but can reduce efficiency due to the cost of enforcement. For prediction, the presence of a costly punishment mechanism does not guarantee higher efficiency; the cost and structure of the institution are critical. Control efficiency (payoff) and the specific institutional design (tax/minimum vs. costly strict punishment) must be considered. The findings are highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially regarding the cost and type of punishment or enforcement.

- source: Del Ponte, A., Masiliunas, A., & Lim, N. (2025). Decentralized voluntary agreements do not reduce emissions in a climate change experiment. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment with a public bads game (3 players, 18 rounds), the introduction of voluntary pledge-and-review agreements, automatic financial penalties for exceeding pledges, and/or peer evaluation (reputation contest) did not reduce average production or disaster risk compared to baseline. Most participants joined agreements and complied with their pledges, but pledges were not ambitious enough to reduce emissions. Financial penalties increased compliance but did not improve group efficiency, as participants could avoid penalties by not joining or by making unambitious pledges. Peer evaluation did not reduce production and even reduced the ambition of pledges. The results suggest that, in decentralized and voluntary settings, punishment mechanisms do not improve efficiency unless participation and ambition can be enforced.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a voluntary, decentralized public bads game with 3 players and 18 rounds, adding automatic financial penalties for exceeding pledges does not improve group efficiency or reduce emissions compared to baseline, because participants can avoid penalties by not joining or by making unambitious pledges. Peer evaluation (reputation-based non-monetary punishment) also does not improve efficiency and may reduce ambition. For prediction, this suggests that in similar voluntary, opt-in, pledge-based games, the presence of punishment mechanisms (even if automatic and with high penalty rates) will not increase efficiency unless there are mechanisms to ensure ambitious pledges and broad participation. Control efficiency is a good predictor of treatment efficiency when punishment is voluntary and avoidable.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

- source: Grimalda, G., Belianin, A., Hennig-Schmidt, H., Requate, T., & Ryzhkova, M. (2022). Sanctions and international interaction improve cooperation to avert climate change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment tested the effect of sanctions (costly punishment) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma with German and Russian participants, both in national and international groups. Sanctions increased group cooperation (as measured by the probability of loss avoidance, PLA), especially for Russian participants and in international groups. However, the cost of sanctioning meant that final payoffs were actually higher in the no-sanction treatments, as shown in the supplementary material. The effect of sanctions was positive for increasing cooperation and loss avoidance, but negative for monetary efficiency. The combination of international interaction and sanctions was particularly effective at raising cooperation among low cooperators (Russians), leading to convergence with high cooperators (Germans). The effect of revealing nationality was minimal. Sanctioning was mostly prosocial in international groups, and antisocial sanctioning (punishing high contributors) was reduced. Only a small fraction of the endowment was spent on sanctions, but this was sufficient to increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment (sanctions) in a repeated collective-risk social dilemma increases group cooperation (PLA) but reduces final payoffs due to the cost of sanctioning. For prediction tasks, this means that the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game may be higher or lower than the control, depending on whether the benefit from increased loss avoidance outweighs the cost of sanctions. In this experiment, the cost of sanctions was high enough that final payoffs were higher in the no-sanction condition, even though cooperation (PLA) was higher with sanctions. The effect is especially pronounced for groups with low baseline cooperation, and in international groups. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and cost of punishment, group composition (nationality/culture), and the structure of the risk/loss. The game is a close variant of a PGG, but with a threshold and probabilistic loss, so direct transfer to standard PGGs should be cautious.

- source: Wang, X. M., Couto, M. C., Wang, N. Y., An, X. M., Chen, B., Dong, Y. L., Hilbe, C., & Zhang, B. Y. (2023). Cooperation and coordination in heterogeneous populations. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endowment inequality is a significant obstacle to successful coordination in threshold public goods games, reducing the proportion of groups that reach the threshold and thus receive the group reward. Productivity inequality does not have a similar negative effect. Participants tend to coordinate on equal relative contributions, even when this is not payoff-maximizing. The study does not include punishment or reward mechanisms, so it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or cooperation. The findings highlight the importance of focal points and fairness heuristics in equilibrium selection, and show that evolutionary game theory models may mis-predict outcomes unless they account for participants' initial expectations and heuristics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design. However, it provides informative context on how inequality (in endowment or productivity) affects coordination and group success in threshold public goods games. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it suggests that baseline efficiency (without punishment) is lower under endowment inequality. This could be relevant for understanding the baseline to which punishment effects would be compared in similar asymmetric games.

- source: Batistoni, T., Barclay, P., & Raihani, N. J. (2022). Third-party punishers do not compete to be chosen as partners in an experimental game. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment and helping are both used as signals of cooperative intent, but helping is a stronger and less ambiguous signal than punishment. Observability increases investments in both, but the possibility of partner choice does not further escalate investments. Higher investments in punishment or helping increase the likelihood of being chosen as a partner and being trusted, but this effect is much stronger for helping. Investments in both are positively associated with actual trustworthiness in a Trust Game. The study does not address group efficiency or total payoff in a public goods game, but provides evidence on the reputational and signaling functions of punishment and helping.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on group efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is adjacent to PGGs, using a Dictator Game with third-party punishment/helping and a subsequent Trust Game. The main outcomes are individual-level signaling, partner choice, and trust/trustworthiness, not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that punishment is a weaker and more ambiguous signal of cooperative intent than helping, and that reputational incentives may increase costly punishment/helping, but do not escalate under partner choice. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in PGGs, this paper provides only indirect, contextual evidence about the social and reputational motivations for punishment, not about its effect on group payoff or efficiency.

- source: Li, S. Y., Ma, S. M., Wang, D. Y., Zhang, H. J., Li, Y. Z., Wang, J. X., Li, J. Y., Zhang, B. Y., Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., Wang, W. X., & Ma, Y. N. (2022). Oxytocin and the Punitive Hub-Dynamic Spread of Cooperation in Human Social Networks. *Journal of Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in repeated networked games (trust game, ultimatum bargaining, and two-stage prisoner's dilemma with punishment), oxytocin administration to central (hub) players increases their willingness to enforce cooperation norms via costly punishment. This enforcement, rather than mere cooperation or trust, is what enables cooperation to spread throughout the network. Agent-based simulations confirm that high punishment rates by central nodes are more effective than high cooperation rates alone in promoting network-wide cooperation. Manipulating peripheral nodes' punishment or cooperation has much weaker effects. The main outcomes are cooperation and punishment rates, not group efficiency or total payoff, though some payoff equality measures are reported in the ultimatum game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and effectiveness of costly punishment, especially when wielded by central/influential players, is a key moderator of cooperation in repeated networked social dilemmas. However, the main outcomes are behavioral (cooperation, punishment, norm enforcement), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings suggest that, in networked repeated games with punishment, enabling effective punishment by central players can substantially increase cooperation rates, and by extension, may improve efficiency, but this is not directly measured. The results are most relevant for predicting behavioral outcomes (cooperation, norm enforcement) rather than efficiency as defined by group payoff relative to full cooperation. The design dimensions most strongly supported are network structure (heterogeneity, centrality), punishment existence and effectiveness, and repeated interaction. The study does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency change due to punishment, so its use for predicting efficiency outcomes is indirect and should be supplemented with studies that report group payoff or efficiency directly.

For the downstream prediction task, this paper is only indirectly relevant: it suggests that the psychological basis for punishment in humans includes sensitivity to intent, which may affect when and how punishment is used, but does not provide quantitative or qualitative evidence about efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not address how punishment affects group payoff or efficiency.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.

  findings: Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.
