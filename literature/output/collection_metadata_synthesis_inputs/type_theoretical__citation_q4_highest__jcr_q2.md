  findings: The paper finds that both decentralized punishment and reward can drive a networked public goods game to high cooperation, but the minimal cumulative cost required depends on the initial cooperation level. If the initial fraction of cooperators is low, reward is more cost-effective; if it is high, punishment is more cost-effective. The study provides analytical and simulation results for the optimal incentive protocols and the conditions under which full cooperation is stable. The main outcome is the cumulative cost of incentives needed to reach a target cooperation level, not group efficiency or total payoff per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in networked public goods games. While it does not report group efficiency directly, it analyzes the cost (incentives spent) required to achieve high cooperation, which is closely related to efficiency when incentive costs are subtracted from group payoffs. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment (in terms of cost to achieve cooperation) depends on initial conditions and the structure of the game (network, group size, synergy factor). For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is not always the most efficient intervention; its relative efficiency compared to reward depends on the starting state of the group. The detailed modeling of incentive cost and cooperation dynamics can inform parameterizations of efficiency in similar networked PGG environments.

  findings: The paper finds that probabilistic sharing of punishment duties in a public goods game can solve the problem of costly punishment and promote cooperation, even when punishment is expensive. In well-mixed populations, probabilistic sanctioning transforms the game into a coordination game with two stable equilibria (full cooperation and full defection). In structured populations, sharing punishment duties leads to high cooperation rates, but only if the severity and frequency of punishment are carefully balanced; both too little and too much punishment can reduce cooperation. The main reported outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. Theoretical payoff expressions are provided, and the analysis explains why intermediate punishment is optimal for cooperation, but efficiency is not directly measured or reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the way punishment is implemented (shared probabilistically or periodically) can have a major impact on cooperation rates in public goods games. While the main outcome is cooperation rate, not efficiency, the derived payoff expressions and analysis of invasion dynamics suggest that intermediate levels of punishment severity and frequency can maximize group welfare by supporting high cooperation at lower cost. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the structure of the population and the sharing of punishment duties, are important moderators of the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, because efficiency is not directly reported, quantitative predictions about efficiency must be inferred indirectly from the cooperation rates and payoff formulas.

- source: Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2012). Self-organization of punishment in structured populations. *New Journal of Physics*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Adaptive punishment in spatial public goods games enables the spontaneous emergence of rare, localized punishers who respond to the spread of defection. This mechanism maintains smooth interfaces between cooperators and defectors, invigorates spatial reciprocity, and prevents the emergence of cyclic dominance that would otherwise allow defectors to persist. As a result, adaptive punishment can achieve full cooperation (pure C phase) in parameter regions where, without punishment, only defectors would survive. The cost of punishment, when adaptively deployed, is globally negligible, making the mechanism highly efficient. Compared to steady (non-adaptive) punishment, adaptive punishment is more effective at eliminating defectors and does so with lower cost.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that adaptive, context-sensitive punishment mechanisms in spatially structured public goods games can substantially increase the likelihood of full cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency, even when punishment is costly. The effect is especially pronounced when punishment is inexpensive, but remains positive even for costly punishment. The findings suggest that models or interventions that allow for adaptive, rather than fixed, punishment are likely to yield higher efficiency than those with no punishment or with steady punishment. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, so predictions should be based on the qualitative pattern that adaptive punishment expands the parameter space for high efficiency.

- source: Helbing, D., Szolnoki, A., Perc, M., & Szabó, G. (2010). Punish, but not too hard: how costly punishment spreads in the spatial public goods game. *New Journal of Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in spatial public goods games, the introduction of punishing cooperators (PC) or punishing defectors (PD) can promote the spread of cooperation, but through different mechanisms. For punishing cooperators, increasing the punishment fine monotonically increases the cooperation level up to a threshold, after which non-punishing cooperators and defectors are eliminated. For punishing defectors, there is a non-monotonic (reentrant) effect: increasing the fine initially increases cooperation, but too high a fine reduces the fraction of punishing defectors, causing cooperation to collapse and the system to revert to defection. The results are presented as phase diagrams and time-evolution of strategy fractions, not as direct efficiency or group payoff measures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence and type of punishment mechanism, as well as the punishment cost and fine, can have large effects on the prevalence of cooperation in spatial public goods games. However, because the outcomes are reported as fractions of strategies and cooperation level, not as group efficiency or total payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency. The results suggest that enabling punishment (especially by punishing cooperators) can increase cooperation and likely efficiency, but the relationship is mediated by the spatial structure and the specific punishment parameters. The non-monotonic effect for punishing defectors highlights the importance of tuning punishment severity. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment can improve efficiency in structured populations, but the mapping to efficiency must be inferred from cooperation levels.

  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, adaptive punishment is more effective than adaptive rewarding at achieving maximal efficiency (defector-free states), given the same cost-to-impact ratio.
