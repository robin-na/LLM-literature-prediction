- source: Sääksvuori, L., Mappes, T., & Puurtinen, M. (2011). Costly punishment prevails in intergroup conflict. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In isolated public goods games, costly punishment increases cooperation but does not significantly increase net pay-off (efficiency), as the costs of punishment offset the gains from higher contributions. When intergroup competition is introduced, groups with punishment opportunities achieve much higher contributions and net pay-offs, both in asymmetric (one group can punish, one cannot) and symmetric (both can punish) competition. In asymmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is dramatic: even the lowest-earning individual in the punishment group outperforms the highest in the no-punishment group. In symmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is positive but smaller and only marginally significant. Punishment also reduces within-group inequality in pay-offs. The paper concludes that intergroup competition is a key moderator of the efficiency effects of costly punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether there is intergroup competition. In the absence of group competition, enabling punishment does not reliably increase efficiency. With group competition, punishment can substantially increase efficiency, especially when only some groups have access to punishment. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of group competition, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (8), number of rounds (30), and the absence of communication or reputation mechanisms. The control efficiency (no punishment) is much lower than the treatment efficiency (with punishment) in the presence of group competition, but not in isolated groups. This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGGs with and without punishment, especially when intergroup competition is a design feature.

- source: O'Gorman, R., Henrich, J., & Van Vugt, M. (2009). Constraining free riding in public goods games: designated solitary punishers can sustain human cooperation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compared three conditions in a repeated public goods game: (1) no punishment (control), (2) all group members can punish (all-punishment), and (3) only one randomly selected group member can punish (one-punishment). Both punishment conditions led to significantly higher and more stable contributions than the control. Profits (efficiency) were higher in the one-punishment condition than in the all-punishment condition, and both were more stable than the control. The cost of punishment reduced profits relative to the control in the short run, but the control condition's profits declined over rounds due to decaying cooperation, while punishment conditions maintained stable profits. The one-punisher regime avoids redundant punishment costs, making it more efficient. The punishment mechanism used a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio. The study provides direct evidence that both diffuse and designated punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency, but a single punisher can do so more efficiently.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, six-round public goods game with a 1:3 punishment cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment increases efficiency (group profit) relative to a no-punishment control. Furthermore, a single designated punisher is as effective as allowing all members to punish in sustaining cooperation, but is more efficient due to lower aggregate punishment costs. For prediction, this suggests that, under similar game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the structure of the punishment regime (single vs. diffuse) can moderate the efficiency gains. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Traulsen, A., Röhl, T., & Milinski, M. (2012). An economic experiment reveals that humans prefer pool punishment to maintain the commons. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with five players per group, the introduction of pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) increases cooperation and reduces defection, but leads to a significant loss in efficiency (average payoff per round drops by about a third). Peer punishment alone does not significantly affect efficiency, as punishment is rarely used when cooperation is high. When both peer and pool punishment are available, pool punishment dominates in the presence of second-order punishment, again with a substantial efficiency loss. The results support the theoretical prediction that pool punishment can stabilize cooperation but at the cost of group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard public goods game with five players, 25 rounds, no communication, binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, and known punishment costs and effectiveness, enabling pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) reduces efficiency compared to the control (no or only peer punishment). The efficiency loss is substantial (about one third) when pool punishment is used with second-order punishment. Peer punishment alone does not significantly reduce efficiency. Thus, for prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment mechanism (peer vs pool), and whether second-order punishment is implemented, are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. The specific parameterization (punishment cost, punishment impact, MPCR) is also informative for quantitative prediction.

- source: Egas, M., & Riedl, A. (2008). The economics of altruistic punishment and the maintenance of cooperation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that punishment only maintains cooperation when it is relatively effective (low cost to punisher, high impact on punished). However, even when cooperation is sustained, group and individual payoffs are lower than in the control without punishment, because the costs of punishment exceed the benefits from increased cooperation. The threshold of free-riding that goes unpunished increases as punishment becomes less effective. The main determinant of punishment's effect is the cost-to-impact ratio. The results suggest that altruistic punishment alone is insufficient to maintain cooperation efficiently, as it reduces group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard PGG with anonymous, one-shot (perfect stranger) interactions, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency and can reduce it, especially when punishment is effective (low cost, high impact). The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost-to-impact ratio: only when punishment is cheap and effective does it sustain cooperation, but even then, group payoffs are lower than in the no-punishment control. For prediction, this suggests that unless punishment is both cheap and highly effective, enabling punishment will not improve and may reduce efficiency relative to control. The control efficiency (without punishment) is always higher than with punishment in this design. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions, especially punishment cost and effectiveness.

- source: Gächter, S., Herrmann, B., & Thöni, C. (2010). Culture and cooperation. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a standard public goods game, the opportunity to punish increases average contributions in some cultures but not in others. Cultural background explains a much larger share of the variance in contributions when punishment is available than when it is not. Punishment patterns themselves (including antisocial punishment) differ strongly across cultures and are linked to differences in cooperation. The presence of punishment reduces within-group variance in contributions but has mixed effects on between-group variance. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but higher contributions in the punishment condition in some cultures suggest likely higher efficiency in those cases, while in other cultures the effect is negligible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation (and by implication, efficiency) is highly culture-dependent, even in otherwise identical public goods game designs. For prediction tasks, this means that game design dimensions alone (such as player count, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are not sufficient to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency without accounting for cultural context. The paper's results suggest that, in some environments, punishment can substantially increase cooperation (and likely efficiency), while in others it has little or no effect. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its quantitative value for efficiency prediction is limited to inferring that higher contributions generally imply higher efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., & Herrmann, B. (2009). Reciprocity, culture and human cooperation: previous insights and a new cross-cultural experiment. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in one-shot public goods games, the introduction of punishment opportunities increases contributions in Swiss participant pools but not in Russian pools. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is much more common in Russia, leading to lower earnings and less effective cooperation. The paper reports that 80% of Russian participants earned less than the selfish prediction, compared to 33% in Switzerland. However, the study does not directly report group efficiency (payoff relative to the cooperative maximum) or compare average payoffs between punishment and no-punishment conditions. The findings highlight that the effect of punishment on cooperation and earnings is strongly moderated by cultural context, with punishment sometimes failing to improve, or even reducing, group welfare due to antisocial punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on cooperation and earnings in public goods games is highly context-dependent, especially on cultural background. While the design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) are well specified, the key moderator in this study is culture, which is not a standard game design variable. The paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency (payoff relative to maximum) from control efficiency and design dimensions, but it does show that punishment can increase, decrease, or have no effect on group earnings depending on the prevalence of antisocial punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of antisocial punishment (which may be proxied by cultural or social variables) is a critical moderator, and that design dimensions alone may be insufficient to predict efficiency effects of punishment without accounting for such context.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Herrmann, B., & Gamella, J. F. (2012). Patient and impatient punishers of free-riders. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that punishment in a one-shot public goods game is implemented by two distinct types of individuals: patient cooperators (who punish free-riders, likely for moralistic/future-oriented reasons) and impatient free-riders (who punish other free-riders, likely for competitive/spiteful reasons). The likelihood of punishing is positively associated with cooperation among patient individuals and with free-riding among impatient individuals. The paper does not analyze or report the effect of punishment on group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides no direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. It does, however, offer insight into the psychological heterogeneity of punishers, which may be relevant for understanding the mechanisms behind punishment behavior. For the downstream prediction task, this paper does not provide usable data on efficiency outcomes or how game design dimensions affect efficiency with or without punishment. Its main value is in highlighting that the motivations for punishment can differ (moralistic vs. competitive) depending on individual patience and contribution behavior, but this is not tied to efficiency outcomes.

- source: Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2004). Strategic investment in reputation. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that the possibility of reputation transfer between a public goods game and an indirect reciprocity game leads to higher cooperation and significantly higher average payoffs in the public goods game. When players' identities are consistent across both games (transferable name), they invest more in cooperation, resulting in higher group earnings. When identities are not transferable, cooperation and payoffs decline. The manipulation of reputation transferability is the key treatment; no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. The findings show that reputation incentives can substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling reputation transfer between a public goods game and another social context (indirect reciprocity game) increases efficiency, as measured by average payoff per player. While the study does not manipulate punishment, it is highly informative for prediction tasks involving reputation mechanisms as an alternative to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds, information structure) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcomes are directly reported. For prediction, this paper supports the view that mechanisms increasing the salience or transferability of reputation can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, even in the absence of punishment.

  findings: The study demonstrates that reputation built through cooperation in a public goods game increases the probability of being helped in subsequent indirect reciprocity rounds, and this effect is equally strong whether the helper is from the same or a different PGG group. Cooperation in the PGG is maintained at a high level while IR rounds are expected, but drops sharply when only PGG rounds remain. The experiment does not include punishment or sanctioning options, and does not report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

It does, however, show that reputation-based reward mechanisms (via indirect reciprocity) can sustain high cooperation rates in PGGs, but this is not equivalent to the effect of punishment. The findings are relevant for understanding how reputation and reward can substitute for or complement punishment, but do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The game design dimensions are well described, but the absence of punishment and lack of efficiency outcomes limit the paper's direct utility for the prediction task.

- source: Puurtinen, M., & Mappes, T. (2009). Between-group competition and human cooperation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that introducing between-group competition to a standard public goods game (with no punishment or communication) leads to a substantial increase in both cooperation (contributions) and group efficiency (mean earnings). In the control (PG) condition, mean earnings over 10 rounds were 251 MUs, while in the group competition (GC) condition, mean earnings were 333 MUs. The effect is robust to order and persists across rounds. The study also finds that group competition increases moral emotions (anger at free riders, guilt at earning more than others) and shifts perception of group members from competitors to collaborators. However, the experiment does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, so it does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. Its findings are relevant for understanding the effect of between-group competition, not punishment. Therefore, it should not be used to predict the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given a control game without punishment. It does, however, provide a clear baseline for a standard public goods game (n=4, 10 rounds, no punishment, no communication, MPCR=0.5), which may be useful for contextualizing other studies.

- source: Vollan, B. (2008). Socio-ecological explanations for crowding-out effects from economic field experiments in southern Africa. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment involved groups of 5 players managing a common-pool resource over 20 rounds, with a baseline (open access) phase followed by an institution phase (external penalty, external reward, or communication) chosen by group vote. Efficiency (group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum) increased from 79.6% in the baseline to 88.6% on average after introducing institutions. However, the effect of punishment (penalty) was highly contingent: when the penalty rule was imposed with low group support (low self-determination), or in high-trust Namibian communities, efficiency actually decreased (crowding-out effect), with final extraction levels as high or higher than the baseline. In contrast, in low-trust South African communities, punishment increased efficiency. Reward (external enabling intervention) did not produce crowding-out in any context and generally improved or maintained efficiency. Communication alone (no external intervention) produced stable, moderate efficiency. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is mixed and moderated by self-determination and social trust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments is not universally positive. Instead, punishment can reduce efficiency (crowding-out) when imposed without group support or in high-trust, norm-driven communities. In low-trust settings, punishment can increase efficiency. Reward mechanisms do not crowd out cooperation and are generally safe for efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment should not be assumed to always increase efficiency; the effect depends on the degree of self-determination (e.g., whether punishment is voted in or imposed) and the baseline level of trust in the group. Control efficiency (baseline) is a strong predictor, but the effect of enabling punishment depends on these moderators. Game design dimensions such as group voting, trust, and the nature of the intervention (controlling vs. enabling) are critical for accurate prediction.

Experimental evidence from a one-shot, three-person public goods game with punishment shows that lower contributions from others evoke more anger and lead to harsher punishment, and that the effect of others' contributions on punishment is fully mediated by experienced anger. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment is deployed, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Castillo, D., Bousquet, F., Janssen, M. A., Worrapimphong, K., & Cardenas, J. C. (2011). Context matters to explain field experiments: Results from Colombian and Thai fishing villages. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing externally imposed rules with probabilistic sanctions (confiscation of yield upon detection) in a CPR field experiment in fishing villages leads to only modest improvements in group efficiency and earnings. The effect is significant only when the quota rule is chosen and followed, but in most groups, rule breaking remains common and efficiency gains are small or absent. The context—such as economic necessity, lack of trust in authorities, and low legitimacy of rules—plays a major role in limiting the effectiveness of sanctions. The findings suggest that punishment mechanisms, as implemented here, do not reliably increase efficiency in real-world resource-dependent communities, except under specific conditions of rule choice and compliance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in field settings with resource-dependent participants, the introduction of rules with probabilistic sanctions (punishment) does not reliably increase efficiency in public-goods-like games. Efficiency gains are observed only when the group chooses and complies with a quota rule; otherwise, rule breaking and contextual barriers (e.g., lack of trust, economic necessity) undermine the effect of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment mechanisms alone is not sufficient to ensure higher efficiency—contextual factors and the type of rule matter. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and treatment efficiency (with punishment) is only modestly higher, and only in some cases. Game design dimensions such as the absence of communication, the probabilistic and relatively weak punishment, and the lack of participant buy-in to rules are important moderators.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that real-world intergroup conflict increases individuals' willingness to punish non-cooperators and reward cooperators in economic games structurally related to public goods dilemmas. However, because the games used (Ultimatum and Trust Games) are not public goods games and the outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward rates) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings are only indirectly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The results suggest that contextual factors (such as intergroup conflict) can increase sanctioning behaviors, which, in public goods games, are often associated with higher cooperation and sometimes higher efficiency, but this link is not directly tested here. The study does not manipulate or report on most game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task (e.g., MPCR, player count, punishment cost/tech, etc.), so its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes is limited.

- source: Baum, W. M., Paciotti, B., Richerson, P., Lubell, M., & McElreath, R. (2012). Cooperation due to cultural norms, not individual reputation. *Behavioural Processes*.
