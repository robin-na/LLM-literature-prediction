- source: Vasconcelos, V. V., Dannenberg, A., & Levin, S. A. (2022). Punishment institutions selected and sustained through voting and learning. *Nature Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that costly punishment institutions can be selected and sustained through voting and learning, but only under certain conditions. When the scale of the institution matches the scale of the public good (e.g., local institutions for local goods, collective institutions for global goods), punishment institutions are more likely to be adopted and to increase cooperation and group efficiency. Individual choice of institution works well for local public goods but fails for global public goods due to free-riding. Collective choice is necessary for global public goods. Learning opportunities (memory and information) are crucial: longer memory and more local information increase the likelihood of institution adoption and higher efficiency. The meta-study of experiments confirms that punishment institutions, when implemented, almost always increase cooperation and efficiency, but their adoption depends on perceived effectiveness and learning. Inefficient institutions are less popular. The model and experiments both show that punishment institutions can solve cooperation problems and increase group payoff, but only under the right institutional and informational conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and comparative evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the alignment of institutional and public good scales, the decision rule for institution adoption (individual vs. collective), and the learning environment (memory, information). When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, one should consider whether the punishment institution is collective or individual, whether the public good is local or global, and the quality of learning opportunities. Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when collective institutions govern global goods, or when individual choice governs local goods. Poor learning conditions or misaligned scales reduce the effectiveness of punishment. The paper's meta-study confirms that these patterns hold in experimental data. Thus, for downstream prediction, the presence and design of punishment institutions, as well as learning/information features, are key moderators of the efficiency effect.

- source: Rosas, A. (2008). The return of reciprocity: a psychological approach to the evolution of cooperation. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. Exclusion-based punishment (TPR) is more stable and efficient, provided defectors cannot exploit cooperators. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency. Theoretical predictions are made for experimental settings, suggesting that people will avoid over-punishment and switch methods when possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Rusch, H. (2012). An evolutionary perspective on the long-term efficiency of costly punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

- source: Quan, J., Chen, X. Y., Yang, W. J., & Wang, X. J. (2023). Cooperation dynamics in spatial public goods games with graded punishment mechanism. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that graded punishment (where the probability of being punished increases with repeated defections) is generally more effective at promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games than traditional fixed-probability punishment. This effect is strongest when the punishment cost is low relative to the fine and when the initial punishment probability is not too small. When the punishment cost approaches the fine or the initial punishment probability is very low, the advantage of graded punishment disappears or reverses. The main reported outcome is the steady-state cooperation density, not group efficiency or total payoff. Payoff is used for local updating and is discussed in mechanism explanations, but efficiency is not directly measured.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment mechanism (graded vs. fixed probability) can significantly affect cooperation rates in spatial PGGs, especially as a function of punishment cost, fine, and initial punishment probability. However, because the main outcome is cooperation density rather than group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that, in similar spatial PGGs, graded punishment is likely to increase cooperation (and thus, by implication, efficiency) except when punishment is very costly or the initial probability is very low. The paper provides detailed parameter sweeps for punishment cost, fine, enhancement factor, and initial punishment probability, which can inform predictions about the conditions under which punishment will be most effective. However, direct predictions of efficiency require caution, as efficiency is not directly reported.

For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment, reputation, and communication mechanisms should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control games without these features.

  findings: The paper shows that introducing prosocial pool exclusion into a PGG leads to higher average payoffs and can stabilize cooperation through periodic oscillations or fixation to full cooperation, depending on whether second-order exclusion is present. Antisocial exclusion can destabilize cooperation but does not fully prevent the effectiveness of prosocial exclusion. Compared to punishment, prosocial pool exclusion is a more robust and effective mechanism for sustaining high efficiency in the population, even in the presence of antisocial strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in infinite well-mixed populations, enabling a prosocial pool exclusion mechanism (where excluders pay a fixed cost to exclude defectors from the public good) increases group efficiency relative to the standard PGG without exclusion. The effect is robust to the presence of antisocial exclusion, and even more pronounced when second-order exclusion is allowed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a pool exclusion mechanism should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment, especially when exclusion is institutionalized and second-order exclusion is possible. However, the results are derived from deterministic replicator dynamics and may not directly translate to finite or experimental settings.

  findings: The paper finds that redundancy in group cooperation can evolve without targeted punishment or coercion if individuals are ignorant of others' strategies and/or face harsh environments. In a threshold public goods game, when players do not know what others will do, they cooperate with an intermediate probability, leading to more cooperators than strictly necessary (redundancy). In contrast, if individuals are well-informed about others' strategies, redundancy does not evolve without punishment. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or group payoff, but discusses these concepts in relation to the model.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how information availability and environmental harshness affect cooperation and redundancy in threshold public goods games. It suggests that punishment is not strictly necessary for redundancy or intermediate cooperation rates if players are ignorant of others' actions. However, it does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff with or without punishment, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment parameters. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of information and environmental context as moderators, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Vanderschraaf, P. (2016). In a Weakly Dominated Strategy Is Strength: Evolution of Optimality in Stag Hunt Augmented with a Punishment Option. *Philosophy of Science*.

  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The effect is strongest when the punishment inflicted on defectors is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.
