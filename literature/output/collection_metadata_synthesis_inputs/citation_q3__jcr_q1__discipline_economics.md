- source: Dickinson, D. L., Masclet, D., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Norm enforcement in social dilemmas: An experiment with police commissioners. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Introducing a costly punishment institution in a standard linear public goods game (and its negative frame) increases cooperation and group efficiency compared to baseline. Sanctions are more effective than rewards at increasing both cooperation and efficiency. When the sanction institution is chosen by majority vote, cooperation and efficiency are even higher than when it is imposed exogenously. Groups with more police commissioners (who are more cooperative and more willing to punish) achieve higher group earnings and efficiency. Efficiency is always higher in the Giving Game (public goods) than in the Taking Game (public bads). The paper provides direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency, with detailed reporting of group earnings and efficiency ratios by treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in linear public goods games. The design closely matches the canonical PGG, with five players, continuous contributions, known MPCR, and explicit reporting of group earnings and efficiency. The effect of punishment is positive and substantial: sanctions increase efficiency relative to baseline, and endogenous (voted) sanctions increase it even more. The paper also provides evidence on the relative effect of rewards, the impact of subject pool (police vs. non-police), and the effect of game framing (positive vs. negative externality). The findings are highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar environments.

- source: Nicklisch, A., Grechenig, K., & Thöni, C. (2016). Information-sensitive Leviathans. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games depend strongly on the information environment and the type of punishment institution. Under perfect information, decentralized punishment leads to the highest contributions and profits (efficiency), outperforming centralized punishment and no punishment. As information becomes less accurate (signals about others' contributions are noisy), both contributions and profits decline in all institutions, but especially in DecPun. Under moderate noise, CenPun can attract more citizens and sometimes matches or slightly exceeds DecPun in efficiency, provided the authority avoids punishing cooperators. Under high noise, neither punishment institution yields substantial efficiency gains over no punishment. The key moderator is the extent to which punishment is misapplied to cooperators, which is more likely in DecPun under noise. The paper provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes with punishment enabled versus disabled, across different institutional and informational settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the information environment and the punishment institution's design. If the control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially decentralized) can substantially increase efficiency under perfect information. However, as information becomes noisy, the efficiency gain from punishment is reduced, and the difference between decentralized and centralized punishment narrows. The presence of a central authority who avoids punishing cooperators can maintain or improve efficiency under moderate noise, but not under high noise. For prediction, the paper suggests that the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost/tech, and especially the information structure (accuracy of contribution signals) are critical moderators. The results also imply that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on these dimensions.

- source: Markussen, T., Putterman, L., & Tyran, J. R. (2016). Judicial error and cooperation. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that error-free formal sanctions increase both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public goods game. When judicial errors are introduced (either type I or type II), both cooperation and efficiency decline. The effect on efficiency is more negative for type I errors than for type II errors, due to the wasteful punishment of cooperators. The negative effect of errors is stronger when the error probability is higher and when errors convert a deterrent sanction regime into a non-deterrent one. The effects of type I and type II errors on contributions are symmetric, but type I errors reduce group earnings more. Subjects show a clear preference for avoiding type I errors, but this preference is fully explained by income maximization rather than additional psychological aversion.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that the introduction of punishment with judicial error reduces efficiency in public goods games, and that the magnitude of the efficiency loss depends on the type and probability of error, as well as the deterrence strength of the sanction regime. For prediction, the findings imply that enabling punishment increases efficiency only if sanctions are deterrent and error-free; otherwise, efficiency may decrease, especially with type I errors or high error probability. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the probability and type of judicial error, and whether sanctions are deterrent. Control efficiency (without punishment) is also reported, allowing for direct comparison.

- source: Kube, S., Schaube, S., Schildberg-Hörisch, H., & Khachatryan, E. (2015). Institution formation and cooperation with heterogeneous agents. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with three players, the introduction of an institution formation stage (via unanimity voting) that, if implemented, imposes binding contribution obligations enforced by deterrent sanctions, leads to large increases in efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the maximum possible). For homogeneous groups, a symmetric institution (equal obligations) is almost always implemented and achieves efficiency above 90%. For heterogeneous groups, a symmetric institution is often rejected by low-benefit players due to inequality aversion, but an asymmetric institution (with obligations tailored to equalize payoffs) is much more likely to be implemented, though at a cost to total efficiency. In all cases, efficiency is higher when the institution is implemented than when it is not. The main moderators are the distribution of marginal per-capita returns (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) and the symmetry of institutional obligations. The study does not manipulate punishment cost or technology directly, as the institution, if implemented, is enforced with sufficiently strong sanctions to ensure compliance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with three players and 20 rounds, the introduction of a binding, deterrent-sanction institution (enabled by unanimity voting) increases efficiency relative to the baseline game without such an institution. The effect is strongest when the institution is both efficient and equitable (i.e., symmetric for homogeneous groups, asymmetric for heterogeneous groups). When the institution is rejected (often due to inequality aversion), efficiency is lower, but still generally higher than in the baseline. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a strong, binding punishment institution (with high compliance) should be expected to increase efficiency, but the magnitude depends on group heterogeneity and the design of institutional obligations. The study does not provide evidence on the effect of varying punishment cost or technology, as these are not manipulated; the punishment regime is always fully deterrent. Control efficiency (without institution) is reported and can be used as a baseline for prediction.

- source: Dai, Z. X., Hogarth, R. M., & Villeval, M. C. (2015). Ambiguity on audits and cooperation in a public goods game. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with centralized sanctions triggered by audits, the study manipulates the frequency and regularity of audits (continuous vs. intermittent) and the severity of sanctions. Under ambiguity (unknown audit probability), intermittent audits with high fines (but lower frequency) produce similar or higher contributions and significantly higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audits with low fines. After audits are withdrawn, groups exposed to intermittent audits maintain higher cooperation and efficiency for longer, as their beliefs about audit probability decay more slowly. High frequency-high severity (continuous audits with high fines) also sustains cooperation and efficiency, but at higher cost. Reducing audit frequency or sanction severity too much undermines these effects. The results suggest that, for a given expected cost of sanction, intermittent audits with high severity are more efficient than continuous audits with low severity.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 3-player, 50-round linear PGG with centralized punishment, intermittent (irregular) audit schemes with high severity (punishment effectiveness) and sufficient frequency lead to higher efficiency (group payoffs) than continuous audit schemes with lower severity, especially after audits are withdrawn. The effect is robust as long as audit frequency and severity are not too low. For prediction, this suggests that, holding control efficiency and other game dimensions constant, enabling punishment via intermittent, ambiguous audits with high severity will increase efficiency more than continuous, predictable audits with low severity. The paper also shows that both audit frequency and punishment effectiveness (severity) are key moderators: too few audits or too low severity reduces the efficiency benefit. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with centralized punishment.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2014). On cooperation in open communities. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that when subjects can self-select into a punishment community (VF-PUN), average contributions are much higher than in a reward community (VF-REW) or in exogenously assigned punishment communities (EX-PUN, FIX-PUN). The effect is attributed to self-selection rather than slow growth of the community. The presence of a punishment option is critical for high cooperation, and adding reward does not further improve outcomes. However, the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff; all main results are in terms of contribution rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that self-selection into punishment-enabled public goods games leads to higher cooperation (contribution) rates than exogenous assignment or reward-based institutions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the results are only indirectly informative: higher contributions likely imply higher efficiency, but actual efficiency or group payoff is not reported. The design details (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified, so the paper is useful for mapping design to expected cooperation, but not for direct efficiency prediction.

- source: Zhou, X. Y., Alysandratos, T., & Naef, M. (2023). Rice Farming and the Origins of Cooperative Behaviour. *Economic Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The study finds that (1) participants from wetland rice regions contribute more in both the no-punishment and punishment conditions of a repeated PGG, and (2) participants from rice regions assign more punishment points to free riders than those from non-rice regions. The difference in contribution is larger in the punishment condition.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of a punishment option increases contribution rates in a repeated PGG, and that the magnitude of this effect is moderated by cultural background (rice vs. non-rice regions). However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are useful for predicting contribution behavior under different cultural backgrounds, but not for direct efficiency prediction as required by the downstream task.

- source: Albrecht, F., Kube, S., & Traxler, C. (2018). Cooperation and norm enforcement - The individual-level perspective. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds substantial heterogeneity in both cooperation and punishment patterns. About half of subjects are pro-social punishers, and about 40% are non-punishers. There is only a moderate correlation between cooperation and punishment types: many conditional cooperators do not punish, and some free-riders do punish pro-socially. In repeated public goods games with punishment, groups with more pro-social punishers achieve higher and more stable contribution levels, even after controlling for the number of conditional cooperators. Regression analyses show that the number of pro-social punishers in a group is a stronger predictor of group contribution levels than the number of conditional cooperators. However, the paper does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, so the effect of punishment on efficiency is not directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public goods games with punishment, the presence of pro-social punishers in a group increases contribution levels. This suggests that enabling punishment can increase cooperation, but the paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoffs. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is informative about behavioral mechanisms (contributions and punishment) but not about the net payoff effects of punishment. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the experimental setup matches the canonical PGG with punishment, so the findings are highly relevant for understanding how group composition and punishment opportunities affect cooperation rates, but not directly efficiency.

- source: Gallier, C. (2020). Democracy and compliance in public goods games. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that a non-deterrent contribution rule in a public goods game increases contributions, regardless of whether it is imposed exogenously or chosen democratically. The aggregate increase in contributions is primarily due to the presence of the rule itself, not the democratic process. Democratic participation (voting) does not have a statistically significant effect on contributions at the aggregate level after accounting for the direct effect of the rule, self-selection, and information effects. At the individual level, participants who are overruled in the group vote tend to reduce their contributions, but this negative effect is counteracted by a small democracy premium if the rule is democratically chosen. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with a mild, non-deterrent automatic sanction, the introduction of the rule (punishment) increases contributions, but the effect is not due to democratic participation per se. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of introducing a weak punishment mechanism on contribution rates, but not directly on efficiency or group payoff, as these are not reported. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contribution, etc.) are clearly specified and can inform parameterization of prediction models. However, since efficiency is not reported, the paper's quantitative results cannot be directly used for efficiency prediction, but the qualitative pattern (punishment increases cooperation even when non-deterrent) is robust.

This effect is strongest among participants (especially workers) with high deference to authority, and weaker or absent among those with low deference to authority (especially students). The effect is context-dependent: in this Chinese sample, exogenous punishment is more effective than democratic (endogenous) punishment, especially among those with high deference to authority. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on cultural context and the method of rule implementation (exogenous vs. endogenous). - source: Zhou, Y. X., Chen, S. W., Chen, Y. F., & Vollane, B. (2022). Does parental migration impede the development of the cooperative preferences in their left-behind children? Evidence from a large-scale field experiment in China. *China Economic Review*.

For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect but informative evidence that punishment is likely to increase efficiency in similar settings, especially when baseline cooperation is low due to social factors.

- source: Gangadharan, L., Nikiforakis, N., & Villeval, M. C. (2017). Normative conflict and the limits of self-governance in heterogeneous populations. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: Allowing communication and rewards in a public goods game leads to near-maximal efficiency in homogeneous groups, but only partial efficiency in heterogeneous groups where returns from the public good differ. In homogeneous groups, communication increases group earnings to 94% of the maximum, while in heterogeneous groups, the increase is only 80% of the maximum. The main reason is that most heterogeneous groups prioritize equality or compromise between equality and efficiency, rather than maximizing group earnings. Rewards are not systematically used to offset inequality. The loss in efficiency in heterogeneous groups is due both to lower contributions and to under-use of rewards. The study demonstrates that normative conflict can limit the effectiveness of self-governance mechanisms in promoting efficiency in heterogeneous populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling communication and rewards in public goods games depend critically on group homogeneity. In homogeneous groups, such mechanisms can achieve near-maximal efficiency. In heterogeneous groups, even with communication and rewards, efficiency gains are much smaller due to normative conflict between equality and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity in returns (mpcr differences) is a strong moderator that limits the efficiency benefit of enabling communication and rewards. The absence of punishment means the results are most directly applicable to reward-based or communication-based interventions, but the findings on normative conflict are likely relevant for predicting the limits of punishment as well, especially in heterogeneous settings.

- source: Messer, K. D., & Zarghamee, H. (2007). New hope for the voluntary contributions mechanism: The effects of context. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that context—specifically, the combination of cheap talk, voting, and a status quo of giving—can dramatically increase and sustain contributions and efficiency in a voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) with a low MPCR. In the baseline (no context), efficiency starts at 82.3% and decays to 72.6% by round 10. Cheap talk and status quo of giving each increase initial contributions, but only their interaction (and with voting) sustains high contributions. Voting alone has no effect. The results show that context can substitute for incentive-compatible mechanisms in achieving high efficiency, but the paper does not test or discuss punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly informative about the effects of communication, voting, and default framing, but not about punishment. For the downstream prediction task—predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency—this paper is not directly relevant. However, it does provide a strong benchmark for how non-punitive contextual factors can achieve high efficiency, which may be useful for comparative purposes or for understanding the limits of punishment's added value in similar settings.
