- source: Webb, J., & Foddy, M. (2004). Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts. *Small Group Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule. Voting for structural change (switching sanctioning systems) is predicted by individual profit and fines, not by group-level resource preservation. The study highlights that the impact of sanctioning systems on efficiency and payoff is heterogeneous and depends on both the structure of the sanction and the distribution of resource access.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

- source: Whitmeyer, J. M. (2004). The group control catastrophe. *Sociological Perspectives*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The paper finds that in a simulated environment where an external agent can sanction group members (individually or collectively) to induce compliance (a public good), increasing monitoring efficacy does not always monotonically increase compliance. Instead, for intermediate monitoring efficacy, compliance can collapse to zero, creating a bifurcation with two possible equilibria (full compliance or full defection), depending on initial conditions (e.g., the number of initial cooperators). The 'catastrophe' is robust across a range of group sizes, sanction levels, internal control efficacies, and production function shapes (except linear). The equilibrium with high compliance yields higher payoffs for group members than the low-compliance equilibrium. The cost to the agent of obtaining compliance can be minimized by using only one type of sanction (individual or collective) and by leveraging initial cooperation to reach the high-compliance equilibrium, especially at moderate monitoring efficacy. However, the model does not directly report efficiency in the sense of group payoff relative to the maximum possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment (sanctions) on group compliance and payoffs in public-goods-like environments is highly non-linear and can depend critically on monitoring efficacy, initial conditions, and the structure of sanctions. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency or group payoff: under some parameter regimes (especially intermediate monitoring efficacy), punishment can lead to catastrophic drops in compliance and thus lower payoffs. The model also highlights the importance of initial cooperation and the potential for multiple equilibria. However, because the paper does not directly report efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, and because it is a simulation/theory paper rather than an empirical study, its findings should be used to inform qualitative expectations about non-monotonic and path-dependent effects of punishment, rather than to provide quantitative predictions.

- source: Rosenbaum, S. M., Billinger, S., & Stieglitz, N. (2012). Safeguarding Common-Pool Resources in Transition Economies: Experimental Evidence from Central Asia. *Journal of Development Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that average contributions to the public good are high and nearly identical in both Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, despite differences in market integration and transition stage. The experiment is a one-shot, anonymous, five-player VCM with no punishment or reward options. The results suggest that, in the absence of sanctioning mechanisms, group size, anonymity, and market integration do not significantly affect contribution rates in this context. The paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, and does not manipulate or analyze punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism was present or analyzed. It does, however, provide contextual evidence about baseline contribution rates in a one-shot, five-player, anonymous VCM with MPCR=0.4 and no communication or sanctions. For prediction tasks, this paper is informative only for estimating control (no-punishment) efficiency in similar game designs, but provides no evidence on the effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Janus, T., & Lim, J. J. (2009). Sticks and carrots: Two incentive mechanisms supporting intra-group cooperation. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper proves that in a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma, the introduction of either collective punishment (Nash reversion by all after any defection) or reward mechanisms (transfers contingent on cooperation) can sustain full cooperation as a subgame perfect equilibrium, even when bilateral incentive compatibility constraints are violated. This means that group efficiency (welfare) can be maintained at the cooperative level under broader conditions than in the absence of such mechanisms. The results are general and apply to settings where players interact repeatedly and can observe each other's actions, and where the game is either infinite or has an unknown end point.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms in repeated social dilemma games can increase group efficiency by sustaining cooperation, even when cooperation would not be sustainable without such mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism (especially collective punishment with Nash reversion) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control without punishment, provided players are sufficiently patient (high discount factor) and can observe actions. The model is adjacent to public goods games (it is a repeated three-player prisoner's dilemma), so the results are most directly applicable to small-group, repeated, fully observable settings. The paper does not provide empirical effect sizes or parameterized predictions, but it does establish that the direction of effect is positive for efficiency when punishment is enabled.

- source: Matsushima, H. (2012). FINITELY REPEATED PRISONERS' DILEMMA WITH SMALL FINES: THE PENANCE CONTRACT. *Japanese Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper proves that with a suitable contract that imposes a fine for deviation in the final period, full cooperation (i.e., maximal efficiency) can be uniquely sustained in finitely repeated prisoners' dilemma games. The required fine can be small relative to total payoffs if the game is long enough. The penance contract is robust to renegotiation, unlike trigger contracts. The result generalizes to other two-player games with similar payoff structure. The findings are theoretical and show that the design of the punishment mechanism (timing, size, and contingency) is crucial for achieving efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in two-player finitely repeated social dilemma games, the introduction of a well-designed punishment mechanism (specifically, a contract that imposes a fine for final-period deviation) can move the group from inefficient (all-defect) equilibria to fully efficient (all-cooperate) equilibria. The effect is robust to renegotiation if the punishment is contingent on final-period behavior and the fine is large enough to outweigh the gain from deviation. The result is directly relevant for predicting that enabling such a punishment mechanism will increase efficiency to the maximum possible, provided the contract is enforceable and the fine is sufficiently large. However, the result is limited to two-player, finitely repeated, contractible games and does not address larger groups or public goods games directly.

- source: Aramendia, M. (2006). Asymmetric finite punishments in repeated games. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper demonstrates that in repeated games, including social dilemmas and Cournot oligopoly, AFP strategies—where punishment is finite and asymmetric—can sustain any feasible and individually rational payoff profile when players are sufficiently patient. The punishment phase is designed so that nondeviators are always better off than the deviator, and after a finite number of periods, cooperation resumes. This approach is less restrictive than some standard equilibrium refinements and can support efficient outcomes (e.g., cartel profits) even in cases where other concepts (like WRP) cannot. The results are illustrated with examples including the prisoner's dilemma and Cournot oligopoly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that finite, asymmetric punishment strategies can sustain efficient, cooperative outcomes in repeated games, even when standard trigger or minimax punishments are not credible or too harsh. For the prediction task, it suggests that the design of the punishment phase (finite duration, asymmetry favoring nondeviators) can be critical for supporting high efficiency, especially as the number of players increases or when renegotiation-proofness is problematic. However, the results are theoretical and do not provide direct quantitative predictions for specific experimental public goods games, but they do indicate that efficiency can be maintained with appropriately designed punishment even in large groups or complex environments.

- source: Heller, W. B., & Sieberg, K. K. (2008). Functional unpleasantness: the evolutionary logic of righteous resentment. *Public Choice*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation can be sustained in populations where some individuals are willing to punish cheaters, even if those punishers are themselves not cooperative. The evolutionary stability of cooperation depends on the relative costs of punishing, being punished, and being cheated. When the cost of punishing is less than the cost of being cheated, punishment can support a stable mix of cooperators and punishers, increasing overall efficiency compared to a world with only cheaters. If punishment is too costly, cheaters dominate and cooperation collapses. The model demonstrates that the existence of punishment mechanisms—regardless of the punishers' motives—can improve group welfare and efficiency in collective action settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment in collective action games (including public goods game variants) can increase efficiency, as measured by average group payoff, provided the cost of punishing is not too high. The findings suggest that the presence and effectiveness of punishment mechanisms are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment. While the model is not an exact public goods game, its structure and payoff logic are closely related, and its results are directly relevant for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency. The paper does not address empirical game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, or communication, but it does provide strong evidence that the cost and effectiveness of punishment are critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Klempt, C. (2012). Fairness, spite, and intentions: Testing different motives behind punishment in a prisoners' dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that cooperators and defectors differ in their motives for punishment. Cooperators punish defectors more when defection is intentional, suggesting a role for retaliation, but still punish unintentional defectors, indicating some concern for payoff inequality. Defectors punish both cooperators and defectors equally, regardless of intentions, consistent with spiteful motives. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or prisoners' dilemma games. Its findings are relevant for understanding the motives behind punishment and how they may depend on the intentionality of actions, but it does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes. Therefore, it offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task, except to suggest that punishment behavior may depend on player type and perceived intentions, which could indirectly affect efficiency in other designs.

- source: Cisarovsky, G., Bshary, A., Bouzelboudjen, M., & Bshary, R. (2012). Spatial Group Structure as Potential Mechanism to Maintain Cooperation in Fish Shoals of Unrelated Individuals. *Ethology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count
  findings: The paper finds that anthias in natural shoals have partially non-overlapping home ranges, and blennies (parasites) show site fidelity, attacking more frequently in certain areas. Individuals more exposed to the blenny may benefit more from punishing (chasing) the blenny, making punishment potentially self-serving rather than purely altruistic. This spatial structuring could help maintain the public good of punishment even in large, unrelated groups. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or direct fitness outcomes; it infers potential payoff implications from spatial and behavioral patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that spatial structure and local exposure can moderate the effectiveness and self-serving nature of punishment in public-goods-like dilemmas in natural systems. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a controlled way. For prediction tasks, the main value is in highlighting that spatial structure and heterogeneity in exposure can affect the distribution and potential benefits of punishment, but the paper does not provide direct input for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in standard PGG designs.

- source: Ishida, J. (2009). Incentives in academics: Collaboration under weak complementarities. *Labour Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated game with voluntary team formation, optimal contracts may use high-powered team incentives and low-powered individual incentives to exploit peer monitoring and reputation effects. This can make collaboration sustainable and efficient even with weak complementarities, overcoming the free-rider problem present in static settings. However, when the opportunity to collaborate is frequent and agents are patient, the optimal contract may under-incentivize individual effort, leading to inefficiency when collaboration is not possible. Thus, repeated interaction and peer sanctions can both improve and harm efficiency, depending on parameter values (discount factor, frequency of collaboration opportunities, degree of complementarities).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling peer sanctions (punishment via reputation loss and exclusion from collaboration) in repeated public-goods-like environments depends on the structure of incentives and the frequency of collaboration opportunities. When peer sanctions are possible, efficiency can increase by overcoming free-rider problems, but if individual incentives are weakened too much to make peer sanctions more effective, efficiency can decrease when collaboration is not possible. For prediction, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not unconditionally positive: it depends on whether the contract design balances team and individual incentives, the patience of agents, and the frequency of opportunities for collaboration. The model is adjacent to public goods games, with peer sanctions implemented via exclusion and reputation rather than explicit costly punishment.

- source: Kusakawa, T., Ogawa, K., & Shichijo, T. (2012). An experimental investigation of a third-person enforcement in a prisoner's dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment shows that when a third party (M) can observe the actions of two players in a one-shot PD, the cooperation rate between those two players is significantly higher than when M cannot observe. This is attributed to the threat of future punishment by M in subsequent repeated PD games. The effect is measured in terms of cooperation rates, not efficiency or total payoff. The study provides evidence that third-party observation and the possibility of future sanctioning can facilitate cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a third-party observer with future sanctioning power can increase cooperation rates in a prisoner's dilemma setting. However, because the study does not use a public goods game or directly measure efficiency or group payoff, its value for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments is limited. The main implication is that third-party monitoring and the threat of future punishment can be a strong facilitator of cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency in related settings, but this is not directly tested. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not manipulate standard punishment mechanisms (e.g., costly punishment within the group), so caution is warranted in transferring the findings.

- source: Szilagyi, M. N., & Somogyi, I. (2010). Agent-Based Simulation of an N-Person Game with Parabolic Payoff Functions. *Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that the final proportion of cooperators depends strongly on the shape and relative position of the parabolic payoff functions, the agent update rule, and the definition of neighborhood (local vs. global). For some parameterizations, stable intermediate levels of cooperation emerge; in others, the system converges to all-cooperation or all-defection. The results are often unpredictable except in special cases (e.g., greedy or conformist agents with global neighborhoods). The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, nor does it include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms as in standard public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The game studied is adjacent to PGGs in that it is an N-person game with binary choices and payoff externalities, but it lacks explicit punishment/reward actions and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The findings may be useful for understanding how nonlinear payoff structures and local interactions affect cooperation rates, but they do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment interventions.

- source: Wittman, D. (2005). The internal organization of the family: Economic analysis and psychological advice. *Kyklos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that advice from clinical psychology on setting boundaries and using natural or logical consequences (rather than punishment) in families is consistent with economic reasoning about property rights and efficiency. It argues that punishment systems in families create externalities and deadweight losses, and that structuring choices so that parents are indifferent to children's decisions (the 'indifference principle') minimizes manipulation and strategic behavior. The paper suggests that minimizing emotional involvement and not using punishment leads to more functional, efficient family relationships, but these claims are theoretical and not empirically tested.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative findings relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its arguments are theoretical and analogical, suggesting that punishment may introduce inefficiencies (externalities, deadweight loss) in small-group settings, and that structuring choices to avoid punishment may be more efficient. However, these claims are not tested in controlled experiments or public goods game variants, so the paper offers only indirect, conceptual support for the idea that punishment can reduce efficiency by increasing transaction costs and strategic manipulation.
