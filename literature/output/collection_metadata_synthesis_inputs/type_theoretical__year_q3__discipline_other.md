- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

- source: Zhu, P. C., Guo, H., Zhang, H. L., Han, Y., Wang, Z., & Chu, C. (2020). The role of punishment in the spatial public goods game. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that peer punishment leads to higher levels of cooperation than pool punishment in spatial public goods games. When the threshold for switching to pool punishment is high (i.e., more peer punishment), cooperation is more prevalent. The coexistence of both punishment types is explored, and the transition between regimes is mapped. The main findings are based on the frequencies of cooperators, defectors, and punishers, not on efficiency or group payoff. The ratio of fine to cost is higher for peer punishment, which is suggested as a reason for its greater effectiveness in promoting cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that peer punishment is more effective than pool punishment in increasing the prevalence of cooperation in spatial PGGs. However, because the outcomes are reported in terms of strategy frequencies rather than efficiency or group payoff, the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. The model and parameterization are clearly described, so the paper can inform mechanistic expectations about how punishment type and its implementation affect cooperation, but not directly about efficiency or welfare outcomes.

- source: Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2019). Evolutionary dynamics of cooperation in the public goods game with pool exclusion strategies. *Nonlinear Dynamics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper shows that introducing prosocial pool exclusion into a PGG leads to higher average payoffs and can stabilize cooperation through periodic oscillations or fixation to full cooperation, depending on whether second-order exclusion is present. Antisocial exclusion can destabilize cooperation but does not fully prevent the effectiveness of prosocial exclusion. Compared to punishment, prosocial pool exclusion is a more robust and effective mechanism for sustaining high efficiency in the population, even in the presence of antisocial strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in infinite well-mixed populations, enabling a prosocial pool exclusion mechanism (where excluders pay a fixed cost to exclude defectors from the public good) increases group efficiency relative to the standard PGG without exclusion. The effect is robust to the presence of antisocial exclusion, and even more pronounced when second-order exclusion is allowed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of a pool exclusion mechanism should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment, especially when exclusion is institutionalized and second-order exclusion is possible. However, the results are derived from deterministic replicator dynamics and may not directly translate to finite or experimental settings.

- source: Pedroso, M. (2021). Blind Cooperation: The Evolution of Redundancy via Ignorance. *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that redundancy in group cooperation can evolve without targeted punishment or coercion if individuals are ignorant of others' strategies and/or face harsh environments. In a threshold public goods game, when players do not know what others will do, they cooperate with an intermediate probability, leading to more cooperators than strictly necessary (redundancy). Harsher environments (higher group-level cost of failure) increase the probability of cooperation and thus redundancy. In contrast, if individuals are well-informed about others' strategies, redundancy does not evolve without punishment. The paper does not provide empirical or simulated data on efficiency or group payoff, but discusses these concepts in relation to the model.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how information availability and environmental harshness affect cooperation and redundancy in threshold public goods games. It suggests that punishment is not strictly necessary for redundancy or intermediate cooperation rates if players are ignorant of others' actions. However, it does not provide direct evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff with or without punishment, nor does it manipulate or analyze punishment parameters. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is most useful for understanding the role of information and environmental context as moderators, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment.

- source: Baker, S., & Choi, A. H. (2018). Reputation and Litigation: Why Costly Legal Sanctions Can Work Better than Reputational Sanctions. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that legal sanctions (punishment) can be more efficient than reputational sanctions (boycotts) in deterring undesirable behavior, even when both are costly. This is because legal sanctions have an inframarginal effect: increasing damages not only deters more lawsuits at the margin but also increases the penalty for existing lawsuits, improving deterrence at lower cost. Reputational sanctions lack this inframarginal effect, so increasing their severity is less efficient. The efficiency advantage of legal sanctions is greatest when courts are accurate and when litigation outcomes are public. When court error is high or when consumers have independent, reliable information about quality, reputational sanctions become more important. The model predicts that the optimal regime combines both types of sanctions, but leans toward legal sanctions unless litigation is highly error-prone or costly. The analysis is theoretical and does not provide experimental or empirical estimates, but it offers clear comparative statics and policy implications for the efficiency of punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (legal sanctions) in repeated moral hazard environments can increase efficiency relative to relying solely on reputational sanctions, especially when punishment is not too costly or error-prone and when outcomes are public. The inframarginal effect of punishment means that its efficiency benefit can be greater than that of reputational sanctions, which only deter at the margin. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment should increase efficiency, particularly when the punishment mechanism is accurate and visible. However, if punishment is highly error-prone or costly, or if reputational information is highly reliable, the efficiency gain from punishment may be reduced or even reversed. The model is not a direct PGG or lab experiment, so quantitative predictions should be made with caution, but the qualitative comparative statics are clear and relevant.

- source: Steimanis, I., Hofmann, R., Mbidzo, M., & Vollan, B. (2020). When female leaders believe that men make better leaders: Empowerment in community-based water management in rural Namibia. *Journal of Rural Studies*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The introduction of conditional neutral punishment—where agents punish neighbors with the opposite strategy if their own payoff is below the neighborhood average—substantially increases the fraction of cooperators in a spatial prisoner's dilemma game. The effect is robust to variations in the temptation to defect and the cost and effectiveness of punishment. Cooperative punishers form protective clusters that shield cooperators, while antisocial punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) is largely ineffective. The main outcome is the fraction of cooperation, not group payoff or efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that conditional neutral punishment can increase cooperation rates in spatial social dilemmas, but it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, the results are only indirectly informative: higher cooperation rates typically—but not always—imply higher efficiency, especially in prisoner's dilemma-like settings. However, without explicit efficiency or payoff data, the magnitude and reliability of efficiency gains from punishment in this design cannot be directly inferred. The spatial structure, conditionality of punishment, and the absence of communication or reward mechanisms are important contextual features.

- source: Liu, W. W., & Yang, J. N. (2018). The Evolutionary Game Theoretic Analysis for Sustainable Cooperation Relationship of Collaborative Innovation Network in Strategic Emerging Industries. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that government intervention through incentives and punishments can significantly influence the frequency of positive cooperation between enterprises and URIs in collaborative innovation networks. When the sum of incentives and punishments exceeds the total additional cost (TAC) of cooperation, positive cooperation becomes stable. If incentives and punishments are too low, negative cooperation prevails. The model does not report group payoffs, efficiency, or welfare, but focuses on the conditions for stable cooperation strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task by showing that punishment (and incentives) can increase the frequency of cooperative behavior in a multi-agent innovation network, but it does not quantify effects on efficiency or group payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves free-riding and cooperation, but the outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies) rather than payoff-based. The findings suggest that, in similar environments, increasing punishment and incentives above a threshold can shift the system toward more cooperation, which may imply higher efficiency, but this is not directly modeled or measured.

- source: Jagers, S. C., Harring, N., Löfgren, Sjöstedt, M., Alpizar, F., Brülde, B., Langlet, D., Nilsson, A., Almroth, B. C., Dupont, S., & Steffen, W. (2020). On the preconditions for large-scale collective action. *Ambio*.

  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper argues that as collective action problems become larger in scale (more actors, greater spatial/temporal distance, more complexity), spontaneous cooperation becomes less likely due to increased stressors such as anonymity, lack of accountability, heterogeneity, and uncertainty. Third-party interventions (which may include sanctions, incentives, or other mechanisms) become increasingly necessary to generate and sustain collective action. The framework is intended to guide future empirical research and policy design, but does not itself provide new empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games or their variants.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct empirical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. However, it offers theoretical context: it suggests that in larger-scale settings, informal mechanisms (like peer punishment) are less effective, and formal third-party interventions (which may include sanctions or rewards) become more important for sustaining cooperation. For prediction tasks, this implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency may diminish as group size, spatial/temporal distance, and complexity increase, unless punishment is institutionalized or externally enforced. The paper does not provide parameter-level guidance for the specific game design dimensions required for quantitative prediction.

- source: Handfield, T., & Thrasher, J. (2019). Two of a kind: Are norms of honor a species of morality?. *Biology & Philosophy*.

  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A

  findings: The paper argues that norms of honor and morality are best understood as alternative solutions to common social problems, particularly assurance and cooperation dilemmas. It suggests that both types of norms can stabilize cooperation, sometimes through mechanisms like punishment or costly signaling (e.g., revenge). The authors propose that honor norms are a subtype of moral norms, functionally similar in their ability to promote group-beneficial behavior, especially in environments lacking formal enforcement. However, the analysis is conceptual and does not provide empirical evidence or outcome data relevant to efficiency or payoff in public goods or punishment games.

Its value for prediction is limited to high-level theoretical framing: it supports the idea that punishment and honor/revenge norms can, in principle, stabilize cooperation and deter defection, but it does not specify under what game design conditions this leads to higher efficiency or group payoff. No direct or indirect evidence is provided for the effect of specific game dimensions (e.g., player count, punishment cost) on efficiency outcomes.

- source: Vlerick, M. (2020). Explaining human altruism. *Synthese*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that human altruism, including costly punishment of norm violators, is best explained by cultural group selection and gene-culture coevolution, rather than genetic group selection. It claims that prosocial norms and punishment mechanisms evolved culturally to support cooperation and altruism, and that these features are human universals. The paper also emphasizes the importance of conscious moral reasoning in extending altruistic behavior beyond what would be predicted by evolutionary adaptation alone. While it references experimental evidence from public goods games and similar paradigms, it does not analyze or report efficiency or payoff outcomes, focusing instead on the existence and universality of altruistic and punitive behaviors.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It is useful for understanding the theoretical and evolutionary context for why punishment and prosocial norms are widespread and may support cooperation, but it does not analyze or report group payoff, efficiency, or related outcomes. Therefore, it should not be used as a primary source for quantitative prediction of efficiency effects from punishment in experimental game settings.

- source: Heath, J., & Rioux, C. (2018). Recent trends in evolutionary ethics: greenbeards!. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that many recent philosophical and scientific models that attempt to explain large-scale human cooperation by introducing commitment or punishment mechanisms (such as strong reciprocity) tacitly rely on greenbeard mechanisms, which are considered implausible in the human case due to their vulnerability to exploitation by 'falsebeards.' The authors argue that these models do not provide a satisfactory solution to the problem of human ultrasociality and that the evolution of cooperation remains an open question. The paper does not provide empirical findings or quantitative outcomes relevant to efficiency or payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative findings that can be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. Its relevance to the prediction task is limited to theoretical critique: it cautions that models relying on punishment to sustain cooperation may be structurally vulnerable unless they avoid greenbeard-like mechanisms. For prediction tasks based on game design dimensions and control efficiency, this paper offers only indirect, high-level theoretical context and does not inform parameter-level predictions.
