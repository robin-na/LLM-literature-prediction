- source: Gummerum, M., López-Pérez, B., Van Dijk, E., & Van Dillen, L. F. (2022). Ire and punishment: Incidental anger and costly punishment in children, adolescents, and adults. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that experimentally induced incidental anger increases costly punishment of unfair offers in the ultimatum game (second-party punishment) for children, adolescents, and adults. In third-party punishment games, incidental anger increases punishment severity for unfair offers in adults and adolescents, but not in children. The effect of anger is specific to unfair (unequal) offers and does not increase punishment of fair (equal) offers. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment rates and severity), not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the motivation for costly punishment in response to unfairness is influenced by emotional state and developmental stage, with self-relevance and norm appraisals playing key roles.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or related games. Its main contribution is to show that emotional state (incidental anger) can increase costly punishment of unfairness, especially when the violation is self-relevant (second-party) or, for adults, when it violates fairness norms (third-party). For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment rates and severity can be modulated by emotional and developmental factors, but it does not inform how these changes in punishment behavior translate into changes in efficiency or group payoff. The game designs used (ultimatum and third-party punishment games) are adjacent to, but not the same as, standard public goods games, and no efficiency or welfare outcomes are reported.

- source: Nunney, S. J., van der Schalk, J., & Manstead, A. S. R. (2022). Emotion and intergroup cooperation: How verbal expressions of guilt, shame, and pride influence behavior in a social dilemma. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab experiments using an intergroup centipede game, the authors find that when a group expresses shame (or guilt) after behaving uncooperatively, the opposing group is more likely to cooperate in subsequent rounds, resulting in higher group payoffs (tickets won) and more prosocial behavior. In contrast, when a group expresses pride after uncooperative behavior, the opposing group is less likely to cooperate, resulting in lower payoffs and more costly punishment. The effect of emotion expression is causal (Study 2) and operates above and beyond the effect of prior behavior. The study does not use a standard public goods game or implement a formal punishment stage as in PGGs, but does include a costly punishment measure in Study 2.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that communication—specifically, the expression of self-conscious emotions—can significantly moderate the effect of prior uncooperative behavior on subsequent group efficiency in repeated, sequential social dilemmas. While not a standard PGG, the centipede game shares key features (sequential cooperation, group payoffs, potential for exploitation). The findings suggest that, in environments where communication is possible, expressions of shame or guilt can substitute for or augment formal punishment mechanisms in restoring cooperation and efficiency, while pride expressions can undermine them. However, the study does not manipulate punishment cost, technology, or other standard PGG design dimensions, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled PGG efficiency is limited. It does, however, highlight the importance of communication and emotional signaling as moderators of efficiency outcomes.
