- source: Huang, S. T., Dong, Y. L., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). THE OPTIMAL INCENTIVE IN PROMOTING COOPERATION: PUNISH THE WORST AND DO NOT ONLY REWARD THE BEST. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, pure punishment or pure reward alone (without tax) cannot sustain cooperation in a well-mixed population. Introducing a tax-based mechanism (where all players pay a tax to fund punishment or reward) enables the stable coexistence of cooperators and tax-based pure punishers or rewarders, thus sustaining cooperation. Tax-based pure reward leads to a higher frequency of cooperators than tax-based pure punishment. The analysis is based on replicator dynamics and focuses on the evolutionary stability of strategy frequencies, not on direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that institutional (tax-based) punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in public goods games, whereas individual-level (pure) punishment or reward cannot. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of the frequency of cooperators, not efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling institutional punishment (via tax) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that institutional reward may be even more effective. However, it does not provide direct quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency is indirect and limited to the expectation that efficiency should increase when tax-based punishment or reward is enabled.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Gavrilets, S., Qin, C. Z., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). Kinship can hinder cooperation in heterogeneous populations. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods and multi-player PD games with heterogeneous cost-benefit ratios, increasing genetic relatedness does not always increase cooperation or efficiency. For low relatedness, direct reciprocity (punishment via GRIM) can sustain cooperation and high efficiency. For high relatedness, cooperation is a dominant strategy and efficiency is high. However, for intermediate relatedness, some individuals become unconditional cooperators (AllC), which undermines the effectiveness of punishment and allows others to defect (AllD), making full cooperation and maximal efficiency unsustainable. This results in a non-monotonic, U-shaped relationship between relatedness and efficiency. The findings are supported by both subgame perfect equilibrium analysis and agent-based evolutionary simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public goods games with heterogeneity in cost-benefit ratios, the effect of mechanisms analogous to punishment (i.e., direct reciprocity via GRIM) on efficiency depends critically on the population structure and relatedness. When relatedness is low, punishment can sustain high efficiency; when relatedness is high, cooperation is self-sustaining. However, at intermediate relatedness, the threat of punishment is undermined, and efficiency drops. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity and the degree of relatedness (or analogous parameters) can cause non-monotonic effects of punishment on efficiency, and that punishment is most effective at low relatedness. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated PGG-like games with heterogeneity, but less so for one-shot games or games with explicit costly punishment mechanisms.

- source: Chessa, M., & Loiseau, P. (2024). Enhancing voluntary contributions in a public goods economy via a minimum individual contribution level. *Public Choice*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper demonstrates that introducing a minimum individual contribution level (while still allowing for full free riding) in a voluntary public goods game can strictly increase the total contribution and social welfare at equilibrium, provided the minimum is not set too high. The analysis covers both heterogeneous and homogeneous cost cases, and characterizes the equilibrium structure as a function of the minimum. The results show that there is an optimal range for the minimum contribution: if set just above the lowest equilibrium contribution in the baseline game, it increases total contributions without causing contributors to drop out. If set too high, some contributors become free riders and total contributions can decrease. The paper provides operational guidance for setting the minimum but does not address punishment, reward, or other sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is not directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not study punishment or sanctions. However, it is highly informative about the effects of restricting the contribution set by imposing a minimum, which is a different intervention. The findings may be useful for understanding how changes to the action space (such as minimums or defaults) affect efficiency, but cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper should not be used as evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Dughera, S. (2022). The evolution of workplace control leadership, obedience and organizational performance. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The model finds that punishment (authoritarian leadership) can increase organizational performance relative to no control when workers are low-skilled or when monitoring is efficient, but is always less efficient than motivation (authoritative leadership) when workers are high-skilled and supervisors are charismatic. Multiple equilibria are possible: (1) 'Discipline' (universal cooperation with punishment), (2) 'Cooperation' (universal cooperation with motivation), (3) 'Conflict' (universal defection with punishment), and (4) mixed or cyclical regimes. The most efficient outcome is universal cooperation with motivation, but this requires high worker skill and supervisor charisma. Punishment can prevent the spread of shirking (the 'bad company' effect) by increasing procedural justice, but also reduces morale and is less efficient. Inefficient equilibria with widespread punishment and/or shirking can persist due to path dependence. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime is stable and analyzes the welfare properties of each.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in a public-goods-like team production setting, conditional on game design dimensions such as monitoring efficiency (punishment tech), team size (player count), and the presence/absence of reward (motivation/charisma). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency relative to no control when monitoring is effective and workers are not highly skilled, but is always less efficient than a well-functioning reward/motivation regime. The model gives explicit parameter boundaries for when punishment is effective, and highlights the importance of initial conditions and path dependence. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to no-punishment in some parameter regimes (especially with low worker skill and high monitoring efficiency), but that the maximum efficiency is achieved with motivation/charisma rather than punishment. The model is highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings where reward/motivation is not available or not effective.

- source: Sugaya, T., & Wolitzky, A. (2023). Bad apples in symmetric repeated games. *Theoretical Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in large repeated public goods or similar games, the possibility of sustaining cooperation and high efficiency depends critically on the ability to punish defectors specifically. If the only available incentives are rewards or withdrawal of provision (i.e., voluntary mechanisms), then cooperation collapses and efficiency is low when there are even a few commitment types (bad apples) who always defect. Involuntary punishment (such as fines) that can be targeted at defectors restores the possibility of cooperation and high efficiency. The main technical result is that if a 'population dominant' action exists (i.e., defecting yields a higher payoff than the population average, regardless of others' actions), then equilibrium efficiency converges to the inefficient outcome as the population grows. If no such action exists, and punishment can be targeted, then high efficiency is possible. The results are general and apply to a wide class of symmetric repeated games, including public goods games with or without transfers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods and related games. It implies that enabling targeted, involuntary punishment (e.g., fines for non-contributors) is necessary for sustaining high efficiency in large groups with possible commitment types. If only rewards or voluntary mechanisms are available, efficiency will be low in the presence of even a small fraction of defectors. The paper's results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is symmetric, the population is large, and there is a possibility of commitment types. For the prediction task, the presence or absence of targeted punishment (punishment_tech), the nature of the incentive (reward_exists, punishment_cost), and the ability to distinguish defectors are critical moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) will be low in large groups with commitment types unless involuntary punishment is available; enabling such punishment can dramatically increase efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that community enforcement via grim punishment can sustain efficient cooperation in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with random matching, provided the cost of cooperation is not too low or too high. Specifically, if the cost-to-benefit ratio exceeds a threshold (less than 0.5), punishment is always credible and cooperation is an equilibrium for any population size and discount factor. For any environment, there exists a 'target cost of cooperation' that enables coordination on cooperation and makes punishment effective. When the cost of cooperation is endogenous, players can use it as a coordination device to achieve efficient outcomes. The results are entirely theoretical and provide explicit payoff-based conditions for when punishment increases group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma environments with random matching and private monitoring, the introduction of credible punishment (grim trigger) can increase group efficiency to the cooperative level, provided the cost of cooperation is within a specific range. The key moderators are the cost-to-benefit ratio, population size, and discount factor. The results suggest that, for a wide range of parameter values, enabling punishment will move efficiency from the defection baseline to the cooperative optimum. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially the cost of cooperation (analogous to MPCR in PGGs), population size, and the presence of punishment. However, the model is not a literal public goods game but an adjacent repeated PD, so some caution is warranted in direct quantitative transfer.

  findings: The model shows that introducing sanctions (punishment) can increase efficiency in CPR games by enabling the enforcement of rules that prevent over-extraction (static inefficiency) and/or promote conservation (dynamic inefficiency). The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the strength of the sanctioning mechanism relative to the incentives to deviate from cooperation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (sanctions) on efficiency in CPR-like games. If sanctions are strong enough relative to the incentives to deviate, enabling rules can be enforced and efficiency increases. If not, efficiency may not improve, or may even decrease if resource value/growth increases incentives to defect. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the logic of how punishment affects efficiency is highly transferable. The paper is especially informative for settings where the punishment mechanism is exogenous and bounded, and where resource dynamics (growth, conservation) are important. For prediction, the model suggests that the effect of enabling punishment depends on whether the sanctioning parameters cross key thresholds relative to the game's other parameters.

  findings: The paper finds that in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games with random matching and temporary partnerships, combining direct punishment (within partnerships) and cyclical community punishment (across partnerships) can support full cooperation (efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, but only for sufficiently patient players (high discount factor). The milder cyclical punishment is less effective at deterring deviations in equilibrium (requires higher patience) but more effective at incentivizing punishment off-equilibrium (punishment is less costly, so more credible). In contrast, the classic grim punishment can sustain cooperation for less patient players when the temptation payoff is low, but fails when temptation is high. The length of partnerships (number of repeated rounds with the same partner) affects these thresholds: longer partnerships make cooperation harder to sustain for impatient players but make punishment more credible for patient players. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on how the structure of repeated interactions (number of rounds per partnership, random rematching frequency), the type of punishment available (direct, community, grim), and player patience (discount factor) interact to determine whether punishment can sustain efficient (fully cooperative) outcomes. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling direct punishment (via temporary partnerships) can increase the set of conditions under which efficiency is sustainable, but only if players are sufficiently patient. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency depends on the interplay between the temptation payoff, the cost/structure of punishment, and the frequency of rematching. The results are most directly applicable to adjacent settings (repeated PD with random matching), but the logic may transfer to PGG-like environments with similar punishment structures. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or lab data, but its explicit equilibrium conditions and comparative statics are informative for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency.

The effect is more pronounced when punishment is mild or infrequent, as real human players may overweight the risk of being punished. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the payoff-based outcomes are directly analyzed. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of punishment (even with low probability or low severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, particularly if the control game has low efficiency due to free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of psychological factors (probability weighting) in moderating the effect of punishment.

- source: Pei, H. (2024). Reputation Effects under Short Memories. *Journal of Political Economy*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated games with a patient player and a sequence of short-run players who observe only summary statistics of the last K actions (not the full sequence), the patient player can always secure at least his commitment payoff by building a reputation, regardless of K. However, the welfare of the short-run players (i.e., group efficiency) is maximized in all equilibria only if K is below a threshold (cutoff). For K above the cutoff, there exist equilibria where the short-run players' welfare is strictly below first-best, because longer memory makes it harder to motivate short-run players to punish deviations. Thus, increasing the memory window K can have a non-monotonic or even negative effect on group efficiency. The results are robust to various extensions, including imperfect monitoring and coarser information structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that, in repeated games with reputation and limited memory, the ability of short-run players to punish deviations (and thus sustain high efficiency) depends critically on the memory length K. If K is too large, the threat of punishment becomes less effective, and group efficiency can fall below first-best in some equilibria. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that in environments where punishment is implemented via reputation and memory is limited to summary statistics, increasing the memory window does not necessarily improve efficiency and may even reduce it. The results are most directly relevant to settings where efficiency is determined by the ability to sustain cooperation through reputation and punishment, rather than direct PGG lab experiments. The findings are less directly applicable to standard PGGs with explicit punishment stages, but the mechanism insights are informative for predicting efficiency in repeated or reputation-based games.
