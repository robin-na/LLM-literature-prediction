- source: Rosas, A. (2008). The return of reciprocity: a psychological approach to the evolution of cooperation. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the efficiency of punishment in sustaining cooperation is contingent on the method of punishment and the group context. Direct costly punishment (TPP) can result in over-punishment (wasting resources) when there are many cooperators and few defectors, and under-punishment when defectors are numerous. Exclusion-based punishment (TPR) is more stable and efficient, provided defectors cannot exploit cooperators. The author argues that humans are motivated by a generalized reciprocity norm that underlies both punishment and retaliation, and that agents will adapt their punishment strategy to maximize efficiency. Theoretical predictions are made for experimental settings, suggesting that people will avoid over-punishment and switch methods when possible.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games based on game design dimensions. It highlights that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the punishment method (direct costly vs. exclusion), the ratio of cooperators to defectors, and the possibility of defectors exploiting cooperators. If the game allows for exclusion or reputation-based punishment, efficiency is likely to be higher and more stable. If only direct costly punishment is available, efficiency may decrease due to over- or under-punishment, especially as group composition changes. The paper suggests that agents are sensitive to efficiency and will adapt their behavior if given a choice of punishment method. These insights should inform predictions about treatment efficiency given control efficiency and game design features.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Rusch, H. (2012). An evolutionary perspective on the long-term efficiency of costly punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in standard laboratory PGGs, punishment often appears less efficient than no-punishment treatments in the short run, as both the punisher and punished incur costs. However, reanalysis of multiple studies shows that, over time, especially in longer games (e.g., 20 or 50 rounds) and in partner (fixed group) settings, efficiency in punishment treatments increases and can surpass that of non-punishment treatments. This is attributed to declining punishment expenditures as cooperation stabilizes. The effect is stronger when punishment is more effective (greater impact per unit cost) and when groups are stable. In contrast, in short games or stranger-matching designs, punishment remains inefficient. Real-world evidence from common pool resource management and hunter-gatherer societies supports the idea that punishment, especially when low-cost and embedded in long-term relationships, is a key to efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment, this paper suggests that the time horizon (number of rounds), group stability (partner vs. stranger matching), and punishment effectiveness are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) is inefficient and the design allows for repeated interactions with stable groups and effective punishment, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency in the long run, despite initial costs. However, if the game is short, groups are unstable, or punishment is weak/expensive, punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency. The paper provides indirect but strong support for including time horizon, group structure, and punishment parameters as key predictors in models of punishment effects on efficiency.

- source: Kraak, S. B. M. (2011). Exploring the 'public goods game' model to overcome the Tragedy of the Commons in fisheries management. *Fish and Fisheries*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods game experiments, the option for costly punishment increases cooperation and, after initial costs, leads to higher average payoffs and group efficiency. When punishment is combined with mechanisms for reputation-building (e.g., non-anonymity, indirect reciprocity), cooperation is further enhanced and the use of punishment becomes more targeted and less frequent, reducing its destructive impact. The author argues that these findings are directly relevant for designing fisheries management institutions: enabling peer monitoring, punishment, and communication can foster cooperation and improve collective outcomes. However, the paper also cautions that externally imposed sanctions may undermine intrinsic motivation to cooperate, and that institutional legitimacy and stakeholder involvement are crucial.
  prediction_guidance: This paper should inform predictions that enabling punishment in public goods game-like environments (including fisheries) generally increases efficiency and group payoff, especially when combined with mechanisms for reputation and communication. The evidence is based on a synthesis of experimental studies, not new data, but the translation to fisheries is conceptually strong. The paper highlights that the effect of punishment is most positive when it is peer-driven, combined with transparency and communication, and not imposed externally. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment, reputation, and communication mechanisms should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control games without these features.

- source: Raihani, N., & Aitken, D. (2011). Uncertainty, rationality and cooperation in the context of climate change. *Climatic Change*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists
  findings: The paper argues that cooperation in large-scale social dilemmas like climate change can be promoted by mechanisms that increase the net benefit of cooperation relative to free-riding, such as punishment, rewards, communication, and reputation. It highlights that uncertainty and irrational responses (e.g., loss aversion, framing effects) can inhibit cooperation. The authors review evidence that punishment and reward can both increase cooperation in public goods games, but that punishment is costly and may only be effective once cooperation is already established, while rewards may be more effective in early stages. Communication and information about others' actions also increase cooperation. The paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, but suggests that these mechanisms can indirectly improve collective outcomes by increasing cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based support for the idea that punishment, reward, communication, and framing can increase cooperation in public goods games and climate change contexts. However, it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or payoff changes due to punishment. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the presence of punishment (especially when combined with rewards and communication) is likely to increase cooperation, but the effect on efficiency is not directly addressed. The review highlights that punishment is more effective when cooperation is already high, and that rewards may be more effective in low-cooperation settings. The paper is useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators but does not provide direct empirical input for efficiency prediction.

- source: Woodcock, S., & Heath, J. (2002). The robustness of altruism as an evolutionary strategy. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment mechanisms, such as enforcement and ostracism, dramatically increase the robustness of altruism in evolutionary simulations of the prisoner's dilemma. While group selection and imitation can help sustain altruism under certain conditions (e.g., small group size, high imitation rates), punishment allows altruism to persist in much larger groups and under a wider range of conditions. The key mechanism is that punishment increases correlation among altruists, allowing them to interact more with each other and less with defectors. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes; it focuses on the evolutionary persistence of altruism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment mechanisms can greatly increase the persistence of altruism (cooperation) in evolutionary environments similar to the prisoner's dilemma. However, because the main outcome is the proportion of altruists rather than group efficiency or payoff, the findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency in public goods games. The results suggest that, all else equal, adding punishment mechanisms is likely to increase cooperation and thus could plausibly increase efficiency, but the paper does not quantify this effect in payoff terms. The design dimensions most relevant here are the presence and effectiveness of punishment, group size, and the structure of interaction (e.g., group partitioning, imitation).

- source: Sripada, C. S. (2005). Punishment and the strategic structure of moral systems. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is a more general and empirically plausible mechanism for sustaining compliance with moral norms than reciprocity. Punishment can support cooperation in large groups and can enforce arbitrary rules, not just those with a cooperative payoff structure. The author reviews theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and ethnographic/experimental evidence to support the claim that punishment, including direct, exclusion-based, and reputation-based forms, is universal in human groups. The paper also discusses the problem of stabilizing costly punishment and the implications for understanding moral diversity. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental results on efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the importance of punishment mechanisms in sustaining cooperation and compliance in public-goods-like environments, especially in large groups. It suggests that, in theory, enabling punishment should increase compliance and cooperation, and thus could improve efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff outcomes. The arguments are useful for understanding why punishment may be effective, but the paper does not supply empirical data or parameterized results that can be directly used for predicting efficiency changes in experimental public goods games. Its value is mainly in supporting the general plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation and norm compliance, especially in large groups.

- source: Nakao, H., & Machery, E. (2012). The evolution of punishment. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that punishment is widespread in nature and often evolves for reasons other than inducing behavioral change in the punishee. In many cases (especially among plants and insects), punishment is a loss-cutting or cost-imposing strategy, not a behavior-modification strategy. Even among vertebrates, where behavioral modification is more common, the evolutionary benefits of punishment often do not depend on the punishee changing its behavior. The paper challenges the view that punishment primarily evolved to promote cooperation or efficiency, and instead highlights alternative evolutionary pathways.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative data relevant to predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It does, however, offer theoretical context suggesting that the evolutionary function of punishment may not always be to increase cooperation or group efficiency, but could instead be to reduce harm to the punisher or its kin. For the prediction task, this paper suggests caution in assuming that punishment mechanisms will always improve efficiency or cooperation, especially in settings where behavioral modification is unlikely or where punishment serves other evolutionary functions. However, it does not provide empirical or experimental data to calibrate such predictions.

- source: Rosas, A. (2008). Multilevel selection and human altruism. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that the evolution of human altruism depends on the degree to which individuals (or their social/psychological mechanisms) can control assortative grouping, such as by punishing defectors and maintaining group cohesion. It claims that in human societies, mechanisms like punishment, reputation, and social norms can, in principle, prevent subversion from within and make altruism individually adaptive. The discussion references public goods games and the importance of punishment for sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and social mechanisms might enable stable cooperation in public goods settings by engineering assortative grouping and deterring free-riders. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or variants. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting the importance of mechanisms (like punishment and reputation) that allow groups to control subversion from within, suggesting that such mechanisms could, in principle, improve group outcomes. For direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment in experimental public goods games, this paper is not directly informative.

- source: Bicchieri, C., Duffy, J., & Tolle, G. (2004). Trust among strangers. *Philosophy of Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in one-shot trust games, strategies that do not trust or reciprocate dominate, resulting in low average payoffs. When the game is repeated for more rounds (increasing the shadow of the future), conditional strategies that punish defection (e.g., grim trigger, hopeful) become viable and eventually dominate the population, leading to the emergence of a norm of trust and reciprocation and higher average payoffs. The transition from non-cooperation to cooperation occurs as the number of rounds increases beyond a threshold (typically r > 2 or 3). The stable outcome is a polymorphic population of conditional strategies supporting trust/reciprocation. The results are robust to initial conditions and to the inclusion of stochasticity in the evolutionary process.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated trust-game-like environments, the length of the repeated interaction (number of rounds) is a critical moderator of the emergence of cooperation and higher efficiency. When the game is one-shot or very short, efficiency remains low due to dominance of non-cooperative strategies. As the number of rounds increases, conditional strategies that implement punishment by withholding trust/reciprocation after defection can support the emergence of trust and higher efficiency. The findings suggest that, in the absence of explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, repeated interaction alone can enable the emergence of efficient, cooperative outcomes if conditional strategies are available. The model does not directly manipulate or analyze explicit punishment cost or technology, but the logic of conditional strategies is analogous to endogenous punishment via future play. The results are most relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated trust games as a function of the number of rounds and the available strategy set.

- source: CAMPBELL, D. T. (1991). A NATURALISTIC THEORY OF ARCHAIC MORAL ORDERS. *Zygon*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists
  findings: The paper theorizes that the universal presence of supernatural sanction systems (rewarding and punishing afterlives) in archaic civilizations functioned to curb individual selfishness and support group-level cooperation, overcoming the problem of genetic competition among cooperators. It suggests that such beliefs and associated costly rituals (e.g., wasteful royal funerals) reinforced the credibility of these sanction systems, thereby enhancing group cohesion and effectiveness. The argument is that these group-level cultural adaptations, including moral norms and invisible sanction systems, were selected because they increased the survival and success of social groups, even if they imposed costs on individuals. However, the paper does not provide empirical evidence or quantitative predictions about efficiency or payoff effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that sanction systems (punishment and reward, especially supernatural or invisible ones) can enhance group-level cooperation and cohesion, potentially increasing group effectiveness or efficiency in a broad sense. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence that can be directly used to predict the efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games or similar environments. Its relevance is mainly conceptual, supporting the plausibility of positive effects of sanction systems on group outcomes, but it does not inform specific game design dimensions or control efficiency values.

- source: Dugatkin, L. A. (2002). Cooperation in animals: An evolutionary overview. *Biology & Philosophy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that cooperation in animals can be explained by several evolutionary mechanisms, including kin selection, reciprocity, byproduct mutualism, and group selection. Punishment is highlighted as an important mechanism that can promote and stabilize cooperation, especially in larger groups where cooperation would otherwise be difficult to sustain. The discussion of punishment draws on theoretical models (e.g., Boyd and Richerson 1992) and qualitative animal examples (e.g., macaques, vervet monkeys, wasps), but does not provide quantitative data on efficiency or group payoff. The paper suggests that the presence of punishment or retribution in models makes cooperation more likely, but does not quantify the effect in public-goods-game-like terms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and qualitative support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation and stabilize prosocial behavior in group settings, including those analogous to public goods games. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence or direct predictions about efficiency or group payoff outcomes with and without punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the plausibility of positive effects of punishment on cooperation, especially in larger groups, but does not inform the magnitude or specific moderators of efficiency effects in experimental public goods games.
