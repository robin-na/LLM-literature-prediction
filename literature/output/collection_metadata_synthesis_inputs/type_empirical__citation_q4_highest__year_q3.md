- source: Arechar, A. A., Gächter, S., & Molleman, L. (2018). Conducting interactive experiments online. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that enabling peer punishment in a repeated four-person public goods game (20 rounds: 10 without, 10 with punishment) substantially increases average contributions and group efficiency in both laboratory and online (MTurk) samples. In the no-punishment condition, average contributions and earnings are lower and decay over time. When punishment is introduced, contributions jump and are sustained at higher levels, and average earnings per participant per period increase (lab: 22.64 to 26.91; online: 22.64 to 26.91, as reported). Punishment is mostly prosocial (cooperators punish defectors), and the effect is robust across both environments. The paper also finds that attrition (dropout) is not related to experimental condition or behavior, supporting the internal validity of the results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with peer punishment (cost 1, impact 3), enabling punishment increases group efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the maximum possible) compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust across both lab and online samples, and the design matches canonical PGG-with-punishment studies. The paper's detailed reporting of game parameters, outcome measures, and the direct comparison of control and punishment conditions make it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Dannenberg, A., & Gallier, C. (2020). The choice of institutions to solve cooperation problems: a survey of experimental research. *Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The review finds that (1) punishment institutions, when adopted, reliably increase both cooperation and group efficiency/payoff in public goods and related games; (2) the effect is strongest when the institution eliminates free-riding incentives and is not too costly; (3) the popularity and effectiveness of punishment institutions increase with repeated choice and learning; (4) endogenously chosen punishment institutions often yield higher efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, especially for weaker institutions, but the aggregate benefit of endogenous choice is ambiguous because some groups fail to implement the institution; (5) institutional cost and incomplete elimination of free-riding are key barriers to adoption and effectiveness; (6) most studies report both behavioral and payoff outcomes, with efficiency and group payoff being central dependent variables.
  prediction_guidance: This review provides strong evidence that, in public goods game-like environments, enabling punishment mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and payoffs, especially when the punishment is effective (eliminates free-riding) and not too costly. The effect is robust across a variety of game designs, player counts, and institutional choice mechanisms. For prediction, the presence of a punishment institution should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, with the magnitude moderated by punishment cost, effectiveness, and whether the institution is adopted. The review also highlights that repeated exposure, information, and learning opportunities increase the likelihood of institution adoption and thus efficiency gains. However, if the institution is costly or does not fully deter free-riding, efficiency gains may be limited or absent. The review's synthesis of many studies with detailed design features makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Pleasant, A., & Barclay, P. (2018). Why Hate the Good Guy? Antisocial Punishment of High Cooperators Is Greater When People Compete To Be Chosen. *Psychological Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that when participants in a public goods game with punishment are competing to be chosen for a subsequent trust game, antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is much higher than in a standard PGG with punishment. This effect is present from the first round and persists across rounds. In the control condition, contributions increase over rounds (as is typical when punishment is available), but in the markets condition, contributions do not increase, suggesting that antisocial punishment suppresses the escalation of cooperation. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoffs, but the behavioral pattern implies that the efficiency gains typically associated with punishment may be undermined or reversed in contexts with high antisocial punishment driven by partner competition.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the context of partner competition (biological markets) can substantially increase antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, which in turn suppresses the usual increase in cooperation seen with punishment. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings imply that in environments where partner choice or competitive altruism is salient, enabling punishment may not improve and may even harm group efficiency, as antisocial punishment undermines cooperation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of partner competition or similar social comparison incentives is a critical moderator: in such settings, the efficiency benefit of punishment is likely reduced or reversed, even if control efficiency is known. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits quantitative prediction.

- source: Lindström, B., Jangard, S., Selbing, I., & Olsson, A. (2018). The Role of a Common Is Moral Heuristic in the Stability and Change of Moral Norms. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across nine experiments, participants observing one-shot PGGs judged common behaviors (whether altruistic or selfish) as more moral and less deserving of punishment than rare behaviors. This effect was robust across lab and online samples, and held for both preexisting moral norms (cooperation/selfishness) and arbitrary behaviors. In one experiment, participants could spend real money to punish, and commonness reduced costly punishment of selfishness. The effect of commonness was explained by a formal model of social influence (Social Impact Theory). Agent-based simulations showed that the CIM heuristic can generate both stability and sudden change in group-level moral norms. However, the study does not measure or report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, and thus does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGGs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived commonness of behaviors in PGGs shapes moral and punishment judgments, and that common selfishness is punished less than rare selfishness. It also shows that costly punishment is reduced when selfishness is common. However, because the experiments are one-shot and do not measure group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare, the findings do not directly inform the prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled. The results are most useful for understanding psychological and behavioral responses to norm violation and punishment, rather than for predicting efficiency effects of punishment interventions in repeated or strategic PGGs.

- source: Yang, C. L., Zhang, B., Charness, G., Li, C., & Lien, J. W. (2018). Endogenous rewards promote cooperation. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The endogenous, budget-balanced reward mechanism in a repeated four-player PGG leads to a substantial and sustained increase in contributions compared to the standard PGG. The mechanism works by taxing each player's income and allowing players to allocate their share of the tax fund as rewards to others. Contributions remain high and even increase over time with rewards, while they decline in the control. The paper finds that most subjects are conditional cooperators, and that reward assignment is strongly correlated with contribution. Net profits (payoffs) are higher under the reward mechanism than in the control, and the mechanism is more efficient than punishment-based mechanisms reported in the literature, which often fail to increase net payoffs due to the cost of punishment. The study does not test punishment directly, but positions endogenous reward as a more efficient and socially desirable alternative.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that a budget-balanced, endogenous reward mechanism can substantially increase both contributions and net payoffs in a repeated PGG compared to a standard control. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling such a reward mechanism (with a 20% tax, peer allocation, and 1:1 reward effectiveness) will lead to higher efficiency than the control, and likely higher than typical punishment treatments (based on literature comparisons). However, the paper does not provide direct evidence on punishment, so its value for predicting the effect of punishment per se is indirect: it shows that reward can outperform punishment in terms of efficiency, especially when punishment is costly. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, feedback, etc.) are well specified and can inform parameterization for similar prediction tasks involving reward mechanisms, but not for punishment.

- source: Lohse, J., & Waichman, I. (2020). The effects of contemporaneous peer punishment on cooperation with the future. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a multigenerational public goods game, voluntary cooperation is difficult to sustain across generations. When peer punishment is available within a generation, the likelihood that the group meets the contribution threshold and sustains future generations more than doubles compared to the baseline. However, the increase in group earnings from more sustained generations is offset by the costs incurred through punishment, resulting in no significant difference in average aggregate earnings (efficiency) between the punishment and no-punishment conditions. The effect of punishment on individual contribution levels is small, but it is sufficient to increase the rate at which groups meet the threshold. Punishment is mainly directed at those who contribute less than their peers, especially when the group fails to reach the threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling peer punishment in a threshold-based, multigenerational public goods game increases the probability of group success (i.e., reaching future generations and thus group-level payoff), but the efficiency gains are offset by the costs of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of peer punishment can increase the likelihood of group success, but unless punishment is low-cost or highly effective, the net effect on efficiency may be limited or null. The design dimensions—such as group size, threshold, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the absence of communication—are all well-specified and should be used to inform predictions. The results are most relevant for settings with threshold public goods, no communication, and contemporaneous peer punishment.

- source: Gross, J., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2019). Individual solutions to shared problems create a modern tragedy of the commons. *Science Advances*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that when individuals can choose between solving a shared problem collectively (public good) or individually (private solution), many opt for the individual solution even when collective action would be more efficient. This leads to inefficient resource allocation and coordination failure, especially at intermediate cost-benefit ratios. When peer punishment is introduced, it increases the rate of collective solutions but also triggers mutual punishment between individualists and collectivists, which reduces overall group welfare. Punishment does not reduce free riding but instead is used in feuds between incompatible strategies, amplifying inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that in public-goods-like games where individual solutions are available, enabling peer punishment can reduce efficiency (group welfare), even if it increases collective action. The effect is driven by wasteful punishment exchanges rather than improved cooperation. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency in similar settings, especially when individual solutions are a viable alternative to collective action. The findings are most relevant when the game allows both individual and collective solutions, and punishment is peer-based, costly, and has a strong impact.

- source: Barclay, P., & Barker, J. L. (2020). Greener Than Thou: People who protect the environment are more cooperative, compete to be environmental, and benefit from reputation. *Journal of Environmental Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that (1) people who engage in more pro-environmental behaviors are more likely to cooperate in a continuous Prisoner's Dilemma; (2) people donate more to an environmental charity when their donations are observed, and even more when competing to be chosen as a partner; (3) observers preferentially choose and cooperate more with those who donated more to the environment; (4) the correlation between environmentalism and cooperation is partially mediated by concern for others. The study does not include punishment or sanctions, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide informative evidence about the effects of observability and partner choice (reputation/competition) on cooperative and pro-environmental behavior in a continuous Prisoner's Dilemma. For prediction tasks focused on punishment, this paper is not directly relevant. For prediction tasks involving the effects of observability or partner choice on cooperation, the findings suggest that such mechanisms can increase cooperative behaviors, but no efficiency or payoff data are available.

- source: Li, X. L., Jusup, M., Wang, Z., Li, H. J., Shi, L., Podobnik, B., Stanley, H. E., Havlin, S., & Boccaletti, S. (2018). Punishment diminishes the benefits of network reciprocity in social dilemma experiments. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment compared three treatments: (1) a well-mixed repeated PD (control), (2) a static network PD with two neighbors (network reciprocity), and (3) a static network PD with punishment available. Network reciprocity increased both cooperation and payoff relative to the well-mixed control. However, adding punishment did not further increase cooperation and actually reduced the median payoff per round back to the control level. Punishment also reduced assortment (clustering of cooperators) and, in larger neighborhoods, erased the positive correlation between cooperation and payoff. Thus, punishment in this setting is ineffective or even counterproductive for group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated prisoner's dilemma games on static networks (with two or four neighbors), enabling costly punishment (with a 1:4 cost-to-impact ratio) does not improve and may even reduce group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The negative effect is especially clear in payoff-based outcomes: median payoff per round is lower with punishment than without, and the benefit of network reciprocity is erased. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar networked PD or adjacent PGG-like environments, adding punishment may not increase efficiency and could reduce it, especially as neighborhood size increases. The findings are most directly applicable to static network PDs with small groups, no communication, and fixed payoff matrices.

- source: Dhaliwal, N. A., Patil, I., & Cushman, F. (2021). Reputational and cooperative benefits of third-party compensation. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The main finding is that third-party compensation leads to greater reputational and partner choice benefits than third-party punishment, as judged by observers in hypothetical scenarios and vignettes. Compensators are seen as more moral, trustworthy, and are more often chosen as partners in economic games (Trust Game, Dictator Game). These effects are robust across different contexts, including workplace and real-life vignettes. The paper also finds that compensators tend to have lower Dark Triad personality traits. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, and does not provide evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or third-party punishment games. Its findings are relevant for understanding reputational and partner choice consequences of punishment versus compensation, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes. The game design dimensions are described in detail for the hypothetical games, but the dependent variables are not payoff-based. Therefore, this paper should not be used as primary evidence for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency.

- source: Molho, C., Tybur, J. M., Van Lange, P. A. M., & Balliet, D. (2020). Direct and indirect punishment of norm violations in daily life. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The study finds that people frequently use both direct and indirect punishment strategies in response to norm violations in daily life. Direct confrontation is more likely when the punisher has been personally victimized, has more power, or values the offender more. Indirect punishment (gossip, social exclusion) is more likely when the violation is severe, the punisher has less power, or values the offender less. Emotional responses (anger, disgust) also differentially predict punishment type. The findings support the idea that punishment is context-sensitive and motivated by both the benefits of changing others' behavior and the risks of retaliation. However, the study does not measure or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or any economic outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar economic games. Its findings are relevant for understanding the psychological and contextual determinants of punishment strategy in naturalistic settings, but not for quantifying the impact of punishment on efficiency. The study may be useful for mechanism discussions or for understanding when punishment is likely to be deployed, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Pedersen, E. J., McAuliffe, W. H. B., & McCullough, M. E. (2018). The Unresponsive Avenger: More Evidence That Disinterested Third Parties Do Not Punish Altruistically. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Across five lab experiments, participants who were directly harmed (insulted) by another person reliably punished the insulter using an aversive sound blast. Participants who witnessed a friend being insulted also punished the insulter, but less strongly. However, participants who witnessed a stranger being insulted generally did not punish the insulter more than they punished uninvolved bystanders, except in one experiment where social censure may have been a factor. The results suggest that third-party punishment is not a robust, general tendency, but is contingent on welfare interdependence (e.g., self or friends). The findings cast doubt on the external validity of economic game results that report high rates of third-party punishment, suggesting those may be inflated by experimental demand. No efficiency or payoff-based outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or group payoff in public goods games with or without punishment. Its main contribution is to question the generalizability of third-party punishment effects observed in economic games, suggesting that such effects may be overestimated due to experimental demand. For the downstream prediction task, this paper signals that the presence of punishment opportunities does not guarantee increased norm enforcement or efficiency, especially when punishment is not personally relevant or when welfare interdependence is low. However, because no efficiency or payoff outcomes are measured, the paper cannot inform quantitative predictions of efficiency changes due to punishment in PGG-like environments.

  findings: The paper finds strong ethnic asymmetries in social norm enforcement: native Germans are more likely to sanction norm violations, and ethnic minorities are more likely to be the targets of sanctions. These patterns are interpreted as resulting from status inequalities between groups. The aggregate probability of being sanctioned for a norm violation (social control) increases with the share of minorities in the population, but this is driven by natives sanctioning minorities at higher rates. The study does not address efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and is not a public goods game or direct variant, but is adjacent in that it studies real-world norm enforcement and punishment behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence about real-world punishment (sanctioning) behavior in response to norm violations, with a focus on ethnic asymmetries. It does not use a public goods game or measure efficiency or payoff outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments. However, it may be useful for understanding how social context (e.g., group identity, status, diversity) can moderate the frequency and direction of punishment, which could be relevant for interpreting or designing PGG experiments with diverse populations. The lack of payoff or efficiency outcomes limits its direct predictive value for the downstream task.

It does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public goods games or similar environments. However, it suggests that impersonal (altruistic/third-party) punishment is more prevalent in societies with loose kinship ties and universal values, which are theoretically linked to higher efficiency in broad cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper is most useful for understanding how cultural and institutional context may moderate the effectiveness of punishment, but it does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency effects in controlled game settings.

- source: Fonseca, M. A., & Peters, K. (2018). Will any gossip do? Gossip does not need to be perfectly accurate to promote trust. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that enabling gossip (reputational information transmission) in a repeated trust game increases both trust and trustworthiness, leading to higher efficiency (joint payoffs) compared to a no-message baseline. Even when gossip is substantially inaccurate (either endogenously or exogenously), efficiency remains higher than in the absence of gossip, though it is lower than with accurate information. The effect is robust across treatments, with the largest efficiency gains observed when information is accurate, but meaningful gains persist even with high inaccuracy. The mechanism is reputational concern and discrimination, not direct costly punishment. The study provides direct evidence that non-monetary, reputation-based social sanctions (gossip) can substitute for formal punishment in promoting efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a reputational mechanism (gossip) in repeated trust-based interactions increases efficiency, even when the information is not perfectly accurate. However, the mechanism is not direct costly punishment, but rather indirect social sanctioning via reputation. The findings are most directly applicable to trust games and adjacent to public goods games, especially in settings where partner choice or discrimination is possible. The results suggest that even imperfect reputation systems can yield substantial efficiency gains, and that the absence of any reputational channel is particularly detrimental to efficiency.
