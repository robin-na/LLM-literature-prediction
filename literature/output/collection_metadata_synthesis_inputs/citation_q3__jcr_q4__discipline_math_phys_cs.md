- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

- source: Deng, K. Y., Li, Z. Z., Kurokawa, S., & Chu, T. G. (2012). Rare but severe concerted punishment that favors cooperation. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that implementing rare but severe concerted punishment in a Public Goods game can make cooperation evolutionarily stable and increase group efficiency. The threshold probability for punishment to be effective decreases as the severity of punishment increases and as the population size increases. The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

- source: Thöni, C. (2014). Inequality aversion and antisocial punishment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper shows that, while inequality aversion can theoretically motivate antisocial punishment under certain conditions, empirical data from several public goods game experiments indicate that most antisocial punishment acts do not fit this explanation. Instead, antisocial punishment is often carried out by the richest group members and is not typically accompanied by punishment of free riders, contrary to the predictions of the inequality aversion model. The findings suggest that other motives, such as revenge or a desire to harm others, are more likely explanations for antisocial punishment. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff, but notes that antisocial punishment is detrimental to the efficiency of informal punishment mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms behind antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment, but it does not provide direct quantitative evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. It does, however, reinforce the point that the presence and frequency of antisocial punishment can undermine the positive effects of punishment on cooperation and efficiency, especially in environments where antisocial punishment is prevalent. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game design features or subject pool characteristics that foster antisocial punishment may reduce or even reverse the efficiency gains from enabling punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, so it cannot be used to calibrate quantitative predictions of efficiency changes.

- source: Kurokawa, S., Wakano, J. Y., & Ihara, Y. (2010). Generous cooperators can outperform non-generous cooperators when replacing a population of defectors. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player public goods games, reciprocators who are 'generous' (i.e., tolerate some defection before retaliating) can outperform less generous reciprocators and defectors in terms of average payoff and fixation probability, especially when group size is at least 4. The optimal generosity level depends on the benefit-to-cost ratio and group size. Generosity facilitates the emergence of cooperation (replacement of defectors by reciprocators) but does not help maintain cooperation once established. The model provides explicit conditions for when generosity is advantageous and quantifies the optimal generosity for maximizing the likelihood of cooperation's evolutionary success.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with n ≥ 4, the design of reciprocation strategies (specifically, the degree of generosity/tolerance for defection) can significantly affect the efficiency outcome when cooperation is replacing defection. While the model does not include explicit punishment mechanisms (such as costly punishment), it analyzes a form of contingent cooperation that functions as a sanctioning mechanism (withholding cooperation from defectors). The findings suggest that, for predicting efficiency in games with repeated interaction and contingent cooperation, allowing some tolerance for defection (generosity) can increase the likelihood of efficient cooperative outcomes, especially in the emergence phase. However, the model does not address explicit costly punishment or its direct efficiency effects, so its relevance to games with explicit punishment is adjacent rather than exact.

- source: Drouvelis, M., Metcalfe, R., & Powdthavee, N. (2015). Can priming cooperation increase public good contributions?. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that priming participants with cooperative concepts (via a word search) leads to significantly higher contributions in a one-shot public goods game compared to a neutrally primed control group. The effect is robust to controls for gender, nationality, and field of study, and does not appear to be mediated by beliefs about others' contributions. The main outcome is contribution level; average earnings are reported but not analyzed in detail. No punishment, reward, or sanction mechanisms are present.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism. Its findings are not directly transferable to the prediction of efficiency with or without punishment. However, it does provide evidence that subtle environmental interventions (like priming) can increase contributions in a standard linear PGG, which may be contextually relevant for understanding other non-incentive-based interventions. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not informative about punishment effects.

- source: Martinsson, P., Villegas-Palacio, C., & Wollbrant, C. (2015). Cooperation and social classes: evidence from Colombia. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that both high and medium-low social class groups contribute about 40% of their endowment in a one-shot public goods game, with no significant difference in unconditional contributions or beliefs about others' contributions. The distribution of contributor types (conditional cooperators, free riders, etc.) is also similar between groups. However, medium-low social class members are more likely to contribute when others contribute nothing (i.e., higher baseline conditional cooperation). The study does not include punishment, reward, or repeated interaction, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism. It does, however, provide a clear description of a standard one-shot linear public goods game with four players, continuous contributions, and MPCR of 0.4, which may be useful as a control or baseline for comparison with studies that do include punishment. The findings on contribution behavior by social class may inform expectations about baseline cooperation in similar environments, but cannot be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Kendal, J., Feldman, M. W., & Aoki, K. (2006). Cultural coevolution of norm adoption and enforcement when punishers are rewarded or non-punishers are punished. *Theoretical Population Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both reward-based and punishment-based metanorms can enable the stable coexistence and fixation of norm-adopting punishers, leading to high group efficiency (mean fitness) under a wide range of parameter values. The presence of a metanorm (either rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) is necessary for this outcome; without it, the only stable equilibrium is full defection (no norm adoption, no punishment). The Reward Model is generally more effective than the Punishment Model in enabling the population to reach the efficient equilibrium, especially when starting from low frequencies of norm-adopters and punishers. The probability of fixation of the efficient equilibrium is higher under the Reward Model, and the region of attraction is larger. The models also show that cultural drift can facilitate the transition from the inefficient to the efficient equilibrium, especially in small populations and when costs of norm observation and punishment are low. The results are robust across a range of parameter values and are relevant to public goods game-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that enabling punishment (especially when reinforced by a metanorm, such as rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) can shift a population from an inefficient equilibrium (low efficiency, no cooperation) to an efficient one (high efficiency, full cooperation and sanctioning), provided the costs and benefits are favorable. The results suggest that, in public goods game-like environments, the introduction of punishment (with or without additional metanorms) can substantially increase group efficiency, especially if the cost of punishing is not too high and the benefit to punishers is sufficient. The Reward Model (rewarding punishers) is particularly effective, suggesting that institutional or social mechanisms that reward sanctioning may be more robust in promoting efficiency than those that only punish non-punishers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the cost/benefit structure and initial conditions. However, the results are theoretical and assume well-mixed populations and specific forms of cultural transmission.

- source: Jaffe, K. (2004). Altruism, altruistic punishment and social investment. *Acta Biotheoretica*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper uses agent-based simulations to examine the effects of altruistic punishment on aggregate wealth (efficiency) in artificial societies. It finds that while punishment increases generosity and norm compliance, it reduces aggregate wealth compared to societies without punishment, unless the punished behaviors trigger synergistic benefits for society. The effect is observed in both structured (with division of labor) and unstructured societies, though structured societies are more susceptible to social control via punishment. The study concludes that punishment is a strong mechanism for norm enforcement but is not, by itself, efficiency-enhancing.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides evidence that enabling punishment in a public-goods-like environment (here, an agent-based resource-sharing simulation) increases norm compliance and generosity but reduces group efficiency (aggregate wealth), unless there are additional synergistic benefits from the enforced behaviors. The negative effect on efficiency is robust to variations in social structure and punishment intensity. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper suggests that, in the absence of explicit synergistic returns, punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to the control. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is costly and does not directly generate group-level surplus.

- source: Blonski, M., & Spagnolo, G. (2015). Prisoners' other Dilemma. *International Journal of Game Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, the riskiness of cooperation equilibria can be precisely characterized by comparing the Nash-products of cooperation and defection equilibria. The least risky cooperation equilibrium is always the grim trigger strategy. There exists a critical discount factor (delta*) above which cooperation can be supported without strategic risk, and below which all cooperation equilibria are risky. The more patient the players (higher discount factor), the more forgiving the punishment strategies can be while still supporting cooperation. The paper also introduces the concept of 'risk perfection', requiring that not only the equilibrium path but also all subgames (punishment phases) are not risky. The results generalize to asymmetric games and to repeated Cournot duopoly (collusion) games, which are structurally similar to public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical guidance on the relationship between punishment harshness, forgiveness, and the viability of cooperation in repeated games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it shows that the ability of punishment to support cooperation depends on the discount factor and the riskiness of the equilibrium. In environments structurally similar to public goods games, the results suggest that harsher punishments (like grim trigger) are less risky and more likely to support cooperation, especially when players are less patient. As patience increases, more forgiving punishments become viable. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the strategic riskiness of the environment and the patience of players, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.
