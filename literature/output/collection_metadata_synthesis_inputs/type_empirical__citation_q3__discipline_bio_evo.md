- source: Skatova, A., & Ferguson, E. (2013). Individual differences in behavioural inhibition explain free riding in public good games when punishment is expected but not implemented. *Behavioral and Brain Functions*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both the implementation and the expectation of punishment (even when not actually implemented) increase individual contributions in a one-shot PGG compared to standard PGGs without punishment. Implemented punishment leads to higher contributions than non-implemented punishment. Individual differences in BIS-anxiety (a measure of behavioral inhibition) predict higher contributions and less free riding when punishment is expected but not implemented. FFFS (flight, fight, freeze system) does not predict contributions. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution levels, free riding), not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that both implemented and expected (but not implemented) centralized punishment increase contributions in a one-shot PGG, with implemented punishment having a stronger effect. However, the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency changes due to punishment. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in contribution rates under different punishment regimes and for understanding the role of individual differences in behavioral inhibition. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling punishment (even if not always implemented) increases cooperation, but the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred from the reported outcomes.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Schroeder, K. B., Nettle, D., & McElreath, R. (2015). Interactions between personality and institutions in cooperative behaviour in humans. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that the opportunity for decentralized punishment in a repeated public goods game increases contributions to the public good compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect is especially pronounced for individuals high in extraversion, who tend to free-ride in the absence of punishment but increase their contributions when punishment is possible. Agreeableness is associated with higher initial contributions regardless of institution, while neuroticism is associated with lower contributions in both conditions. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but the increase in contributions with punishment suggests a likely increase in efficiency, subject to the cost of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game increases contributions, particularly among certain personality types (notably extraverts). However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be quantified from this paper alone. The game design dimensions are well specified, and the punishment mechanism is standard (costly, decentralized, 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio). The findings support the general prediction that punishment increases cooperation, but do not allow direct prediction of efficiency outcomes for the downstream task.

- source: Jolly, E., & Chang, L. J. (2021). Gossip drives vicarious learning and facilitates social connection. *Current Biology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=weak | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Allowing private communication (gossip) in a repeated public goods game increases average group contributions and slows the decline of cooperation over rounds, compared to no-communication conditions. Gossip is especially important when direct observation of others' behavior is limited, as it enables vicarious learning and helps participants adjust their own contributions based on information received from others. Gossip also increases social bonding and alignment of impressions between conversation partners. The study does not include any formal punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling communication (gossip) in public goods games increases contribution rates and sustains cooperation, especially under incomplete information. However, it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on punishment effects or efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that social information transmission (gossip) can substitute for formal sanctions in sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative estimates for efficiency outcomes or for games with punishment enabled. The design dimensions most directly addressed are player count, number of rounds, chat/communication, and information visibility. The absence of punishment and efficiency outcomes limits its direct applicability to the prediction task.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., El Mouden, C., & West, S. A. (2017). Social learning and the demise of costly cooperation in humans. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that providing social information about other groups' behaviors or payoffs does not increase cooperation in repeated public goods games; in most cases, it leads to a significant decline in cooperation. The decline is strongest when individuals can observe the behavior and payoffs of the most successful individuals, supporting the idea of payoff-biased or prestige-biased social learning. There is no evidence for conformity bias (copying common behaviors) or group-prestige bias (copying successful groups). The study does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, and does not report efficiency or group payoff as an outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the design. Its findings are relevant for understanding how social learning and information about others' behavior/payoff affect cooperation, but not for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. For the downstream prediction task, this paper should be considered as not providing evidence about punishment effects, but may be informative about baseline cooperation dynamics in the absence of punishment.

- source: Gatiso, T. T., Vollan, B., & Nuppenau, E. A. (2015). Resource scarcity, and democratic elections in commons dilemmas: An experiment on forest use in Ethiopia. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment shows that resource scarcity (lower initial stock) leads to less cooperative behavior (higher HR), and that the way sanctions are implemented matters: democratically elected sanctions increase cooperation (lower HR) both under abundance and scarcity, while imposed sanctions can reduce cooperation (higher HR) under abundance (crowding-out effect), but not under scarcity. The main outcome is behavioral (HR), not efficiency or group payoff. Theoretical efficiency levels are provided, but not empirically analyzed as outcomes. The findings suggest that participatory rule-making is more effective than top-down sanctions for sustaining cooperation, especially when resources are not extremely scarce.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how the method of sanction implementation (election vs. imposition) moderates the effect of punishment on cooperation in a common-pool resource game with real-world framing. However, because the main outcome is behavioral (harvest ratio) rather than efficiency or group payoff, the results must be mapped with caution to efficiency prediction tasks. Theoretical efficiency levels are given, but empirical results focus on HR. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, no chat, continuous contribution, no explicit MPCR, probabilistic punishment, no rewards, summary info shown, anonymous punishment) are well described, supporting mapping to prediction models. The main implication is that democratically elected sanctions are more likely to increase cooperation (and thus efficiency) than imposed sanctions, especially under resource abundance. Under scarcity, the difference is smaller, but elections still outperform imposition.

- source: Seip, E. C., van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2009). On Hotheads and Dirty Harries The Primacy of Anger in Altruistic Punishment. *Values, Empathy, and Fairness Across Social Barriers*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  findings: The main finding is that anger, rather than the perception of unfairness, is the primary driver of altruistic punishment in social dilemma games. Experimental evidence from a one-shot, three-person public goods game with punishment shows that lower contributions from others evoke more anger and lead to harsher punishment, and that the effect of others' contributions on punishment is fully mediated by experienced anger. However, it does suggest that the presence and intensity of punishment are strongly influenced by emotional responses (especially anger) to defection or low contribution. For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment is likely to be used when players experience anger at norm violations, but the paper does not address whether this increases or decreases group efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment is deployed, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Vollan, B., Prediger, S., & Frölich, M. (2013). Co-managing common-pool resources: Do formal rules have to be adapted to traditional ecological norms?. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This field-lab experiment with communal farmers in southern Africa shows that the efficiency of formal, democratically chosen rules for managing a common-pool resource depends on their alignment with local ecological norms. The quota rule (limiting extraction) increases group earnings and resource availability in both high and low sustainability groups, but is unpopular. The rotation rule only increases efficiency in low sustainability groups (who previously failed to coordinate), but is ineffective or even counterproductive in high sustainability groups (who already tacitly coordinated for sustainability), due to high rates of rule breaking. The lottery rule is ineffective in both types of groups. Rule compliance is lower when rules conflict with local norms, and rule breaking is not mainly motivated by profit. Thus, the effect of rule-based interventions on efficiency is highly context-dependent.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of introducing a formal rule (akin to a punishment or restriction mechanism) on efficiency in a common-pool resource game, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect depends on the compatibility of the rule with pre-existing group norms. Rules that align with local sustainability norms (e.g., quotas in groups that value sustainability) can increase efficiency, while rules that conflict with these norms (e.g., rotation in high-norm groups) may reduce efficiency due to non-compliance. The presence of a sanctioning mechanism (imperfect monitoring and penalty) is not sufficient to ensure compliance or efficiency gains. Thus, the effect of punishment-like interventions on efficiency is moderated by group-level norms and prior behavior, not just by the formal game design. This paper is highly informative for settings where the main intervention is a formal rule or restriction, but less so for classic peer-punishment PGGs.

- source: Wang, X. M., Couto, M. C., Wang, N. Y., An, X. M., Chen, B., Dong, Y. L., Hilbe, C., & Zhang, B. Y. (2023). Cooperation and coordination in heterogeneous populations. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.

The findings highlight the importance of focal points and fairness heuristics in equilibrium selection, and show that evolutionary game theory models may mis-predict outcomes unless they account for participants' initial expectations and heuristics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the experimental design. However, it provides informative context on how inequality (in endowment or productivity) affects coordination and group success in threshold public goods games. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is not directly informative, but it suggests that baseline efficiency (without punishment) is lower under endowment inequality. This could be relevant for understanding the baseline to which punishment effects would be compared in similar asymmetric games.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.

- source: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R. (2010). Self-Serving Punishment of a Common Enemy Creates a Public Good in Reef Fishes. *Current Biology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that (1) punishment by victim fish (chasing the blenny) reduces the likelihood of being attacked again, (2) in shoaling species, this punishment increases the probability that the blenny switches to another species, creating a public good for the group, and (3) blennies can learn to discriminate between punishers and nonpunishers, which selects against free riding. The findings are based on both field observations and controlled laboratory experiments using artificial plates to simulate fish. The outcomes are behavioral and do not include direct measures of group efficiency or total payoff, but the results are relevant to understanding how punishment can create public goods in repeated natural interactions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that in repeated, naturalistic interactions, self-serving punishment can create public good benefits for a group, and that the ability of the 'cheater' (blenny) to identify and target nonpunishers can sustain punishment even in the absence of kin structure. However, the study does not use a formal public goods game, does not manipulate standard game design dimensions (e.g., MPCR, player count, explicit payoff structure), and does not report efficiency or group payoff.
