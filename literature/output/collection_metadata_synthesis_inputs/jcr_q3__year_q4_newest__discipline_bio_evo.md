- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

- source: Duong, M. H., Durbac, C. M., & Han, T. A. (2024). Cost Optimisation of Individual-Based Institutional Reward Incentives for Promoting Cooperation in Finite Populations. *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the expected total cost to the institution for achieving a target cooperation level in finite-population PGGs depends strongly on the threshold for incentive application (t), the strength of selection (β), mutation rate (μ), and the type of incentive (reward, punishment, hybrid). For small thresholds (t=1), the cost function is always non-decreasing in the per-capita incentive cost, and explicit formulas are derived. For larger thresholds, the cost function can exhibit non-monotonic behavior and phase transitions, especially at high selection strength. Numerical results show that both reward and punishment incentives can be optimized for cost, but the optimal cost and its behavior depend on the game parameters and incentive scheme. The paper provides a framework for predicting the institutional cost required to achieve a desired cooperation level, given the game design and population parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and numerical evidence on how the cost of institutional incentives (reward, punishment, hybrid) required to achieve a target cooperation level in PGG-like environments depends on game design dimensions such as player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, and the structure of the incentive scheme (threshold t, cost per unit, effectiveness). While the main outcome is the institutional cost rather than group efficiency, the results are directly relevant for predicting how changes in punishment or reward design (e.g., cost, threshold, effectiveness) will affect the efficiency of institutional intervention. The paper is most informative for settings where the goal is to optimize the cost of achieving cooperation, rather than to maximize group payoff per se. It provides explicit formulas and numerical results that can be used to inform predictions about the efficiency of punishment or reward interventions, conditional on game design and baseline cooperation levels.

Antisocial peer exclusion alone suppresses cooperation, but when both types coexist, prosocial exclusion remains effective at sustaining cooperation through complex coexistence and cyclic dominance patterns.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that prosocial peer exclusion can maintain high levels of cooperation in spatial public goods games, even when antisocial exclusion is possible, except at low exclusion costs. However, because the outcomes are reported as cooperation rates and not as efficiency or group payoff, the direct quantitative effect of exclusion on efficiency cannot be extracted. The results suggest that, in structured populations, enabling prosocial exclusion (a form of targeted sanctioning) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that the negative impact of antisocial exclusion does not fully undermine this effect. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect support: higher cooperation rates likely correspond to higher efficiency, but the mapping is not explicit. The findings are most relevant for settings with spatial structure, peer-based exclusion, and variable exclusion costs.

- source: Kristensen, N. P., Chisholm, R. A., & Ohtsuki, H. (2025). Many-strategy games in groups with relatives and the evolution of coordinated cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that kin selection (homophily) can facilitate the initial invasion of coordinated cooperation in public goods games with nonlinear (sigmoidal) benefits, even when such cooperation cannot invade in a well-mixed population due to cognitive or coordination costs. Once established, coordinated cooperation can persist as homophily declines, provided that the benefit function is sufficiently nonlinear and the switching gain from non-contribution to contribution at the coordination point exceeds the cost. If this condition is not met, some level of homophily must be maintained to prevent the invasion of defectors or 'liars' (who commit to contribute but do not follow through). The framework provides analytical and numerical tools for predicting evolutionary outcomes (including efficiency and group payoff) as a function of group size, relatedness, benefit function nonlinearity, and strategy set. The results are directly relevant to predicting efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but do not address punishment or sanctions directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a general theoretical framework for predicting efficiency and group payoff in public goods games with arbitrary group size, number of strategies, and non-additive (e.g., sigmoidal) benefit functions, under varying levels of kin structure (homophily). It is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency of control games (without punishment) as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR (via the benefit function), and group structure. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The framework could, in principle, be extended to include punishment as a discrete strategy, but this is not done in the present paper. Therefore, the paper is highly informative for predicting baseline efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but provides only indirect or contextual support for predicting the effect of punishment.

- source: Cooney, D. B. (2025). Exploring the Evolution of Altruistic Punishment with a PDE Model of Cultural Multilevel Selection. *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment, when combined with group-level selection, can promote higher levels of cooperation and increase group efficiency compared to scenarios without punishment. However, the effect of punishment on efficiency is sensitive to the cost structure (per-interaction vs. fixed), the strength of punishment, and the form of group-level competition. In some models, increasing punishment strength or cost leads to higher cooperation but lower efficiency, due to the collective cost of punishment outweighing its benefits. The paper provides analytical expressions for the threshold group selection strength needed to sustain cooperation and for the steady-state average payoff, showing that these can depend non-monotonically on punishment parameters. The results highlight that the design of punishment mechanisms and the structure of group competition are critical moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, mechanistic, and quantitative guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like environments with group structure. It shows that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on the cost and strength of punishment, the type of cost (per-interaction or fixed), and the form of group-level competition. For prediction, the results suggest that: (1) punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when costs are per-interaction rather than fixed; (2) increasing punishment strength generally increases cooperation, but may decrease efficiency if costs are high; (3) the effect of punishment on efficiency can be non-monotonic, especially under group-level competition rules that normalize by maximum/minimum group payoffs; (4) the threshold group selection strength required for cooperation is lower with stronger or cheaper punishment. The paper's analytical formulas and numerical results can be used to estimate treatment efficiency from control efficiency and game design parameters, especially in settings with group-structured populations and multilevel selection.

- source: Kurokawa, S. (2023). Evolution of spite in an n-player game with an opting-out option. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated n-player games with an opting-out option, spiteful (costly harming) strategies can sometimes persist, but only under restrictive conditions: low cost-to-harm ratio, small group size, and specific opting-out rules. As group size increases, it becomes harder for spiteful strategies to achieve higher payoffs than nonspiteful strategies, and the basin of attraction for all-nonspiteful populations grows. The opting-out rule (how easily groups disband) is a key moderator: when homogeneous groups (all spiteful or all nonspiteful) are forced to disband, spite can persist more easily, but this is a somewhat artificial rule. The model is mathematically explicit about when spiteful strategies can dominate, and these conditions almost always correspond to lower group efficiency compared to all-nonspiteful populations. The analysis is directly about payoff and efficiency, not just behavior.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in public-goods-like n-player games with an opting-out mechanism, the introduction of costly punishment (here, spiteful harming) generally reduces group efficiency unless the cost-to-harm ratio is extremely low and the group size is small. As group size increases, the conditions for punishment/spite to persist become more restrictive, and group efficiency is more likely to be maximized when punishment/spite is absent. The details of the opting-out rule (e.g., whether homogeneous groups are forced to disband) can moderate this effect, but in most realistic settings, punishment/spite is not efficiency-enhancing. The model is not an exact PGG with standard punishment, but the payoff structure and evolutionary logic are closely related, and the results are directly about payoff/efficiency. Thus, the paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment/spite in large groups with standard cost structures will not increase efficiency, and may reduce it.

- source: Fontanari, J. F., & Santos, M. (2024). Solving the prisoner's dilemma trap in Hamilton's model of temporarily formed random groups. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that in the 'others-only' n-person prisoner's dilemma (a strong altruism scenario), cooperation cannot evolve under asexual reproduction or sexual reproduction with selfing, but can evolve and become stable under biparental sexual reproduction if the group benefit multiplier (r) is sufficiently large relative to group size (n) and the cost of cooperation (c) is not too high. The model does not include punishment or reward, and the main mechanism enabling cooperation is the genetic structure imposed by biparental reproduction. The main outcome is the probability that the population reaches the all-cooperator state (fixation probability), which is a direct measure of group efficiency or welfare. The results are summarized in phase diagrams showing regions where cooperation is stable, bistable, or unstable, as a function of c, r, and n. The findings are robust to demographic noise and extend to dioecious populations. The paper does not provide evidence about the effects of punishment, communication, or other institutional design features.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments that lack punishment or reward mechanisms, especially when the game structure is the 'others-only' variant of the n-person prisoner's dilemma. It shows that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency (measured as fixation of cooperation and group payoff) is only achievable under specific genetic and demographic conditions—namely, biparental sexual reproduction, sufficiently high group benefit multiplier (r), and not-too-large group size (n) or cost (c). The results suggest that, without punishment, efficiency is generally low unless these conditions are met. However, the paper does not provide any evidence about the effect of enabling punishment or sanctions, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the efficiency difference between control and punishment-enabled conditions. Its main value is in establishing a baseline: in the absence of punishment, efficiency is generally low except under the specified genetic/demographic conditions. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it does not manipulate or analyze punishment.

  findings: The paper finds that the efficiency of costly punishment in sustaining cooperation depends critically on the detectability of defection. When defection is difficult to detect (i.e., high probability that defection is misperceived as cooperation), social norms that include costly punishment (CP norms) can be the most efficient ESS, achieving high cooperation and high average payoff. Execution errors and high punishment costs shrink the parameter space where punishment is efficient. The results challenge previous theoretical work that found only a narrow efficiency margin for punishment, showing that under asymmetric detection errors, punishment can be broadly efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, parameterized theoretical predictions for the effect of punishment on efficiency in indirect reciprocity games with pairwise donation structure. While not a standard public goods game, the model is adjacent and the efficiency results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment in environments where defection is hard to detect. The explicit formulas and phase diagrams allow mapping from game design dimensions (punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, error rates) and baseline efficiency to the expected efficiency with punishment enabled. The main moderator is the detectability of defection: when it is low, punishment can substantially increase efficiency; when high, punishment is unnecessary or even harmful. The results are most transferable to settings with pairwise interactions, reputation, and indirect reciprocity, but provide strong mechanistic insight for broader PGG-like environments.

- source: Kroumi, D. (2025). The role of selective extinction in promoting cooperation in a many demes model. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that structuring a population into demes and introducing selective extinction—whereby groups with more defectors are more likely to go extinct—can dramatically increase the evolutionary success (fixation probability) of cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma. Without selective extinction (\alpha=0), defection is always favored, and cooperation cannot persist. As the selective extinction parameter \alpha increases, the fixation probability of cooperation increases, and that of defection decreases. There are explicit threshold values of \alpha (functions of game payoffs and demographic parameters) above which cooperation is favored by selection, and above which it is more likely to fix than defection. The model also shows how the baseline extinction rate (v) and the structure of payoffs (T-R, P-S) modulate these thresholds. The results are robust to several alternative life-cycle assumptions. The main outcome is that group-level demographic feedback (selective extinction) can overcome the individual-level advantage of defection and promote group-level efficiency (cooperation).
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in structured populations with group-level demographic feedback (selective extinction of defect-dominated groups), the efficiency of the system (measured by the fixation probability of cooperation and thus long-run group payoff) can be substantially increased by increasing the strength of selective extinction (\alpha). In the absence of such feedback, efficiency remains low (defection dominates). The model gives explicit formulas for the critical value of \alpha needed to shift the system from defection-dominated to cooperation-dominated, as a function of game payoffs and demographic parameters (deme size, number of demes, baseline extinction rate). While the model is not a direct public goods game and does not include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms, the selective extinction process is functionally analogous to a group-level sanction. Thus, the findings support the prediction that introducing or strengthening group-level sanctions (even if not individual punishment) can increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, especially when the control (no-sanction) efficiency is low.
