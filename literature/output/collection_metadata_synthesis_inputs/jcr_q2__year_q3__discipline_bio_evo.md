- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, G. R., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Altered Voluntary Cooperative Norms Compliance Under Equal Decision-Making Power. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rDLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects voluntary cooperative norm compliance (measured as contribution) in a linear public goods game with four players, asymmetric endowments, and no punishment or sanction options. Anodal stimulation increases contributions, cathodal decreases them, and sham is intermediate. The effect is observed for both high and low endowment players. The study also finds that participants' own norm perceptions (norm.own) are more predictive of compliance than their perceptions of others' norms (norm.other), and that anodal stimulation changes the relative influence of these norm perceptions. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported, and the game does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its findings are limited to the effect of neural stimulation on voluntary cooperation (contribution) in a standard linear PGG. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are reported and may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform the downstream prediction task regarding the effect of punishment on efficiency. It may be useful as a control or baseline for voluntary cooperation in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Frey, U. J. (2019). Long-term evidence on cooperation and cultural differences in public goods dilemmas. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that in a real-world, online public goods game, average cooperation rates are low and relatively stable over time, with only minor differences between countries. Individual differences are more important than cultural, economic, or religious factors. The results suggest that cooperation in this context is lower than in typical laboratory public goods games, especially those with punishment. No punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the game, and the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a useful baseline for public goods games without punishment: average contribution rates are much lower than in laboratory experiments, and cultural background has little effect. However, it does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff, as no punishment mechanism is present or analyzed. For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment, this paper is only relevant as a control or baseline for no-punishment environments, especially in more naturalistic, long-term, or online settings.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, P. C., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation of the Right Lateral Prefrontal Cortex Changes a priori Normative Beliefs in Voluntary Cooperation. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally shifts participants' a priori normative beliefs about cooperation in a public goods game. Anodal stimulation increases, and cathodal stimulation decreases, both the perceived normative standard (norm.belief) and the expected contributions of others (pg.belief). These effects are robust across player types (high and low endowment) and are statistically significant. However, the study does not measure or report actual contribution behavior, group payoffs, or efficiency, nor does it include any punishment or sanction mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It uses a standard linear PGG design but does not include punishment, nor does it report any efficiency, payoff, or contribution outcomes. The only outcomes are beliefs about norms and others' contributions. Therefore, it does not inform the downstream prediction task of how punishment affects efficiency, given game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Chen, S., Shi, J. C., Yang, X. L., Ye, H., & Luo, J. (2019). Modulating Activity in the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex Changes Punishment in the 3-Player Prisoner's Dilemma: A Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Study. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that modulating DLPFC activity via tDCS can significantly decrease the amount of punishment assigned by cooperators to defectors (both in second-party and third-party punishment), depending on the stimulation mode. One bilateral stimulation (left anodal/right cathodal) increases cooperation rates. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding neural mechanisms of punishment and cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or prisoner's dilemma games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, as it does not report these outcomes. It does, however, provide detailed evidence on how punishment behavior and cooperation rates are affected by neural modulation in a 3-player prisoner's dilemma with punishment. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment behavior can be modulated by factors unrelated to game design (e.g., neural stimulation), and that cooperation can be increased by certain interventions. However, without efficiency or payoff data, the paper's direct value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited.

- source: Nhim, T., Richter, A., & Zhu, X. Q. (2019). The resilience of social norms of cooperation under resource scarcity and inequality - An agent-based model on sharing water over two harvesting seasons. *Ecological Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model shows that strong punishment (high punishment strength) and high social capital (frequent encounters) support the emergence and resilience of cooperation in a common-pool resource dilemma. However, resource scarcity, high variability in resource inflow, and increased inequality (in land or skill) undermine cooperation, making collapse of social norms more likely. The system tends toward either full cooperation or full defection, with little evidence for stable mixed equilibria. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the collapse of cooperation is interpreted as a breakdown of socially optimal resource use.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that punishment can sustain cooperation in public-goods-like resource dilemmas, but its effectiveness is strongly moderated by resource scarcity, variability, and inequality. The model is not a direct PGG but is structurally similar, with a focus on seasonal water allocation and peer punishment. The main outcome is the presence or absence of cooperation, not efficiency or group payoff, so the paper is most useful for predicting the conditions under which punishment will be effective at sustaining cooperation, rather than for quantitative prediction of efficiency gains. The findings suggest that in environments with high scarcity, variability, or inequality, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (if any) is likely to be weaker or absent.

- source: Aguilar, E. G., & Akçay, E. (2018). Gene-Culture Coinheritance of a Behavioral Trait. *American Naturalist*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=weak | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=N/R | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The main finding is that for traits inherited both genetically and culturally, cultural selection can drive the evolution of a trait even when genetic selection opposes it, provided the variance in culture is high relative to genetic variance. The model is applied to altruism, showing that cultural selection can favor the spread of altruistic behaviors even when they are genetically maladaptive. The framework also demonstrates that cultural transmission can modify the effect of genetic selection itself. The paper does not address punishment, sanctions, or efficiency in public goods games, nor does it report empirical or simulated outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how cultural and genetic selection interact in the evolution of altruism, which is conceptually adjacent to cooperation in public goods games. However, it does not address punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze the game design dimensions relevant to public goods games. Its main relevance is in highlighting that cultural mechanisms (such as social learning) can enable the spread of cooperative or altruistic behaviors even when they are costly in terms of genetic fitness, especially when cultural variance is high. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public goods games with or without punishment, this paper offers only indirect, high-level theoretical context.

- source: Powers, S. T., Ekárt, A., & Lewis, P. R. (2018). Modelling enduring institutions: The complementarity of evolutionary and agent-based approaches. *Cognitive Systems Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that participants' choices in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma games are influenced by information about the other player's action and by individual personality traits. The disjunction effect (greater cooperation under uncertainty) is present but weaker than in previous studies and is not explained by a general bias to cooperate. Higher potential payoffs (high variability matrices) reduce the disjunction effect, suggesting more optimal responding when incentives are larger. Reward Responsiveness predicts more optimal (defecting) choices when the optimal strategy is to defect and the other player's action is known. Agreeableness predicts more cooperative choices when the optimal strategy is to cooperate. No evidence is found for a general bias toward cooperation, and the results are similar whether the task is framed as a social dilemma or a neutral card game. The study does not include punishment, sanctions, or efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It does not include punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff-based outcomes. Its findings on the influence of payoff variability and personality on cooperation/defection may be contextually informative for understanding individual decision-making in social dilemmas, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task regarding punishment effects or efficiency.
