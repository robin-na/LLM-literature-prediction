- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Gürerk (2013). Social learning increases the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y. (2013). Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

  findings: The experiment shows that when peer punishment is available in a one-shot PGG, the likelihood and intensity of punishment for low contributions is significantly lower in identity-homogeneous (in-group) groups than in identity-heterogeneous (out-group) or random groups. Anger-like emotions (anger, contempt, irritation) are a strong driver of punishment, but their effect is moderated by group identity: they lead to more punishment in out-groups than in-groups. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, but argues that higher punishment in out-groups, being costly, likely reduces social welfare relative to in-groups, where punishment is less frequent and less intense.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on behavior in PGGs is moderated by group identity: punishment is less frequent and less intense in in-groups, and anger-like emotions drive punishment more in out-groups. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in settings with identity-heterogeneous groups, punishment is more likely to be used, which (given the costliness of punishment) may reduce efficiency relative to in-groups. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms and moderators of punishment, but not about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Fatas, E., Morales, A. J., & Ubeda, P. (2010). Blind justice: An experimental analysis of random punishment in team production. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a public goods game with a novel random punishment mechanism: after each round, each group member is excluded from the group benefit with probability 1-R, where R is the normalized group contribution. This 'blind' punishment does not target individual defectors. The experiment compares three conditions: standard VCM (no punishment), random punishment without redistribution (RP), and random punishment with redistribution (RPR). Results show that both RP and RPR significantly increase contributions compared to VCM. However, only RPR increases group earnings (efficiency), as RP's punishment cost outweighs the contribution gains. The effect of random punishment is similar whether introduced after a baseline or from the start. Behavioral analysis shows that high contributors reduce their contributions if punished, but overall, conditional cooperation persists and the net effect is positive for contributions. The mechanism does not fully prevent the decline in contributions over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that random, non-individually-targeted punishment can increase contributions in a standard public goods game, but its effect on efficiency depends on whether the penalty is redistributed. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism that is not individually targeted but is probabilistically linked to group performance will likely increase contributions, but may not increase efficiency unless the punishment cost is recycled within the group. The effect is robust to order and does not depend on communication or knowledge of individual contributions. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5, and the group size is 4. The findings suggest that, for similar designs, enabling random punishment will increase contributions and may increase efficiency if the punishment cost is not lost to the group.

The findings suggest that expressive law (weak obligations) can have a short-term effect on cooperation, but do not inform about the efficiency effects of endogenous peer punishment. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of a standard punishment treatment limits direct applicability to the prediction of efficiency under peer punishment.

- source: Aakre, S., Helland, L., & Hovi, J. (2016). When Does Informal Enforcement Work?. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.

If the design allows punishment of both nonparticipants and noncompliant participants (or, in standard PGGs, if participation is forced), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency. If only one type of free riding can be punished, enabling punishment will not increase efficiency. For prediction, the presence or absence of both enforcement channels is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency unless the enforcement regime is specified.
