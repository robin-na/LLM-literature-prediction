- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Boyd, R., Schonmann, R. H., & Vicente, R. (2014). Hunter-Gatherer population structure and the evolution of contingent cooperation. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods and threshold punishment games with realistic hunter-gatherer population structure, the efficiency gains from punishment or contingent cooperation depend critically on group size (player count), relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. When groups are small (e.g., n=10) and relatedness is moderate to high (r_e > 0.05), contingent cooperation and punishment can increase expected fitness and invade, especially if the threshold for cooperation/punishment is low (i.e., only a minority need to cooperate/punish). In larger groups (e.g., n=40) or with low relatedness (r_e ~ 0.01), efficiency gains are much harder to achieve, requiring implausibly high repetition or relatedness. The model shows that punishment can support widespread cooperation and higher efficiency if a minority of punishers is sufficient to induce cooperation, but contingent cooperation alone is unlikely to sustain high efficiency in large, low-relatedness groups. Equilibrium outcomes are typically mixed, with both cooperators and non-cooperators present, and the equilibrium frequency of cooperators (and thus efficiency) depends on the tolerance threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of player count, relatedness (assortment), and the threshold for cooperation or punishment. It suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions: small group size, high relatedness, and low thresholds for action. In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.

- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

- source: Kurzban, R., Burton-Chellew, M. N., & West, S. A. (2015). The Evolution of Altruism in Humans. *Annual Review of Psychology, Vol 66*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: This review synthesizes theory and empirical evidence on the evolution of altruism, with a focus on public goods games and the role of punishment. It highlights that punishment mechanisms increase contributions to the public good, but typically reduce overall efficiency or group payoffs because the costs of punishing and being punished are not fully offset by increased cooperation. Exceptions exist, such as when punishment is cheap or the game is played for many rounds, but these are not the norm. The review also notes the existence of 'perverse' punishment (punishing high contributors) and the importance of contextual factors (e.g., possibility of counter-punishment, cultural variation).
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this review suggests that, in most standard lab PGGs, enabling punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to the direct costs of punishment. However, if punishment is relatively cheap or the game is played for many rounds, efficiency losses may be mitigated or reversed. The review provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the importance of punishment cost, number of rounds, and the structure of punishment opportunities as moderators of the efficiency effect. It also cautions that the presence of counter-punishment or 'perverse' punishment can further reduce efficiency.

- source: Barclay, P., & Raihani, N. (2016). Partner choice versus punishment in human Prisoner's Dilemmas. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.

  findings: Across four studies, the paper finds that (1) punishers are rated as more trustworthy and group focused than nonpunishers; (2) after repeated PGG rounds, punishers are trusted more and receive more money in trust games, but this effect is absent after a single round; (3) justified punishment (punishing free riders) leads to increased trust and monetary benefits, while unjustified punishment leads to reduced trust and earnings; (4) the reputational and monetary benefits of justified punishment can offset or even exceed the cost of punishing, especially in repeated interactions. The paper does not directly report group efficiency ratios, but does show that punishment can increase individual and group monetary outcomes via increased trust and cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated PGGs with punishment, justified punishment can increase the monetary benefits to punishers via enhanced trust and subsequent transfers, partially or fully offsetting the cost of punishment. The effect is contingent on repeated interaction (number of rounds) and the justification for punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in repeated PGGs (with visible identities and reputation) is likely to increase group efficiency and individual payoffs, especially when punishment is justified and recognized as such. In single-round games, these benefits do not materialize. The paper does not provide direct group efficiency ratios, but the monetary outcomes and trust game results are closely related and informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Muthukrishna, M., Francois, P., Pourahmadi, S., & Henrich, J. (2017). Corrupting cooperation and how anti-corruption strategies may backfire. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). In the standard IPGG (control), stronger leaders (higher punishment multiplier) increase contributions and public good provisioning. However, when bribery is possible (bribery game, BG), stronger leaders actually decrease public good provisioning, as they use their power to extract bribes rather than enforce cooperation. Anti-corruption strategies (partial/full transparency, leader investment) can increase contributions in some contexts (strong leaders or high economic potential), but may have no effect or even reduce contributions further in contexts with weak leaders and poor economic potential. The main outcome is mean contribution to the public good, which is a close proxy for efficiency. The results show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be reversed or undermined by corruption opportunities.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the presence of corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) can reverse the usual positive effect of institutional punishment on efficiency. The effect of punishment depends critically on whether leaders can be corrupted, the strength of the leader's punitive power, and the economic potential (mpcr). In standard IPGGs, stronger punishment increases efficiency, but in bribery-enabled games, stronger punishment can decrease efficiency. Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions. When using this paper for prediction, it is important to consider whether the game design allows for corruption/bribery, the strength of punishment, and the economic context. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported directly, but the relative changes are clear: punishment increases efficiency in standard IPGG, but decreases it when bribery is possible.

  findings: The paper finds that both punishment and reward mechanisms are effective at promoting cooperation in public goods games and similar social dilemmas, with meta-analytic evidence indicating that their effects on cooperation are roughly equal and of medium size. The presence of punishment opportunities leads to higher contributions to public goods over time, and thus to higher group efficiency and collective welfare. The effectiveness of punishment is moderated by factors such as trust (more effective in high-trust societies), group size (less effective in large groups), and the iterated nature of the game (more effective in repeated interactions). The review also notes potential downsides of punishment, such as undermining intrinsic motivation or evoking retaliation, but concludes that, overall, structural solutions like punishment are robust tools for increasing cooperation and efficiency in public goods settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper should inform prediction by reinforcing that, across a wide range of public goods game designs, enabling punishment (or reward) mechanisms generally increases group efficiency and collective welfare compared to control conditions without such mechanisms. The effect is robust across many studies and is supported by meta-analytic evidence. However, the magnitude of the effect may depend on moderators such as group size, trust, and the iterated nature of the game. The review provides indirect evidence for the predictive value of design dimensions such as punishment cost, identifiability, communication, and group size. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, especially in high-trust, small-group, iterated settings.

In contrast, reputation based on generosity (contributions) or rewards is much less effective: generosity-based reputation rarely leads to cooperation in large groups, and reward-based systems are unstable due to invasion by non-rewarders. The results are most informative for predicting the effect of punishment in large, anonymous, repeated PGGs with reputation tracking, but do not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment shows that people condition their helping decisions on both first-order (recipient's past helping) and second-order (context of recipient's past helping/defection) information. Donors are especially interested in 2nd-order information about defection, and tend to reward 'justified defection' (denying help to defectors). There is strong individual heterogeneity: some use only first-order information, some use both, and a minority are unconditional cooperators or defectors. The availability of 2nd-order information (free or costly) does not significantly increase overall helping rates compared to the control, though helping rates are numerically higher when 2nd-order information is available. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games, as there is no punishment or sanctioning mechanism in the design. The game is an iterated indirect helping game with binary helping decisions, and the main outcomes are behavioral (helping rates, information use, strategy classification). While the findings are relevant for understanding mechanisms of reputation-based cooperation, they do not inform prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The only indirect relevance is that richer information about others' behavior can (non-significantly) increase helping rates, but this is not about efficiency or payoff, nor about punishment.
