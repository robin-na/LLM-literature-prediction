- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, cooperation and group efficiency decline over time when punishment is not available, as self-regarding individuals free ride and conditional cooperators reduce their contributions in response. When a costly punishment mechanism is introduced, cooperation and group efficiency increase dramatically, approaching full cooperation and maximum group earnings. This effect is robust even when punishment is costly and when group composition changes to prevent repeated interactions. The review also highlights that a minority of strong reciprocators can sustain high efficiency when punishment is possible, but even a majority cannot do so without punishment. The findings are supported by experimental evidence from Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and others.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games substantially increases group efficiency, even when punishment is costly to the punisher and when group composition is randomized. The effect is robust across different group sizes and settings. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency from low or declining levels (in the control) to near-maximal levels (in the treatment), provided that punishment is sufficiently effective and visible. The review also suggests that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are important moderators, but the main qualitative result is that punishment enables high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

Material sanctions (with moderate cost and linear production) are most effective at achieving and sustaining high efficiency, while moral sanctions are vulnerable to start-up problems unless the cost structure is highly favorable.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game can increase group efficiency, especially when the punishment system is not too costly and is effective at inducing compliance. The effect is moderated by the type of sanction (material vs. moral vs. internalized), the cost structure of enforcement, and the learning dynamics of the group. For prediction, the findings suggest that, all else equal, adding a reasonably efficient punishment mechanism to a PGG-like environment will increase efficiency, but the magnitude and sustainability of the effect depend on the details of the punishment technology and cost. The model also highlights the risk of overcooperation with internalized norms and the importance of self-limiting enforcement. The results are most directly applicable to binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games with group sizes around 100, moderate jointness of supply, and no communication.

  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

In large groups or with low relatedness, punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective and requires only a minority to act. The model also highlights that equilibrium efficiency may be limited by the coexistence of cooperators and non-cooperators, especially for tolerant strategies. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on these structural parameters, and that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient for efficiency gains in all settings.
