- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Prétôt, L., Taylor, Q., & Mcauliffe, K. (2024). Children cooperate more with in-group members than with out-group members in an iterated face-to-face Prisoner's Dilemma Game. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (CPR settings), cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained if and only if the group collectively invests a sufficient fraction of its resources into monitoring and sanctioning rule violations. When monitoring is funded unilaterally (peer punishment), self-interested agents will not invest enough, leading to a second-order free-rider problem and low efficiency. When monitoring is funded collectively from the common-pool resource (institutional punishment), there exists a threshold investment (beta) above which full cooperation and high efficiency are stable equilibria. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum investment required and shows that the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment technology), the cost of monitoring, and the severity of sanctions all moderate the efficiency gains from punishment. The model also predicts that initial 'free' monitoring (by some agents ignoring costs) may be needed to escape the no-cooperation equilibrium, but once cooperation is established, monitoring can be fully incentivized and efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on game design dimensions. It shows that the presence and structure of punishment (especially whether it is institutionally organized and collectively funded) are critical moderators of efficiency. The explicit inequalities derived in the paper allow one to predict whether enabling punishment will increase efficiency, given the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the fraction of resources invested in monitoring. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The results are most directly applicable to settings where monitoring and sanctioning are collective, and less so to purely peer-based punishment. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Wu, Z. H., & Sun, Y. (2022). How to Treat Gossip in Internet Public Carbon Emission Reduction Projects?. *Sustainability*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that gossip can promote cooperation in public goods game environments, even under heterogeneous interpersonal relationships. Among the three mechanisms for dealing with gossip (punishment only, punishment with reputation compensation, and punishment with monetary compensation), punishment with monetary compensation is most effective at increasing cooperation rates and total contributions. The model also identifies optimal parameter ranges for tolerance, penalty time, and rebirth coefficient to maximize cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of behavioral measures (cooperation speed, total contributions) rather than efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that monetary compensation for those wrongly affected by gossip, combined with punishment for false gossipers, can best promote cooperative behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing punishment (especially with monetary compensation for those wrongly affected) can increase cooperation rates and total contributions in public-goods-game-like environments. However, because the outcomes are not reported as efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be interpreted as supporting a more positive effect of punishment on cooperation and contribution behavior, rather than on efficiency per se. The model's design and parameter exploration may inform predictions about the likely direction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, but quantitative predictions about efficiency require caution. The paper is most useful for understanding the behavioral mechanisms and the relative effectiveness of different punishment/compensation schemes.

- source: Wang, T. M., Heine, F., & van Witteloostuijn, A. (2023). Pro-social risk-taking and intergroup conflict: A volunteer's dilemma experiment. *Games and Economic Behavior*.

  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, pure punishment or pure reward alone (without tax) cannot sustain cooperation in a well-mixed population. Introducing a tax-based mechanism (where all players pay a tax to fund punishment or reward) enables the stable coexistence of cooperators and tax-based pure punishers or rewarders, thus sustaining cooperation. Tax-based pure reward leads to a higher frequency of cooperators than tax-based pure punishment. The analysis is based on replicator dynamics and focuses on the evolutionary stability of strategy frequencies, not on direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that institutional (tax-based) punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in public goods games, whereas individual-level (pure) punishment or reward cannot. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of the frequency of cooperators, not efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling institutional punishment (via tax) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that institutional reward may be even more effective. However, it does not provide direct quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency is indirect and limited to the expectation that efficiency should increase when tax-based punishment or reward is enabled.

For small thresholds (t=1), the cost function is always non-decreasing in the per-capita incentive cost, and explicit formulas are derived.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and numerical evidence on how the cost of institutional incentives (reward, punishment, hybrid) required to achieve a target cooperation level in PGG-like environments depends on game design dimensions such as player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, and the structure of the incentive scheme (threshold t, cost per unit, effectiveness). The paper is most informative for settings where the goal is to optimize the cost of achieving cooperation, rather than to maximize group payoff per se. It provides explicit formulas and numerical results that can be used to inform predictions about the efficiency of punishment or reward interventions, conditional on game design and baseline cooperation levels.

- source: Falvey, R., Lane, T., & Luckraz, S. (2025). On a mechanism that improves efficiency and reduces inequality in voluntary contribution games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

- source: Kristensen, N. P., Chisholm, R. A., & Ohtsuki, H. (2025). Many-strategy games in groups with relatives and the evolution of coordinated cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that kin selection (homophily) can facilitate the initial invasion of coordinated cooperation in public goods games with nonlinear (sigmoidal) benefits, even when such cooperation cannot invade in a well-mixed population due to cognitive or coordination costs. Once established, coordinated cooperation can persist as homophily declines, provided that the benefit function is sufficiently nonlinear and the switching gain from non-contribution to contribution at the coordination point exceeds the cost. If this condition is not met, some level of homophily must be maintained to prevent the invasion of defectors or 'liars' (who commit to contribute but do not follow through). The framework provides analytical and numerical tools for predicting evolutionary outcomes (including efficiency and group payoff) as a function of group size, relatedness, benefit function nonlinearity, and strategy set. The results are directly relevant to predicting efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but do not address punishment or sanctions directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides a general theoretical framework for predicting efficiency and group payoff in public goods games with arbitrary group size, number of strategies, and non-additive (e.g., sigmoidal) benefit functions, under varying levels of kin structure (homophily). It is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency of control games (without punishment) as a function of game design dimensions such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR (via the benefit function), and group structure. However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it analyze the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency. The framework could, in principle, be extended to include punishment as a discrete strategy, but this is not done in the present paper. Therefore, the paper is highly informative for predicting baseline efficiency in PGGs with kin structure and nonlinear payoffs, but provides only indirect or contextual support for predicting the effect of punishment.

- source: van Klingeren, F., & Buskens, V. (2024). Graduated sanctioning, endogenous institutions and sustainable cooperation in common-pool resources: An experimental test. *Rationality and Society*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a dynamic common-pool resource (CPR) game with four players and repeated rounds, the introduction of sanctioning (punishment) mechanisms—either strict or graduated—leads to higher cooperation and resource sustainability compared to no sanctioning. Strict punishment is more effective in the short term, but graduated punishment becomes equally or more effective over longer time horizons. Endogenously chosen punishment mechanisms (via group voting) further enhance cooperation and resource outcomes, though the effect on profit is small.
