- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2012). The Structure of Mutations and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

- source: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2012). I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

- source: Jaffe, K., & Zaballa, L. (2010). Co-Operative Punishment Cements Social Cohesion. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that co-operative punishment (where the cost of punishing free-riders is shared among group members) is much more effective at maintaining a high proportion of contributors in a simulated public goods game than either no punishment or altruistic punishment (where punishers pay the cost individually). The effectiveness of punishment depends on the ratio of cost to punished versus cost to punisher, and on the efficiency of detecting free-riders. Co-operative punishment allows pro-social behavior to invade and persist in populations of free-riders, while altruistic punishment does not. However, the primary outcome is the proportion of contributors, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the structure of punishment (co-operative vs. individual) is a critical moderator of cooperation in public goods games. While it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes directly, the maintenance of high contribution rates under co-operative punishment suggests that efficiency would likely be higher in such settings, especially when the cost of punishment is shared and detection of free-riders is effective. For prediction tasks, the results imply that enabling co-operative punishment mechanisms (where costs are distributed) should be expected to yield higher efficiency than individual punishment, all else equal. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the precision of quantitative predictions.

- source: Sell, J., & Wilson, R. K. (1999). The maintenance of cooperation: Expectations of future interaction and the trigger of group punishment. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that requiring a grim trigger punishment rule (where defection locks a player into permanent defection) substantially increases cooperation rates in a repeated public goods game. The effect of the discount parameter (expected number of rounds) on cooperation is only significant when the grim trigger is imposed, and only between the highest and lower values. Without a mandated punishment phase, increasing the discount parameter does not increase cooperation. The main dependent variable is the proportion of cooperative choices, not efficiency or group payoff. The results suggest that group-level punishment threats can coordinate expectations and sustain cooperation, but the value of future interaction alone is insufficient unless paired with credible punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the imposition of a group-level, long punishment phase (grim trigger) increases cooperation rates in repeated public goods games, especially in the early rounds. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency is not possible. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a strong group punishment mechanism (even if not individual-targeted) will increase cooperation and likely improve efficiency, especially when players expect many rounds. The effect of expected future interaction (discount parameter) is only realized when punishment is credible and severe. For prediction tasks, the presence of a mandated, long punishment phase should be treated as a strong positive moderator of efficiency, but the absence of direct efficiency data limits quantitative inference.

However, it is relevant for understanding how institutional design (e.g., group size limits) can promote cooperation and higher payoffs without the need for punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that capacity constraints can increase the relative payoff of cooperators, but does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is not directly useful for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment.

- source: Messer, K. D., & Zarghamee, H. (2007). New hope for the voluntary contributions mechanism: The effects of context. *Journal of Public Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that context—specifically, the combination of cheap talk, voting, and a status quo of giving—can dramatically increase and sustain contributions and efficiency in a voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) with a low MPCR. In the baseline (no context), efficiency starts at 82.3% and decays to 72.6% by round 10. The results show that context can substitute for incentive-compatible mechanisms in achieving high efficiency, but the paper does not test or discuss punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly informative about the effects of communication, voting, and default framing, but not about punishment. For the downstream prediction task—predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency—this paper is not directly relevant. However, it does provide a strong benchmark for how non-punitive contextual factors can achieve high efficiency, which may be useful for comparative purposes or for understanding the limits of punishment's added value in similar settings.

- source: Mulder, L. B., van Dijk, E., De Cremer, D., & Wilke, H. A. M. (2006). When sanctions fail to increase cooperation in social dilemmas: Considering the presence of an alternative option to defect. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.

  findings: Across two lab experiments, the authors find that introducing a sanctioning system (punishment for defection) increases collective outcomes (efficiency) in a standard two-option social dilemma (cooperate or defect). However, when a third option to defect (alternative defection, more harmful to the group) is available, sanctions do not increase efficiency and can even decrease it, particularly when participants have low expectations of others' cooperation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games is not uniform: it is positive in standard two-choice settings but can be null or negative when alternative, more harmful defection options are available and when group members have low expectations of cooperation. For prediction, this means that the presence of alternative defection options (i.e., more than two choices, with at least one being more harmful to the group) and low trust/expectations are critical moderators. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the game allows for alternative defection, enabling punishment may not improve and could even worsen efficiency, especially if group trust is low. In standard two-choice games, punishment is likely to increase efficiency.

- source: Tarui, N., Mason, C. F., Polasky, S., & Ellis, G. (2008). Cooperation in the commons with unobservable actions. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The analysis shows that the threat of the harshest possible group punishment (worst perfect equilibrium) is sufficient to deter defection, even when individual actions are not observable. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

- source: Bracht, J., Figuières, C., & Ratto, M. (2008). Relative performance of two simple incentive mechanisms in a public goods experiment. *Journal of Public Economics*.

The compensation (reward-only) mechanism also increases efficiency, but less so, and the effect is less stable. For prediction, the paper supports a positive effect of punishment/reward mechanisms on efficiency, especially when the mechanism is well-calibrated. The results are most directly applicable to small, fixed pairs, continuous contribution, no chat, and full feedback settings.
