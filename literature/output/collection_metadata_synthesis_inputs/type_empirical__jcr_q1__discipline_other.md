# Paper Set Evidence Digest

Number of papers in this paper set: 10

Each item below is a compact paper-level analysis digest. Use only this digest.

- source: Grechenig, K., Nicklisch, A., & Thöni, C. (2010). Punishment Despite Reasonable Doubt-A Public Goods Experiment with Sanctions Under Uncertainty. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a standard repeated public goods game with and without punishment, and with varying levels of noise in information about others' contributions, the paper finds that: (1) Punishment increases efficiency and cooperation only when information is accurate; (2) With moderate or high noise, punishment does not improve cooperation and reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline; (3) Subjects continue to punish under noise, but punishment is less targeted and more frequent, leading to more antisocial punishment and welfare loss; (4) The efficiency loss from punishment under noise is substantial and persistent, even though subjects could choose not to punish. The results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing mechanism is highly contingent on the accuracy of information about others' behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency benefit of enabling punishment in public goods games depends critically on the accuracy of information about others' contributions. When information is accurate, punishment increases efficiency; when information is noisy, punishment reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring is a key moderator: if the game design includes noisy or imperfect information about contributions, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency. If information is accurate, punishment is likely to increase efficiency. Other design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are held constant in this study, so the main actionable moderator is information accuracy.

- source: Eisenberg, T., & Engel, C. (2014). Assuring Civil Damages Adequately Deter: A Public Good Experiment. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that damages rules analogous to civil litigation (compensatory, class action, treble damages) can deter free riding and promote cooperation in a repeated public good game. The baseline (no punishment) shows rapid decay of contributions and group payoffs. Compensatory damages (limited to individual harm) slow but do not prevent decay. Class action damages (based on group harm) and treble damages, when sufficiently likely or severe, stabilize contributions and maintain higher group payoffs. The effect is not due to the theoretical threat of punishment, but to participants' actual experience of being punished. The option for the punisher to forfeit rather than retain damages (i.e., to punish without personal gain) is rarely used and does not affect outcomes. Thus, the deterrent effect of damages is robust to whether the punisher benefits personally. The findings suggest that for punishment to sustain cooperation and group welfare, it must be sufficiently severe and/or likely, and that actual experience with sanctions is more important than their mere possibility.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (damages) in a repeated public good game increases group efficiency (payoff) relative to the no-punishment baseline, especially when punishment is severe (treble damages) or based on group harm (class action) and is likely to be imposed. The effect is not simply on average contribution, but on the stability of cooperation and group payoffs over time. For prediction tasks, the presence and design of punishment (severity, scope, probability) are key moderators of efficiency gains. Control efficiency (baseline group payoff) is improved by adding punishment, but the magnitude depends on the punishment regime. The paper also shows that the actual experience of being punished is a stronger driver than the theoretical threat, suggesting that models should account for realized, not just expected, punishment. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially for settings with repeated interaction, group feedback, and endogenous punishment.

- source: Engel, C. (2019). When Does Transparency Backfire? Putting Jeremy Bentham's Theory of General Prevention to the Experimental Test. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment manipulates the level of feedback about punishment and contributions in a standard linear public goods game with centralized punishment. Three feedback treatments (low, medium, high) vary the precision of information about others' contributions and punishment. The main finding is that full transparency (high treatment: individual-level information about both contributions and punishment) leads to significantly lower contributions than aggregate or less precise feedback. Information about punishment of others does not increase cooperation; instead, it can reduce it. Participants are more responsive to descriptive norms (others' contributions) than to injunctive signals (punishment). The study does not directly analyze efficiency or group payoff as a dependent variable, but reports total earnings descriptively.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a linear public goods game with centralized punishment, increasing transparency about individual punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation (contributions), contrary to standard deterrence theory. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment with high transparency may not increase efficiency and could even reduce it, especially if the control (no punishment) game already has moderate contributions. However, since efficiency or group payoff is not directly analyzed, the findings should be interpreted as applying primarily to contribution behavior. The design dimensions most relevant here are the feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), centralized punishment, and the linear PGG structure.

- source: Bell, A., Zhang, W., & Nou, K. (2016). Pesticide use and cooperative management of natural enemy habitat in a framed field experiment. *Agricultural Systems*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment uses a one-shot public goods game with three treatments: (1) Base (no punishment), (2) UCPun (conditional decision maker can be punished by an unconditional decision maker), and (3) CPun (conditional decision maker can punish an unconditional decision maker). The threat of punishment (UCPun) increases the slope of conditional cooperation but reduces the probability of being a conditional cooperator (more free riders). The power to punish (CPun) does not reduce conditional cooperation (no substitution effect on cooperation), but does lead to more sensitive punishment behavior. Responsibility effects (increased cooperation when given punishment power) are stronger than substitution effects. The overall effect of punishment on cooperation is positive, but the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment mechanisms affect conditional cooperation in a one-shot public goods game with four players, MPCR 0.4, and costless punishment. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff, the findings suggest that enabling punishment increases the responsiveness of cooperators to others' contributions and may increase overall cooperation, but also increases free riding. For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is likely to increase cooperation-related behaviors, but the net effect on efficiency is ambiguous due to the rise in free riders. The absence of direct efficiency analysis limits the ability to quantitatively predict treatment efficiency from control efficiency, but the behavioral mechanisms identified are informative for modeling.

- source: Brick, K., & Visser, M. (2010). Meeting a national emission reduction target in an experimental setting. *Climate Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Voluntary cooperation alone is insufficient to meet a group emission reduction target in a public good game with heterogeneous players. Allowing communication increases average contributions and the proportion of groups meeting the target (from 35% to 50%), but also increases polarization between full cooperation and free-riding. Introducing a tax-based sanction (automatic fine for under-contribution) leads to near-universal compliance with the target (88–91% of groups meet the target), but also crowds out voluntary contributions above the minimum required. The effect of taxation on contributions above the minimum differs by player type: for capital, taxation reduces excess contributions; for labour, taxation can encourage excess contributions relative to baseline. The study does not directly analyze efficiency as a ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff, but the compliance rates and average earnings suggest that tax-based sanctions are highly effective at achieving the group target.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that automatic, rule-based sanctions (taxes/fines for under-contribution) are highly effective at increasing compliance with group targets in public good games, even with heterogeneous players. Communication alone improves cooperation but is less reliable than sanctions. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of institutional (not peer) punishment on group compliance and contributions, especially in settings with asymmetric player types and marginal returns. However, because the study does not report efficiency in the standard sense (group payoff as a share of maximum possible), its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. Still, the high compliance rates under tax-based sanctions suggest that efficiency would be much higher with punishment enabled than in the voluntary or communication-only conditions.

- source: Rommel, J. (2015). What can economic experiments tell us about institutional change in social-ecological systems?. *Environmental Science & Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that allowing third-party punishment of traders (who cause negative externalities) leads to a significant reduction in market entry rates compared to the baseline, especially for sellers. About 86% of observers are willing to punish, spending on average more than half their endowment to do so. The conditional entry treatment also reduces market entry, as about a quarter of participants condition their entry on others' behavior, reflecting norm conformity. The main outcome is the proportion of participants who choose to trade (i.e., cause the externality), not group efficiency or total payoff. The findings show that punishment can enforce social norms and reduce harmful market behavior, but the study does not measure efficiency as defined in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the availability of costly punishment reduces the frequency of norm-violating behavior (trading that causes negative externalities) in a large market setting. However, because the outcome is market entry rate (not group efficiency or total payoff), the results are only indirectly informative for predicting efficiency in public goods games. The punishment mechanism is effective at deterring harmful behavior, especially among sellers, and most observers are willing to punish at a cost. For prediction tasks, the main signal is that enabling punishment reduces the rate of norm violation, but the mapping to efficiency or group payoff in standard PGGs is indirect. The market structure, one-shot design, and externality to a third party differ from standard PGGs, so caution is needed in transferring quantitative effects.

- source: Wei, J., Zhong, Z. W., Chen, H. R., Arango-Aramburo, S., & Zhao, X. L. (2025). Exploring green electricity certificate purchasing behavior via a laboratory experiment. *Renewable & Sustainable Energy Reviews*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This laboratory experiment tests whether job rotation can reduce the ratchet effect in a principal-agent setting. Three treatments are compared: no rotation (baseline), exogenous rotation (forced switch), and endogenous rotation (principal can pay to switch). In the baseline, workers often restrict effort to avoid revealing private information, resulting in lower efficiency. Exogenous rotation nearly eliminates the ratchet effect, with almost all workers providing high effort and firms earning significantly more. Endogenous rotation, where the principal can pay a small cost to rotate workers, also reduces the ratchet effect and increases high effort, but the increase in firm earnings is not statistically significant. The findings support the idea that job rotation (especially exogenous) can improve efficiency by mitigating strategic output restriction, and that even a costly, non-credible threat of rotation (punishment) can have positive behavioral effects.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment-like mechanisms on efficiency, this paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costly punishment option (here, job rotation) can increase efficiency by reducing strategic shirking, especially when the punishment is exogenously imposed. The effect is weaker but still present when the punishment is endogenous and costly. The experimental design is not a standard public goods game, but the principal-agent structure with costly punishment and efficiency outcomes is closely related. The results suggest that, in similar environments, enabling punishment (even at a cost) is likely to increase efficiency, with the effect size depending on whether the punishment is exogenous or endogenous and on the costliness of punishment.

- source: Brevers, D., Noël, X., Ermer, E., Dabiri, D., Verbanck, P., & Kornreich, C. (2013). Unfairness sensitivity and social decision-making in individuals with alcohol dependence: A preliminary study. *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds
  findings: Alcohol-dependent individuals are more likely to reject unfair offers in the Ultimatum Game compared to healthy controls, suggesting greater emotional reactivity to unfairness and reduced ability to make self-advantageous decisions in social contexts involving frustration. The study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, and the design does not include a punishment phase or a repeated public goods structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides limited direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar repeated social dilemmas. The Ultimatum Game is adjacent to the target environment, as rejection of unfair offers can be interpreted as a form of costly punishment, but the context is one-shot, dyadic, and does not involve group cooperation or repeated interactions. The findings may inform understanding of individual differences in punishment sensitivity, but do not provide evidence on efficiency or the effect of punishment mechanisms in public goods games.

- source: Montoya, L. A., Montoya, I., & González, O. D. S. (2015). Lessons from collaborative governance and sociobiology theories for reinforcing sustained cooperation: a government food security case study. *Public Health*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that sustained interagency cooperation in public policy (here, food security in Bogotá) depends on (1) strong sponsorship (e.g., from central government or consensus), (2) stability of staff in the coordination system, and (3) mechanisms to reward or punish collaboration levels. The absence of these factors leads to decreased collaboration. The paper draws analogies from sociobiology (e.g., reciprocal altruism, punishment, reward) and collaborative governance theory, suggesting that similar mechanisms are relevant in human institutional settings. However, the findings are qualitative and do not include quantitative measures of efficiency or payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. It is adjacent to the prediction task, as it discusses the importance of reward and punishment mechanisms for sustaining cooperation in real-world, multi-agent institutional settings, and draws on sociobiological theory. However, it does not manipulate or measure game design dimensions, nor does it report efficiency or payoff outcomes. Its main value is in supporting the general idea that mechanisms for reward and punishment can help sustain cooperation, especially when other supports (like sponsorship or staff stability) are present. It does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence relevant to the prediction task.

- source: Albergaria, M., & Saes, M. S. (2018). Measuring externalities in an information commons: the case of libraries. *Journal of Cleaner Production*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in a library (information commons) setting, users subject to non-monetary sanctions (professors, employees) generate negative externalities for users subject to monetary sanctions (students): an increase in non-student borrowing is associated with a nearly one-to-one decrease in student borrowing. Peer effects are positive but small. The analysis is based on observational data and does not report efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding behavioral externalities and the impact of different sanction types in common-pool resource settings, but do not directly inform about efficiency or payoff effects of punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency or payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment. Its findings are limited to behavioral externalities (borrowing counts) in a field setting with different sanction types. While it highlights that sanction type can affect user behavior and externalities, it does not analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and the setting is only adjacent to public goods games. Thus, its value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited and indirect.
