- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Harrell, A., & Simpson, B. (2016). The Dynamics of Prosocial Leadership: Power and Influence in Collective Action Groups. *Social Forces*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of four first played six rounds without punishment, then six rounds with punishment under one of three conditions: (1) only a prosocial leader could punish, (2) only a proself leader could punish, or (3) all members could punish (peer-punishment). The introduction of punishment increased contributions in all conditions, but over time, prosocial-led groups maintained the highest contributions and group earnings, followed by peer-punishment and proself-led groups. Peer-punishment groups experienced much higher punishment costs, leading to lower overall efficiency (group earnings) than prosocial-led groups, and even lower than proself-led groups. The influence of the leader's contribution behavior on the rest of the group was a key mechanism. The study demonstrates that the design of the punishment institution (centralized vs. peer) and the social value orientation of the leader are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, but the effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution's design. Peer-punishment (all can punish all) increases contributions but leads to high punishment costs and lower efficiency than centralized punishment by a prosocial leader. If the leader is proself, efficiency gains are limited. For prediction, the presence of peer-punishment should be expected to yield lower efficiency than centralized, prosocial leadership, due to over-punishment and coordination problems. The paper's detailed reporting of group earnings, contributions, and punishment costs under each condition makes it directly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y. (2013). Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

  findings: This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions.
