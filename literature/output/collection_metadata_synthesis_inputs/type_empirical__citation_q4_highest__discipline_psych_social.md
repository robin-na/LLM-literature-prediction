- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2009). Public Goods Provision and Sanctioning in Privileged Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares standard (normal) and privileged public goods groups, with and without the option for decentralized costly punishment. In normal groups (all members have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment substantially increases contributions and group earnings, especially in later rounds. In privileged groups (one member has a high marginal return, others low), punishment increases contributions and earnings only slightly, and it increases inequality. The main reason is that punishment is less targeted and less effective in privileged groups: high-benefit members punish in an unstructured way, and low-benefit members respond weakly to punishment. As a result, the efficiency gains from punishment are much smaller in privileged groups than in normal groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends strongly on group composition, specifically on whether the group is homogeneous (normal) or contains privileged members (heterogeneous marginal returns). For standard linear PGGs with 3 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, punishment increases efficiency, especially in later rounds. However, if the group is privileged (one high-benefit, two low-benefit), the efficiency gain from punishment is much smaller, and inequality increases. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to account for heterogeneity in marginal returns. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher in privileged groups, but the treatment effect of punishment is much weaker than in normal groups.

- source: Decker, T., Stiehler, A., & Strobel, M. (2003). A comparison of punishment rules in repeated public good games - An experimental study. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that collective punishment rules generally induce more punishment activity and higher contributions than the individual rule. The most severe collective rule (maxi) achieves the highest contributions but not the highest efficiency, as the costs of punishment offset the gains from increased cooperation. Efficiency (group profit after punishment costs) does not differ significantly between rules, and the relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is reversed compared to the relationship with contributions. Emotional punishment, especially in the most severe rule, can damage efficiency. Justice (as measured by profit-contribution correlation and variance) improves with more severe collective rules. Participants are more likely to support collective rules if they expect higher profits and if the rule requires a higher degree of consent (e.g., unanimity).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public good game with punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not guarantee higher efficiency or group profit. The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the severity and structure of the punishment rule: more severe rules can increase cooperation but may reduce efficiency if punishment costs are high, especially due to emotional punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency with punishment is non-monotonic and depends on the balance between increased cooperation and punishment costs. Game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology, and the collective/individual nature of punishment are critical moderators. The paper's results caution against assuming that more severe or more available punishment will always improve efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Barclay, P. (2004). Trustworthiness and competitive altruism can also solve the tragedy of the commons. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment with Han Chinese and Mongolian Chinese students uses a standard public goods game with and without a costly punishment stage. Each group has 4 players, and each subject plays 10 rounds without punishment and 10 rounds with punishment. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.4. Punishment costs 1 token to the punisher and reduces the punished's payoff by 3 tokens. The main findings are: (1) Punishment increases mean contribution in all treatments, but the efficiency of punishment (measured as the increase in contribution per unit of punishment) varies by group composition. (2) In all-Han and mixed-ethnicity groups, punishment is efficient (marginal effect > 1), while in all-Mongolian groups, punishment is inefficient (marginal effect < 1). (3) Ethnic diversity (mixed groups) boosts punishment efficiency for both Han and Mongolian Chinese. (4) Mongolian Chinese punish more harshly within their own group due to cultural norms ('Anda' tradition), leading to lower efficiency. (5) Han Chinese show strong ingroup bias, contributing more in all-Han groups, especially with punishment. (6) Cultural fusion (Mongolian Chinese with Han cultural background) increases contribution in mixed groups. (7) Overall, the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive in all-Han and mixed groups, but not in all-Mongolian groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence for predicting the effect of introducing punishment on efficiency in public goods games with different group compositions. For games with 4 players, 10 rounds, MPCR 0.4, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3, punishment increases efficiency in all-Han and mixed-ethnicity groups (marginal effect > 1), but is inefficient in all-Mongolian groups (marginal effect < 1). If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, the expected efficiency with punishment will increase substantially in all-Han and mixed groups, but only modestly or not at all in all-Mongolian groups. The results are robust to a 'clear-cut' Han Chinese sample. The findings are most applicable to lab settings with similar design parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact, no chat, continuous contribution, no rewards, anonymous punishment, and known round count). The paper is highly informative for the prediction task, especially regarding the moderating role of group composition and cultural background.

- source: Fehr, E., Fischbacher, U., & Gächter, S. (2002). Strong reciprocity, human cooperation, and the enforcement of social norms. *Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the introduction of a costly punishment option leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation and a 40% increase in aggregate monetary payoff, even after subtracting the costs of punishment. Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency unravel over time, with most subjects defecting by the end. With punishment, cooperation is sustained or even increases, and group earnings approach the social optimum. The effect is observed both in stable groups (partner design) and in randomly re-matched groups (stranger design), though the effect is stronger in stable groups. The paper also shows that punishment is targeted at defectors and is proportional to the degree of free-riding. The findings are robust across cultures and with high monetary stakes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard public goods game with known MPCR, group size, and round structure leads to a large, positive effect on group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust to group composition (fixed or random), and the paper provides enough detail on the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) to inform predictions for similar designs. The findings support the prediction that, controlling for control-game efficiency, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently effective and observable.

- source: Gintis, H., Bowles, S., Boyd, R., & Fehr, E. (2003). Explaining altruistic behavior in humans. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Experimental public goods games consistently show that when participants can punish defectors at a personal cost, cooperation rates remain high or even increase over time, and group payoffs (efficiency) are substantially higher than when punishment is not allowed. In the Fehr & Gächter (2000) experiments, for example, allowing punishment led to a 40% increase in net payoffs to all subjects, even after accounting for the costs of punishment. The effect is observed even under strict anonymity and in one-shot or non-repeated interactions, indicating that the mechanism is not driven by expectations of future reciprocity. The paper also reviews cross-cultural evidence and evolutionary models, but the core experimental result is that punishment opportunities robustly improve group efficiency in public goods game environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, direct evidence that enabling costly punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control conditions without punishment. The effect is robust across standard lab PGG designs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR. The magnitude of the efficiency gain is substantial (e.g., 40% increase in net payoffs in Fehr & Gächter 2000). The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency given the control game design and efficiency, especially in standard PGG settings. The paper also discusses the importance of group structure (e.g., partner vs. stranger matching), but the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is consistent across these variations.

- source: Barclay, P., & Raihani, N. (2016). Partner choice versus punishment in human Prisoner's Dilemmas. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across four studies, the paper finds that (1) punishers are rated as more trustworthy and group focused than nonpunishers; (2) after repeated PGG rounds, punishers are trusted more and receive more money in trust games, but this effect is absent after a single round; (3) justified punishment (punishing free riders) leads to increased trust and monetary benefits, while unjustified punishment leads to reduced trust and earnings; (4) the reputational and monetary benefits of justified punishment can offset or even exceed the cost of punishing, especially in repeated interactions. The paper does not directly report group efficiency ratios, but does show that punishment can increase individual and group monetary outcomes via increased trust and cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated PGGs with punishment, justified punishment can increase the monetary benefits to punishers via enhanced trust and subsequent transfers, partially or fully offsetting the cost of punishment. The effect is contingent on repeated interaction (number of rounds) and the justification for punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling punishment in repeated PGGs (with visible identities and reputation) is likely to increase group efficiency and individual payoffs, especially when punishment is justified and recognized as such. In single-round games, these benefits do not materialize. The paper does not provide direct group efficiency ratios, but the monetary outcomes and trust game results are closely related and informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment.

- source: Muthukrishna, M., Francois, P., Pourahmadi, S., & Henrich, J. (2017). Corrupting cooperation and how anti-corruption strategies may backfire. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). In the standard IPGG (control), stronger leaders (higher punishment multiplier) increase contributions and public good provisioning. However, when bribery is possible (bribery game, BG), stronger leaders actually decrease public good provisioning, as they use their power to extract bribes rather than enforce cooperation. Anti-corruption strategies (partial/full transparency, leader investment) can increase contributions in some contexts (strong leaders or high economic potential), but may have no effect or even reduce contributions further in contexts with weak leaders and poor economic potential. The main outcome is mean contribution to the public good, which is a close proxy for efficiency. The results show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be reversed or undermined by corruption opportunities.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the presence of corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) can reverse the usual positive effect of institutional punishment on efficiency. The effect of punishment depends critically on whether leaders can be corrupted, the strength of the leader's punitive power, and the economic potential (mpcr). In standard IPGGs, stronger punishment increases efficiency, but in bribery-enabled games, stronger punishment can decrease efficiency. Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions. When using this paper for prediction, it is important to consider whether the game design allows for corruption/bribery, the strength of punishment, and the economic context. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported directly, but the relative changes are clear: punishment increases efficiency in standard IPGG, but decreases it when bribery is possible.

  findings: Exposure to environments that support cooperation (via repeated PDs with high continuation probability and favorable payoffs) leads to higher prosociality in subsequent one-shot games, including the public goods game, trust game, dictator game, and ultimatum game. In a second experiment, exposure to cooperative environments increases third-party punishment of selfishness and reduces antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment. These effects are strongest among subjects who rely on intuitive (heuristic) decision-making. However, the study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional environments can shape prosocial and punishment behaviors in public-goods-game-like settings, and that these behaviors spill over into new contexts. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency. The design details (e.g., group size, punishment cost/tech, MPCR) are reported and can inform mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

- source: Chen, X. P., Pillutla, M. M., & Yao, X. (2009). Unintended Consequences of Cooperation Inducing and Maintaining Mechanisms in Public Goods Dilemmas: Sanctions and Moral Appeals. *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Sanctions (punishment or reward) in public goods games increase cooperation while present, but when removed, cooperation drops sharply—sometimes to levels below those in groups that never had sanctions. Moral appeals also increase cooperation, but this effect is more persistent after the appeal is removed. The drop in cooperation after removing sanctions is mediated by reduced trust in others' cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings are based on contribution rates, not direct payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment (and reward) increases cooperation in the short term, but this effect is not sustained after removal, and may even backfire. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes for games with punishment enabled versus disabled. The findings are most useful for predicting contribution rates and for understanding the psychological mechanisms (trust) that may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. For efficiency prediction, the results suggest caution: increased cooperation under punishment may not translate to sustained efficiency gains, especially if punishment is later removed.

  findings: The main finding is that, in one-shot public goods games, participants are much more likely to support cooperation through reward (both first- and second-order) than through punishment. First-order punishment of defectors occurs, but second-order punishment of nonpunishers is rare or absent, and punishers are not socially endorsed.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, four-player public goods games with binary contribution and surprise sanctioning opportunities, punishment is not socially supported at the second order, and reward is more likely to be used and socially endorsed. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The behavioral results suggest that, in similar game designs, punishment may not be self-sustaining and may not improve efficiency unless supported by other mechanisms. The findings are most relevant for predicting the likelihood of punishment and reward behaviors, not their direct impact on efficiency.

- source: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K. (2013). Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  findings: In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.
