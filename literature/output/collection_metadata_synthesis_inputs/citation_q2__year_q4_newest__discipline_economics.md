- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Punishment mechanisms and cooperation in public goods games: Experimental evidence. *Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with four players per group, the introduction of either individual or majority-voting punishment mechanisms increases average contributions relative to the no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether MPCRs are homogeneous or heterogeneous. However, group payoffs (efficiency) do not significantly differ between the no-punishment, individual punishment, and majority-voting punishment conditions. The cost of punishment offsets the gains from increased cooperation. The majority-voting punishment mechanism reduces the frequency of antisocial punishment compared to individual punishment, particularly in homogeneous groups, but antisocial punishment remains present in all conditions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, six rounds per condition, and either homogeneous (MPCR=0.5) or heterogeneous (MPCR=0.3/0.5/0.7) returns, enabling punishment (either individual or majority-voting) increases contributions but does not increase efficiency relative to the control. The cost of punishment absorbs the surplus from higher cooperation. Thus, when predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs, the presence of punishment should not be expected to increase efficiency unless the cost structure or punishment technology is more favorable. The majority-voting mechanism may reduce antisocial punishment but does not improve efficiency over individual punishment. The findings are most applicable to small, anonymous, stranger-matched groups with no communication and known round structure.

- source: Noussair, C. N., Tucker, S., Xu, Y. L., & Breaban, A. (2024). The role of emotions in public goods games with and without punishment opportunities. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that both monetary and non-monetary punishment increase cooperation relative to a baseline with no punishment, with monetary punishment sustaining higher contributions over time. Emotional states, especially happiness and negative valence, are correlated with contribution and punishment behavior, but not directly with payoffs. The effect of punishment on future contributions is stronger for monetary punishment, and the emotional state of the recipient moderates the effect of punishment. The study does not report efficiency or group earnings, but the behavioral patterns are consistent with prior literature showing that punishment increases group welfare by increasing cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players and 10 rounds, both monetary and non-monetary punishment increase cooperation compared to a no-punishment baseline. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so it cannot be used to directly predict the change in efficiency from enabling punishment. The findings support the general expectation that punishment increases efficiency via increased cooperation, but for quantitative prediction, one must rely on prior studies that report payoff or efficiency outcomes. The design details (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and match canonical PGG lab experiments.

- source: Ramalingam, A., & Stoddard, B. (2024). Does reducing inequality increase cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a 2x2 design varying punishment (present/absent) and instruction format (short/long, less/more explicit). In the punishment treatments, longer and more explicit instructions lead to higher and more stable contributions, with punishment more effectively targeted at low contributors. In the absence of punishment, instruction format has little effect on average contributions over time. Decision times are lower with more explicit instructions, indicating better comprehension. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be determined, but the results suggest that the effectiveness of punishment in raising contributions depends on subjects' comprehension of the social dilemma.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in raising contributions in public goods games depends on participants' comprehension of the incentive structure, which can be improved by more explicit instructions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the realized effect of punishment on efficiency may be moderated by factors affecting comprehension, such as instruction clarity. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, it cannot be used to directly predict efficiency changes due to punishment. Instead, it provides indirect support that punishment is more likely to increase efficiency when comprehension is high and punishment is well-targeted.

- source: Weber, T. O., Schulz, J. F., Beranek, B., Lambarraa-Lehnhardt, F., & Gächter, S. (2023). The behavioral mechanisms of voluntary cooperation across culturally diverse societies: Evidence from the US, the UK, Morocco, and Turkey. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that differences in cooperation across culturally diverse societies are mainly explained by differences in beliefs about others' cooperativeness, not by differences in cooperative preferences or punishment behavior. In the presence of punishment, only participants in the US and UK significantly increase their contributions compared to the no-punishment condition. Actual punishment of free riding is similar across all four countries, and antisocial punishment is low everywhere. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome, so the direct effect of punishment on efficiency is not analyzed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence on how punishment affects contribution rates in one-shot public goods games across different societies, but does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive in settings similar to the US and UK (where contributions increase with punishment), but negligible in settings similar to Morocco and Turkey (where contributions do not increase). However, because efficiency is not directly measured, this inference is indirect and based on contribution behavior. The game design dimensions are well specified, allowing for mapping to prediction inputs, but the lack of direct efficiency data is a limitation.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

- source: Riehm, T., Fugger, N., Gillen, P., Gretschko, V., & Werner, P. (2022). Social norms, sanctions, and conditional entry in markets with externalities: Evidence from an artefactual field experiment. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that all-can-win intergroup competition (where multiple groups can be ranked first and avoid penalties) leads to large increases in cooperation and group output across all treatments. The effect is strongest when penalties for not being ranked first are sharp (T1, T2), supporting near-perfect cooperation and benefiting a majority of individuals, though not all. In these cases, average earnings are about the same with and without competition, as severe penalties for a few groups offset gains for the majority. In the weaker-penalty treatment (T3), cooperation increases less, and the effect on earnings is neutral. The results suggest that the design of the penalty function (i.e., the sharpness of incentives) is crucial for both cooperation and efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a rank-based, all-can-win intergroup competition mechanism in a standard linear public goods game can substantially increase cooperation and group output, especially when penalties for low-ranked groups are sharp. However, the effect on efficiency (average earnings) is not always positive: while most individuals benefit, severe penalties for a minority can offset group-level gains. For prediction, the sharpness of the penalty function (punishment tech) and the degree of coordination among groups are key moderators. If the control game (no competition) has low efficiency, introducing sharp all-can-win competition is likely to increase group output and may increase average earnings, but the risk of severe losses for some groups must be considered. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGG-like environments with intergroup competition as a sanctioning mechanism.

  findings: The study finds that ambient temperature in the range of 18°C to 28°C does not affect social behavior or perception in incentivized economic games, including the Public Goods Game. All behavioral outcomes (contribution, sharing, trust, etc.) and social perception measures (communion, agency, social distance, empathy) are statistically equivalent across temperature conditions. No evidence is found for either the 'warmth-prosociality' or 'heat-aggression' hypotheses in this context. The study does not include punishment or analyze efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides no direct evidence for the prediction of efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, as punishment is not present in any game and efficiency or group payoff is not analyzed. However, it does provide high-quality evidence that ambient temperature in the tested range does not affect cooperation or related social behaviors in a standard two-player Public Goods Game. For prediction tasks, this suggests that temperature (within this range) is unlikely to be a relevant moderator of punishment effects or efficiency in similar lab settings. The absence of punishment and efficiency outcomes means this paper is not directly informative for the core prediction task.

- source: Ntuli, H., Crépin, A. S., Schill, C., & Muchapondwa, E. (2023). Sanctioned Quotas Versus Information Provisioning for Community Wildlife Conservation in Zimbabwe: A Framed Field Experiment Approach. *Environmental & Resource Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that introducing a sanctioned quota system (with costly punishment for exceeding quotas) leads to significantly higher efficiency in resource use compared to a baseline with no intervention. Information about ecological thresholds also improves efficiency, but not as much as punishment. Combining both interventions does not yield efficiency gains beyond punishment alone. The results are robust across multiple measures (efficiency, group payoff, resource stock, depletion cases) and are supported by regression analysis controlling for individual and group characteristics. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in CPR games with similar design features.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that enabling punishment (in the form of sanctioned quotas with costly, probabilistic enforcement) increases efficiency in a dynamic, multi-round, continuous-harvest CPR game with communication allowed. The effect is substantial: average efficiency rises from 0.42 (baseline) to 0.71 (punishment) and 0.77 (punishment+information). The design closely matches public-goods-game-like environments, though the context is a renewable resource with dynamic feedbacks. The results suggest that, for similar games with 4 players, communication, and continuous contribution/harvest, enabling punishment is likely to yield a large positive effect on efficiency, even when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The effect is robust to the presence of communication and does not depend on end-game knowledge. The paper also provides evidence that the marginal per-capita return (mpcr) and punishment cost/tech are important, but the main result is that punishment is highly effective in this context.

- source: Dughera, S. (2022). The evolution of workplace control leadership, obedience and organizational performance. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The model finds that punishment (authoritarian leadership) can increase organizational performance relative to no control when workers are low-skilled or when monitoring is efficient, but is always less efficient than motivation (authoritative leadership) when workers are high-skilled and supervisors are charismatic. Multiple equilibria are possible: (1) 'Discipline' (universal cooperation with punishment), (2) 'Cooperation' (universal cooperation with motivation), (3) 'Conflict' (universal defection with punishment), and (4) mixed or cyclical regimes. The most efficient outcome is universal cooperation with motivation, but this requires high worker skill and supervisor charisma. Punishment can prevent the spread of shirking (the 'bad company' effect) by increasing procedural justice, but also reduces morale and is less efficient. Inefficient equilibria with widespread punishment and/or shirking can persist due to path dependence. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime is stable and analyzes the welfare properties of each.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in a public-goods-like team production setting, conditional on game design dimensions such as monitoring efficiency (punishment tech), team size (player count), and the presence/absence of reward (motivation/charisma). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency relative to no control when monitoring is effective and workers are not highly skilled, but is always less efficient than a well-functioning reward/motivation regime. The model gives explicit parameter boundaries for when punishment is effective, and highlights the importance of initial conditions and path dependence. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to no-punishment in some parameter regimes (especially with low worker skill and high monitoring efficiency), but that the maximum efficiency is achieved with motivation/charisma rather than punishment. The model is highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings where reward/motivation is not available or not effective.

- source: Xu, C. Y., Qin, B. T., & Rawlings, D. (2022). Motivational crowding effects of monetary and nonmonetary incentives: Evidence from a common pool resources experiment in China. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab CPR game with 5 players per group and 18 rounds, the introduction of mandates with fines (punishment) for over-extraction significantly reduced extraction (i.e., increased conservation and group welfare) compared to a normative information baseline. High fines had a persistent 'crowd-in' effect on conservation, especially among those who initially extracted the least. Low fines had weaker or no persistent effects. In contrast, a public revelation mechanism (where one player's extraction is revealed each round) did not reduce extraction and led to a 'crowd-out' effect among conditional cooperators, likely because the revealed extractions were higher than expected, shifting descriptive norms upward. The study does not report efficiency as a ratio, but the extraction results map directly to group welfare. Heterogeneity analyses show that the effects of punishment and public revelation depend on behavioral type and prosocial orientation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a CPR game with 5 players, continuous extraction choices, no chat, and a known social optimum, the introduction of monetary punishment (especially with high fines) increases group welfare by reducing extraction, both during and after the intervention. The effect is strongest for high fines and among those with low initial extraction. Public revelation mechanisms may backfire, especially among conditional cooperators, if the revealed behavior is less cooperative than expected. For prediction, the presence of monetary punishment (with sufficient magnitude and justification) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a normative information baseline. The mapping from extraction to efficiency is direct, so these results are highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in similar CPR or PGG-like environments.

- source: Libois, F. (2022). Success and failure of communities managing natural resources: Static and dynamic inefficiencies. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.

  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The model shows that introducing sanctions (punishment) can increase efficiency in CPR games by enabling the enforcement of rules that prevent over-extraction (static inefficiency) and/or promote conservation (dynamic inefficiency). The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the strength of the sanctioning mechanism relative to the incentives to deviate from cooperation. When sanctions are strong enough, the first-best (fully efficient) outcome can be achieved. With limited sanctions, only partial improvements are possible, and sometimes increasing the resource's value or growth rate can make enforcement harder, reducing efficiency. The model also shows that the number of players, extraction cost, and inequality among players all moderate the effect of sanctions on efficiency. In particular, more players or greater inequality can make conservation harder to enforce, while higher extraction costs can make it easier. The model provides explicit formulas and comparative statics for these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (sanctions) on efficiency in CPR-like games. It shows that the effect of punishment depends on the interplay between the sanctioning technology (maximum enforceable sanction), the number of players, the resource's growth rate, extraction cost, and initial value. If sanctions are strong enough relative to the incentives to deviate, enabling rules can be enforced and efficiency increases. If not, efficiency may not improve, or may even decrease if resource value/growth increases incentives to defect. The model is not a standard public goods game but is closely related, and the logic of how punishment affects efficiency is highly transferable. The paper is especially informative for settings where the punishment mechanism is exogenous and bounded, and where resource dynamics (growth, conservation) are important. For prediction, the model suggests that the effect of enabling punishment depends on whether the sanctioning parameters cross key thresholds relative to the game's other parameters.

- source: Gallo, E., Riyanto, Y. E., Roy, N., & Teh, T. H. (2022). Cooperation and punishment mechanisms in uncertain and dynamic social networks. *Games and Economic Behavior*.

  findings: In a repeated networked Prisoner's Dilemma with 12 players per group and endogenous link formation/removal, the presence of reputational uncertainty (15% chance that intended actions are flipped) leads to a substantial decrease in both cooperation rate and welfare (group payoff), regardless of whether the network is updated slowly or quickly.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in dynamic networked repeated games with endogenous partner selection, the effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (here, network punishment via link removal) in increasing efficiency is highly sensitive to the accuracy of reputation signals. When there is reputational uncertainty, punishment is less effective and group efficiency (welfare) is significantly reduced. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring should be treated as a major moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The results also indicate that simply increasing the frequency of punishment opportunities (e.g., faster network updating) does not robustly increase efficiency, especially when uncertainty is present. The findings are most directly relevant to networked PD games, but the mechanisms may transfer to PGG-like environments with endogenous partner choice and noisy monitoring.

The results suggest that the design and magnitude of penalties (punishment costs) are critical for achieving near-efficient outcomes, but the context is bargaining, not public goods provision. The findings may be useful for understanding the general role of sanctions in promoting efficiency, but should be transferred to PGG-like settings with caution.
