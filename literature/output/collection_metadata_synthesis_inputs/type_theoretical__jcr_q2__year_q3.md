  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Zhang, J. L., & Cao, M. (2020). Strategy Competition Dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems in the Framework of Evolutionary Game Theory. *Ieee Transactions on Circuits and Systems Ii-express Briefs*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, insurance (speculation), and loner options, the evolutionary outcome (and thus group efficiency) depends on the relationship between the punishment fine (alpha), insurance cost (lambda), group size (N), and the public goods multiplier (r). If punishment is strong enough (i.e., r > 2 - 2*alpha and 1 - r/N - alpha < 0), full cooperation is the only stable equilibrium, leading to maximal group efficiency. If punishment is weaker, or if insurance is cheaper than the expected fine, defection or speculation can dominate, reducing group efficiency. The presence of loners can also destabilize cooperation, leading to cycles or dominance of non-participation. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime occurs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given the game design parameters. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting efficiency depends on the size of the punishment fine relative to the public goods multiplier and group size. If the punishment is strong enough (alpha high, r high, N not too large), enabling punishment can shift the system from low-efficiency (defection or cycles) to high-efficiency (full cooperation). However, if punishment is weak or can be circumvented by cheap insurance (speculation), or if the loner option is attractive, punishment may not improve efficiency and may even be ineffective. The model is most informative for settings matching the theoretical assumptions (well-mixed, large populations, no repeated encounters, continuous strategy evolution).

- source: Sui, X. K., Wu, B., & Wang, L. (2018). Rationality alters the rank between peer punishment and social exclusion. *Epl*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the evolutionary advantage (interpreted as efficiency in promoting cooperation and group payoff) of peer punishment versus social exclusion in PGGs is not fixed, but depends on the intensity of selection (rationality), the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, and whether participation is compulsory or voluntary. Under weak selection, peer punishment can be favored if it is efficient or exclusion is costly. Under strong selection, social exclusion with low cost dominates. The presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not change the condition for which punishment is favored, but reduces the difference in their abundances. The rank between peer punishment and social exclusion can change as rationality changes, and this sensitivity is mapped for a range of parameter values.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGGs, especially regarding how the cost and effectiveness of punishment and exclusion, group size, and rationality (selection intensity) interact. It shows that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is not uniform: it can be positive or negative depending on whether punishment is efficient and/or exclusion is costly, and on the rationality of agents. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, theoretical PGGs with the specified parameters. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is conditional on the cost/benefit structure and the rationality of players, and that the presence of loners (voluntary participation) does not fundamentally alter the ranking condition but can affect the magnitude of effects.

- source: Zhang, B. J., Cui, Z. G., & Yue, X. H. (2019). Cluster evolution in public goods game with fairness mechanism. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a well-mixed PGG, adding a fairness mechanism and peer punishment (with costs shared among cooperators) can achieve full cooperation and maximum efficiency in small clusters. In larger clusters, full cooperation is difficult, but increasing the fairness factor (weight on fairness utility) and decreasing the cost of punishment both increase the equilibrium number of cooperators and group utility. The model provides explicit formulas for the utilities of cooperators and defectors with and without punishment, and shows that punishment is only effective (i.e., leads to higher group efficiency) when the number of cooperators exceeds a threshold. Simulation results confirm that lower punishment cost and higher fairness factor lead to higher group efficiency and larger stable clusters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in PGGs with peer punishment, efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) increases when punishment is enabled, especially when punishment cost is low and the fairness factor is high. The model allows explicit calculation of group efficiency under different parameter settings, including player count, punishment cost, and fairness factor. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, with the effect size moderated by punishment cost and the fairness factor. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, continuous-contribution PGGs with no communication, and where punishment costs are shared among cooperators.

- source: Wang, Q., Liu, L. J., & Chen, X. J. (2020). Evolutionary Dynamics of Cooperation in the Public Goods Game with Individual Disguise and Peer Punishment. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment increases cooperation and group payoff (efficiency) in public goods games, but this effect is undermined if defectors can cheaply disguise themselves to avoid punishment. When the cost of disguise is high, or when punishment is strong (high fine, low cost), punishment is effective at promoting cooperation and high group payoff. Second-order punishment further stabilizes cooperation and punishment strategies, even in the presence of disguise. In finite populations, the stationary distribution of strategies shows that low disguise cost leads to defection (low efficiency), while high disguise cost or strong punishment leads to cooperation or punishment (high efficiency). Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the interplay between punishment parameters and the cost/effectiveness of disguise.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the fine imposed, and the possibility/cost of disguise by defectors. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency due to high defection, enabling punishment will increase efficiency only if punishment is strong enough and disguise is not too cheap. If disguise is cheap, punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The model also shows that second-order punishment can further increase efficiency by stabilizing punishment strategies. For prediction, the paper supports that lower punishment cost, higher punishment effectiveness, and higher fine on defectors all increase the efficiency gain from punishment, but this is moderated (and can be negated) by the ability of defectors to disguise at low cost.

The findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment (in terms of cost to achieve cooperation) depends on initial conditions and the structure of the game (network, group size, synergy factor). For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is not always the most efficient intervention; its relative efficiency compared to reward depends on the starting state of the group. The detailed modeling of incentive cost and cooperation dynamics can inform parameterizations of efficiency in similar networked PGG environments.

  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing first-order costly punishment into a finite-population public goods game can shift the system from an all-defection equilibrium to a coexistence of cooperation and punishment, provided that both the punishment intensity and the return on investment are large enough. This shift implies higher group welfare and efficiency, as the all-defection state is inefficient. Second-order punishment (punishing cooperators who do not punish defectors) does not dramatically change the evolutionary dynamics except in certain critical parameter combinations, and mainly increases the proportion of punishers among cooperators in coexistence states. The model provides a detailed mapping of parameter regions where cooperation is stochastically stable.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that, in finite-population public goods games, enabling costly punishment (with sufficient intensity and return on investment) can substantially increase the probability of the system being in cooperative or mixed cooperative-punisher states, which are more efficient than all-defection. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence and strength of punishment, as well as the return on investment (mpcr), are key moderators of the efficiency gain from punishment. However, the paper does not report direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must be inferred from the stability of cooperative states. Second-order punishment adds little except in narrow parameter regions. The results are most applicable to settings matching the model's assumptions (finite, well-mixed populations, no communication, discrete strategies).

The findings suggest that punishment can backfire if the cost to punishers is too high relative to the fine, and that targeting a minority of low contributors is more effective for increasing cooperation. These insights may inform the qualitative direction of efficiency effects in similar designs, but quantitative prediction is not possible from this paper alone.
