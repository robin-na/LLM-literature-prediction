- source: Bone, J., Silva, A. S., & Raihani, N. J. (2014). Defectors, not norm violators, are punished by third-parties. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that third-party punishment in a one-shot public goods game is overwhelmingly directed at defectors, regardless of whether defection is the norm in the group. The manipulation of norm violation (defection being typical or atypical) does not affect the likelihood of defectors being punished. Cooperators are rarely punished, even when they are in the minority. The cost structure of punishment is fixed, so punishing multiple players is not more expensive than punishing one. Social rejection patterns mirror punishment: defectors are less preferred as future partners, regardless of norm status. The study does not report on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a one-shot PGG with third-party punishment and a fixed punishment cost, punishment is targeted at defectors rather than norm violators. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design details (one-shot, third-party punishment, fixed cost, no communication, binary contribution) are informative for mapping to other studies, but the lack of payoff outcomes limits its direct utility for efficiency prediction. The findings suggest that, under these design features, punishment is likely to be used against defectors, but the impact on group efficiency remains unmeasured.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, G. R., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Altered Voluntary Cooperative Norms Compliance Under Equal Decision-Making Power. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rDLPFC activity via tDCS causally affects voluntary cooperative norm compliance (measured as contribution) in a linear public goods game with four players, asymmetric endowments, and no punishment or sanction options. Anodal stimulation increases contributions, cathodal decreases them, and sham is intermediate. The effect is observed for both high and low endowment players. The study also finds that participants' own norm perceptions (norm.own) are more predictive of compliance than their perceptions of others' norms (norm.other), and that anodal stimulation changes the relative influence of these norm perceptions. No efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes are reported, and the game does not include punishment or reward mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not include any punishment or sanction mechanism, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. Its findings are limited to the effect of neural stimulation on voluntary cooperation (contribution) in a standard linear PGG. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are reported and may be useful for context, but the absence of punishment means this paper cannot inform the downstream prediction task regarding the effect of punishment on efficiency. It may be useful as a control or baseline for voluntary cooperation in the absence of sanctions.

- source: Javaid, A., & Falk, T. (2015). Incorporating local institutions in irrigation experiments: evidence from rural communities in Pakistan. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implemented a sequential common-pool resource (CPR) game with five players per group, modeling irrigation dilemmas with asymmetric access. In the baseline (no institutional intervention), groups achieved high efficiency (average group earnings at 74% of the social optimum, group investment at 98% of the optimum). When external punishment (costly, probabilistic fines) was introduced, group earnings and efficiency significantly decreased (regression coefficient for group earnings: -9.03, p<0.1). The punishment mechanism reduced over-extraction by head-users but did not increase tail-user earnings; instead, it led to more water being wasted and lower group welfare. Communication and traditional authority treatments did not significantly affect group earnings or investment. The high baseline efficiency is attributed to strong local norms and the public revelation of individual extraction decisions. The main effect of punishment was to create additional coordination problems, not to improve efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in environments with high baseline efficiency and strong social information (public feedback on individual actions), introducing an external punishment mechanism can reduce group efficiency and welfare. The punishment treatment in this design (costly, probabilistic, not directly linked to rule violations) led to more resource wastage and lower group earnings. For prediction tasks, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high and social information is available, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency. The effect is specific to the punishment design used here (costly, probabilistic, not directly rewarding the punisher). Other institutional interventions (communication, traditional authority) had no significant effect. The findings are most applicable to sequential CPR games with asymmetric access, public feedback, and close-knit communities.

For the prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment behavior can be modulated by factors unrelated to game design (e.g., neural stimulation), and that cooperation can be increased by certain interventions. - source: Inglis, R. F., West, S., & Buckling, A. (2014). An experimental study of strong reciprocity in bacteria. *Biology Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that in bacterial populations, cooperative punishers (who both produce a public good and punish non-cooperators) can invade a population of cheats only when the traits for cooperation and punishment are tightly linked. When the linkage is broken (by mixing punishing and non-punishing cooperators), invasion fails. Non-punishing cooperators alone also cannot invade. The main outcome is the frequency and survival/extinction of the different strains, not group efficiency or total payoff. The results support theoretical predictions that linkage is necessary for the evolutionary success of strong reciprocity in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to the prediction task. It shows that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and by implication, group efficiency) depends critically on the linkage between cooperation and punishment. However, the study does not measure efficiency or group payoff directly, and the outcomes are evolutionary (strain invasion/extinction) rather than economic payoffs. The findings suggest that in systems where punishment and cooperation are not tightly linked, punishment may not improve group outcomes. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the importance of linkage or coupling between cooperation and punishment, but less so about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency in standard public goods games.

- source: Gelcich, S., Guzman, R., Rodríguez-Sickert, C., Castilla, J. C., & Cárdenas, J. C. (2013). Exploring External Validity of Common Pool Resource Experiments: Insights from Artisanal Benthic Fisheries in Chile. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: This paper reports a field-lab experiment with Chilean artisanal fishers playing a 20-round common pool resource game. In the first 10 rounds (open access, no enforcement), overharvesting increased and efficiency declined, especially among low-performance unions and nonunionized fishers. In rounds 11-20, a weak enforcement regime was introduced: two players per round were randomly inspected, and if caught overharvesting, their entire round's harvest was confiscated. This sanction led to a sharp reduction in overharvest and a large increase in group earnings (efficiency) among unionized fishers, especially those from high-performance unions, who sustained cooperation and high efficiency through the end of the game. Low-performance unions showed a temporary improvement but cooperation eroded over time. Nonunionized fishers did not respond to enforcement and remained inefficient. The paper provides direct evidence that even weak sanctions can improve efficiency in CPR settings, but the effect is strongly moderated by group social capital and prior cooperative norms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a punishment/sanction mechanism (even if weak and probabilistic) in a repeated CPR game with continuous contribution/extraction choices and no communication can substantially increase group efficiency, especially when baseline efficiency is not already at floor. The effect is strongest in groups with high social capital or prior cooperative norms. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment in similar CPR/Pgg-like games will increase efficiency, with the magnitude depending on group composition and baseline cooperativeness. The design features—5 players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous extraction, no rewards, and a probabilistic, high-impact punishment—are all relevant for mapping to prediction. The control (no punishment) condition yields low efficiency, while the treatment (punishment enabled) yields much higher efficiency for unionized groups.

- source: Perez, I., Yu, D. J., Janssen, M. A., & Anderies, J. M. (2015). Social roles and performance of social-ecological systems: evidence from behavioral lab experiments. *Ecology and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that group success in a lab irrigation game is best explained by the presence of certain combinations of emergent social roles, rather than any single role. All groups had leaders, knowledge generators, and followers, but successful groups also had connectors and either moralists or observers. The enforcer role (which involves punishing others' behavior, e.g., via threats to withhold investment) was not necessary for group success and was more common in unsuccessful groups. The presence of enforcers appears to be a symptom of conflict and lack of cooperation, not a cause of improved efficiency. The experiment does not include a formal punishment mechanism (e.g., costly punishment with explicit payoff consequences), so findings about 'enforcer' roles are based on communication and threats rather than actual sanctioning actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling or disabling formal punishment mechanisms on efficiency in public goods or common-pool resource games. Instead, it provides evidence that informal enforcement roles (as expressed in chat) are not sufficient or necessary for group success, and that their presence may indicate underlying conflict. For prediction tasks about the effect of punishment on efficiency, this paper suggests that the mere presence of enforcement or sanctioning behavior (in the absence of formal mechanisms) does not guarantee improved efficiency. The findings are most relevant for settings with communication and emergent informal roles, not for games with explicit, costly punishment options.

- source: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C. (2014). Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In reputation-based helping games (without punishment), cognitive disturbance (superfluous words) reduces cooperation and lowers group payoffs, indicating that efficiency is sensitive to cognitive constraints. In reputation-based punishment games, disturbance increases antisocial punishment and reduces punishment of defectors, but does not significantly change overall cooperation or mean final payoffs. The link between reputation and payoff is weakened under disturbance. The study highlights that experimental outcomes in reputation and punishment games are highly sensitive to environmental and cognitive factors, and that the expected efficiency gains from punishment or reputation mechanisms may not materialize under cognitive load.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effects of punishment and reputation mechanisms in indirect reciprocity games are sensitive to cognitive context. For prediction tasks, it suggests that efficiency gains from punishment (or reputation) may be undermined or nullified if cognitive load is high, even when game design parameters are otherwise favorable. The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

  findings: The study finds that the ability to punish defectors, combined with the visibility of punishment reputation, leads to higher cooperation and higher group payoffs. Punishers are compensated for their costs through increased cooperation from others, and in some groups, punishers even achieve net benefits. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment and the visibility of punishment reputation are key moderators. - source: Konishi, N., & Ohtsubo, Y. (2015). Does dishonesty really invite third-party punishment? Results of a more stringent test. *Biology Letters*.

  findings: The study finds that, even after removing five key methodological confounds (including adding a reward option and ensuring anonymity), a significant minority of third-party observers are willing to incur a personal cost to punish a dishonest trustee, even when the trustee's allocation is fair. The rate of punishment is lower than in previous studies (27% vs. 53%), but still significantly higher in the dishonesty condition than in the honesty condition.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that third-party punishment of norm violations (specifically dishonesty) can occur even when methodological confounds are controlled. However, the design is a modified trust game with third-party punishment and reward, not a public goods game or direct group efficiency context. The outcomes are behavioral (punishment/reward rates and costs), not efficiency or group payoff. Thus, while the paper supports the robustness of third-party punishment as a behavioral phenomenon, it does not provide direct evidence for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. Its main value is in showing that punishment can persist even when alternative explanations are minimized, but it does not inform the quantitative effect of punishment on group efficiency.

However, because the outcome is not group efficiency or total payoff, the results should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The punishment is costless to the punisher and has a fixed impact, which should be considered when mapping to other designs.
