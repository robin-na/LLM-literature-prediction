- source: Chen, J. (2022). Carrots and sticks: new evidence in public goods games with heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Interaction and Coordination*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with heterogeneous groups (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.4), allowing punishment does not significantly increase group efficiency or average earnings compared to the control (no incentives) condition. Average per-period earnings in the punishment condition (17.007 tokens) are actually lower than in the control (23.860 tokens), and after adjusting for the resource destruction effect of punishment, final earnings in the punishment condition (23.630 tokens) are still not higher than control. In contrast, reward opportunities lead to much higher efficiency and earnings. The lack of effectiveness of punishment is attributed to antisocial punishment by high-benefit members and lack of strategic punishment by low-benefit members. The results are robust across group and individual analyses, and the paper provides direct evidence that punishment does not improve efficiency in this heterogeneous PGG setting.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous MPCRs, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. For prediction tasks, if the game design matches this paper (3 players, MPCRs 0.9/0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost 1:3, no rewards in punishment condition, 10 rounds, full feedback), the expected efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to or lower than the control efficiency. The paper also shows that reward is much more effective than punishment in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Wang, T. M., Heine, F., & van Witteloostuijn, A. (2023). Pro-social risk-taking and intergroup conflict: A volunteer's dilemma experiment. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a standard public goods game, pure punishment or pure reward alone (without tax) cannot sustain cooperation in a well-mixed population. Introducing a tax-based mechanism (where all players pay a tax to fund punishment or reward) enables the stable coexistence of cooperators and tax-based pure punishers or rewarders, thus sustaining cooperation. Tax-based pure reward leads to a higher frequency of cooperators than tax-based pure punishment. The analysis is based on replicator dynamics and focuses on the evolutionary stability of strategy frequencies, not on direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that institutional (tax-based) punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation in public goods games, whereas individual-level (pure) punishment or reward cannot. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of the frequency of cooperators, not efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling institutional punishment (via tax) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that institutional reward may be even more effective. However, it does not provide direct quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting treatment efficiency is indirect and limited to the expectation that efficiency should increase when tax-based punishment or reward is enabled.

- source: Karakostas, A., Kocher, M. G., Matzat, D., Rau, H. A., & Riewe, G. (2023). The team allocator game: Allocation power in public goods games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper experimentally compares standard public goods games (VCM) to versions with a team allocator (TAG) who can redistribute the public good among team members. Three production technologies are tested: linear, weakest-link, and best-shot. In the linear case, the team allocator increases both contributions and group efficiency (total profit), as the allocator uses allocation power to reward contributors and sanction free-riders without explicit punishment costs. In the best-shot case, the allocator increases contributions but causes over-contribution and reduces efficiency due to coordination problems. In the weakest-link case, the allocator has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. Thus, the effect of hierarchical allocation power as an implicit sanction/reward mechanism depends crucially on the production technology.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of introducing a sanctioning/reward mechanism (via a team allocator) on efficiency depends on the public goods production function. In linear PGGs, such mechanisms can increase efficiency, but in best-shot games, they may reduce efficiency due to over-contribution. In weakest-link games, the effect is negligible. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the production technology (linear, best-shot, weakest-link), the presence of an allocator with discretionary redistribution power (as a proxy for punishment/reward), and the absence of explicit punishment costs. Control efficiency (VCM) is a strong predictor, but the treatment effect varies by technology. The findings suggest that the efficiency impact of punishment-like mechanisms is positive in linear games, negative in best-shot, and neutral in weakest-link games, even when the mechanism is costless to the allocator.

- source: Falvey, R., Lane, T., & Luckraz, S. (2025). On a mechanism that improves efficiency and reduces inequality in voluntary contribution games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

- source: Oechssler, J., Reischmann, A., & Sofianos, A. (2022). The conditional contribution mechanism for repeated public goods-The general case. *Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The CCM leads to much higher efficiency (as measured by average group contributions and the share of Pareto optimal outcomes) than the standard VCM in repeated public goods games with complete information. The CCM achieves about 78% Pareto optimal outcomes, compared to 24% for the VCM. The SCCM (one-message version) and VCMC (VCM with non-binding communication) perform between the two, with SCCM catching up to CCM in later periods. Under incomplete information, both CCM and VCM achieve similar, moderate efficiency, and the difference is not significant. The risk of being a 'sucker' (earning less than the endowment) is much lower in CCM than in VCM. The study provides strong evidence that the CCM can sustain high efficiency in repeated public goods games without requiring knowledge of others' preferences, but does not test any punishment or sanctioning treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated public goods games with mechanisms that allow conditional contributions, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. The experimental design and outcome measures are directly relevant to efficiency prediction, but since no punishment is present in any treatment, the paper cannot inform the effect of enabling punishment. It does, however, provide a strong benchmark for efficiency in control games without punishment, especially for games with 5 players, 60 rounds, continuous contributions, and no communication or punishment. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper can only inform the baseline (control) efficiency, not the treatment effect of punishment.

- source: Ball, S., Sarangi, S., & Upadhyay, S. (2025). Coalitions Improve the Coordination and Provision of Public Goods: Theory and Experimental Evidence. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that allowing players to form coalitions before making contribution decisions in a binary public goods game leads to higher contributions and higher group payoffs. Coalition members are much more likely to contribute than in the standard VCM, and the decline in contributions over rounds is mitigated. Higher MPCR increases both coalition size and the likelihood of contribution, resulting in higher payoffs for all group members, including the least well-off. The coalition mechanism works by sorting players with stronger social preferences into the coalition, who then coordinate to contribute. The experiment directly compares the standard VCM (control) to the coalition treatment, showing clear increases in group payoff and efficiency when coalitions are allowed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in binary (all-or-nothing) public goods games with induced social preferences, adding a coalition formation stage (where coalition members vote and are bound by majority rule) increases group payoff and efficiency relative to the standard VCM. The effect is stronger at higher MPCR. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without coalition mechanisms, but not for punishment-based interventions, as no punishment or sanctioning is present. The findings suggest that coordination mechanisms (like coalitions) can substitute for punishment in increasing efficiency, especially when MPCR is high and social preferences are present.

- source: Dughera, S. (2022). The evolution of workplace control leadership, obedience and organizational performance. *Journal of Evolutionary Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The model finds that punishment (authoritarian leadership) can increase organizational performance relative to no control when workers are low-skilled or when monitoring is efficient, but is always less efficient than motivation (authoritative leadership) when workers are high-skilled and supervisors are charismatic. Multiple equilibria are possible: (1) 'Discipline' (universal cooperation with punishment), (2) 'Cooperation' (universal cooperation with motivation), (3) 'Conflict' (universal defection with punishment), and (4) mixed or cyclical regimes. The most efficient outcome is universal cooperation with motivation, but this requires high worker skill and supervisor charisma. Punishment can prevent the spread of shirking (the 'bad company' effect) by increasing procedural justice, but also reduces morale and is less efficient. Inefficient equilibria with widespread punishment and/or shirking can persist due to path dependence. The model provides explicit conditions for when each regime is stable and analyzes the welfare properties of each.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence on how punishment affects efficiency in a public-goods-like team production setting, conditional on game design dimensions such as monitoring efficiency (punishment tech), team size (player count), and the presence/absence of reward (motivation/charisma). It shows that punishment can increase efficiency relative to no control when monitoring is effective and workers are not highly skilled, but is always less efficient than a well-functioning reward/motivation regime. The model gives explicit parameter boundaries for when punishment is effective, and highlights the importance of initial conditions and path dependence. For prediction, the paper suggests that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to no-punishment in some parameter regimes (especially with low worker skill and high monitoring efficiency), but that the maximum efficiency is achieved with motivation/charisma rather than punishment. The model is highly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings where reward/motivation is not available or not effective.

- source: Sugaya, T., & Wolitzky, A. (2023). Bad apples in symmetric repeated games. *Theoretical Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in large repeated public goods or similar games, the possibility of sustaining cooperation and high efficiency depends critically on the ability to punish defectors specifically. If the only available incentives are rewards or withdrawal of provision (i.e., voluntary mechanisms), then cooperation collapses and efficiency is low when there are even a few commitment types (bad apples) who always defect. Involuntary punishment (such as fines) that can be targeted at defectors restores the possibility of cooperation and high efficiency. The main technical result is that if a 'population dominant' action exists (i.e., defecting yields a higher payoff than the population average, regardless of others' actions), then equilibrium efficiency converges to the inefficient outcome as the population grows. If no such action exists, and punishment can be targeted, then high efficiency is possible. The results are general and apply to a wide class of symmetric repeated games, including public goods games with or without transfers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods and related games. It implies that enabling targeted, involuntary punishment (e.g., fines for non-contributors) is necessary for sustaining high efficiency in large groups with possible commitment types. If only rewards or voluntary mechanisms are available, efficiency will be low in the presence of even a small fraction of defectors. The paper's results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is symmetric, the population is large, and there is a possibility of commitment types. For the prediction task, the presence or absence of targeted punishment (punishment_tech), the nature of the incentive (reward_exists, punishment_cost), and the ability to distinguish defectors are critical moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) will be low in large groups with commitment types unless involuntary punishment is available; enabling such punishment can dramatically increase efficiency.

- source: Gallo, E., Riyanto, Y. E., Roy, N., & Teh, T. H. (2022). Cooperation and punishment mechanisms in uncertain and dynamic social networks. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated networked Prisoner's Dilemma with 12 players per group and endogenous link formation/removal, the presence of reputational uncertainty (15% chance that intended actions are flipped) leads to a substantial decrease in both cooperation rate and welfare (group payoff), regardless of whether the network is updated slowly or quickly. Fast network updating (more frequent opportunities to change links) has only a marginally positive effect on welfare and cooperation, and does not offset the negative impact of uncertainty. The mechanism is that uncertainty makes players more lenient and forgiving in network punishment (less likely to remove links from defectors), which disrupts the formation of cooperative clusters. Action punishment (retaliatory defection) is also weakened under uncertainty. The study provides direct evidence that punishment mechanisms relying on accurate reputation signals are less effective in promoting efficiency when monitoring is noisy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in dynamic networked repeated games with endogenous partner selection, the effectiveness of punishment mechanisms (here, network punishment via link removal) in increasing efficiency is highly sensitive to the accuracy of reputation signals. When there is reputational uncertainty, punishment is less effective and group efficiency (welfare) is significantly reduced. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of noise or uncertainty in monitoring should be treated as a major moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency. The results also indicate that simply increasing the frequency of punishment opportunities (e.g., faster network updating) does not robustly increase efficiency, especially when uncertainty is present. The findings are most directly relevant to networked PD games, but the mechanisms may transfer to PGG-like environments with endogenous partner choice and noisy monitoring.
