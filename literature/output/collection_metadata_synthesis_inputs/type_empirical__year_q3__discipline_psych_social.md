- source: Benard, S., & Barclay, P. (2020). Democratic Competition for Rank, Cooperation, and Deception in Small Groups. *Social Science Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic competition for high rank in small groups increases group efficiency and public goods contributions compared to resource-based (nondemocratic) competition for rank. In both conditions, the high-ranking member can use costly punishment, but only the method of attaining high rank differs. Democratic groups reward high contributors with high rank, and overall contribute more, leading to higher group efficiency (b = 0.117, p = 0.011). There is no significant difference in the manipulation of apparent threats between conditions. Punishment is more effective at increasing cooperation in democratic groups, but democratically elected leaders may spend more on punishment. The experiment uses a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with fluctuating external threats and a single high-ranking member per group.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-person, 21-round public goods game with costly punishment available to a single high-ranking member, democratic competition for rank (elections) increases group efficiency compared to resource-based competition for rank. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, etc.) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcome is directly measured. The findings suggest that, holding punishment structure constant, the method of selecting the punisher (democratic vs. resource-based) can significantly affect efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the view that democratic selection of punishers/leaders can enhance efficiency in PGG-like environments with similar parameters.

- source: Castillo, J. G., & Hamman, J. (2021). Political Accountability and Democratic Institutions: An Experimental Assessment. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment increases group efficiency (measured as profit) compared to the no-punishment baseline. There is no significant difference in group efficiency or profit between groups with democratically elected managers and those with exogenously appointed managers, regardless of whether managers face repeated elections (political accountability) or not. The presence of punishment, not the method of authority selection or accountability, is the main driver of increased efficiency. The only notable behavioral effect is that managers in the democracy-with-accountability condition do not free ride more than other group members, unlike in other treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a public goods game with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline. However, the specific design features of how the punishing authority is selected (democratic vs. exogenous) and whether there is repeated selection (accountability) do not further increase efficiency. For prediction, the main effect to expect is a positive jump in efficiency when punishment is enabled, but little or no additional effect from varying the selection or accountability mechanism for the punisher. The game design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are clearly specified and can be mapped directly to the prediction task.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Ertör-Akyazi, P. (2019). Formal versus informal institutions: Extraction and earnings in framed field experiments with small-scale fishing communities in Turkey. *Marine Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Turkish small-scale fishers, four treatments were compared: baseline (open-access), face-to-face communication, monetary penalty (punishment for exceeding quota), and purchase of fishing rights (market-based over-quota payment). All groups played a linear PGG extraction game for 15 rounds (pre-treatment), then 14 rounds under one of the four treatments. The monetary penalty (punishment) significantly reduced extraction and moderately increased group earnings compared to baseline, but the effect was smaller than that of communication, which yielded the highest earnings and lowest inequality. The purchase of fishing rights (identical cost to penalty) did not significantly reduce extraction or increase earnings. Thus, punishment can improve efficiency, but its effect is sensitive to framing and is less than that of informal communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that introducing a monetary penalty (punishment) in a linear PGG extraction game with small groups (n=5) and low MPCR (0.4) increases group efficiency (earnings) relative to open-access, but the effect is moderate. The effect is smaller than that of enabling face-to-face communication. The framing of the punishment matters: a market-based 'purchase of rights' with identical cost is less effective than a penalty. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency over baseline, but the magnitude depends on the punishment's framing and the presence of communication. Control efficiency in this context is already above the selfish prediction, so the incremental gain from punishment is moderate. The results are most applicable to small groups, continuous extraction, no chat in baseline/punishment, and low but certain punishment.

- source: Waichman, I., & Stenzel, L. (2019). When Punishment Strikes Late: The Effect of a Delay in Punishment and Punishment Feedback on Cooperation and Efficiency. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public good game compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect holds even when punishment is delayed by five periods, as long as participants receive salient feedback linking punishment to their past contributions. If this feedback is absent (i.e., participants are punished after a delay and are not reminded of their past behavior), punishment still increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, due to increased punishment costs and less targeted norm enforcement. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency thus depends on the informational environment, specifically whether punished individuals can clearly associate punishment with their own prior actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with four players and 20 rounds, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, provided that feedback links punishment to past contributions. The effect is robust to a five-period delay in punishment, as long as feedback is salient. If the design omits such feedback, punishment may still increase cooperation but not efficiency, due to higher punishment costs and less effective norm targeting. For prediction, the presence or absence of feedback linking punishment to behavior is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is lower than treatment efficiency (with punishment and feedback), but not when feedback is absent. Other design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are held constant in this study, so the main moderator evidenced is the feedback/identification of norm violation.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

Importantly, even when herding, buyers still consider seller reputation, and the effect of reputation on sales and price remains significant. There is no evidence that herding undermines the efficiency or trust-enhancing effect of reputation systems in this market context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that reputation mechanisms (a form of informal sanctioning) increase efficiency in online market environments by raising both the probability of sale and the price sellers can obtain. While not a public goods game or direct punishment setting, the findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of reputation-based sanctions on efficiency in environments where trust and cooperation are important. The results suggest that, even in the presence of herding (social influence), reputation systems continue to enhance efficiency. However, the market context differs from standard PGGs, so direct quantitative transfer should be cautious.
