- source: Shen, Y., Yin, W. K., Kang, H. W., Zhang, H. G., & Wang, M. (2022). High-reputation individuals exert greater influence on cooperation in spatial public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper develops and analyzes a theoretical model of a public goods game with tax-based punishment and reward mechanisms. It shows that: (1) Tax-based rewards given directly to cooperators are more effective at promoting cooperation and group payoff than rewards given to punishers. (2) When rewards are used to support punishers, this can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce overall cooperation and efficiency, as punishers and cooperators end up in competition. (3) The effect of punishment and reward on efficiency is highly sensitive to the way these mechanisms are implemented, with some parameter regimes leading to lower group payoff than the baseline. (4) Increasing taxes (used as rewards for cooperators) generally increases the proportion of cooperators and system efficiency, but excessive rewards to punishers can be detrimental.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the design of the reward and punishment mechanisms, especially whether rewards are given to cooperators or punishers. For prediction, it suggests that enabling punishment does not always increase efficiency: if rewards are used to support punishers, efficiency may decrease compared to control. If tax-based rewards are given to cooperators, efficiency is more likely to increase. The model also provides explicit payoff functions and equilibrium conditions that can be used to estimate efficiency under different parameterizations (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost, reward structure). However, the results are theoretical and may not capture all behavioral nuances seen in experiments.

The effect is present for both perfectly rational and boundedly rational agents. The degree of rationality (K) also modulates the equilibrium, but punishment always leads to higher system payoffs and more cooperation than the control. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment in a similar game design will increase efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low due to defection. The specific parameterization (N=6, r=3.6, c=1.25, punishment fine and cost) is informative for quantitative prediction.

- source: Sigmund, K., Hauert, C., Traulsen, A., & De Silva, H. (2011). Social Control and the Social Contract: The Emergence of Sanctioning Systems for Collective Action. *Dynamic Games and Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Punishment (peer or pool) can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, especially when participation is voluntary. (2) Pool punishment with second-order punishment is particularly effective, leading to stable, high-efficiency cooperative regimes. (3) Peer punishment can also support cooperation, but is less robust and can be undermined by second-order free riders. (4) Compulsory participation (no option to abstain) undermines the effectiveness of punishment, resulting in dominance by defectors and low efficiency. (5) The costs of punishment (especially pool punishment) can reduce efficiency if not balanced by increased cooperation, but with sufficient rounds or strong imitation, efficiency gains are realized. (6) The structure of the punishment mechanism (peer vs. pool, presence of second-order punishment) and the option for voluntary participation are key moderators of the efficiency effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases efficiency, especially when participation is voluntary and when pool punishment with second-order punishment is available. The model shows that the design dimensions—such as player count, number of rounds, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and whether participation is voluntary—critically shape the efficiency outcome. In particular, pool punishment with second-order punishment and voluntary participation are predicted to yield the highest efficiency. Peer punishment can also increase efficiency, but is less stable and more vulnerable to free-riding. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency, enabling punishment (especially with the above features) is likely to substantially increase efficiency. However, if punishment is costly and not well-targeted, or if participation is compulsory, efficiency gains may be limited or negative.

  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that peer punishment increases cooperation and group payoff (efficiency) in public goods games, but this effect is undermined if defectors can cheaply disguise themselves to avoid punishment. When the cost of disguise is high, or when punishment is strong (high fine, low cost), punishment is effective at promoting cooperation and high group payoff. Second-order punishment further stabilizes cooperation and punishment strategies, even in the presence of disguise. In finite populations, the stationary distribution of strategies shows that low disguise cost leads to defection (low efficiency), while high disguise cost or strong punishment leads to cooperation or punishment (high efficiency). Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment depends on the interplay between punishment parameters and the cost/effectiveness of disguise.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the fine imposed, and the possibility/cost of disguise by defectors. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency due to high defection, enabling punishment will increase efficiency only if punishment is strong enough and disguise is not too cheap. If disguise is cheap, punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The model also shows that second-order punishment can further increase efficiency by stabilizing punishment strategies. For prediction, the paper supports that lower punishment cost, higher punishment effectiveness, and higher fine on defectors all increase the efficiency gain from punishment, but this is moderated (and can be negated) by the ability of defectors to disguise at low cost.
