- source: Sefton, M., Shupp, R., & Walker, J. M. (2007). The effect of rewards and sanctions in provision of public goods. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with four players and ten rounds per sequence, the introduction of costly sanctioning (punishment) opportunities after an initial baseline phase leads to sustained higher contributions and, after an initial efficiency loss due to sanctioning costs, to higher group efficiency compared to the baseline. Reward opportunities alone do not maintain high contributions or efficiency, as the use of rewards declines quickly. The combination of sanctions and rewards produces the highest average contributions and earnings, but the effect is primarily due to the sanctioning mechanism. The cost structure is linear: each token used for punishment costs the punisher 10 cents and reduces the target's earnings by 10 cents (total group loss: 20 cents per token). The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is 0.5. Players cannot identify who punished or rewarded them, and all information is anonymous. No communication is allowed. The number of rounds is known in advance. The findings are robust across groups, though there is heterogeneity in group-level outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a standard linear public goods game with four players, ten rounds, no communication, and known round structure. The results show that enabling costly punishment (with a 1:1 cost-impact ratio) increases efficiency over the baseline, but only after an initial period where the direct costs of punishment outweigh the gains from increased cooperation. Over time, as the threat of punishment sustains cooperation and the use of punishment declines, efficiency improves. The effect is robust to the absence of communication and anonymity of punishment. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs (4 players, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.5, linear punishment, no chat, anonymous), enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control, especially in later rounds. The cost structure of punishment is critical: with a 1:1 cost-impact, the efficiency gain is positive but moderate; more effective punishment (higher impact per cost) would likely yield larger efficiency gains. The paper also shows that reward mechanisms alone are insufficient to sustain efficiency. These results should be directly used for prediction in similar lab PGG settings.

- source: Noussair, C., & Tucker, S. (2005). Combining monetary and social sanctions to promote cooperation. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with groups of 4 over 15 rounds, three treatments were compared: monetary punishment (MP), nonmonetary punishment (NP), and both punishments (BP). All sanctioning systems increased contributions and welfare relative to no-sanction baselines from prior literature. NP (informal) sanctions alone initially produced high welfare but lost effectiveness over time. MP (monetary) sanctions sustained higher contributions but incurred enforcement costs, reducing efficiency. The BP treatment, combining both, resulted in the highest group earnings and contributions in the later rounds, with lower deadweight loss from punishment than MP. The combined system allows for nuanced sanctioning, using costless informal sanctions when effective and costly formal sanctions when needed, maximizing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard VCM with 4 players, 15 rounds, and no communication, enabling both monetary and nonmonetary punishment increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) above either system alone. The efficiency gain is especially pronounced in later rounds, as informal-only sanctions lose effectiveness. The combined system also reduces the deadweight loss from punishment compared to monetary-only systems. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling both types of punishment should be expected to yield a substantial and sustained increase in efficiency, with the combined system outperforming either punishment type alone. The results are directly relevant for standard PGG/VCM designs with similar parameters.

- source: Casari, M., & Luini, L. (2009). Cooperation under alternative punishment institutions: An experiment. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares three peer punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game: Baseline (standard, individual punishment), Sequential (punishment with information about others' punishment actions), and Consensual (punishment only enacted if at least two group members request it). All treatments use groups of 5, 20 rounds (10 without punishment, 10 with), no communication, and full information about group contributions and payoffs (but not identities). The marginal per capita return is 0.4. Punishment costs 1 token per point and reduces the target's payoff by 3 tokens per point. The main findings are: (1) Punishment opportunities increase contributions in all treatments, but only the Consensual institution increases net earnings (efficiency) above the no-punishment baseline. In Baseline and Sequential, punishment reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. (2) The Consensual institution achieves higher cooperation and efficiency with less punishment, as it censors anti-social punishment (punishment of high contributors) and requires consensus to punish. (3) Providing more information about others' punishment actions (Sequential) does not improve coordination or efficiency. (4) The form of the punishment institution is a critical moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public goods games is highly sensitive to the institutional details of the punishment mechanism. In standard and sequential punishment settings (where any individual can punish unilaterally), punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs. In contrast, a consensual punishment rule (requiring at least two agents to agree to punish) both increases contributions and improves efficiency, even surpassing the no-punishment baseline. For prediction, this means that the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be inferred from control efficiency and game structure alone; the specific punishment institution (especially whether it censors anti-social punishment and requires consensus) is a key moderator. The paper also shows that increasing information about others' punishment actions does not necessarily improve efficiency. Thus, for games with similar structure, expect punishment to reduce efficiency unless the institution filters out anti-social punishment and requires consensus.

- source: Nikiforakis, N., Normann, H. T., & Wallace, B. (2010). Asymmetric Enforcement of Cooperation in a Social Dilemma. *Southern Economic Journal*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This laboratory experiment compares symmetric and asymmetric punishment institutions in a standard public goods game with punishment. Asymmetry is introduced by giving one player higher punishment effectiveness (impact per unit cost) than the other three. The main findings are: (1) Asymmetric punishment institutions are as effective as symmetric ones in sustaining cooperation and efficiency, as measured by group earnings. (2) There is no significant difference in efficiency (group earnings) between symmetric and asymmetric punishment institutions, controlling for average punishment effectiveness. (3) Higher average punishment effectiveness increases contributions, but does not significantly increase efficiency. (4) Strong players (with higher punishment effectiveness) punish more and earn more than weak players, but do not contribute less. (5) The behavioral regularities of symmetric punishment institutions (e.g., punishment targeting free riders, response to punishment) carry over to asymmetric institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the presence of asymmetry in punishment effectiveness (i.e., one player can punish more effectively than others) does not significantly affect group efficiency compared to symmetric punishment, as long as average punishment effectiveness is held constant. Thus, for the prediction task, the efficiency effect of enabling punishment should be predicted primarily based on the average punishment effectiveness, not on whether punishment power is symmetric or asymmetric. The paper also confirms that higher average punishment effectiveness increases contributions, but this does not necessarily translate into higher efficiency due to the costs of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between symmetric and asymmetric punishment with the same average effectiveness.

- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2009). Public Goods Provision and Sanctioning in Privileged Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares standard (normal) and privileged public goods groups, with and without the option for decentralized costly punishment. In normal groups (all members have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment substantially increases contributions and group earnings, especially in later rounds. In privileged groups (one member has a high marginal return, others low), punishment increases contributions and earnings only slightly, and it increases inequality. The main reason is that punishment is less targeted and less effective in privileged groups: high-benefit members punish in an unstructured way, and low-benefit members respond weakly to punishment. As a result, the efficiency gains from punishment are much smaller in privileged groups than in normal groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends strongly on group composition, specifically on whether the group is homogeneous (normal) or contains privileged members (heterogeneous marginal returns). For standard linear PGGs with 3 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, punishment increases efficiency, especially in later rounds. However, if the group is privileged (one high-benefit, two low-benefit), the efficiency gain from punishment is much smaller, and inequality increases. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to account for heterogeneity in marginal returns. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher in privileged groups, but the treatment effect of punishment is much weaker than in normal groups.

- source: Decker, T., Stiehler, A., & Strobel, M. (2003). A comparison of punishment rules in repeated public good games - An experimental study. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that collective punishment rules generally induce more punishment activity and higher contributions than the individual rule. The most severe collective rule (maxi) achieves the highest contributions but not the highest efficiency, as the costs of punishment offset the gains from increased cooperation. Efficiency (group profit after punishment costs) does not differ significantly between rules, and the relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is reversed compared to the relationship with contributions. Emotional punishment, especially in the most severe rule, can damage efficiency. Justice (as measured by profit-contribution correlation and variance) improves with more severe collective rules. Participants are more likely to support collective rules if they expect higher profits and if the rule requires a higher degree of consent (e.g., unanimity).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public good game with punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not guarantee higher efficiency or group profit. The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the severity and structure of the punishment rule: more severe rules can increase cooperation but may reduce efficiency if punishment costs are high, especially due to emotional punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency with punishment is non-monotonic and depends on the balance between increased cooperation and punishment costs. Game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology, and the collective/individual nature of punishment are critical moderators. The paper's results caution against assuming that more severe or more available punishment will always improve efficiency.

- source: Bochet, O., Page, T., & Putterman, L. (2006). Communication and punishment in voluntary contribution experiments. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a standard voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM) public goods game, adding a costly punishment option increases contributions but does not increase efficiency or group earnings, because the costs of punishing and being punished offset the gains from higher contributions. Communication, especially face-to-face or via anonymous chat, dramatically increases both contributions and efficiency, with chat being nearly as effective as face-to-face. Adding punishment to communication does not significantly increase contributions or efficiency further. Numerical 'cheap talk' communication (non-binding numerical announcements) has no significant effect on contributions or efficiency. The study also finds that a notable fraction of punishment is 'perverse' (targeted at high contributors), which can reduce their future contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 4-player, 10-round VCM with MPCR=0.4, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not improve efficiency or group earnings compared to the control (no-punishment) condition. The cost of punishment cancels out the benefit from higher contributions. If the control game has low efficiency, enabling punishment is unlikely to increase efficiency unless communication is also enabled. Communication (especially open-ended, verbal or chat) is a much more effective lever for increasing efficiency. The specific punishment parameters (cost=0.25 per $1 reduced, impact=1 per $0.25 spent) are important for generalization. The findings suggest that, for similar lab VCMs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be similar to the control, unless communication is also present.

- source: Lopez, M. C., Murphy, J. J., Spraggon, J. M., & Stranlund, J. K. (2012). COMPARING THE EFFECTIVENESS OF REGULATION AND PRO-SOCIAL EMOTIONS TO ENHANCE COOPERATION: EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FROM FISHING COMMUNITIES IN COLOMBIA. *Economic Inquiry*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a framed field experiment with Colombian fishing communities, groups of 5 played a 15-round linear public goods game with a marginal per capita return of 0.4. Treatments included a baseline, a 'frame' (reminder of social optimum), 'guilt' (private feedback), 'shame' (random public revelation of non-cooperation), and two punishment treatments (low and high penalty, both with 1/5 audit probability and per-unit fines). Both punishment treatments increased contributions and expected earnings relative to baseline, but the high-penalty treatment (designed to induce full compliance) resulted in the lowest average expected earnings due to fines. The shame treatment produced the highest contributions and expected earnings, significantly outperforming both punishment treatments. The paper concludes that social mechanisms (shame) can be more effective and efficient than regulatory punishment in promoting cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with 5 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known round count, introducing monetary punishment (with random audits and per-unit fines) increases efficiency relative to baseline, but the effect is limited: high-penalty punishment can reduce efficiency due to fines. The effect of punishment is smaller than that of a social mechanism (random public revelation/shame). For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment will not always increase efficiency above control, especially if fines are high and compliance is imperfect. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit probability, punishment cost/tech) are all specified, and the results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency under punishment in similar field or framed settings.

  findings: In a linear public goods game with five players and repeated rounds, the introduction of a nonbinding voting mechanism alone does not significantly increase efficiency or sustain higher contributions compared to the standard individual contribution baseline. However, when a costly peer punishment mechanism is added—allowing group members to punish those who deviate from the majority-voted contribution plan—both contributions and efficiency increase substantially and persistently. The efficiency in the nonbinding-vote-with-punishment treatment is significantly higher than in the nonbinding-vote treatment and approaches the efficiency observed in the binding-vote (third-party enforced) treatment. The cost of punishment is more than offset by the gains in group payoff, so overall efficiency rises with punishment enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with five players, 10 rounds per stage, and a marginal per-capita return of 0.4, enabling costly peer punishment in a nonbinding voting environment leads to a large and persistent increase in efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust and the efficiency gain outweighs the cost of punishment. Thus, when predicting the efficiency of a public goods game with punishment enabled, this paper supports a substantial positive adjustment relative to the control (no-punishment) efficiency, provided the punishment mechanism is similar (costly, peer-administered, and targeted at non-cooperators). The findings are directly relevant for games with similar design parameters.

- source: Nikiforakis, N., & Engelmann, D. (2011). Altruistic punishment and the threat of feuds. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This experiment compares three public goods game treatments: (1) Baseline (B), where punishment is possible but cannot be avenged (single punishment stage, random IDs each round); (2) Short Feuds (SF), where multiple punishment stages are possible within a round but IDs are randomized each round (so feuds cannot span rounds); and (3) Long Feuds (LF), where multiple punishment stages are possible and IDs are fixed, so feuds can span rounds. The study finds that as the threat of feuds increases (from B to SF to LF), the frequency of altruistic punishment decreases, and so does the threat of punishment for free-riding. In LF, where feuds are most possible, both cooperation and group earnings decline over time relative to the baseline. Severe punishment is used more in SF to preempt counter-punishment, but in LF, punishment is rare and less targeted at free riders. Overall, the possibility of feuds undermines the efficiency gains from punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether punishment can be avenged (i.e., whether feuds are possible). In standard designs where punishment is one-shot and anonymous, punishment can increase or maintain efficiency. However, when punishment can be countered and feuds can develop (especially across rounds), the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is greatly reduced or even reversed, as both cooperation and group earnings decline. For prediction, the presence of counter-punishment opportunities and the ability to identify punishers are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher than with punishment when feuds are possible, due to the breakdown of cooperation and increased costs from feuding.

  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the introduction of a costly punishment option leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation and a 40% increase in aggregate monetary payoff, even after subtracting the costs of punishment. The findings support the prediction that, controlling for control-game efficiency, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently effective and observable.

  findings: In a four-person linear public good game, groups with heterogeneous productivity (MPCRs 0.9 and 0.3, average 0.6) contribute less than homogeneous groups (all MPCR 0.6) when punishment is not allowed. Introducing a costly punishment mechanism (punisher pays 1, target loses 2 per point) in heterogeneous groups significantly increases average contributions to levels similar to homogeneous groups without punishment. - source: Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2012). Self-organization of punishment in structured populations. *New Journal of Physics*.

  findings: Adaptive punishment in spatial public goods games enables the spontaneous emergence of rare, localized punishers who respond to the spread of defection. This mechanism maintains smooth interfaces between cooperators and defectors, invigorates spatial reciprocity, and prevents the emergence of cyclic dominance that would otherwise allow defectors to persist. As a result, adaptive punishment can achieve full cooperation (pure C phase) in parameter regions where, without punishment, only defectors would survive. The cost of punishment, when adaptively deployed, is globally negligible, making the mechanism highly efficient. Compared to steady (non-adaptive) punishment, adaptive punishment is more effective at eliminating defectors and does so with lower cost.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that adaptive, context-sensitive punishment mechanisms in spatially structured public goods games can substantially increase the likelihood of full cooperation and, by implication, group efficiency, even when punishment is costly. The effect is especially pronounced when punishment is inexpensive, but remains positive even for costly punishment. The findings suggest that models or interventions that allow for adaptive, rather than fixed, punishment are likely to yield higher efficiency than those with no punishment or with steady punishment. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff, so predictions should be based on the qualitative pattern that adaptive punishment expands the parameter space for high efficiency.
