- source: Sääksvuori, L., Mappes, T., & Puurtinen, M. (2011). Costly punishment prevails in intergroup conflict. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In isolated public goods games, costly punishment increases cooperation but does not significantly increase net pay-off (efficiency), as the costs of punishment offset the gains from higher contributions. When intergroup competition is introduced, groups with punishment opportunities achieve much higher contributions and net pay-offs, both in asymmetric (one group can punish, one cannot) and symmetric (both can punish) competition. In asymmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is dramatic: even the lowest-earning individual in the punishment group outperforms the highest in the no-punishment group. In symmetric competition, the efficiency benefit is positive but smaller and only marginally significant. Punishment also reduces within-group inequality in pay-offs. The paper concludes that intergroup competition is a key moderator of the efficiency effects of costly punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on whether there is intergroup competition. In the absence of group competition, enabling punishment does not reliably increase efficiency. With group competition, punishment can substantially increase efficiency, especially when only some groups have access to punishment. The design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of group competition, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (8), number of rounds (30), and the absence of communication or reputation mechanisms. The control efficiency (no punishment) is much lower than the treatment efficiency (with punishment) in the presence of group competition, but not in isolated groups. This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes in PGGs with and without punishment, especially when intergroup competition is a design feature.

- source: O'Gorman, R., Henrich, J., & Van Vugt, M. (2009). Constraining free riding in public goods games: designated solitary punishers can sustain human cooperation. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compared three conditions in a repeated public goods game: (1) no punishment (control), (2) all group members can punish (all-punishment), and (3) only one randomly selected group member can punish (one-punishment). Both punishment conditions led to significantly higher and more stable contributions than the control. Profits (efficiency) were higher in the one-punishment condition than in the all-punishment condition, and both were more stable than the control. The cost of punishment reduced profits relative to the control in the short run, but the control condition's profits declined over rounds due to decaying cooperation, while punishment conditions maintained stable profits. The one-punisher regime avoids redundant punishment costs, making it more efficient. The punishment mechanism used a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio. The study provides direct evidence that both diffuse and designated punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency, but a single punisher can do so more efficiently.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard four-player, six-round public goods game with a 1:3 punishment cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment increases efficiency (group profit) relative to a no-punishment control. Furthermore, a single designated punisher is as effective as allowing all members to punish in sustaining cooperation, but is more efficient due to lower aggregate punishment costs. For prediction, this suggests that, under similar game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, and that the structure of the punishment regime (single vs. diffuse) can moderate the efficiency gains. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Traulsen, A., Röhl, T., & Milinski, M. (2012). An economic experiment reveals that humans prefer pool punishment to maintain the commons. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with five players per group, the introduction of pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) increases cooperation and reduces defection, but leads to a significant loss in efficiency (average payoff per round drops by about a third). Peer punishment alone does not significantly affect efficiency, as punishment is rarely used when cooperation is high. When both peer and pool punishment are available, pool punishment dominates in the presence of second-order punishment, again with a substantial efficiency loss. The results support the theoretical prediction that pool punishment can stabilize cooperation but at the cost of group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard public goods game with five players, 25 rounds, no communication, binary (all-or-nothing) contributions, and known punishment costs and effectiveness, enabling pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) reduces efficiency compared to the control (no or only peer punishment). The efficiency loss is substantial (about one third) when pool punishment is used with second-order punishment. Peer punishment alone does not significantly reduce efficiency. Thus, for prediction tasks, the presence and type of punishment mechanism (peer vs pool), and whether second-order punishment is implemented, are critical moderators of the efficiency effect. The specific parameterization (punishment cost, punishment impact, MPCR) is also informative for quantitative prediction.

  findings: The study finds that punishment only maintains cooperation when it is relatively effective (low cost to punisher, high impact on punished). However, even when cooperation is sustained, group and individual payoffs are lower than in the control without punishment, because the costs of punishment exceed the benefits from increased cooperation. The threshold of free-riding that goes unpunished increases as punishment becomes less effective. The main determinant of punishment's effect is the cost-to-impact ratio. The results suggest that altruistic punishment alone is insufficient to maintain cooperation efficiently, as it reduces group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard PGG with anonymous, one-shot (perfect stranger) interactions, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency and can reduce it, especially when punishment is effective (low cost, high impact). The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost-to-impact ratio: only when punishment is cheap and effective does it sustain cooperation, but even then, group payoffs are lower than in the no-punishment control. For prediction, this suggests that unless punishment is both cheap and highly effective, enabling punishment will not improve and may reduce efficiency relative to control. The control efficiency (without punishment) is always higher than with punishment in this design. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions, especially punishment cost and effectiveness.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Sasaki, T., & Zhang, B. Y. (2019). The competitive advantage of institutional reward. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

- source: Mathew, S., & Boyd, R. (2009). When does optional participation allow the evolution of cooperation?. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that optional participation only allows punishment to invade and stabilize cooperation (and thus increase efficiency) under specific conditions: (1) loners must have higher payoffs than defectors, (2) two cooperators must have higher per capita payoffs than loners, and (3) the public good must have constant or increasing returns to scale. If these conditions are not met, the system does not support oscillations that allow punishment to invade, and the long-run outcome is either all defectors or all loners, both of which are inefficient. Thus, the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the game's payoff structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with optional participation is not universal, but depends on specific game design dimensions: the relative payoffs of loners, defectors, and cooperators, and the returns to scale in public goods production. For prediction, this means that knowing the control efficiency is not sufficient; one must also know whether the game's payoff structure meets the three key conditions identified. If so, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency; if not, punishment may have little or no effect. The paper does not provide quantitative predictions but offers clear qualitative moderators.

- source: Powers, S. T., Perret, C., & Currie, T. E. (2023). Playing the political game: the coevolution of institutions with group size and political inequality. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that institutionally arranged punishment in public goods games allows groups to sustain high levels of cooperation and achieve much larger group sizes (higher efficiency/welfare) than groups without such institutions. As groups grow, the cost of forming consensus over rules increases, but the evolution of hierarchy (a skewed distribution of influence) allows large groups to maintain efficient cooperation-promoting institutions by reducing the cost of consensus formation. Groups that invest just enough in punishment to deter defection, while allocating the rest to technology (increasing carrying capacity), achieve the highest efficiency. If hierarchy cannot evolve, large groups cannot sustain cooperation and remain small and inefficient. The model also shows that if the political game (consensus formation) is too difficult, groups may fail to maintain cooperation-promoting institutions, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency, especially as group size increases, provided that institutional rules are set optimally (not over- or under-investing in punishment). The model also highlights that the cost of consensus formation (political game) can limit the efficiency gains from punishment in large groups unless hierarchy evolves to reduce these costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly moderated by group size, the cost of institutional rule formation, and the ability to evolve or implement hierarchy. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where punishment is institutionally arranged and the costs of monitoring/sanctioning are covered by the group, rather than being individually altruistic.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2013). The evolution of cooperation by social exclusion. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that social exclusion is a more effective and robust mechanism than costly punishment for sustaining cooperation and high efficiency in public goods games. When excluders can effectively and cheaply exclude defectors, the group can reach and maintain the efficient outcome where all contribute and defectors are eliminated. Costly punishment, even when effective, is vulnerable to invasion by non-punishing cooperators (second-order freeriders), leading to a decline in punishers and eventual dominance by defectors, which collapses efficiency. The model demonstrates that social exclusion can solve both the emergence and maintenance problems of cooperation, provided the cost of exclusion is not too high. The results are robust to changes in group size and to stochastic effects. The paper also notes that if monitoring costs are high, or if exclusion is not self-serving, the advantage of social exclusion disappears.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling a punishment mechanism that takes the form of social exclusion (where defectors are excluded from benefit sharing and the cost of exclusion is not too high) will lead to a large increase in group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across group sizes and does not require repeated interaction or reputation. In contrast, enabling costly punishment (where punishers pay to reduce defectors' payoffs) is less likely to increase efficiency, especially in the long run, due to vulnerability to second-order freeriders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social exclusion mechanism with low cost and high effectiveness should be treated as a strong positive moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment. The specific model parameters (group size, multiplication factor, cost of exclusion/punishment, effectiveness) are all explicitly analyzed, allowing for quantitative predictions if mapped to empirical settings.

- source: Adami, C., Schossau, J., & Hintze, A. (2016). Evolutionary game theory using agent-based methods. *Physics of Life Reviews*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. The effect is quantitatively predicted by mean-field theory and confirmed by simulations: higher probability or density of punishment lowers the threshold for cooperation. However, because punishment is costly, it creates a second-order dilemma, and the evolutionary dynamics can lead to meta-stable states and hysteresis. When punishment is allowed to evolve, its effect is strongest near the critical point, and in highly cooperative or highly defective regimes, punishment is not maintained by selection. The findings are robust across different group sizes and mutation rates, and the paper provides explicit equations and simulation results for the effect of punishment on group payoff and cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (payoff) in the dilemma region by shifting the critical point for cooperation to lower values of the synergy parameter (r). The effect size and direction depend on the punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness), group size, and whether punishment is fixed or can evolve. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.

- source: Archetti, M., Scheuring, I., Hoffman, M., Frederickson, M. E., Pierce, N. E., & Yu, D. W. (2011). Economic game theory for mutualism and cooperation. *Ecology Letters*.

  findings: The paper demonstrates that in nonlinear public goods games (which are more biologically realistic than linear ones), a mixed equilibrium with both cooperators and defectors can exist, and group payoff (efficiency) can be substantial and close to maximum even without assortment or repeated interactions. The effect of punishment or sanctions (host sanctions) is formally distinguished from partner fidelity feedback, with different evolutionary implications. The models show that the shape of the public good function (nonlinearity) is a key moderator of efficiency and cooperation, and that punishment/sanctions can maintain cooperation, but the resulting equilibrium is generally not fully efficient. The paper does not present new empirical data but provides a theoretical framework for interpreting efficiency and punishment effects in public-goods-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions, especially the shape of the public good function (linearity vs. nonlinearity), group size, and cost/benefit ratio. It clarifies that punishment/sanctions (host sanctions) can support cooperation and increase group payoff, but the efficiency gain depends on whether the mechanism is true punishment (requiring commitment) or partner fidelity feedback (self-serving host response). The models suggest that in nonlinear public goods games, efficiency can be high even without punishment, and the marginal effect of punishment may be limited by the structure of the game. The paper is highly relevant for interpreting the likely efficiency effects of punishment in both linear and nonlinear public goods games, but does not provide direct empirical estimates.

- source: Guala, F. (2012). Reciprocity: Weak or strong? What punishment experiments do (and do not) demonstrate. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that laboratory experiments with public goods games and punishment show that while punishment can increase cooperation rates, it often reduces group efficiency due to the destruction of resources. Efficiency gains from punishment are only observed under specific conditions, such as very long repeated interactions with the same group, and even then, the need for punishment diminishes as cooperation stabilizes. In field and ethnographic studies, there is little evidence for the use of costly, uncoordinated punishment to sustain cooperation; instead, low- or no-cost sanctions (gossip, ostracism, coordinated punishment) are prevalent and effective. The paper argues that the external validity of laboratory punishment experiments is limited, and that real-world institutions that sustain cooperation do so by reducing the cost of punishment, extending the time horizon, and improving information flow.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that enabling costly, uncoordinated punishment in public goods games is unlikely to increase efficiency except under narrow conditions (e.g., long repeated games with stable groups). In most laboratory settings, punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to resource destruction. In real-world or field-like settings, cooperation is more effectively sustained by low-cost, coordinated, or symbolic sanctions, not by costly punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper implies that the presence of costly punishment (especially if uncoordinated and not paired with communication or institutional support) should not be expected to reliably increase efficiency over the control condition, and may even reduce it. Game design dimensions that reduce the cost of punishment, allow communication, or enable coordination are likely to moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.
