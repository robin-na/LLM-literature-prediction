- source: Bühren, C., Haarde, J., Hirschmann, C., & Kesten-Kühne, J. (2023). Social preferences in the public goods game-An Agent-Based simulation with EconSim. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The agent-based model, using reinforcement learning and a social preference framework, closely replicates the experimental findings of [20] in a public goods game with endogenous punishment. For low cooperators, severe punishment increases contributions and can raise payoffs toward the social optimum, but mild punishment is ineffective and reduces payoffs due to its cost. For high cooperators, punishment (both mild and severe) increases contributions only slightly but reduces payoffs because the cost of punishment outweighs the marginal gain in cooperation. Middle cooperators benefit from mild punishment, which increases both contributions and payoffs. The model demonstrates that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly dependent on the underlying social preference structure of the group. Endogenous institution choice in the simulation leads agents to select the institution that maximizes their own payoffs, mirroring the learning observed in the experimental Sorted-Info treatment but not in the Sorted treatment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games with known design parameters. The simulation results, validated against experimental data, show that the impact of punishment on efficiency depends on the group’s baseline cooperativeness: severe punishment can increase efficiency for low cooperators but is costly and can reduce efficiency for high cooperators. The model’s detailed reporting of payoffs under different punishment regimes, player types, and institution selection mechanisms makes it highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings highlight the importance of considering both the cost and effectiveness of punishment, as well as the social preference composition of the group.

- source: Zefferman, M. R. (2023). Constraints on cooperation shape hierarchical versus distributed structure in human groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the efficiency of punishment and monitoring technologies fundamentally shapes the optimal structure of cooperation in public goods games. When punishment is efficient (i.e., the cost to punishers is low relative to the impact on free-riders), hierarchical (tree-like) organizations are optimal and can support universal cooperation (full efficiency) even in large groups. When punishment is inefficient (costly for punishers), distributed (collective) monitoring and punishment are optimal, but these structures are limited in scale by monitoring costs. The model provides explicit analytic conditions for when universal contribution (full cooperation) is a Nash equilibrium and socially efficient, and when universal free-riding is the only equilibrium. The model also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment networks (hierarchical vs. distributed) is an endogenous outcome of the costs and effectiveness of these institutions. Thus, the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency depends critically on the cost-effectiveness of punishment and monitoring, as well as group size.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games as a function of game design dimensions. It shows that enabling punishment can increase group efficiency (by supporting universal cooperation) if punishment is sufficiently efficient (low cost, high impact), and if monitoring is not prohibitively costly. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds at which universal cooperation is efficient and stable, depending on group size, public goods multiplier, monitoring efficiency, and punishment efficiency. It also shows that the structure of monitoring and punishment (hierarchical vs. distributed) is determined by these parameters, which in turn affects the scalability of cooperation. For prediction, the model implies that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be more positive when punishment is cheap and effective, monitoring is efficient, and group size is not too large for the given monitoring technology. If punishment is costly or monitoring is inefficient, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency, especially in larger groups.

- source: Ohdaira, T. (2022). The probabilistic pool punishment proportional to the difference of payoff outperforms previous pool and peer punishment. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the proposed probabilistic pool punishment mechanism, which ties the probability of paying the punishment cost to the difference in payoffs, results in a population with more punishers and fewer cooperators (who do not punish) compared to traditional pool punishment. This state is more robust to the invasion of defectors and maintains an average payoff that is comparable to or better than peer punishment. The results hold across both regular and random one-dimensional lattice structures. The study also notes that the fixed cost problem of pool punishment is solved, and that the mechanism is effective even when the number of defectors is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism—specifically, making punishment probabilistic and proportional to payoff differences—can improve group efficiency (average payoff) in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a more adaptive, probabilistic, and context-sensitive punishment regime (as opposed to fixed-cost or deterministic punishment) is likely to yield higher efficiency, especially in structured populations. The results are most directly applicable to games with 5-player groups, 1000-player populations, 600 rounds (generations), no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR and punishment cost parameters as specified. The study does not vary most game design dimensions, so its predictive value is strongest for the specific parameter regime tested.

- source: Wang, C. Q., Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2024). Evolutionary dynamics of any multiplayer game on regular graphs. *Nature Communications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper analytically demonstrates that in structured populations (regular graphs), the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer or pool) in public goods games can lead to the extinction of defection or to cyclic dominance among strategies, depending on the punishment type and strength. For peer punishment, if the punishment fine exceeds a critical threshold, defection is eliminated and only cooperators and punishers coexist, maximizing group efficiency. For pool punishment, strong punishment leads to cyclic dominance, preventing fixation of defection and maintaining higher group welfare. The paper provides explicit formulas for the critical punishment thresholds and shows that these effects are unique to structured populations and do not occur in well-mixed populations, where defection always retains a basin of attraction. The results are presented in terms of group payoff, mean payoff, and phase diagrams, all directly related to efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for predicting that, in public goods games on regular graphs, enabling punishment (peer or pool) will increase group efficiency and welfare, provided the punishment strength exceeds a critical threshold. The analytical results specify how the effect depends on the number of neighbors (player_count), the punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech), and the structure of the game (e.g., mpcr, payoff function). The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings with structured populations and multi-strategy dynamics. The explicit threshold formulas for punishment strength can be used to estimate when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency.

  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in a compulsory and fully observable PGG, enabling punishment (even if not universal) can substantially increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline. The model gives explicit conditions (in terms of player count, MPCR, punishment severity, and punishment scope) under which full cooperation is stable. For prediction tasks, the results suggest that introducing a sufficiently strong and well-targeted punishment mechanism will likely move efficiency close to 1, even if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low. However, the model assumes perfect monitoring, compulsory participation, and centralized punishment, so predictions should be adjusted if these assumptions do not hold in the target environment.

The model's structure and parameterization are relevant for mapping to game design dimensions, but the lack of direct efficiency outcomes limits its predictive value for the downstream task.

  findings: The paper finds that in a nested PGG environment with both local and global public goods, indirect reciprocity (reputation-based conditional aid) can stabilize cooperation at a single scale, but when multiple scales are present, local cooperation is more stable and can invade global cooperation. This occurs even when the global PGG has a higher theoretical maximum payoff. Thus, indirect reciprocity, as typically modeled, cannot by itself sustain efficient, large-scale cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the structure of group membership and the scale at which reputational incentives operate are critical moderators of the efficiency gains from indirect reciprocity-based 'punishment' (here, conditional aid/reward) in PGG-like environments. Specifically, if the game design allows for multiple, nested groups and reputations can be built at the local level, enabling local cooperation, then enabling indirect reciprocity (punishment/reward) may not increase, and can even decrease, efficiency at the global (large-group) level. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of multiple group scales and the ability to build local reputations are key negative moderators of the efficiency effect of punishment/reward mechanisms. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the control (no punishment/reward) game is inefficient, but the addition of indirect reciprocity does not guarantee higher efficiency at the largest scale.
