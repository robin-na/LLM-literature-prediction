- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Gürerk (2013). Social learning increases the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions in social dilemmas. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that endogenously chosen punishment institutions in a repeated public goods game become more efficient and more widely accepted when participants are provided with social history (detailed information about the outcomes of a previous cohort). Social history leads to higher initial acceptance of the punishment institution, higher initial and sustained contributions, lower punishment expenses, and higher average payoffs. The efficiency (measured as surplus ratio and average payoff) is significantly higher in the social history treatment than in the baseline. The effect is attributed to social learning rather than mere imitation, as shown by a control treatment with only partial history information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with endogenous institution choice, enabling social learning (by providing detailed social history) increases both the acceptance and the efficiency of punishment institutions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is larger when participants have access to social learning or history information. The paper also provides detailed information on game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, no reward, etc.), making it directly informative for mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. The findings support a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency when social learning is present, and provide baseline efficiency values for both control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions.

- source: Grieco, D., Faillo, M., & Zarri, L. (2017). Enforcing cooperation in public goods games: Is one punisher enough?. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: Qin, X. D., & Wang, S. Y. (2013). Using an exogenous mechanism to examine efficient probabilistic punishment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing an exogenous, probabilistic punishment mechanism in a repeated public goods game significantly increases both contributions and average payoffs (efficiency) compared to a control with no punishment. Among three punishment treatments with equal expected penalty (10 points), the treatment with a 50% probability of a 20-point punishment produces the highest average payoff and contribution, outperforming both a 100% probability of a 10-point punishment and a 20% probability of a 50-point punishment. The 20% probability/50-point punishment treatment does not significantly increase efficiency over the control. The results show an inverse-U relationship between punishment probability and efficiency, given constant expected penalty. The findings highlight that the design of the punishment mechanism (probability and severity) crucially affects efficiency, not just the expected value of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with exogenous, automatic punishment for the lowest contributor, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the probability and severity of punishment. For prediction, the presence of exogenous punishment (with moderate probability and severity) should be expected to increase efficiency, but low-probability, high-severity punishment may not improve efficiency over baseline. The results also show that the structure of punishment (probability vs. severity) matters even when expected penalty is held constant. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Kumakawa, T. (2013). Social Rewards and Sanctions: A Voluntary Contribution Mechanism Experiment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a two-player public goods game, the opportunity to send social (free-form) or monetary rewards/sanctions does not increase first-round contributions compared to a baseline with no such opportunity. However, when participants actually experience social sanctions (i.e., receive a negative message from their partner), their contributions in the next round increase significantly. Social rewards (positive messages) do not have a significant effect. Monetary sanctions and rewards are rarely used and do not significantly affect subsequent contributions. The study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player public goods game with a game-theoretic payoff table and no prior knowledge of a second round, social sanctions (blame via free-form messages) can increase subsequent contributions, but only after they are actually experienced. The mere opportunity for sanctions or rewards does not affect cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (contribution) responses to social sanctions, not efficiency outcomes. The design (two players, two rounds, no chat except for the sanction/reward stage, full information about partner's contribution, and a linear MPCR of 0.7) should be considered when generalizing to other settings.

- source: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K. (2013). Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). In the stranger condition (random rematching), punishment sustains higher contributions (11.38) than reward (9.21). The effect of punishment on cooperation is robust to group reshuffling, while reward is much less effective when strategic reciprocity is not possible. The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.

- source: Romaniuc, R., Farrow, K., Ibanez, L., & Marciano, A. (2016). The perils of government enforcement. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer disapproval (private enforcement) increases contributions to the public good compared to baseline. Adding a mild, centralized monetary punishment (government enforcement) to peer disapproval stabilizes contributions but does not significantly increase average contributions compared to peer disapproval alone. Critically, if government enforcement is removed after being in place, the effectiveness of peer disapproval is undermined: low contributors become unresponsive to disapproval, and average contributions drop significantly below the level observed when only private enforcement was ever present. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the results on contributions imply that punishment can have both stabilizing and crowding-out effects depending on the sequence of enforcement mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the sequence and combination of enforcement mechanisms (private and government) can have important effects on cooperation (as measured by contributions) in public goods games. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings suggest that enabling punishment can stabilize or increase contributions, but that removing punishment after it has been in place can lead to a sharp decline in cooperation, even below the level observed with private enforcement alone. For prediction tasks, this implies that the history of enforcement mechanisms and their removal are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency outcomes.

Anger-like emotions (anger, contempt, irritation) are a strong driver of punishment, but their effect is moderated by group identity: they lead to more punishment in out-groups than in-groups.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on behavior in PGGs is moderated by group identity: punishment is less frequent and less intense in in-groups, and anger-like emotions drive punishment more in out-groups. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. It does suggest that in settings with identity-heterogeneous groups, punishment is more likely to be used, which (given the costliness of punishment) may reduce efficiency relative to in-groups. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the behavioral mechanisms and moderators of punishment, but not about the quantitative effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: Riedel, N., & Schildberg-Hörisch, H. (2013). Asymmetric obligations. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  findings: The experiment shows that both high and low minimum contribution obligations (even when only weakly enforced) increase contributions in the initial periods compared to no obligation, but this effect is not sustained in the long run. Asymmetric obligations are as effective as symmetric ones: for a given own obligation, the partner's obligation does not affect behavior. The findings suggest that expressive law (weak obligations) can have a short-term effect on cooperation, but do not inform about the efficiency effects of endogenous peer punishment. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.) are well specified, but the absence of a standard punishment treatment limits direct applicability to the prediction of efficiency under peer punishment.

- source: Aakre, S., Helland, L., & Hovi, J. (2016). When Does Informal Enforcement Work?. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.

  findings: The experiment shows that enabling punishment of both nonparticipants and noncompliant participants (i.e., enforcing both participation and compliance) leads to a substantial increase in total group contributions (efficiency) when punishment is introduced. If the design allows punishment of both nonparticipants and noncompliant participants (or, in standard PGGs, if participation is forced), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency. If only one type of free riding can be punished, enabling punishment will not increase efficiency. For prediction, the presence or absence of both enforcement channels is a critical moderator. - source: Drouvelis, M., Nosenzo, D., & Sefton, M. (2017). Team incentives and leadership. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: Introducing a leader with discretionary reward power in a repeated team production game (a close variant of the public goods game) increases group efficiency from about 30-33% to 72-76% of the maximum possible. Leaders use their power to reward high contributors and withhold rewards from low contributors, effectively incentivizing effort. This effect holds for both homogeneous and heterogeneous teams. However, the distribution of gains is highly unequal, with leaders capturing most of the surplus. When workers can choose how much power to delegate to the leader, they typically restrict it to less than half the team output, and the potential efficiency gains are not fully realized. The main reason is that workers do not benefit much from leadership, as leaders only compensate them for effort costs and keep most of the residual output.
