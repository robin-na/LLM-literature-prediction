- source: Duong, M. H., & Han, T. A. (2021). Cost efficiency of institutional incentives for promoting cooperation in finite populations. *Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in finite-population PGGs, the expected total cost to an institution of achieving a minimum cooperation level via punishment or reward can be exactly calculated and optimized. The cost efficiency of punishment versus reward depends on the required cooperation threshold: for low cooperation targets, reward is more cost-efficient; for high targets, punishment is more cost-efficient, with the threshold typically around 50% cooperation. The cost functions exhibit phase transitions as the intensity of selection changes. The analysis provides explicit formulas and algorithms for determining the optimal incentive size and minimal institutional cost, given the game parameters, population size, and selection intensity. The results are validated by numerical simulations and apply to both the PGG and the donation game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, theoretically grounded formulas for predicting the efficiency (cost-effectiveness) of institutional punishment (and reward) in finite-population PGGs, as a function of player count, group size, marginal per-capita return, selection intensity, and the required cooperation level. It shows that the efficiency of punishment depends critically on the required cooperation threshold and the selection intensity. For prediction tasks, the paper enables calculation of the minimal institutional cost (and thus efficiency) for any specified game design, provided the relevant parameters are known. The results are most directly applicable to settings where the institution can set the punishment/reward level to achieve a target cooperation rate, and where the main outcome of interest is the total cost to the institution (i.e., efficiency in terms of budget spent per unit of cooperation achieved). The findings are less directly applicable to settings where individual payoffs or group welfare (rather than institutional cost) are the main outcome, but the cost formulas can be mapped to group efficiency if the institutional cost is subtracted from total group payoffs.

- source: Fang, Y. H., Benko, T. P., Perc, M., Xu, H. Y., & Tan, Q. M. (2019). Synergistic third-party rewarding and punishment in the public goods game. *Proceedings of the Royal Society A-mathematical Physical and Engineering Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment and reward, when applied together, strongly promote cooperation in a spatial public goods game. The synergy between punishment and reward is more effective than either alone, especially at lower cost to the third party. Lower thresholds for intervention (greater tolerance for defection) and higher intensity of intervention both increase cooperation. More frequent intervention (shorter time intervals) also promotes cooperation. However, the main outcome is the fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its findings are only indirectly relevant to predicting efficiency effects of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the combination of punishment and reward can robustly increase cooperation rates in spatial public goods games, and that the effect is sensitive to the threshold, intensity, and frequency of intervention. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. Its findings are most useful for understanding how design dimensions like intervention intensity, threshold, and frequency affect cooperation rates, which may correlate with efficiency in some settings, but this is not demonstrated here.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schurtenberger, I. (2018). Normative foundations of human cooperation. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. The review emphasizes that the presence of punishment alone is not sufficient for efficiency gains; the structure and normative context of punishment matter greatly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and integrative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff unless punishment is normatively constrained to reduce antisocial punishment and collateral costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the presence of normative or institutional constraints, the prevalence of antisocial punishment, and the cultural context. Game design dimensions that affect the cost, targeting, and normative context of punishment are likely to be critical moderators of efficiency outcomes. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes, but it cautions against assuming that increased cooperation from punishment always translates into higher efficiency.

- source: Yang, C. L., Zhang, B., Charness, G., Li, C., & Lien, J. W. (2018). Endogenous rewards promote cooperation. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The endogenous, budget-balanced reward mechanism in a repeated four-player PGG leads to a substantial and sustained increase in contributions compared to the standard PGG. The mechanism works by taxing each player's income and allowing players to allocate their share of the tax fund as rewards to others. Contributions remain high and even increase over time with rewards, while they decline in the control. The paper finds that most subjects are conditional cooperators, and that reward assignment is strongly correlated with contribution. Net profits (payoffs) are higher under the reward mechanism than in the control, and the mechanism is more efficient than punishment-based mechanisms reported in the literature, which often fail to increase net payoffs due to the cost of punishment. The study does not test punishment directly, but positions endogenous reward as a more efficient and socially desirable alternative.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that a budget-balanced, endogenous reward mechanism can substantially increase both contributions and net payoffs in a repeated PGG compared to a standard control. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that enabling such a reward mechanism (with a 20% tax, peer allocation, and 1:1 reward effectiveness) will lead to higher efficiency than the control, and likely higher than typical punishment treatments (based on literature comparisons). However, the paper does not provide direct evidence on punishment, so its value for predicting the effect of punishment per se is indirect: it shows that reward can outperform punishment in terms of efficiency, especially when punishment is costly. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, feedback, etc.) are well specified and can inform parameterization for similar prediction tasks involving reward mechanisms, but not for punishment.

- source: Alvarez-Rodriguez, U., Battiston, F., de Arruda, G. F., Moreno, Y., Perc, M., & Latora, V. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics of higher-order interactions in social networks. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper shows that in PGGs with higher-order interactions (hypergraphs), the critical synergy factor required for cooperation decreases as group size increases in uniform random hypergraphs, meaning larger groups can foster cooperation more easily (at a given synergy factor). Heterogeneity in group membership (hyperdegree) and group size can either facilitate or hinder cooperation, depending on the structure: scale-free (hub-dominated) hypergraphs inhibit cooperation, while uniform or less heterogeneous structures do not. The framework is validated with empirical data from scientific collaborations, showing that the synergy factor (group benefit) is a non-monotonic function of group size, with an optimal group size for maximum benefit. The paper does not study punishment, rewards, or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency and payoff outcomes in PGG-like environments with varying group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. It is directly relevant for understanding how group size and network heterogeneity affect the critical conditions for cooperation and group payoff, but cannot inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment. Use this paper to inform baseline efficiency predictions in control (no-punishment) conditions as a function of group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but not for treatment effects of punishment.

- source: Lohse, J., & Waichman, I. (2020). The effects of contemporaneous peer punishment on cooperation with the future. *Nature Communications*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a multigenerational public goods game, voluntary cooperation is difficult to sustain across generations. When peer punishment is available within a generation, the likelihood that the group meets the contribution threshold and sustains future generations more than doubles compared to the baseline. However, the increase in group earnings from more sustained generations is offset by the costs incurred through punishment, resulting in no significant difference in average aggregate earnings (efficiency) between the punishment and no-punishment conditions. The effect of punishment on individual contribution levels is small, but it is sufficient to increase the rate at which groups meet the threshold. Punishment is mainly directed at those who contribute less than their peers, especially when the group fails to reach the threshold.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling peer punishment in a threshold-based, multigenerational public goods game increases the probability of group success (i.e., reaching future generations and thus group-level payoff), but the efficiency gains are offset by the costs of punishment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of peer punishment can increase the likelihood of group success, but unless punishment is low-cost or highly effective, the net effect on efficiency may be limited or null. The design dimensions—such as group size, threshold, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the absence of communication—are all well-specified and should be used to inform predictions. The results are most relevant for settings with threshold public goods, no communication, and contemporaneous peer punishment.

- source: Gross, J., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2019). Individual solutions to shared problems create a modern tragedy of the commons. *Science Advances*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that when individuals can choose between solving a shared problem collectively (public good) or individually (private solution), many opt for the individual solution even when collective action would be more efficient. This leads to inefficient resource allocation and coordination failure, especially at intermediate cost-benefit ratios. When peer punishment is introduced, it increases the rate of collective solutions but also triggers mutual punishment between individualists and collectivists, which reduces overall group welfare. Punishment does not reduce free riding but instead is used in feuds between incompatible strategies, amplifying inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that in public-goods-like games where individual solutions are available, enabling peer punishment can reduce efficiency (group welfare), even if it increases collective action. The effect is driven by wasteful punishment exchanges rather than improved cooperation. For prediction, if the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency in similar settings, especially when individual solutions are a viable alternative to collective action. The findings are most relevant when the game allows both individual and collective solutions, and punishment is peer-based, costly, and has a strong impact.

- source: Góis, A. R., Santos, F. P., Pacheco, J. M., & Santos, F. C. (2019). Reward and punishment in climate change dilemmas. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in collective-risk dilemmas (threshold public goods games with risk of group failure), both reward and punishment policies improve group achievement (η_G) compared to no policy. Reward is particularly effective at initiating cooperation when the risk of failure is low, while punishment is more effective at sustaining high levels of cooperation, especially when the risk is high or the threshold for group success is stringent. The optimal policy is to use reward when the fraction of cooperators is low and switch to punishment when cooperation is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit formulas for the optimal switching point and shows that combining both policies synergistically yields the highest group achievement. The results are robust across a range of parameter values, including group size, population size, and policy efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment (and/or reward) in threshold public goods games will increase group achievement (a proxy for efficiency) compared to a control without such mechanisms. The model allows for direct mapping of game design dimensions such as player count, group size, number of rounds (in evolutionary time), presence and efficiency of punishment and reward, and the structure of the public goods dilemma (threshold, risk). The findings suggest that punishment is most effective at maintaining cooperation once it is established, while reward is more important for initiating cooperation. The optimal policy is a dynamic combination of both. For prediction tasks, the paper implies that adding punishment (especially with sufficient budget and efficiency) will generally increase efficiency, but the effect size and optimality depend on the risk, threshold, and whether reward is also available. The model is theoretical, so empirical calibration may be needed for quantitative predictions.

- source: Sun, W. W., Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., Szolnoki, A., & Vasconcelos, V. V. (2021). Combination of institutional incentives for cooperative governance of risky commons. *Iscience*.

  findings: The paper finds that in collective-risk social dilemma games, institutional pure reward strategies (where executors only reward cooperators) are more effective than pure punishment or hybrid strategies in promoting group achievement (i.e., the fraction of groups that reach the collective target). This result holds for both fixed and flexible incentive schemes, and is robust to changes in risk level, mutation rate, and enhancement factor. Local sanctioning schemes are more effective than global ones. The main outcome is group achievement, not efficiency or total group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency effects is not possible from this paper.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games. It suggests that, in collective-risk social dilemmas, institutional punishment alone is less effective than reward or hybrid schemes in promoting group achievement (a proxy for cooperation). However, because the main outcome is not efficiency or group payoff, but rather the fraction of groups reaching a behavioral target, the findings should be used with caution for efficiency prediction. The game structure (collective-risk, institutional incentives, evolutionary dynamics) is close but not identical to standard PGGs. The results suggest that institutional punishment may not increase efficiency as much as reward, but do not provide quantitative efficiency estimates.

- source: Li, X. L., Jusup, M., Wang, Z., Li, H. J., Shi, L., Podobnik, B., Stanley, H. E., Havlin, S., & Boccaletti, S. (2018). Punishment diminishes the benefits of network reciprocity in social dilemma experiments. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment compared three treatments: (1) a well-mixed repeated PD (control), (2) a static network PD with two neighbors (network reciprocity), and (3) a static network PD with punishment available. Network reciprocity increased both cooperation and payoff relative to the well-mixed control. However, adding punishment did not further increase cooperation and actually reduced the median payoff per round back to the control level. Punishment also reduced assortment (clustering of cooperators) and, in larger neighborhoods, erased the positive correlation between cooperation and payoff. Thus, punishment in this setting is ineffective or even counterproductive for group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated prisoner's dilemma games on static networks (with two or four neighbors), enabling costly punishment (with a 1:4 cost-to-impact ratio) does not improve and may even reduce group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The negative effect is especially clear in payoff-based outcomes: median payoff per round is lower with punishment than without, and the benefit of network reciprocity is erased. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar networked PD or adjacent PGG-like environments, adding punishment may not increase efficiency and could reduce it, especially as neighborhood size increases. The findings are most directly applicable to static network PDs with small groups, no communication, and fixed payoff matrices.

- source: Molho, C., Tybur, J. M., Van Lange, P. A. M., & Balliet, D. (2020). Direct and indirect punishment of norm violations in daily life. *Nature Communications*.

  findings: The study finds that people frequently use both direct and indirect punishment strategies in response to norm violations in daily life. Direct confrontation is more likely when the punisher has been personally victimized, has more power, or values the offender more. Indirect punishment (gossip, social exclusion) is more likely when the violation is severe, the punisher has less power, or values the offender less. Emotional responses (anger, disgust) also differentially predict punishment type.   prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or similar economic games. Its findings are relevant for understanding the psychological and contextual determinants of punishment strategy in naturalistic settings, but not for quantifying the impact of punishment on efficiency. The study may be useful for mechanism discussions or for understanding when punishment is likely to be deployed, but it does not inform the downstream prediction task of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency.
