- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Lee, J., & Min, S. (2021). The effects of repeated induction of emotions on cooperation and punishment. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment used a repeated public goods game with costly punishment and stranger matching. In each round, groups of three played five rounds, with either happiness or anger induced via video clips after the contribution stage. The only difference between treatments was the induced emotion. The main findings are: (1) Contributions decay over rounds in the happiness treatment but remain stable in the anger treatment; (2) Final-round welfare and group earnings are higher in the anger treatment than in the happiness treatment; (3) The key mechanism is that antisocial-punished cooperators reduce their contributions in the happiness treatment but not in the anger treatment; (4) Prosocial and antisocial punishment levels do not differ significantly between treatments, but their trends and effects do. Thus, the emotional context moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency: punishment sustains efficiency only when anger is present, not when happiness is induced.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated public goods games depends on the emotional environment. With standard parameters (3 players, 5 rounds, MPCR 0.6, costly punishment with 1:3 cost:impact), punishment sustains or increases efficiency when anger is induced, but not when happiness is induced. In the absence of induced happiness, the efficiency effect of punishment is positive or neutral; with induced happiness, the effect is negative. For prediction, the emotional context (incidental happiness vs. anger) is a critical moderator. The control (no-punishment) baseline is not directly reported, but a baseline without emotion induction (see Appendix J) shows similar trends to the anger treatment, suggesting that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is typical unless happiness is induced. Thus, when predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, consider whether the environment is emotionally neutral/angry (expect positive or stable efficiency effect) or happy (expect negative effect).

- source: Angelovski, A., Di Cagno, D., Güth, W., Marazzi, F., & Panaccione, L. (2018). Does heterogeneity spoil the basket? The role of productivity and feedback information on public good provision. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 4-player, 10-round public goods game with centralized punishment, the presence of a sanctioner (punishment enabled) increases both average contributions and total group payoffs (social welfare) compared to a control with no punishment. However, if the sanctioner's payoff is made contingent on group cooperation (pool punishment), both contributions and efficiency are lower than when the sanctioner receives a fixed endowment. The negative effect of the pool punishment scheme is not due to differences in punishment frequency or severity, but rather to contributors' aversion to the sanctioner's extrinsic incentives (perceived as indirect free-riding). The paper provides direct evidence that punishment increases efficiency, but the institutional details of the punishment mechanism (especially the sanctioner's payoff scheme) are crucial moderators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the institutional design—specifically, whether the sanctioner is paid a fixed or variable (pool) payoff. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger when the punishment institution uses fixed payoffs for sanctioners. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence and type of punishment, the sanctioner's payoff scheme, and the marginal per-capita return (MPCR). Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the treatment effect of punishment is moderated by the sanctioner's incentive structure.

- source: Mantilla, C., Zhou, L., Wang, C., Yang, D. H., Shen, S. P., & Seabright, P. (2021). Favoring your in-group can harm both them and you: Ethnicity and public goods provision in China. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in public goods games with punishment, ethnically mixed groups experience lower contributions and lower group earnings than homogeneous groups. This is primarily because participants are less likely to punish co-ethnic free-riders when out-group members are present, reducing the deterrent effect of punishment. The effect is strongest in unbalanced mixed groups (3+1 composition), where efficiency losses are largest. The study provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment as a cooperation-enforcing institution is undermined by in-group favoritism in diverse groups, leading to lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with punishment, the efficiency gains from punishment are reduced or even reversed in ethnically mixed groups due to in-group favoritism in punishment assignment. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the group composition (homogeneous vs mixed) is a critical moderator: punishment is less effective (or even harmful) in mixed groups because in-group members are less likely to punish each other, reducing deterrence and lowering contributions and earnings. The game uses standard PGG parameters (4 players, 5 rounds, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost=2, punishment impact=5, no chat, no rewards, anonymous punishment). Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the main comparison is between group types with punishment enabled. The findings suggest that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling punishment in mixed-ethnicity groups may not increase efficiency and can even decrease it relative to homogeneous groups.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., & Meraglia, S. (2019). Endogenous sanctioning institutions and migration patterns: Experimental evidence. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with two groups and endogenous migration, the presence of formal sanctioning institutions (a fixed fee plus a fine on private account allocations) leads to higher contributions and significantly higher average payoffs compared to sanction-free groups. When only one group has sanctions (No-Voting treatment), that group becomes overpopulated, and payoff inequality emerges. When groups can vote on whether to implement sanctions (Voting treatment), most groups eventually choose sanctions, group sizes become more balanced, migration decreases, and overall efficiency and average payoffs increase further. The ability to endogenously choose institutions (via voting) leads to more efficient and equitable outcomes than exogenously imposed sanctions. The effect of sanctions on efficiency is robust and positive, and the endogenous choice of sanctions amplifies this effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling formal punishment in a repeated PGG with migration increases efficiency and average payoffs, relative to a control without punishment. The effect is even stronger when the sanctioning institution is chosen endogenously by group vote. The design dimensions—such as player count (10, split into two groups of 5), number of rounds (30), absence of chat, continuous contributions, and a formal, automatic punishment mechanism—are all specified. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) varies with group size but is always less than 1, and the punishment is highly effective (80% fine on private account, plus a fixed fee). There is no reward mechanism. Players are shown summary information about both groups' contributions and rule-sets. The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will reliably increase efficiency, and allowing endogenous institutional choice (voting) will further boost efficiency and reduce inequality. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is substantially lower than with punishment enabled. This paper is highly informative for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Fatas, E., Melendez-Jimenez, M. A., & Solaz, H. (2020). SOCIAL HIERARCHIES: A LABORATORY STUDY ON PUNISHMENT PATTERNS ACROSS NETWORKS. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that enabling punishment in repeated public goods games increases contributions in all network structures, but the effect on efficiency (group profit) depends on the network. In the complete and star (hierarchical) networks, punishment leads to higher efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline. In the circle and line networks, punishment increases contributions but the cost of punishment outweighs the gains, resulting in lower efficiency than the no-punishment baseline. Central players in the star network use punishment in a prosocial way and do not retaliate, leading to higher group efficiency. In contrast, antisocial punishment and retaliation are more common in the line and circle networks, undermining efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the network structure. If the game design includes a hierarchical (star) or complete network, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. In other incomplete networks (circle, line), punishment may reduce efficiency due to costly antisocial punishment and retaliation. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the game (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech), making it highly informative for prediction tasks that use these dimensions. Control (VCM) and treatment (PUN) efficiency values are reported for each network, allowing for direct calibration.

- source: Waichman, I. (2020). LINKING WEALTH AND PUNISHMENT EFFECTIVENESS: PUNISHMENT AND COOPERATION UNDER CONGRUENT HETEROGENEITIES. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This lab experiment uses a linear public good game with peer punishment to study the effects of heterogeneity in endowment and punishment effectiveness, both separately and jointly. The main findings are: (1) Heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness alone does not affect efficiency or cooperation compared to symmetric groups. (2) Endowment heterogeneity (rich and poor members) reduces efficiency and cooperation, as rich members contribute a lower share of their endowment. (3) When both endowment and punishment effectiveness are heterogeneous (either the rich or the poor is the most effective punisher), efficiency and cooperation are not reduced compared to symmetry, and are higher than under endowment heterogeneity alone. (4) Among groups that achieve high cooperation, efficiency is higher in the 'Rich strong' treatment (richest is most effective punisher) than in the 'Poor strong' treatment, because less punishment is needed to sustain cooperation. (5) Overall, congruent heterogeneities (richest is most effective punisher) do not hinder and may even facilitate efficiency and cooperation compared to endowment heterogeneity alone.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in linear public good games with peer punishment, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency compared to control conditions with endowment heterogeneity alone. However, the efficiency gain from punishment is moderated by the structure of heterogeneity: punishment is most effective (in terms of efficiency) when either all players are symmetric or when the richest player is also the most effective punisher. Endowment heterogeneity without matching punishment effectiveness reduces efficiency, but joint heterogeneities (especially congruent ones) restore efficiency to levels comparable to symmetry. For prediction, this suggests that punishment will generally increase efficiency, but the magnitude of the effect depends on the alignment of endowment and punishment effectiveness. The control efficiency (without punishment) is lowest under endowment heterogeneity, and the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is largest in this case, especially if punishment effectiveness is also heterogeneous and congruent with endowment.

- source: Gordon, D. S., & Puurtinen, M. (2021). High Cooperation and Welfare Despite-and Because of-the Threat of Antisocial Punishments and Feuds. *Journal of Experimental Psychology-general*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study demonstrates that enabling peer punishment in a repeated public goods game with full information and fixed groups results in high cooperation and group earnings, even when punishment is costly (1:1 cost:impact ratio). All punishment treatments (cost-free, costly, asymmetric cost, and asymmetric immunity) yield significantly higher group earnings than the no-punishment control. However, when a group member is immune from punishment, that individual contributes less, and group earnings are reduced relative to other punishment treatments. The actual use of punishment is low, and antisocial punishment is rare, likely due to the high visibility of all actions and the possibility of long-term retaliation. The results suggest that the mere threat of punishment, in a context where all can be held accountable, is sufficient to maintain high efficiency, even if punishment is not cost-effective. The exception is when immunity allows a powerful individual to exploit the group, reducing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with fixed groups, fixed identities, and full information about others' actions, enabling peer punishment (even if costly and inefficient) substantially increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment controls. The effect is robust across symmetric and asymmetric punishment cost structures, as long as all players are subject to punishment. However, if any player is immune from punishment, group efficiency drops due to free-riding by the immune individual. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of punishment, full information, and universal accountability are key moderators of positive efficiency effects, and that the cost-effectiveness of punishment is less important in such settings. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is a strong baseline, but the addition of punishment in this design predicts a large positive shift in efficiency, unless immunity is present.

- source: De Geest, L. R., & Kingsley, D. C. (2021). Norm enforcement with incomplete information *. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with two high-endowment and two low-endowment players per group, and costly peer punishment, the ability to observe others' endowments (complete information) leads to the emergence and enforcement of efficient 'contribute-your-endowment' norms. This results in higher contributions and group earnings, especially from high-endowment players, and higher payoffs for low-endowment players. When endowments are private (incomplete information), groups adapt by enforcing a 'contribute-the-Low-endowment' norm, primarily enforced by low-endowment players. This norm prevents total unraveling of cooperation but enables high-endowment players to mimic low-endowment behavior and contribute less, reducing group efficiency. The expected costs and payoffs of noncompliance are explicitly estimated, showing that punishment is less targeted and less effective at sustaining high efficiency under incomplete information.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the information structure. When group members can observe both contributions and endowments, punishment supports high efficiency by enabling enforcement of optimal contribution norms. When endowments are unobservable, punishment still deters total free-riding but cannot sustain full efficiency, as high-endowment members can hide behind the low-endowment norm. For prediction, this means that the presence of punishment will increase efficiency relative to no punishment, but the magnitude of the effect is moderated by whether individual capacities (endowments) are observable. The paper also provides detailed parameterization of the punishment technology and payoff structure, which can be used to calibrate predictive models.

- source: Koch, C., Nikiforakis, N., & Noussair, C. N. (2021). Covenants before the swords: The limits to efficient cooperation in heterogeneous groups. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment with heterogeneous groups (different returns from the public good), the authors compare three treatments: (1) communication and punishment available together (ComEarly), (2) punishment first, then communication added later (ComLate), and (3) communication only (ComOnly). Efficiency, measured as group earnings relative to the maximum possible, is highest when both communication and punishment are available from the start (ComEarly: 87% of maximum), lower with only one instrument (ComLate: 60-68%; ComOnly: 76% in part 1, 60% overall). However, even with both instruments, most groups do not achieve full efficiency, as covenants typically require compromise between efficiency and equality. Early communication is crucial: it enables groups to establish covenants and avoid costly punishment cycles (feuds), leading to higher efficiency. Late communication cannot fully overcome the negative effects of prior sanctioning. The study demonstrates that while punishment and communication together improve efficiency, concerns for equality limit the ability of heterogeneous groups to reach the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with heterogeneous returns, enabling both communication and punishment increases efficiency (group earnings) compared to either instrument alone. However, the efficiency gain is limited by the tendency of groups to compromise between efficiency and equality, especially when group members have different returns. The timing of communication is a key moderator: early communication (before punishment) is much more effective at raising efficiency than late communication. For prediction, this suggests that the presence of both communication and punishment, especially with early communication, should be expected to yield higher efficiency than punishment alone, but not full efficiency. Control efficiency (with only communication) is a strong predictor, but the incremental effect of punishment depends on whether communication is also present and when it occurs. The results are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in heterogeneous PGGs.

- source: Castillo, J. G., Xu, Z. P., Zhang, P., & Zhu, X. C. (2021). The effects of centralized power and institutional legitimacy on collective action. *Social Choice and Welfare*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that centralized punishment institutions (where a single manager can punish group members) significantly increase both contributions and efficiency (net profit) compared to a baseline public goods game without punishment. This result holds across two experimental variants: one with restricted and costly punishment (1:1 cost, only one person can be punished per round) and one with unrestricted and 'cheap' punishment (3:1 cost, multiple people can be punished per round). However, the mechanism by which the manager is selected (random/exogenous vs. by group vote/endogenous) does not significantly affect efficiency or contributions. The efficiency gains are attributed to the presence of punishment, not to the legitimacy conferred by democratic selection. The design also shows that the prosociality of the manager (their prior contribution behavior) is more important for group outcomes than the selection mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with centralized punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (payoff) relative to a no-punishment baseline, regardless of whether the manager is selected by vote or at random. The effect is robust across different punishment cost/effectiveness regimes. For prediction tasks, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency, and the details of the manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. exogenous) are not predictive of further efficiency gains. The results also suggest that punishment cost and effectiveness (within the tested range) do not moderate the efficiency effect of punishment in this centralized setting.

- source: Kamei, K. (2019). The power of joint decision-making in a finitely-repeated dilemma. *Oxford Economic Papers-new Series*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Joint decision-making in pairs, where members can communicate, leads to higher and more sustained efficiency (measured by average group payoff/contributions) than individual decision-making in finitely repeated two-player public goods games. This effect is strongest under partner matching (fixed pairs), where efficiency remains high until the end-game. Under stranger matching (random rematching), efficiency declines more rapidly, but is still higher than individual decision-making in early rounds if social distance is close. When compared to individual decision-making with informal punishment (where subjects can punish at a cost), joint decision-making achieves similar efficiency and average payoffs. The effect of joint decision-making is driven by communication and, to a lesser extent, by close social distance within pairs. Removing social distance (anonymous pairs) reduces efficiency, especially under stranger matching. The paper provides direct evidence on the efficiency impact of punishment institutions versus joint decision-making, and indirect evidence on the role of communication, matching protocol, and social distance.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in two-player finitely repeated public goods games, enabling informal punishment (with a cost to the punisher and a larger cost to the punished) increases efficiency to a level comparable to that achieved by joint decision-making in pairs with communication. The efficiency boost from punishment is robust under both partner and stranger matching, though the effect is stronger under partner matching. The design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency with punishment enabled are: player count (2), number of rounds (15), MPCR (0.8), punishment cost (0.5 per point), punishment tech (1.5 per point), and matching protocol (partner vs stranger). Communication within pairs (chat) is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, especially under partner matching. The paper's direct comparison of control (no punishment), punishment, and joint decision-making treatments makes it highly informative for predicting efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions and control efficiency.
