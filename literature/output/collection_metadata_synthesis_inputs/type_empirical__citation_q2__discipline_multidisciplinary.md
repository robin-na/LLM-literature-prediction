- source: Kamijo, Y., Kira, Y., & Nitta, K. (2020). Even Bad Social Norms Promote Positive Interactions. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in an inefficient public goods game (where contributing reduces total group welfare), enabling punishment alone does not significantly increase contributions or group payoffs compared to no-sanction control. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher contributions and, importantly, higher final payoffs, even though the public good is inefficient. This is because mutual rewarding compensates for the losses from inefficient contributions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is weak or non-significant, while reward has a strong positive effect. These findings are robust to explicit instruction and comprehension checks about the inefficiency of the public good. The results suggest that punishment alone is not effective at increasing efficiency in this context, but reward can create positive-sum outcomes through social exchange, even when the underlying norm is inefficient.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in a public goods game with MPCR<1 (inefficient PGG), this paper provides strong evidence that punishment alone does not significantly increase efficiency or group payoff compared to control. The effect direction for punishment is weak or null. In contrast, enabling reward (with or without punishment) leads to higher efficiency (final payoffs). Thus, when only punishment is enabled, do not expect a substantial increase in efficiency; when reward is enabled, expect a strong positive effect. The findings are robust to explicit understanding of inefficiency and are based on a 4-player, 20-round, fixed-ID, no-chat, continuous-contribution design with full feedback and identified sanctioning. The cost/impact ratio for punishment and reward is 4:12. These design details should be matched when applying the findings.

- source: Eriksson, K., & Strimling, P. (2012). The Hard Problem of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game, groups of participants pre-sorted by cooperative inclination (high/cooperative vs. low/non-cooperative) played under four conditions: no institution, weak institution (low threshold, low punishment), strong institution (high threshold, high punishment), and evolving institution (parameters set by group voting). Under no or weak institution, non-cooperative groups had much lower payoffs than cooperative groups. Under a strong institution, both group types achieved equally high payoffs, with non-cooperative groups benefiting the most from the institutional change. However, when institutional parameters were set by voting, non-cooperative groups failed to increase punishment and contribution thresholds, resulting in lower payoffs. The results show that strong, externally imposed punishment institutions can solve the 'hard problem' of cooperation and efficiency, but self-governance by non-cooperative groups does not reliably produce such institutions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with four players, 4–16 rounds, and no communication, the introduction of a strong, externally imposed punishment institution (high fine, high contribution threshold, automatic monitoring and punishment) substantially increases group efficiency/payoff, especially for groups with low baseline cooperation. The effect is robust and large: mean payoffs rise from near the Nash equilibrium (10–12 units) to near the social optimum (16.6–16.9 units). However, if the institution's strength is determined by group voting, non-cooperative groups do not adopt strong punishment, and efficiency gains are not realized. For prediction, this paper supports a strong positive effect of punishment on efficiency when punishment is externally imposed and severe, but little or no effect when punishment is weak or endogenous to non-cooperative groups. The design dimensions most critical for this result are punishment cost/tech, the presence of a fixed strong institution, and the absence of communication.

- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Lefebvre, M., & Stenger, A. (2020). Short- & long-term effects of monetary and non-monetary incentives to cooperate in public good games: An experiment. *Plos One*.

  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Monetary and non-monetary punishments and rewards all significantly increase contributions to the public good compared to baseline. Monetary punishment and non-monetary reward are the most effective. After incentives are removed, contributions remain higher than baseline for several rounds, indicating some long-lasting effect, but eventually decline. The effect of punishment and reward on later contributions depends on whether individuals were high or low contributors and how many points they received. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the findings are limited to contribution behavior.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (especially monetary punishment) increases contribution rates in a standard linear public goods game with four players and 30 rounds. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results are highly informative for predicting contribution behavior, but less so for efficiency unless one assumes a direct mapping from contribution to efficiency (which is not always valid, especially when punishment is costly). The design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is useful for understanding the behavioral response to punishment and reward mechanisms in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Chapkovski, P. (2021). Strike one hundred to educate one: Measuring the efficacy of collective sanctions experimentally. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Collective sanctions are not effective at increasing cooperation or contributions in a public good game, and may even reduce them compared to individual sanctions, especially when peer punishment is available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions overall, but under collective sanctions, contributions are significantly lower than under individual sanctions with peer punishment. Average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions, but this difference is not statistically significant. There are notable gender differences: men contribute more under collective sanctions, women less. Perceptions of fairness mediate these effects, with collective sanctions seen as less fair, especially by women.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard PGG with 3 players, 15 rounds, continuous contributions, and a fixed threshold for external punishment, enabling collective sanctions (punishing the whole group for one member's under-contribution) does not improve efficiency or group payoff compared to individual sanctions, and may even reduce cooperation and contributions, especially when peer punishment is also available. The presence of peer punishment increases contributions, but collective sanctions reduce this effect. The paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but average earnings are slightly lower under collective sanctions. For prediction tasks, this suggests that switching from individual to collective sanctions is unlikely to increase efficiency, and may decrease it, particularly in small groups with peer punishment. Gender composition may moderate effects.

- source: Paál, T., & Bereczkei, T. (2015). Punishment as a Means of Competition: Implications for Strong Reciprocity Theory. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 10-round, 4-player PGG with a competitive, rank-based payoff and costly punishment enabled from round 6 onward, average contributions declined steadily and did not increase after punishment was introduced. Punishment was not targeted at low contributors but at those with higher accumulated profits, indicating strategic rather than norm-enforcing use. There was no association between contributions and punishment received or imposed. The results suggest that in competitive environments, punishment can become a tool for rivalry rather than for sustaining cooperation, and does not reverse the decline in cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that in a PGG with a competitive, tournament-style payoff structure and full information about others' profits, enabling punishment does not increase efficiency or group payoff. Instead, punishment is used for strategic competition, and cooperation continues to decline. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent: in competitive, rank-based settings, punishment may not improve and may even undermine group efficiency. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so this inference is based on contribution behavior and the described use of punishment.

- source: Kubena, A. A., Houdek, P., Lindová, J., Príplatová, L., & Flegr, J. (2014). Justine Effect: Punishment of the Unduly Self-Sacrificing Cooperative Individuals. *Plos One*.

However, it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, which are central to the downstream prediction task. The finding that punishment increases average contributions suggests a possible positive effect on efficiency, but the presence of antisocial punishment (which reduces the payoffs of high contributors) could offset these gains. Without explicit efficiency or payoff data, the paper's value for predicting treatment efficiency is limited to indirect inference based on contribution patterns and punishment behavior.

- source: Dickson, E. S., Gordon, S. C., & Huber, G. A. (2022). Identifying legitimacy: Experimental evidence on compliance with authority. *Science Advances*.

The paper is most useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators of cooperation under punishment, rather than for direct efficiency prediction.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2016). Institutionalize Reciprocity to Overcome the Public Goods Provision Problem. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a repeated four-person public goods game, embedding a reciprocal strategy as an institution (institutionalized reciprocity, IR) leads to significantly higher group efficiency (total profit) than a standard control game. The effect is strongest when the institution enforces a strict upper bound on contributions (S-IR), which enables reciprocators to coordinate against free riders and prevents excessive cooperation. In S-IR, group profits are significantly higher than in both the control and a less restrictive IR variant (NL-IR). The IR mechanism operates as a collective punishment system: if any player fails to meet the target, the group's future benefit is reduced for all. The study does not implement standard peer punishment, but the IR mechanism provides a collective sanction that is effective at increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutionalized, rule-based collective punishment (via reduction of future group benefit after a deviation) can substantially increase efficiency in repeated public goods games, especially when the institution strictly limits contributions to a target. While the study does not test standard peer punishment, it shows that collective, institutionally enforced sanctions can outperform standard repeated games without punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a credible, enforced institutional sanction (even if not peer punishment) can raise efficiency well above control, particularly when the rules are clear and binding. The results are most directly applicable to games with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions (in increments), and full feedback on others' contributions and payoffs.

  findings: The study finds that both within-group and between-group competition increase trust (investment) among acquaintances in an investment game. However, within-group competition reduces trustworthiness (return ratio) and leads to lower payoffs for trustors but higher payoffs for trustees. Between-group competition increases trustee payoffs but does not significantly affect trustworthiness or trustor payoffs compared to the non-competition condition. The results suggest that competition type differentially affects efficiency for trustors and trustees, and that within-group competition can undermine overall group efficiency by harming trustor payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, introducing within-group competition can increase trust but reduce trustworthiness, leading to lower payoffs for trustors and higher payoffs for trustees. Between-group competition increases trust and trustee payoffs but does not harm trustor payoffs. Since the study does not include punishment, it does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency, but it does show that competitive structures can have asymmetric effects on efficiency depending on the role. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that the presence or absence of competition (and its type) can moderate efficiency outcomes, but the absence of punishment means the results are not directly transferable to punishment-enabled PGGs.

It is not directly informative for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in public-goods-like environments.
