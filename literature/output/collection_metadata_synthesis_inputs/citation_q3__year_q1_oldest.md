- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: Ye, H., Tan, F., Ding, M., Jia, Y. M., & Chen, Y. F. (2011). Sympathy and Punishment: Evolution of Cooperation in Public Goods Game. *Jasss-the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with costly punishment, punishment alone is not sufficient to sustain cooperation or high group efficiency due to the second-order free rider problem. When a mechanism of 'sympathy' (a transfer payment to punishers) is introduced, punishers become evolutionarily stable, leading to persistent cooperation and higher group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: punishment increases efficiency only when accompanied by a compensatory mechanism for punishers. The results are robust to variations in period length, initial population state, and the strength of sympathy.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive; it depends on whether there is a mechanism to compensate punishers for their costs (e.g., 'sympathy'). In standard settings without such compensation, punishment does not improve efficiency and may even fail to sustain cooperation. When designing or predicting the effect of punishment in a PGG-like environment, it is crucial to consider whether any form of reward or compensation for punishers exists. The game design dimensions most relevant here are the presence of a reward/compensation mechanism and the cost/effectiveness of punishment. Control efficiency (without punishment) is low, and enabling punishment alone does not guarantee higher efficiency unless compensation is also present.

  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with punishment, both altruism (cooperation) and punishment can be maintained at moderate to high levels, resulting in group productivity well above the baseline of no cooperation. Punishment is effective at deterring cheating and thus increases group efficiency, but the costliness of punishment, group size, and the number of rounds all moderate this effect. When punishment is too costly or the game is too short, neither altruism nor punishment is maintained. The model also shows that punishment need not be performed by altruists: 'selfish punishers' (cheaters who punish other cheaters) can maintain high group efficiency by removing the worst cheaters, thereby protecting a pool of cooperators to exploit. The model directly emulates the structure of experimental public goods games and provides parameter sweeps for punishment cost, group size, and round length, showing their effects on equilibrium levels of cooperation and punishment, and thus on group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation), except when punishment is extremely costly or the game is one-shot. The model is directly parameterized in terms of group size (player_count), number of rounds (num_rounds), punishment cost (punishment_cost), and other key design dimensions. The findings suggest that the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of parameter values, but is diminished as punishment becomes more costly, group size increases, or the number of rounds decreases. The model does not include communication (chat), rewards, or identification of punishers, so its predictions are most applicable to games without these features. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, especially in small groups, with moderate punishment costs, and multiple rounds.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2012). The Structure of Mutations and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

The total cost of inhibiting defection (i.e., the sum of punishment costs) also decreases with more severe punishment and larger populations. Simulations confirm that the mechanism can spread and that cooperation can become fixed with a probability greater than neutral selection, provided enough groups adopt the mechanism. The model predicts that, compared to standard costly punishment, concerted punishment can achieve higher efficiency and lower cost, especially in large groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in Public Goods games, enabling a rare but severe concerted punishment mechanism (where punishment is shared and severe but infrequent) can substantially increase efficiency compared to no-punishment or standard costly punishment. The effect is especially pronounced in larger groups and with higher punishment severity. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, when the game design includes concerted punishment (shared cost, severe impact, probabilistic triggering), one should expect a significant increase in efficiency relative to the control. The model also provides explicit formulas for the threshold probability and cost-efficiency tradeoffs, which can be used to inform quantitative predictions based on game parameters such as player count, group size, punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness.

This reduces the mean fitness and group payoff that would otherwise be achieved through cooperation and pro-social punishment. When anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it, especially in larger groups or when between-group variance is low. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of anti-social punishment, larger group sizes, and less frequent dispersal all reduce the likelihood that punishment will improve efficiency. The paper also clarifies that the effect-to-cost ratio of punishment and cooperation modulate these effects, but the negative impact of anti-social punishment is robust across a range of parameter values.

The model shows that the design dimensions—such as player count, number of rounds, marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and whether participation is voluntary—critically shape the efficiency outcome. In particular, pool punishment with second-order punishment and voluntary participation are predicted to yield the highest efficiency. Peer punishment can also increase efficiency, but is less stable and more vulnerable to free-riding. If the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency, enabling punishment (especially with the above features) is likely to substantially increase efficiency. However, if punishment is costly and not well-targeted, or if participation is compulsory, efficiency gains may be limited or negative.
