- source: Gürerk, Irlenbusch, B., & Rockenbach, B. (2009). Motivating teammates: The leader's choice between positive and negative incentives. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Leaders overwhelmingly prefer to use rewards initially, but some switch to punishment if free-riding is high. Punishment schemes (NEG) lead to higher contributions than reward schemes (POS), both for leaders and teammates. However, overall payoffs and efficiency are higher under rewards, because the reward mechanism increases total team surplus (each reward token increases team payoff, while each punishment token reduces it). Leaders benefit more from punishment, but teammates benefit more from rewards. The most efficient outcome (in terms of team payoff relative to the maximum possible) is achieved when leaders stick to rewards throughout. Switching from rewards to punishment increases contributions immediately and persistently, but does not increase efficiency. The effectiveness of incentive schemes is path-dependent and history matters. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure and cost/impact of incentives affect efficiency and payoffs in public goods games with centralized leader-administered incentives.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment in a repeated public goods game with centralized leader-administered incentives. It shows that while punishment increases contributions, it does not increase efficiency or total team payoff compared to rewards; in fact, efficiency is slightly higher with rewards. The marginal per-capita return, punishment/reward cost and effectiveness, and the structure of the incentive mechanism are all specified and can be mapped to prediction dimensions. The results suggest that enabling punishment may increase contributions but not efficiency, and that the net effect on efficiency depends on the cost/impact structure of the punishment mechanism. The findings are most applicable to settings with centralized, leader-chosen incentives, continuous contributions, and full information about contributions and payoffs.

- source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: van der Weele, J. (2012). The Signaling Power of Sanctions in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Law Economics & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public good games with both egoists and conditional cooperators, the optimal sanctioning policy for a government depends on the underlying type distribution. When conditional cooperators are prevalent, low sanctions can be optimal because they signal a cooperative environment and 'crowd in' trust, leading to higher voluntary cooperation and lower enforcement costs. When egoists are prevalent, high sanctions are needed to induce cooperation. Under asymmetric information, sanctions have a dual role: they both deter defection and signal information about the population, affecting beliefs and thus cooperation. The model predicts that raising sanctions can sometimes reduce trust and voluntary cooperation, offsetting the direct deterrence effect. The overall effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent and can be positive, neutral, or even negative depending on the population mix and information structure.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public good games is moderated by the prevalence of conditional cooperators and the information structure. If the control game (no punishment) already achieves high efficiency due to social norms or trust, introducing or increasing punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it by crowding out trust. Conversely, in environments with many egoists and low baseline efficiency, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The model also highlights that the costliness of sanctions and their signaling effects must be considered: low-cost, low-level sanctions may be optimal in cooperative environments, while high-cost, high-level sanctions are only justified in less cooperative ones. For prediction, this suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment depends on baseline efficiency, the cost and visibility of sanctions, and the expected prevalence of conditional cooperators.

- source: Kocher, M., Martinsson, P., & Visser, M. (2012). Social background, cooperative behavior, and norm enforcement. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This paper reports on one-shot public goods games with and without punishment, conducted in four South African high schools representing different socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. The introduction of punishment increased contributions significantly only in the white community, and only modestly or insignificantly in the others. Punishment was more frequent and severe in the African communities, leading to lower average earnings in the punishment condition for those groups. The study finds that social background variables (such as trust and household violence) explain much of the variation in cooperation and punishment. Overall, the efficiency impact of punishment is mixed: in some groups, punishment increases contributions but also reduces earnings due to the cost of punishment, sometimes resulting in no net efficiency gain.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency in one-shot, three-player public goods games with MPCR=0.5 and a punishment technology of 1:3 (cost:impact) is highly context-dependent. In groups with low baseline contributions, punishment may increase contributions and potentially efficiency, but in groups with already high contributions, punishment may not increase cooperation and can reduce efficiency due to punishment costs. The presence of anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) can further reduce efficiency. Control efficiency (without punishment) is a strong moderator: if baseline contributions are high, punishment may not improve and can even worsen efficiency. Social background and group norms are important moderators, but are not directly encoded in the game design dimensions. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will reliably increase efficiency, especially in one-shot or low-trust settings.

- source: Fatas, E., Morales, A. J., & Ubeda, P. (2010). Blind justice: An experimental analysis of random punishment in team production. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study implements a public goods game with a novel random punishment mechanism: after each round, each group member is excluded from the group benefit with probability 1-R, where R is the normalized group contribution. This 'blind' punishment does not target individual defectors. The experiment compares three conditions: standard VCM (no punishment), random punishment without redistribution (RP), and random punishment with redistribution (RPR). Results show that both RP and RPR significantly increase contributions compared to VCM. However, only RPR increases group earnings (efficiency), as RP's punishment cost outweighs the contribution gains. The effect of random punishment is similar whether introduced after a baseline or from the start. Behavioral analysis shows that high contributors reduce their contributions if punished, but overall, conditional cooperation persists and the net effect is positive for contributions. The mechanism does not fully prevent the decline in contributions over time.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that random, non-individually-targeted punishment can increase contributions in a standard public goods game, but its effect on efficiency depends on whether the penalty is redistributed. For prediction tasks, the presence of a punishment mechanism that is not individually targeted but is probabilistically linked to group performance will likely increase contributions, but may not increase efficiency unless the punishment cost is recycled within the group. The effect is robust to order and does not depend on communication or knowledge of individual contributions. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is 0.5, and the group size is 4. The findings suggest that, for similar designs, enabling random punishment will increase contributions and may increase efficiency if the punishment cost is not lost to the group.

- source: Reuben, E., & Tyran, J. R. (2010). Everyone is a winner: Promoting cooperation through all-can-win intergroup competition. *European Journal of Political Economy*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing tolerance-based expulsion in a spatial public goods game can substantially increase the level of cooperation (measured as the fraction of cooperators and expellers). The effect is strongest at intermediate tolerance thresholds and when expulsion costs are high. The mechanism allows expellers to balance the cost of expulsion with the benefit of removing defectors, leading to higher and more stable cooperation levels. However, the paper does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare outcomes, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined from the reported results.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that tolerance-based expulsion mechanisms can increase cooperation rates in spatial public goods games, especially when the cost of expulsion is high and the tolerance threshold is optimally set. However, because the reported outcomes are cooperation levels rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency effects. The results suggest that enabling punishment (in the form of expulsion) is likely to increase cooperation, but the impact on efficiency is not directly quantified and may depend on the costliness of expulsion and the structure of the population. The model is theoretical and simulation-based, not empirical.

- source: Orr, S. W. (2001). The economics of shame in work groups: How mutual monitoring can decrease cooperation in teams. *Kyklos*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, reward_exists, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds, through theoretical argument and reference to motivation crowding theory, that mutual monitoring and social sanctions (including social disapproval) in public-goods-like group incentive schemes can decrease cooperation among normatively motivated individuals due to crowding out of intrinsic motivation. The effect is non-linear: some monitoring may initially increase cooperation, but excessive or controlling monitoring leads to suspicion and a shift from normative to gain frames, reducing contributions. For individualistic types, monitoring may crowd in normative motivation and increase cooperation. The overall effect of mutual monitoring or social sanctions on group performance is thus frequency-dependent on the motivational composition of the group. The paper does not provide direct predictions or data on efficiency or group payoff, but suggests that in groups with many normatively motivated members, mutual monitoring may reduce cooperation and thus likely reduce efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical caution that enabling mutual monitoring or social sanctions (punishment) in public-goods-like environments may not always increase efficiency or cooperation, especially in groups with many normatively motivated members. The effect of punishment or monitoring is predicted to be non-linear and frequency-dependent: it may increase cooperation among individualists but decrease it among cooperators if perceived as controlling. For the prediction task, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and may depend on group composition and the perceived nature of monitoring. However, the paper does not provide quantitative or empirical evidence on efficiency, so its value is mainly in highlighting possible negative or mixed effects of punishment mechanisms, especially social (rather than material) sanctions.

- source: Messer, K. D., & Zarghamee, H. (2007). New hope for the voluntary contributions mechanism: The effects of context. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The study finds that context—specifically, the combination of cheap talk, voting, and a status quo of giving—can dramatically increase and sustain contributions and efficiency in a voluntary contributions mechanism (VCM) with a low MPCR. In the baseline (no context), efficiency starts at 82.3% and decays to 72.6% by round 10. In the full-context treatment, efficiency is 99.0% initially and 98.1% in the final round, with no significant decay. Cheap talk and status quo of giving each increase initial contributions, but only their interaction (and with voting) sustains high contributions. Voting alone has no effect. The results show that context can substitute for incentive-compatible mechanisms in achieving high efficiency, but the paper does not test or discuss punishment or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is highly informative about the effects of communication, voting, and default framing, but not about punishment. For the downstream prediction task—predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency—this paper is not directly relevant. However, it does provide a strong benchmark for how non-punitive contextual factors can achieve high efficiency, which may be useful for comparative purposes or for understanding the limits of punishment's added value in similar settings.

- source: Cadigan, J., Wayland, P. T., Schmitt, P., & Swope, K. (2011). An experimental dynamic public goods game with carryover. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing dynamic carryover (either via endowment or MPCR) can increase first-stage contributions in public goods games, but this does not reliably translate into higher efficiency. Efficiency, defined as the share of maximal cooperative gains captured, is not significantly different from the baseline in most treatments, except when MPCR is increased. The main determinants of contributions and efficiency are endowment and MPCR, consistent with prior VCM literature. There is no evidence on punishment or sanctioning, as these mechanisms are not present in the design.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence on how dynamic features like carryover in endowment or MPCR affect efficiency and contributions in public goods games without punishment. It shows that increasing contributions via dynamic incentives does not necessarily increase efficiency, especially if the maximum possible surplus also increases. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency is primarily determined by MPCR and endowment, and that dynamic features alone do not guarantee higher efficiency. The paper does not inform about the effects of punishment, so it should not be used to predict the effect of enabling punishment in similar games.

- source: Benchekroun, H., & Van Long, N. (2008). The build-up of cooperative behavior among non-cooperative selfish agents. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated public goods game with no punishment or sanctions, but where agents can condition their behavior on a public 'stock of cooperation' (social history), a continuum of Markovian Nash equilibria exist. These equilibria can support steady-state contribution and welfare levels above the static Nash equilibrium but below the social optimum. The social optimum cannot be stably achieved without punishment. The model provides explicit analytical characterizations of these equilibria and shows that the more patient the agents (lower discount rate), the closer the best equilibrium can approach the social optimum. The paper emphasizes that, unlike models with punishment, the highest sustainable efficiency is strictly less than the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly relevant for predicting efficiency in public goods games without punishment or sanctions, especially when social history or trust mechanisms are present. It provides strong theoretical evidence that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency can be improved above the Nash baseline if agents coordinate on social-history-based strategies, but the social optimum is not achievable. For the downstream prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (which is absent here) would allow efficiency to reach or exceed the best equilibrium described in this model, and potentially reach the social optimum. The paper is not informative about the effect of punishment per se, but it provides a theoretical upper bound for efficiency in the absence of punishment, given social history mechanisms.

In the VP-16 treatment (exit payoff = 16), the decay in contributions over time is counteracted, and in some groups, contributions even increase in later rounds. It does, however, provide informative context about the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency and contribution dynamics in a standard linear PGG with and without voluntary participation. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only useful for understanding the control (no-punishment) condition, not the treatment (punishment-enabled) condition. It does not inform the effect of punishment or moderators of punishment effects.

- source: Tarui, N., Mason, C. F., Polasky, S., & Ellis, G. (2008). Cooperation in the commons with unobservable actions. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a dynamic common property resource game with unobservable actions, a two-part symmetric group punishment strategy (harsh group punishment after a defection, then forgiveness and return to cooperation) can support full cooperation (first-best efficiency) as a subgame perfect equilibrium, provided certain conditions on the discount factor, resource growth rate, and group size are met. The analysis shows that the threat of the harshest possible group punishment (worst perfect equilibrium) is sufficient to deter defection, even when individual actions are not observable. The paper provides explicit mathematical conditions for when this is possible and uses numerical simulations to illustrate how the sustainability of cooperation depends on group size and other parameters. Notably, the effect of group size is non-monotonic: increasing group size can initially make cooperation easier to sustain (by making punishment more credible), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect eventually dominates, making cooperation harder to sustain.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that, in public-goods-like environments with unobservable actions, the availability of credible, symmetric group punishment can enable full efficiency (relative to the first-best cooperative outcome), provided the punishment is sufficiently harsh and the parameter conditions (discount factor, resource growth, group size) are favorable. The analysis is directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in games where only aggregate outcomes are observable. The findings suggest that punishment can have a strongly positive effect on efficiency, but only if the punishment is credible and harsh enough to deter defection. The effect of group size is nuanced: increasing group size can initially make punishment more credible (and thus support efficiency), but for larger groups, the incentive to defect increases and efficiency becomes harder to sustain. The paper does not address other design dimensions such as chat, reward, or information feedback, so its predictive value is strongest for settings matching the model's assumptions (no chat, no reward, symmetric group punishment, unobservable individual actions, continuous contribution/harvest choices).

The frequency of efficient equilibria increases with higher sanction levels and larger population sizes, and spatial clustering of cooperators and enforcers is crucial for sustaining cooperation. The paper's parameter sensitivity analysis can inform predictions about how changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and group size affect the efficiency gains from punishment.

- source: Guillen, P., Schwieren, C., & Staffiero, G. (2007). Why feed the Leviathan?. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that a centralized sanctioning mechanism (CSM) in a step-level public good game leads to a dramatic increase in the rate at which groups successfully provide the public good (i.e., reach the threshold). In the baseline (no CSM), groups almost never reach the threshold after a few rounds. With CSM, the threshold is reached in nearly 90% of rounds.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a centralized punishment mechanism in a threshold (step-level) public good game increases the rate of successful public good provision, both during and after the punishment phase. The effect persists even after punishment is removed, supporting the idea of an 'educational' or coordination effect. The CSM is costly, so the net efficiency gain depends on the balance between increased public good provision and the cost of the mechanism. For prediction, the presence of a centralized punishment mechanism should be expected to substantially increase efficiency (proxied by provision rate) compared to a no-punishment baseline, especially in step-level games with coordination problems. The effect is robust to whether removal is automatic or by vote. However, the paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions must use provision rate as a proxy.

  findings: This field-lab experiment in rural South Africa and Namibia used a repeated common-pool resource game with 5-person groups over 20 rounds. After 10 rounds, groups voted to implement one of three rules: penalty (punishment), reward, or communication. The introduction of any rule led to a significant reduction in resource extraction (i.e., more cooperation), but there was no significant difference between the effects of penalty, reward, or communication rules on extraction levels after accounting for group dynamics. Payoff-related outcomes (individual and group payoffs) were analyzed as predictors of behavior, but efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum) was not directly reported. The main behavioral outcome is the number of sheep chosen, which is inversely related to cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing a punishment (penalty) rule in a repeated common-pool resource game with 5 players and 20 rounds improves cooperation (reduces extraction), thus likely increasing efficiency relative to the control (no-rule) condition. However, the effect of punishment is not significantly different from reward or communication rules after accounting for group dynamics. The efficiency gain from punishment is thus positive, but the magnitude is similar to that of other rule-based interventions. Group composition and social context are strong moderators. For prediction, the presence of a punishment rule should be expected to increase efficiency compared to no rule, but the effect size may be modest and context-dependent.
