- source: Wu, Z. W., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2014). Punishment Mechanism with Self-Adjusting Rules in Spatial Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

- source: Engelmann, D., & Nikiforakis, N. (2015). In the long-run we are all dead: on the benefits of peer punishment in rich environments. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated linear public goods game with 4 players and 30 rounds, the authors compare three treatments: (1) No Punishment (NP), (2) Standard Punishment (SP, one anonymous punishment stage per round, IDs randomized each round), and (3) Rich Punishment (RP, multiple punishment stages per round, fixed IDs, full information, allowing for retaliation and complex strategies). In SP, group earnings increase significantly over time and surpass NP in the second half of the experiment. In RP, group earnings do not increase significantly over time and are not significantly different from NP, even in later rounds. Contributions are higher in both punishment treatments than in NP, but only in SP do they increase over time. In RP, some groups achieve full cooperation, but others experience breakdowns and feuds, leading to high punishment costs and no net efficiency gain. The results suggest that the efficiency of peer punishment is highly sensitive to the structure of the punishment environment, especially the possibility of retaliation and the richness of available strategies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends on the design of the punishment mechanism. When punishment is limited to a single, anonymous stage per round (SP), enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to the control. When punishment is unconstrained and allows for retaliation (RP), enabling punishment does not increase efficiency relative to the control, despite higher contributions, due to offsetting punishment costs and feuding. For prediction, the presence of multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full information about who punished whom are critical moderators that can nullify the efficiency gains of punishment. Control efficiency (NP) is similar across treatments, but the treatment effect of punishment depends on these design features.

Pure punishment or pure reward is suboptimal: too much punishment collapses the institution, while too much reward allows institutional defectors to dominate. The efficiency of punishment and reward (i.e., their impact per unit cost) increases the range of conditions under which cooperation prevails. It suggests that enabling punishment alone may not always increase efficiency; the presence and strength of reward, and the cost-effectiveness of both, are crucial moderators. For prediction tasks, the model implies that intermediate punishment (with some reward) is optimal for efficiency, and that both the cost and impact of punishment and reward should be considered. The findings are directly relevant for settings where institutional incentives are endogenous and both punishment and reward are possible.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with optional participation, institutional punishment (penalties) is highly effective at promoting full cooperation and maximizing group welfare, even when the same level of punishment would only produce bi-stability (coordination problem) under compulsory participation.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games with optional participation and institutional punishment, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency (group welfare) compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across a wide range of group sizes, rounds, and incentive strengths, provided the punishment is above a threshold that decreases with group size. The model also predicts that institutional rewards are much less effective in this context. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially when optional participation and institutional punishment are present. However, the results are theoretical and assume infinite populations and well-mixed groups, so empirical generalizability may be limited.

  findings: The paper finds that in spatial public goods games with both prosocial and antisocial punishment, the negative effects of antisocial punishment on cooperation and efficiency are largely neutralized by second-order free-riding among defectors. When the synergy factor (multiplication factor) is high, cooperation is sustained by network reciprocity alone, and antisocial punishment is negligible. When the synergy factor is low, prosocial punishment can still restore cooperation and efficiency, because nonpunishing defectors (second-order free-riders) undermine antisocial punishers, allowing punishing cooperators to dominate. The result is that, across a wide range of parameters, the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency, even in the presence of antisocial punishment. The paper also identifies parameter regions with stable coexistence of strategies, but the main result is that prosocial punishment is robustly effective in structured populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in spatially structured public goods games, enabling punishment (even when antisocial punishment is possible) will increase group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition, except in cases where punishment is extremely costly or the synergy factor is very low. The results suggest that the presence of antisocial punishment does not undermine the efficiency gains from prosocial punishment in structured populations, due to the protective effect of second-order free-riding. The findings are most directly applicable to games with local interactions, fixed group size (G=5), and continuous contribution choices. The mapping from strategy composition to efficiency is direct, so the phase diagrams and stationary states can be used to predict efficiency outcomes under different parameter settings (e.g., player count, mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness). The paper does not address chat, communication, or information dimensions, so predictions for those features are not informed.
