- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Prétôt, L., Taylor, Q., & Mcauliffe, K. (2024). Children cooperate more with in-group members than with out-group members in an iterated face-to-face Prisoner's Dilemma Game. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (CPR settings), cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained if and only if the group collectively invests a sufficient fraction of its resources into monitoring and sanctioning rule violations. When monitoring is funded unilaterally (peer punishment), self-interested agents will not invest enough, leading to a second-order free-rider problem and low efficiency. When monitoring is funded collectively from the common-pool resource (institutional punishment), there exists a threshold investment (beta) above which full cooperation and high efficiency are stable equilibria. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum investment required and shows that the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment technology), the cost of monitoring, and the severity of sanctions all moderate the efficiency gains from punishment. The model also predicts that initial 'free' monitoring (by some agents ignoring costs) may be needed to escape the no-cooperation equilibrium, but once cooperation is established, monitoring can be fully incentivized and efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on game design dimensions. It shows that the presence and structure of punishment (especially whether it is institutionally organized and collectively funded) are critical moderators of efficiency. The explicit inequalities derived in the paper allow one to predict whether enabling punishment will increase efficiency, given the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the fraction of resources invested in monitoring. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The results are most directly applicable to settings where monitoring and sanctioning are collective, and less so to purely peer-based punishment. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T. (2023). Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Espín, A. M., Brañas-Garza, P., Gamella, J. F., Herrmann, B., & Martín, J. (2022). Culture and group-functional punishment behaviour. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that Gitanos punish much less than non-Gitanos in homogeneous groups, but increase punishment (especially Gitano males) in mixed groups with non-Gitanos, targeting Gitano free-riders. Non-Gitanos punish more overall, and in mixed groups, non-Gitano males punish Gitano free-riders more harshly than non-Gitano free-riders. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is generally low, but somewhat higher when outgroup members are present. Gender moderates punishment: Gitano females almost never punish, while males increase punishment in mixed groups. Contribution rates are high and do not differ significantly by ethnicity or group composition. The paper does not analyze efficiency or group payoff as a main outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides detailed evidence on how punishment behavior varies by culture, gender, and group composition in a one-shot PGG with punishment. However, it does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and behavioral results may be useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. If using this paper for prediction, one should note that the effect of punishment on efficiency is not measured, and that the main findings concern who punishes whom, not whether punishment increases group payoff.

Groups with higher consensus scores in chat showed larger increases in contributions to the group pot in the next round. However, it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this paper is only indirectly informative: it supports the importance of communication (chat) as a moderator of cooperation, but does not address punishment or efficiency directly. It is most useful for understanding the role of chat/communication as a game design dimension.

- source: van Klingeren, F., & Buskens, V. (2024). Graduated sanctioning, endogenous institutions and sustainable cooperation in common-pool resources: An experimental test. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a dynamic common-pool resource (CPR) game with four players and repeated rounds, the introduction of sanctioning (punishment) mechanisms—either strict or graduated—leads to higher cooperation and resource sustainability compared to no sanctioning. Strict sanctioning (fixed, high-cost punishment) initially results in higher resource size and profit, but graduated sanctioning (punishment severity increases with repeated defection) leads to greater cooperation and lower appropriation over time, eventually closing the gap in resource size and profit. When groups can endogenously choose their sanctioning mechanism (via majority vote every five rounds), cooperation and resource outcomes improve further, especially in the long run. However, the differences in group and individual profit between sanctioning types are small and diminish over time. The study provides partial support for the effectiveness of graduated and endogenously chosen sanctioning, especially for long-term cooperation, but strict sanctioning may be more effective in short games or early rounds.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated, dynamic CPR games with endogenous resource dynamics and mutual monitoring. The results suggest that both strict and graduated punishment mechanisms increase efficiency (resource sustainability and group profit) relative to no punishment. Strict punishment is more effective in the short term, but graduated punishment becomes equally or more effective over longer time horizons. Endogenously chosen punishment mechanisms (via group voting) further enhance cooperation and resource outcomes, though the effect on profit is small. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude and time course depending on the punishment regime and whether the institution is endogenously chosen. The findings are most applicable to settings with 4 players, 40 rounds, continuous contribution/appropriation, no chat, and mutual monitoring.

- source: Kido, Y., & Takezawa, M. (2024). Coevolution of norm psychology and cooperation through exapted conformity. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The model shows that cooperation can evolve in collective action dilemmas when injunctive norms strongly encourage punishment, but not when they only encourage cooperation. Conformity (descriptive norm psychology) alone can support intermediate cooperation, but is unstable and easily undermined. When conformity is exapted (pre-existing), it can expand the conditions under which punishment-based cooperation evolves, acting as a scaffold for the emergence of stable, high-cooperation societies. The model identifies two stable types of cooperative societies: those based on conformity (unstable, lower cooperation) and those based on punishment (stable, higher cooperation). However, all outcomes are reported in terms of cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency or group payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the importance of punishment norms (and the cost/benefit structure of punishment) in enabling high cooperation in public-goods-like environments, especially when conformity is present. However, because the model does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings suggest that punishment is more effective than conformity at sustaining high cooperation, and that the presence of conformity can expand the parameter space where punishment-based cooperation emerges. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment mechanisms (especially when normatively encouraged) are likely to increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency outcomes.

- source: Nunney, S. J., van der Schalk, J., & Manstead, A. S. R. (2022). Emotion and intergroup cooperation: How verbal expressions of guilt, shame, and pride influence behavior in a social dilemma. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab experiments using an intergroup centipede game, the authors find that when a group expresses shame (or guilt) after behaving uncooperatively, the opposing group is more likely to cooperate in subsequent rounds, resulting in higher group payoffs (tickets won) and more prosocial behavior. In contrast, when a group expresses pride after uncooperative behavior, the opposing group is less likely to cooperate, resulting in lower payoffs and more costly punishment.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that communication—specifically, the expression of self-conscious emotions—can significantly moderate the effect of prior uncooperative behavior on subsequent group efficiency in repeated, sequential social dilemmas. While not a standard PGG, the centipede game shares key features (sequential cooperation, group payoffs, potential for exploitation). The findings suggest that, in environments where communication is possible, expressions of shame or guilt can substitute for or augment formal punishment mechanisms in restoring cooperation and efficiency, while pride expressions can undermine them. However, the study does not manipulate punishment cost, technology, or other standard PGG design dimensions, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled PGG efficiency is limited. It does, however, highlight the importance of communication and emotional signaling as moderators of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Büyükboyaci, M., Karagözoglu, E., & Küçüksenel, S. (2025). Productive and Unproductive Effort Choices in Groups and Sharing: An Experimental Study. *Managerial and Decision Economics*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that increasing the opportunity cost of unproductive investment (i.e., making productive investment more attractive) does not significantly increase productive investment rates. Subjects' own allocation choices do not significantly affect their giving in the distribution stage, but they give less to human partners who chose unproductive investment, indicating a punishment mechanism. This punishment is not applied when the unproductive choice is made by a computer. The size of the surplus (group payoff) is determined by the sum of productive investments, but the paper does not directly analyze efficiency or compare group payoffs across treatments.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence relevant to predicting efficiency in public-goods-like games with punishment. The design is adjacent to a PGG: resource allocation between productive and unproductive uses, with endogenous surplus and a contest for control. Punishment (via lower giving) is selectively applied to human partners who make unproductive choices, but this does not feed back to increase productive investment rates. Thus, the presence of a punishment-like mechanism does not guarantee higher efficiency in this context. For prediction, this suggests that punishment may not always improve efficiency, especially if it is not structured as a direct, costly sanction and if it does not change initial contribution behavior.
