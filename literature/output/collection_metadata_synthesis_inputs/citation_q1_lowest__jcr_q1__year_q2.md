- source: Kroupa, S. (2014). Love or Fear: Can Punishment Promote Cooperation?. *Evolutionary Anthropology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: num_rounds, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that punishment in public goods games increases cooperation but does not always increase efficiency; in fact, in many standard lab designs, the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits, leading to lower group payoffs. However, when games are run for more rounds, or when communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation are allowed, the frequency and cost of punishment decline over time, and efficiency improves—sometimes surpassing the no-punishment baseline. The paper also notes that antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding can undermine efficiency, but these problems are less severe in real-world or more naturalistic experimental settings where communication and reputation are present. The review suggests that the negative effects of punishment on efficiency have been exaggerated by restrictive lab designs, and that in real-world settings, punishment is often less costly and more effective due to social mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the impact depends strongly on game design dimensions: short, anonymous, communication-free games with fixed groups and no reputation or coordination tend to show punishment as inefficient (lower group payoff), while longer games, or those with communication, reputation, or endogenous group formation, show punishment as both effective and efficient. The presence of antisocial punishment and high punishment costs can reduce efficiency, but these are mitigated by communication and coordination. Thus, when predicting treatment efficiency, one should adjust expectations upward if the design includes communication, reputation, or longer time horizons, and downward if the design is short, anonymous, and lacks these features.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper argues that status hierarchies can endogenously coordinate punishment in public goods games, leading to more optimal (i.e., less costly and more effective) use of punishment. High status actors are more likely to be expected to punish when punishment is seen as group-oriented, and their punishment is more likely to promote subsequent cooperation and reduce the need for further punishment. The author suggests that these dynamics can help groups realize public goods more efficiently, but this is a theoretical claim rather than an empirically demonstrated effect. The paper also discusses why typical experimental designs may fail to observe these effects, due to their suppression of status emergence and communication.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical arguments and literature synthesis suggesting that the presence of status hierarchies and the assignment of punishment responsibility to high status actors could improve the efficiency of punishment in public goods games by reducing unnecessary or counterproductive punishment and fostering cooperation. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that game designs allowing status differentiation, communication, or leadership roles may see more efficient punishment use, but this is not quantified. The paper is less useful for direct prediction of efficiency from game design dimensions in the absence of empirical data.

For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that punishment's positive effect on efficiency is conditional and may be weak or absent without additional incentives.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive

  findings: The agent-based model simulates a common good game in a residential complex, with agent strategies grounded in qualitative fieldwork. The model shows that in blocks with more defectors, common good production and payoffs collapse. Artificial experiments demonstrate that (1) introducing punishers in place of defectors, (2) increasing all agents' desirability for the common good, and (3) combining both interventions, all increase common good production and total payoffs. Thus, punishment mechanisms, especially when agents value the common good, can transform a low-efficiency, low-payoff equilibrium into a high-efficiency, high-payoff one.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment (and increasing the number of punishers) in a public-goods-like environment can increase efficiency and group payoffs, especially when agents also value the common good. The model is not a standard PGG but is structurally similar, with continuous contributions, punishment, and payoff functions analogous to PGGs. The results suggest that, all else equal, introducing punishment will increase efficiency, particularly in settings where baseline cooperation is fragile or low. The effect is amplified if agents' intrinsic motivation (desirability) for the common good is high. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low.

- source: Ezeigbo, I. C. (2017). How costly punishment, diversity, and density of connectivity influence cooperation in a biological network. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study uses agent-based models of repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games on networks (18 players, 1000 rounds per game) to examine how costly punishment, diversity, and network connectivity affect cooperation and average payoff. Costly punishment (paying 1 unit to reduce another's payoff by 4 units) reduces defection but also significantly reduces average payoff compared to no-punishment conditions. Decreasing network connectivity (lower density of connections) increases average payoff when punishment is absent, but not when punishment is present. Diversity (randomizing connections and neighbors) can promote cooperation, but only in synergy with low connectivity. The main efficiency result is that costly punishment, while reducing defection, comes at a substantial cost to group welfare.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-like network games reduces group efficiency (average payoff), even though it reduces defection. The effect is robust across different network densities and diversity conditions. If the control (no-punishment) game has higher efficiency, enabling punishment is likely to lower efficiency, unless other mechanisms (like diversity and low connectivity) are also present and interact synergistically. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in networked social dilemmas with similar payoff structures.

- source: McBride, M., Kendall, R., D'Orsogna, M. R., & Short, M. B. (2016). Crime, punishment, and evolution in an adversarial game. *European Journal of Applied Mathematics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that, unlike standard social dilemma games (such as the public goods game), the adversarial game studied here can have multiple equilibria, including both efficient (no-crime, high-payoff) and inefficient (high-crime, low-payoff) outcomes. The presence of punishment (reporting and conviction) transforms the game from a pure social dilemma into a coordination game, where the effectiveness of punishment depends on the collective willingness to report. The Informant strategy (defectors who also report) is critical: under imitation dynamics, any presence of Informants is sufficient to reach efficiency, but under best response dynamics, Informants are necessary but not sufficient. The model shows that too severe punishment or high retaliation risk can undermine the emergence of efficient equilibria. Thus, the design of punishment institutions and the social context (e.g., support for reporting) are crucial moderators of the efficiency impact of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like adversarial games is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism, the available strategy set (especially the presence of Informants), and the evolutionary dynamics. For prediction tasks, it suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only under certain parameter regimes: moderate punishment severity, low retaliation risk, and the availability of strategies that combine defection and punishment. The findings caution against assuming that punishment will always improve efficiency; instead, the effect is conditional and can be negative or null if punishment is too harsh or if key strategies are unavailable. The results are most directly applicable to adversarial games with crime and reporting, but the mechanism insights may transfer to public goods games with similar punishment structures.

  findings: The paper finds that adding a costly punishment option to a two-player Stag Hunt game (a coordination game with a payoff-dominant and a risk-dominant equilibrium) dramatically increases the basin of attraction for efficient, cooperative outcomes under evolutionary replicator dynamics. This means that, in evolutionary terms, populations are much more likely to end up in efficient, high-payoff states when punishment is possible, even though the punishment strategy is weakly dominated. The results are robust across a range of parameterizations, and the paper provides both analytical and numerical (simulation) support. The findings are interpreted as theoretical support for the hypothesis that altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency in social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in two-player coordination games structurally similar to public goods games, the introduction of a costly punishment option can substantially increase the likelihood of efficient, cooperative outcomes, as measured by the basin of attraction under evolutionary dynamics. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a game with similar structure (even if punishment is costly and weakly dominated) should be expected to increase average efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, especially when the punishment inflicted is at least as large as the cost to the punisher. However, the results are derived for two-player games and evolutionary (not repeated-game) settings, so direct quantitative extrapolation to standard multi-player repeated public goods games should be done with caution. The qualitative direction of effect—punishment increases efficiency—is strongly supported.
