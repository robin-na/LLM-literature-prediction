- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Nockur, L., Pfattheicher, S., & Keller, J. (2021). Different punishment systems in a public goods game with asymmetric endowments. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment (peer, democratic, or central) in a standard public goods game with either symmetric or asymmetric endowments increases relative contributions compared to no punishment. However, only democratic and central punishment systems avoid a significant reduction in group profit (efficiency) compared to no punishment; peer punishment reduces profit due to high punishment costs. The effect of punishment on efficiency does not significantly differ between symmetric and asymmetric groups. Democratic punishment yields the highest satisfaction and fairness ratings. Punishment systems also reduce profit inequality between advantaged and disadvantaged group members.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a 4-player, 24-round PGG with MPCR=0.4, adding punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency: peer punishment reduces group profit, while democratic and central punishment do not significantly change profit compared to no punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to whether endowments are symmetric or asymmetric. For prediction, the presence and type of punishment system (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators: peer punishment may reduce efficiency, while democratic and central punishment are neutral on average. Control efficiency (profit) is a good baseline; adding peer punishment may lower it, while democratic/central punishment may leave it unchanged. Other design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well specified and should be matched to this context for prediction.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Sasaki, T., & Zhang, B. Y. (2019). The competitive advantage of institutional reward. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of prosocial punishment in unstructured and structured populations and in the presence of antisocial punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that prosocial punishment can evolve and promote cooperation (and thus high efficiency) in public goods games, even without auxiliary mechanisms like network structure or voluntary participation. The efficiency of the group (measured by which strategy dominates and thus the average group payoff) is increased when prosocial punishment dominates. The evolution of prosocial punishment (and thus efficiency) is facilitated by higher returns to the public good (mpcr), higher punishment effectiveness, and optimal punishment cost. Allowing unused punishment resources to be redistributed (non-wasteful punishment) further facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency. Antisocial punishment can dominate under low punishment cost or low public good return, reducing efficiency. Network structure can both hinder and facilitate the evolution of punishment, but under favorable conditions, it helps prosocial punishment dominate. The model provides phase diagrams mapping out these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment can increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective, the cost is optimal (not too low or high), and the returns to the public good are high. The model also shows that if punishment is too cheap, antisocial punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency, while if it is too costly, punishment does not evolve. The presence of a reward mechanism (redistribution of unused punishment resources) further increases the likelihood of high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions such as mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward. The results are theoretical and should be interpreted as providing mechanistic and qualitative guidance rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2019). Evolution of coordinated punishment to enforce cooperation from an unbiased strategy space. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, the ability to observe the presence of punishment institutions is crucial for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff. When institutions are not observable, anti-social punishment can invade and reduce cooperation and efficiency. When institutions are observable, a small minority of pro-social institution supporters can sustain high levels of cooperation and group payoff, even in the presence of anti-social punishment strategies. The model demonstrates that the evolutionary outcome is robust to the inclusion of all possible strategies, and that the coexistence of institution supporters and opportunists is stable. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore context-dependent: positive when observability and conditional strategies are present, negative or neutral when they are absent.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on whether players can observe the presence of punishment institutions and condition their behavior accordingly. If the game design allows for observability and conditional strategies, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. If observability is absent, enabling punishment—especially if anti-social punishment is possible—may reduce efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of supporting institutions and group size affect the probability of institution implementation and thus the efficiency outcome. For prediction, the presence or absence of observability/conditionality, the possibility of anti-social punishment, and the cost structure are key moderators.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Evolution of cooperation under punishment. *Physical Review E*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both autonomous and consensual punishment can promote cooperation and increase average payoffs in public goods games. Consensual punishment (where a group vote determines whether defectors are punished) can be more effective than autonomous punishment when the consensus threshold is low and the willingness to punish is sufficiently high. The model provides explicit conditions under which cooperation dominates defection in terms of average payoffs. The effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency depends on the group size, the marginal per-capita return, the cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the consensus threshold for punishment. The paper also shows that increasing the cost of punishment or the group size can make autonomous punishment more effective relative to consensual punishment, while increasing the fine or the marginal return favors consensual punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, given game design parameters such as group size, number of rounds (implicitly infinite in the model), marginal per-capita return, punishment cost and effectiveness, and the decision rule for punishment (autonomous vs. consensual, and the consensus threshold). The explicit payoff formulas and comparative statics allow for quantitative or qualitative prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment (especially with low consensus thresholds and high willingness to punish) will increase efficiency, but the effect is moderated by punishment cost, group size, and the consensus rule.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Kraft-Todd, G. T., Rand, D. G., Nowak, M. A., & Norton, M. (2021). Invisible inequality leads to punishing the poor and rewarding the rich. *Behavioural Public Policy*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with unequal endowments, allowing punishment and/or reward, the visibility of income distribution has a strong effect on sanctioning and contribution behavior. When incomes are hidden, participants tend to punish the poor (for low absolute contributions) and reward the rich (for high absolute contributions). When incomes are revealed, this pattern reverses: the poor are rewarded (for high percentage contributions) and the rich are punished (for low percentage contributions). Revealing incomes increases total contributions to the public good and reduces final payoff inequality (lower Gini coefficient). The effect is robust whether incomes are assigned randomly or earned via performance. The paper does not report group efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, but the increase in total contributions and reduction in inequality suggest a more positive effect on efficiency when income is revealed and punishment/reward is available.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with unequal endowments and punishment/reward options, making income information visible increases total contributions and reduces payoff inequality, especially by shifting sanctioning away from the poor and toward the rich. For prediction tasks, the presence of income transparency (showing others' endowments) is a key moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency-related outcomes. The game design dimensions most directly supported are player count (5), number of rounds (10), presence of punishment and reward, unequal endowments, and the manipulation of information about others' incomes. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment in a context where income is visible will have a more positive effect on efficiency and equality than when income is hidden. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the fully cooperative outcome, so the exact magnitude of the effect is not available.

- source: Podder, S., Righi, S., & Pancotto, F. (2021). Reputation and punishment sustain cooperation in the optional public goods game. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the OPGG, punishment alone (including anti-social punishment) does not increase cooperation or group fitness, and can even reduce them by increasing the prevalence of loners. Reputation alone can increase cooperation and fitness, but only under specific social norms (e.g., when defectors are assigned a strictly worse reputation than loners). The combination of reputation and punishment leads to the highest levels of cooperation and population fitness, with most agents adopting conditional cooperation strategies and rarely using punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size, synergy factor, punishment cost and effectiveness, and other parameters. The findings suggest that reputation and punishment interact synergistically to sustain cooperation and improve group outcomes, but that reputation can substitute for costly punishment in many cases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in optional public goods games, the addition of punishment alone (especially when anti-social punishment is possible) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and may even reduce it. However, when reputation mechanisms are also present—especially those that allow agents to distinguish defectors from loners—punishment and reputation together can synergistically increase cooperation and group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the presence and nature of reputation mechanisms and social norms. In the absence of reputation, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency; with reputation, especially under certain norms, enabling punishment can have a more positive effect. The model's robustness checks indicate these findings are not highly sensitive to group size, rounds, or punishment cost within the tested ranges.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Conditional punishment is a double-edged sword in promoting cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.

Instead, the average normative view within a group predicts the group's contribution level.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on the effect of normative disagreement in a linear PGG with peer punishment, but does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, nor does it include a no-punishment control. For prediction tasks, it shows that, in a 3-player, 10-round, continuous-contribution PGG with punishment (cost 1, impact 3), with full information about others' contributions and payoffs but anonymous punishment, group-mean normative views are a strong predictor of contribution levels. The presence of punishment maintains moderate cooperation, but the paper does not allow direct estimation of the efficiency gain from enabling punishment versus disabling it. The results suggest that, under these parameters, punishment supports cooperation even in the presence of normative disagreement, but the efficiency effect relative to no-punishment is not measured.
