- source: Wang, S. Y., & Qin, X. D. (2015). The Effect of Digitalization on Penalty Payments: An Experimental Investigation. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that exogenous punishment mechanisms (both cash and electronic token penalties) significantly increase cooperation and group earnings in a repeated public goods game compared to a no-punishment control. Cash penalties are more effective than token penalties at increasing both contributions and earnings, especially in the early rounds, but the difference between cash and token punishment diminishes over time. The deterrent effect of punishment is strong enough to compensate for the deadweight loss from penalties, resulting in higher overall group welfare. The results suggest that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) matters for short-run deterrence, but adaptation reduces this effect with experience.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that introducing exogenous monetary punishment in a standard linear public goods game increases group efficiency (earnings as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) relative to a no-punishment control. The effect is robust for both cash and digital (token) punishment, but is stronger for cash, especially in early rounds. The design dimensions are well specified: 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous integer contributions, MPCR=0.4, punishment is exogenous and automatic, and no reward mechanism is present. The control group efficiency (average earnings) is lower than in the punishment treatments, and the difference is statistically significant. This paper should be used as a strong positive case for predicting that exogenous monetary punishment increases efficiency in similar lab PGG designs, and that the tangibility of punishment (cash vs. digital) can moderate the effect, especially in the short run.

- source: Grieco, D., Faillo, M., & Zarri, L. (2017). Enforcing cooperation in public goods games: Is one punisher enough?. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with punishment, concentrating punishment power in the hands of the top contributor (with frequent turnover) significantly increases both cooperation and group earnings compared to the standard decentralized punishment baseline. Randomly assigning a single punisher per round does not improve efficiency. The mechanism works not because of immunity from punishment, but because the right to punish motivates higher contributions. However, antisocial punishment remains present. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment institution—specifically, how the punisher is selected—has a strong effect on both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and a convex punishment cost function, assigning punishment power to the top contributor (with frequent turnover) increases group earnings (i.e., efficiency) relative to both the no-punishment and the standard decentralized punishment baseline. The effect is not observed when the single punisher is selected randomly. For prediction, this suggests that the selection mechanism for the punisher is a critical moderator: 'top contributor as punisher' yields higher efficiency than both unrestricted and random single punisher regimes, holding other design features constant. The results are most directly applicable to games with similar structure (N=4, 20 rounds, MPCR=0.4, no chat, anonymous, continuous contributions, punishment cost and impact as specified).

- source: Waichman, I., & Stenzel, L. (2019). When Punishment Strikes Late: The Effect of a Delay in Punishment and Punishment Feedback on Cooperation and Efficiency. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency in a linear public good game compared to a no-punishment baseline. This effect holds even when punishment is delayed by five periods, as long as participants receive salient feedback linking punishment to their past contributions. If this feedback is absent (i.e., participants are punished after a delay and are not reminded of their past behavior), punishment still increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, due to increased punishment costs and less targeted norm enforcement. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency thus depends on the informational environment, specifically whether punished individuals can clearly associate punishment with their own prior actions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear PGG with four players and 20 rounds, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment, provided that feedback links punishment to past contributions. The effect is robust to a five-period delay in punishment, as long as feedback is salient. If the design omits such feedback, punishment may still increase cooperation but not efficiency, due to higher punishment costs and less effective norm targeting. For prediction, the presence or absence of feedback linking punishment to behavior is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (no-punishment) is lower than treatment efficiency (with punishment and feedback), but not when feedback is absent. Other design dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are held constant in this study, so the main moderator evidenced is the feedback/identification of norm violation.

- source: Kumakawa, T. (2013). Social Rewards and Sanctions: A Voluntary Contribution Mechanism Experiment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that in a two-player public goods game, the opportunity to send social (free-form) or monetary rewards/sanctions does not increase first-round contributions compared to a baseline with no such opportunity. However, when participants actually experience social sanctions (i.e., receive a negative message from their partner), their contributions in the next round increase significantly. Social rewards (positive messages) do not have a significant effect. Monetary sanctions and rewards are rarely used and do not significantly affect subsequent contributions. The study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player public goods game with a game-theoretic payoff table and no prior knowledge of a second round, social sanctions (blame via free-form messages) can increase subsequent contributions, but only after they are actually experienced. The mere opportunity for sanctions or rewards does not affect cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (contribution) responses to social sanctions, not efficiency outcomes. The design (two players, two rounds, no chat except for the sanction/reward stage, full information about partner's contribution, and a linear MPCR of 0.7) should be considered when generalizing to other settings.

- source: Otten, K., Buskens, V., Przepiorka, W., & Ellemers, N. (2021). Cooperation between newcomers and incumbents: The role of normative disagreements. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Normative disagreement between newcomers and incumbents in a public goods game with peer punishment does not reduce average contributions to the public good. While group identification and the emergence of a shared social norm are negatively affected by normative disagreement, cooperation (as measured by contribution rate) is maintained. Punishment is used as a norm enforcement tool, especially targeting low-contributing newcomers, who subsequently increase their contributions to match incumbent standards. The study does not report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that, in a 3-player repeated public goods game with peer punishment, normative disagreement does not reduce average contributions, and punishment is effective at norm enforcement. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used to directly predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The design and findings are relevant for understanding behavioral mechanisms, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. The game design dimensions are well specified, so the paper can inform the mapping from design to expected contribution rates, but not to efficiency.

- source: Schlösser, T., Berger, S., & Fetchenhauer, D. (2018). Justice Sensitivity and Cooperation Dynamics in Repeated Public Good Games. *Social Justice Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that allowing costly punishment in a repeated public good game leads to a substantial increase in average contributions (from 9.18 to 18.34 tokens per round out of 20). This replicates the classic result that punishment options strongly promote cooperation. The effect of individual justice sensitivity on cooperation is strong in the absence of punishment but disappears when punishment is available, as cooperation becomes strategic to avoid sanctions. Punishment is used infrequently, but its mere availability is sufficient to sustain high cooperation. Although efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported, the high contribution rates in the punishment condition imply much higher group efficiency compared to the no-punishment condition.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a standard repeated public good game with four players, ten rounds, and a punishment technology similar to Fehr & Gächter (2002) leads to a large increase in group efficiency, as measured by average contributions. The control (no-punishment) condition yields about 46% of maximum possible contributions, while the punishment condition yields about 92%. This suggests that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will approximately double efficiency relative to the control. The paper's design and results are highly relevant for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated linear PGGs with similar parameters.

- source: Bogdan, P. C., Dolcos, F., Moore, M., Kuznietsov, I., Culpepper, S. A., & Dolcos, S. (2023). Social Expectations are Primarily Rooted in Reciprocity: An Investigation of Fairness, Cooperation, and Trustworthiness. *Cognitive Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four studies, including a reanalysis of a large cross-cultural PGG with punishment, the paper finds that people punish others whose contributions deviate from their own prior behavior, rather than from the group norm. This applies to both prosocial and antisocial punishment. The effect is robust across cultures and game types. However, the paper does not report on how punishment affects group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare. The only payoff-related results are manipulation checks (e.g., in Study 4, generous partners yield higher earnings). Thus, the findings are highly informative about the psychological mechanisms of punishment and norm enforcement, but do not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in PGGs with punishment, the main driver of punishment is the dissimilarity between a punisher's own prior contributions and the target's contribution, rather than deviation from the group norm. This insight is useful for predicting when and whom players will punish, including antisocial punishment. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency. The findings may be used to inform mechanistic models of punishment assignment, but not to calibrate the effect of punishment on group efficiency.

- source: Romaniuc, R., Farrow, K., Ibanez, L., & Marciano, A. (2016). The perils of government enforcement. *Public Choice*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer disapproval (private enforcement) increases contributions to the public good compared to baseline. Adding a mild, centralized monetary punishment (government enforcement) to peer disapproval stabilizes contributions but does not significantly increase average contributions compared to peer disapproval alone. Critically, if government enforcement is removed after being in place, the effectiveness of peer disapproval is undermined: low contributors become unresponsive to disapproval, and average contributions drop significantly below the level observed when only private enforcement was ever present. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the results on contributions imply that punishment can have both stabilizing and crowding-out effects depending on the sequence of enforcement mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the sequence and combination of enforcement mechanisms (private and government) can have important effects on cooperation (as measured by contributions) in public goods games. While the study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, the findings suggest that enabling punishment can stabilize or increase contributions, but that removing punishment after it has been in place can lead to a sharp decline in cooperation, even below the level observed with private enforcement alone. For prediction tasks, this implies that the history of enforcement mechanisms and their removal are important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency. However, the lack of direct efficiency or payoff data limits the ability to make quantitative predictions about efficiency outcomes.

- source: Morgan, S. N., Mason, N. M., & Shupp, R. S. (2019). The effects of voice with(out) punishment: Public goods provision and rule compliance. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that: (1) Comments alone do not significantly increase contributions or compliance with the MCR compared to control. (2) Enforcement (punishment) alone increases contributions and compliance, but the effect is significantly larger when comments are combined with enforcement. (3) The comment with enforcement treatment leads to the highest and most sustained contributions and compliance. (4) Rule-makers exposed to comments set higher MCRs, which in turn increases contributions. (5) The effect of comments on contributions is partly mediated by the higher MCRs set by rule-makers. (6) The paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the increased contributions and compliance in the comment with enforcement treatment imply higher efficiency in that condition.
  prediction_guidance: For the prediction task, this paper provides strong evidence that in a linear public goods game with a probabilistic punishment mechanism, adding a comment-based participation mechanism (private, one-way comments to a rule-maker who sets a minimum contribution rule) increases contributions and compliance above punishment alone. While efficiency is not directly measured, the structure of the game (MPCR = 0.4, group size 5, continuous contributions, no chat, no rewards) means that higher contributions and compliance will translate into higher efficiency. The effect of punishment alone is positive but smaller than the effect of punishment plus comments. The paper does not provide direct evidence on efficiency, so predictions should be based on the observed contribution and compliance patterns. The findings are most relevant for games with third-party rule-making, probabilistic exogenous punishment, and no peer-to-peer punishment or reward.

- source: Moreno, S. F. G. Y., & Gutierrez-Garcia, J. O. (2018). Collective action in organizational structures. *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory*.

  findings: The paper finds that the structure of hierarchical organizations and the design of punishment mechanisms significantly affect collective action, as measured by aggregate contribution levels. Organizations with more hierarchical levels achieve higher contributions when supervisor-based punishment is used. The findings suggest that, in similar agent-based or organizational settings, enabling peer punishment and structuring organizations into smaller units with more levels may increase contributions, which could correlate with higher efficiency if punishment costs are not excessive.
