- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Hauser, O. P., Nowak, M. A., & Rand, D. G. (2014). Punishment does not promote cooperation under exploration dynamics when anti-social punishment is possible. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in both compulsory and voluntary public goods games, when all forms of punishment are allowed (including anti-social punishment), the introduction of punishment does not increase the average payoff or efficiency of the group under high mutation rates. Analytical results and agent-based simulations show that the average payoff of cooperators is not increased by punishment when anti-social punishment is possible; in fact, the outcome is equivalent to the no-punishment baseline. Only when punishment is restricted to pro-social forms (punishing defectors only) does punishment increase efficiency. These findings are robust to changes in mutation rate and selection intensity.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in PGG-like environments with high exploration/mutation and the possibility of anti-social punishment, enabling punishment will not increase group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative when anti-social punishment is possible, regardless of other game parameters such as player count, rounds, or selection intensity. If the game design allows anti-social punishment, do not expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2013). The evolution of cooperation by social exclusion. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that social exclusion is a more effective and robust mechanism than costly punishment for sustaining cooperation and high efficiency in public goods games. When excluders can effectively and cheaply exclude defectors, the group can reach and maintain the efficient outcome where all contribute and defectors are eliminated. Costly punishment, even when effective, is vulnerable to invasion by non-punishing cooperators (second-order freeriders), leading to a decline in punishers and eventual dominance by defectors, which collapses efficiency. The model demonstrates that social exclusion can solve both the emergence and maintenance problems of cooperation, provided the cost of exclusion is not too high. The results are robust to changes in group size and to stochastic effects. The paper also notes that if monitoring costs are high, or if exclusion is not self-serving, the advantage of social exclusion disappears.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling a punishment mechanism that takes the form of social exclusion (where defectors are excluded from benefit sharing and the cost of exclusion is not too high) will lead to a large increase in group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across group sizes and does not require repeated interaction or reputation. In contrast, enabling costly punishment (where punishers pay to reduce defectors' payoffs) is less likely to increase efficiency, especially in the long run, due to vulnerability to second-order freeriders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social exclusion mechanism with low cost and high effectiveness should be treated as a strong positive moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment. The specific model parameters (group size, multiplication factor, cost of exclusion/punishment, effectiveness) are all explicitly analyzed, allowing for quantitative predictions if mapped to empirical settings.

- source: Adami, C., Schossau, J., & Hintze, A. (2016). Evolutionary game theory using agent-based methods. *Physics of Life Reviews*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. The effect is quantitatively predicted by mean-field theory and confirmed by simulations: higher probability or density of punishment lowers the threshold for cooperation. However, because punishment is costly, it creates a second-order dilemma, and the evolutionary dynamics can lead to meta-stable states and hysteresis. When punishment is allowed to evolve, its effect is strongest near the critical point, and in highly cooperative or highly defective regimes, punishment is not maintained by selection. The findings are robust across different group sizes and mutation rates, and the paper provides explicit equations and simulation results for the effect of punishment on group payoff and cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (payoff) in the dilemma region by shifting the critical point for cooperation to lower values of the synergy parameter (r). The effect size and direction depend on the punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness), group size, and whether punishment is fixed or can evolve. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.

- source: Schoenmakers, S., Hilbe, C., Blasius, B., & Traulsen, A. (2014). Sanctions as honest signals - The evolution of pool punishment by public sanctioning institutions. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the presence and public visibility of a punishment institution in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency (average payoff) compared to control conditions without punishment. The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Muthukrishna, M., Francois, P., Pourahmadi, S., & Henrich, J. (2017). Corrupting cooperation and how anti-corruption strategies may backfire. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: This paper experimentally demonstrates that allowing bribery in an institutional punishment public goods game (IPGG) substantially reduces public good provisioning (by about 25%). In the standard IPGG (control), stronger leaders (higher punishment multiplier) increase contributions and public good provisioning. However, when bribery is possible (bribery game, BG), stronger leaders actually decrease public good provisioning, as they use their power to extract bribes rather than enforce cooperation. Anti-corruption strategies (partial/full transparency, leader investment) can increase contributions in some contexts (strong leaders or high economic potential), but may have no effect or even reduce contributions further in contexts with weak leaders and poor economic potential. The main outcome is mean contribution to the public good, which is a close proxy for efficiency. The results show that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be reversed or undermined by corruption opportunities.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the presence of corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) can reverse the usual positive effect of institutional punishment on efficiency. The effect of punishment depends critically on whether leaders can be corrupted, the strength of the leader's punitive power, and the economic potential (mpcr). In standard IPGGs, stronger punishment increases efficiency, but in bribery-enabled games, stronger punishment can decrease efficiency. Anti-corruption interventions (transparency, leader investment) can help, but only under certain structural conditions. When using this paper for prediction, it is important to consider whether the game design allows for corruption/bribery, the strength of punishment, and the economic context. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not reported directly, but the relative changes are clear: punishment increases efficiency in standard IPGG, but decreases it when bribery is possible.

- source: Szolnoki, A., & Perc, M. (2013). Effectiveness of conditional punishment for the evolution of public cooperation. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that conditional punishment, where the severity of punishment is proportional to the number of punishers in a group, is more effective than unconditional punishment in promoting cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when punishment is costly. Conditional punishers can maintain cooperation at lower fines and costs, and are more efficient in terms of cooperation per unit cost. Unconditional punishers only outperform conditional punishers when punishment is very cheap. The results are robust across different values of the multiplication factor (mpcr), and the findings are explained through the mechanism of indirect territorial competition and pattern formation on the spatial grid.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial public goods games, the design of the punishment mechanism (conditional vs. unconditional) and the cost-to-fine ratio are critical moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperation and cost-effectiveness. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, the results suggest that conditional punishment is more likely to yield higher cooperation per unit cost, especially when punishment is not cheap. However, the paper does not directly report group efficiency as a fraction of the maximum possible payoff, so predictions about absolute efficiency should be made with caution. The findings are most applicable to spatially structured games with local interactions and no communication.

The effect of punishment is much stronger when implemented locally rather than globally. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of local punishment should be expected to yield a more positive effect on efficiency-related outcomes than global punishment or no punishment, particularly in small groups and under high risk. However, the main outcome is group achievement, not exact efficiency, so predictions should be interpreted accordingly.

Reputation mechanisms, such as indirect reciprocity, can also sustain cooperation and do so more efficiently by avoiding these costs. When both punishment and reputation mechanisms are available, punishment is used less frequently and more selectively, and overall group efficiency is increased. The review also notes that reward mechanisms are generally less effective than punishment in sustaining cooperation. The findings are based on a synthesis of multiple experimental studies, with particular emphasis on the interaction between punishment and reputation in public goods environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper supports the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games generally increases cooperation and can improve group efficiency, especially when combined with reputation mechanisms. However, punishment alone may not always increase efficiency due to its costs. The review provides indirect evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency is moderated by the presence of reputation systems and the ability to observe or communicate about others' actions. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the efficiency gain from punishment is context-dependent and is maximized when reputation mechanisms are also present. The review does not provide new quantitative estimates but synthesizes qualitative and comparative findings from the literature.

  findings: The paper synthesizes theoretical and experimental literature on the evolution and maintenance of cooperation in humans, with a focus on public goods games and related social dilemmas. It finds that: (1) Repetition and targeted reciprocity (including punishment and reward) can promote cooperation in public goods games; (2) Both punishment and reward can increase cooperation, but reward is generally more efficient (i.e., produces higher group payoffs) than punishment, which can destroy value and provoke retaliation or antisocial punishment; (3) The effectiveness of punishment and reward depends on their implementation and the broader social context; (4) Cooperation in one-shot anonymous games is explained as an overgeneralization of strategies learned in repeated or reputation-based settings. Reward mechanisms are generally more efficient than punishment. The review does not provide quantitative data or direct predictions about efficiency changes, so it should be used for qualitative guidance: expect that punishment can promote cooperation, but its effect on efficiency is context-dependent and may be negative if punishment is misapplied. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the design of punishment (cost, effectiveness, possibility of antisocial use) and the presence of reward options are important moderators.
