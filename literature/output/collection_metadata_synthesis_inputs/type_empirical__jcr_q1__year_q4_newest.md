- source: Jiang, X., & Wang, X. H. (2024). Gender difference in leaders framed with responsibility or authority: An experimental study. *China Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that introducing a leader with the power to punish increases group contributions and efficiency compared to the no-leader baseline. When the leader's role is framed as responsibility (rather than authority), and especially when the leader is selected by voting, female leaders are more effective at increasing group income and efficiency than male leaders. The responsibility framing increases both leader contributions and the effectiveness of punishment, but the positive effect on efficiency is robustly observed only in the voting stage for female-led groups. In other conditions (authority framing, male leaders, or self-selected leaders), the efficiency gains from punishment are smaller or not significant. The study also shows that leader contributions are a more important driver of efficiency than punishment per se, as punishment can offset its own benefits through costs. Overall, the presence of punishment increases efficiency, but the magnitude depends on leader gender, framing, and selection method.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in a public good game increases efficiency, especially when the leader is female, the leadership role is framed as responsibility, and the leader is selected by voting. The game design dimensions most relevant for predicting efficiency gains from punishment are: presence of a leader with punishment power, punishment cost and effectiveness, group size (4), number of rounds (5), and information structure (no chat, summary feedback). The control (no-leader) efficiency is reported, and the treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency is higher, particularly in the responsibility/voting/female leader condition. For prediction, the paper suggests that punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when the leader is prosocial (as selected or induced by responsibility framing), and that leader contributions are a key mechanism. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with punishment, especially with comparable group size, round number, and punishment parameters.

- source: Lo Iacono, S., Przepiorka, W., Buskens, V., Corten, R., van Assen, M., & van de Rijt, A. (2023). The competitive advantage of sanctioning institutions revisited: A multilab replication. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Across seven European labs, allowing participants to choose between a sanctioning institution (SI) and a sanctioning-free institution (SFI) in a repeated public goods game leads to the majority of participants migrating to SI. In five labs, SI groups achieve significantly higher average profits and contributions than SFI groups, replicating the original findings. In the other two labs, the effect is weaker but still in the same direction. The payoff advantage of SI emerges after several rounds, as peer punishment sustains cooperation. The results are robust to variations in lab, language, and data collection method, but all parameter settings (e.g., group size, MPCR, punishment cost) are held constant as in the original study.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a 12-player, 30-round linear public goods game with endogenous institution choice, MPCR of 0.133, and a peer punishment institution with a 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio, enabling punishment leads to higher group efficiency (average profit) compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust across multiple European labs. The design dimensions are tightly controlled and fixed, so the findings are most directly applicable to similar parameterizations. The paper does not vary key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, group size), so it cannot inform how the effect would change under different settings, but it provides a strong baseline for the canonical design.

- source: Bahbouhi, J. E., Bouderba, S. I., Elkouay, A., & Moussa, N. (2024). Effects of individual and collective decision rule on cooperation in public goods game. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing individuals and teams (majority and unanimity rule) in a repeated public goods game, teams with unanimity rule contributed more and achieved higher net profits than individuals in the absence of punishment. When punishment was introduced, all groups increased contributions, but teams (especially unanimity teams) used punishment less, particularly anti-social punishment. As a result, teams—especially unanimity teams—achieved higher net profits (efficiency) than individuals. The reduction in punishment was due to both a lower probability of punishing and a lower level of punishment when it occurred. The team decision process, especially under unanimity, filtered out extreme punishment preferences, reducing destructive punishment and increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency (net profits) for all groups, but the increase is larger for teams using unanimity rule than for individuals. The efficiency gain is due to both higher contributions and less costly punishment, especially less anti-social punishment. The game design dimensions—such as group size (individuals vs. 3-person teams), number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, absence of chat, and feedback—are all clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The findings suggest that, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, team decision rules (especially unanimity) are important moderators: unanimity teams achieve higher efficiency with punishment than individuals, mainly by reducing wasteful punishment. The control (no-punishment) efficiency is also higher for unanimity teams. Thus, for similar designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency, especially for teams with strong coordination requirements.

- source: Salahshour, M., Oberhauser, V., & Smerlak, M. (2022). The cost of noise: Stochastic punishment falls short of sustaining cooperation in social dilemma experiments. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stochastic (noisy) punishment in a repeated public goods game significantly reduces group efficiency and welfare compared to deterministic punishment. As the noise in the punishment multiplier increases (while keeping the mean constant), average payoffs per round drop sharply (from 10.1 MU in control to 5.6 MU in high noise), contributions decrease, and both the frequency and intensity of punishment rise. Notably, antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) becomes more prevalent under noise, further undermining cooperation and group payoffs. Regression analyses confirm that the negative effect of noise on payoffs is mediated by increased antisocial punishment and reduced contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with punishment, introducing noise into the punishment technology (i.e., making the punishment impact stochastic rather than deterministic) leads to a substantial and monotonic reduction in efficiency, even when the mean punishment impact is held constant. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and degree of punishment noise is a critical moderator: efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much lower (or even negative) when punishment is noisy. The findings are directly relevant for settings with deterministic punishment (high efficiency) versus noisy punishment (low efficiency), holding other design dimensions constant. The results also highlight the importance of antisocial punishment as a mechanism for efficiency loss under noisy punishment.

- source: Molenmaker, W. E., Gross, J., de Kwaadsteniet, E. W., van Dijk, E., & de Dreu, C. K. W. (2023). Discriminatory punishment undermines the enforcement of group cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, punishment opportunities increased group contributions and group wealth in uniform groups (where all members shared the same academic background). In pluriform groups (mixed academic backgrounds), punishment was applied more to dissimilar others regardless of their contribution, leading to discriminatory punishment. As a result, punishment failed to increase cooperation or group wealth in pluriform groups. The paper provides direct evidence that the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency depends on group structure: it is effective in uniform groups but not in pluriform groups, where it can even undermine group outcomes due to discrimination.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is moderated by group structure. When groups are uniform, punishment increases efficiency (group wealth, public good provision). When groups are pluriform (members differ in salient characteristics), punishment is less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency, due to discriminatory punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of group heterogeneity (pluriformity) should be considered a key moderator: control efficiency is not a sufficient predictor of treatment efficiency unless group structure is accounted for. The game design dimensions most relevant here are group composition (uniform vs pluriform), punishment cost/tech, and information about group member identity.

- source: Krügel, J. P., & Maaser, N. (2025). How do higher-order punishment institutions shape cooperation and norm-enforcement?. *Review of International Organizations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares three conditions in a repeated linear public goods game with third-party punishment: Baseline (third party can punish), Fourth Party (third party can be sanctioned by an external observer), and Competition (two third-party candidates compete for selection by proposing punishment plans). Both Fourth Party and Competition increase average contributions and group earnings relative to Baseline. Fourth Party leads to the highest contributions and most severe/widespread punishment, but Competition achieves the highest average earnings for public good players, as punishment is more targeted and less frequent. Regression analyses confirm that both higher-order institutions significantly increase earnings and contributions, with Competition outperforming Fourth Party in terms of player payoffs. The results are robust across all periods and when focusing on later periods only.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment (and higher-order punishment institutions) on efficiency in public goods games. The experimental design is a standard repeated linear PGG with three players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full feedback. The Baseline (punishment-only) condition provides a control for efficiency, and the two treatments show how additional institutional features (Fourth Party monitoring, Competition) further increase efficiency. The findings support the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency, and that the design of the punishment institution (especially targeted, competitive punishment) can further enhance efficiency by reducing unnecessary punishment. The paper's detailed reporting of payoffs, contributions, and punishment allows for quantitative calibration of efficiency effects under different institutional designs.

- source: Gross, J., De Dreu, C. K. W., & Reddmann, L. (2022). Shadow of conflict: How past conflict influences group cooperation and the use of punishment. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a lab experiment with groups of four, participants either experienced an asymmetric intergroup conflict (attacker-defender game) or not, and then played a repeated public goods game with peer punishment. The main findings are: (1) Prior conflict does not significantly reduce average cooperation rates, but does reduce group earnings (efficiency) due to less effective punishment. (2) When previous conflict roles are identifiable, punishment is less likely to be targeted at free-riders and is less effective at increasing cooperation, especially when administered by previous attackers. (3) Hiding previous conflict roles (resetting group tags) increases both cooperation and group earnings over time, and punishment regains its effectiveness. (4) The negative effect of conflict on efficiency is not due to lower cooperation per se, but to maladaptive punishment patterns that reduce the welfare gains from cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated public goods game with peer punishment, the presence of a prior asymmetric conflict (with identifiable roles) reduces group efficiency (earnings) even if average cooperation rates are not significantly lower. The reduction in efficiency is due to less effective punishment: free-riders are punished less, and punishment by previous attackers is less effective. If prior conflict roles are hidden, efficiency increases and punishment regains its effectiveness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is moderated by the social context—specifically, whether there is a 'shadow of conflict' and whether past roles are identifiable. In standard PGG designs without such conflict history, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, identifiability) are all well specified, and the control efficiency is directly comparable.

- source: Duell, D., Mengel, F., Mohlin, E., & Weidenholzer, S. (2024). Cooperation through collective punishment and participation. *Political Science Research and Methods*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring (noisy signals about individual contributions, but perfect information about total group contribution), the authors experimentally compare five regimes: no punishment, peer-to-peer punishment, collective punishment, peer-to-peer punishment with commitment, and collective punishment with commitment. The key finding is that only collective punishment with commitment (where group members can opt in to a regime that automatically punishes the whole group if total contributions fall short) leads to significantly higher group payoffs and cooperation rates compared to both no punishment and peer-to-peer punishment. Peer-to-peer punishment does not improve payoffs or cooperation relative to no punishment in this noisy environment. The positive effect of collective punishment with commitment is especially pronounced in the later rounds, suggesting a learning or coordination effect. The study also shows that the ability to participate in the decision to implement collective punishment (i.e., voting/commitment) is crucial for its effectiveness.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling collective punishment with commitment in a repeated public goods game with imperfect monitoring. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known, enabling collective punishment with commitment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff) substantially, especially over time, provided the punishment is collective, pre-committed, and participation in the institution is possible. The effect is not observed for peer-to-peer punishment or for collective punishment without commitment. The experimental design and reporting allow for direct mapping to prediction tasks involving the effect of punishment on efficiency, given the game design dimensions.

- source: Chen, J. C., D'Attoma, J., Fonseca, M. A., & Malezieux, A. (2025). Promoting Cooperation in an Unequal World: Experimental Evidence on the Role of Transparency and Punishment. *Journal of Business Ethics*.

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across two lab experiments using a four-player linear public goods game with unequal endowments (determined by merit and luck), the paper finds that: (1) Peer punishment increases contributions and group payoffs, but only when endowments are transparent. (2) When endowments are secret, punishment has a limited effect on cooperation and does not reduce inequality; advantaged participants contribute a lower fraction of their endowment and earn much higher payoffs. (3) When endowments are made public, punishment becomes much more effective at increasing contributions and reducing inequality, especially among the advantaged. (4) Willingness to pay for secrecy is higher among advantaged participants, especially when their advantage is due to luck.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly dependent on whether endowments (wealth) are transparent. In games with secret endowments, punishment has little effect on group payoff or efficiency; in games with public endowments, punishment significantly increases contributions and group payoffs, especially among the advantaged. For prediction, the presence of transparency (showing endowments or relevant summaries) is a key moderator: punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when transparency is high. The game uses continuous contributions, no chat, and a fixed MPCR of 0.4. Punishment is costly (cost 1, impact 3), and there is no reward option. The findings are most directly applicable to games with similar design features.
