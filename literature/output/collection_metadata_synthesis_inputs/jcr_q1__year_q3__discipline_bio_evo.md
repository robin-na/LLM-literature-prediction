- source: Dong, Y. L., Sasaki, T., & Zhang, B. Y. (2019). The competitive advantage of institutional reward. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional incentives, institutional reward is generally more efficient (i.e., leads to higher group average fitness) than institutional punishment, especially when players are boundedly rational and make decision errors. Without errors, a mixture of reward and punishment can be optimal for intermediate incentive sizes, but with intermediate or large decision errors, punishment almost always reduces group welfare, and reward is the most efficient. These results are robust across a range of game parameters and model variants.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment tends to reduce efficiency compared to reward, especially when decision errors are present. If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already low, adding institutional punishment is unlikely to improve efficiency and may worsen it, unless the system is nearly error-free and incentives are finely tuned. The findings suggest that institutional reward is a safer and more robust mechanism for improving efficiency than punishment.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Podder, S., Righi, S., & Pancotto, F. (2021). Reputation and punishment sustain cooperation in the optional public goods game. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the OPGG, punishment alone (including anti-social punishment) does not increase cooperation or group fitness, and can even reduce them by increasing the prevalence of loners. Reputation alone can increase cooperation and fitness, but only under specific social norms (e.g., when defectors are assigned a strictly worse reputation than loners). The combination of reputation and punishment leads to the highest levels of cooperation and population fitness, with most agents adopting conditional cooperation strategies and rarely using punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size, synergy factor, punishment cost and effectiveness, and other parameters. The findings suggest that reputation and punishment interact synergistically to sustain cooperation and improve group outcomes, but that reputation can substitute for costly punishment in many cases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in optional public goods games, the addition of punishment alone (especially when anti-social punishment is possible) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and may even reduce it. However, when reputation mechanisms are also present—especially those that allow agents to distinguish defectors from loners—punishment and reputation together can synergistically increase cooperation and group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the presence and nature of reputation mechanisms and social norms. In the absence of reputation, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency; with reputation, especially under certain norms, enabling punishment can have a more positive effect. The model's robustness checks indicate these findings are not highly sensitive to group size, rounds, or punishment cost within the tested ranges.

- source: Molleman, L., Kölle, F., Starmer, C., & Gächter, S. (2019). People prefer coordinated punishment in cooperative interactions. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiments show that in a one-shot, three-player binary public goods game with a costly punishment stage, a substantial fraction of participants prefer to punish free riders only if another group member also punishes (coordinated punishment). This preference is especially prevalent among those who do not punish unconditionally. The presence of coordinated punishment is robust across two large samples and is not correlated with conditional cooperation preferences. The study provides detailed behavioral evidence on how punishment decisions are conditioned on others' actions, but does not report on group efficiency or total payoffs with or without punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative about the behavioral mechanisms of punishment in public goods games, especially the prevalence and motivations for coordinated punishment. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on group efficiency or total payoff, nor does it compare efficiency between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper suggests that the structure of punishment (e.g., whether it is coordinated) may matter, but does not provide quantitative or comparative efficiency data. The game design dimensions are well specified, which may help in mapping to other studies that do report efficiency outcomes.

- source: Fehr, E., & Schurtenberger, I. (2018). Normative foundations of human cooperation. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that social norms, particularly conditional cooperation norms, are central to explaining cooperation in public goods games. Punishment opportunities increase cooperation rates, but the welfare (efficiency) effects are often limited or negative due to the costs of punishment and the occurrence of antisocial punishment. Normative constraints on punishment—such as those enabled by institutional or group-level norm formation—can reduce antisocial punishment and make punishment more effective, leading to higher cooperation with less collateral cost. The review emphasizes that the presence of punishment alone is not sufficient for efficiency gains; the structure and normative context of punishment matter greatly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and integrative evidence that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation rates, but does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff unless punishment is normatively constrained to reduce antisocial punishment and collateral costs. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the presence of normative or institutional constraints, the prevalence of antisocial punishment, and the cultural context. Game design dimensions that affect the cost, targeting, and normative context of punishment are likely to be critical moderators of efficiency outcomes. The paper does not provide direct quantitative estimates of efficiency changes, but it cautions against assuming that increased cooperation from punishment always translates into higher efficiency.

- source: Murase, Y., & Baek, S. K. (2021). Friendly-rivalry solution to the iterated n-person public-goods game. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the CAPRI-n strategy achieves full cooperation and maximal efficiency in the iterated n-person public-goods game, even with implementation errors. When all players use CAPRI-n, the group achieves the cooperative payoff in the limit of small error probability. The strategy is defensible (cannot be exploited), efficient (achieves full cooperation), and distinguishable (can exploit unconditional cooperators). Evolutionary simulations show that CAPRI-n dominates the population across a wide range of benefit-to-cost ratios and population sizes, outperforming other memory-one strategies. The results are robust for n=2 and 3, and supported by Monte Carlo simulation for n=4.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public-goods games with error, a well-designed punishment/reciprocity strategy (CAPRI-n) can achieve near-maximal efficiency, even as group size increases. The findings suggest that, when the game allows for memory-based contingent punishment and recovery, enabling such mechanisms can dramatically increase efficiency relative to baseline. The results are most directly applicable to settings with continuous contributions, no explicit punishment cost, and no external punishment/reward mechanism, but where strategies can implement contingent retaliation. The paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency in repeated PGGs with endogenous, strategy-based punishment/reciprocity, especially as group size increases.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of cooperation in costly institutions exhibits Red Queen and Black Queen dynamics in heterogeneous public goods. *Communications Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr
  findings: The model demonstrates that introducing a costly institution (with a participation fee) alongside a free institution in a public goods game can increase cooperation and group efficiency. For low enhancement factors (low profitability), only the costly institution supports cooperation, and average payoffs remain close to the entrance cost (i.e., not much higher than the baseline). For higher enhancement factors, cooperation emerges in both institutions, and group payoffs increase above zero. The costly institution is more likely to be populated by cooperators, as defectors avoid the entrance cost unless there are enough cooperators to exploit. The model also shows that the effect of the entrance cost is context-dependent: it is beneficial for cooperation and efficiency at low profitability, but can be detrimental at high profitability if the cost is too high, as it reduces participation. The results hold in both well-mixed and structured populations, though spatial structure removes some bistability and further supports cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that introducing a costly institution (entrance fee) in a public goods game can increase efficiency and group payoff, especially when the baseline profitability (mpcr/enhancement factor) is low. The entrance cost acts as a filter, keeping defectors out and allowing cooperators to achieve higher payoffs. For prediction tasks, the presence of a costly institution (or, by analogy, a punishment mechanism with a cost to participate) should be expected to increase efficiency relative to a control with no such cost, particularly when the marginal per-capita return is low. However, if the cost is too high and the game is already highly profitable, the effect may reverse. The model does not include explicit punishment, but the costly institution's effect is adjacent to punishment in that it deters free-riding through a cost barrier. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where institutional participation costs or costly precommitment mechanisms are present, rather than direct peer punishment.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & D'Amico, V. (2021). A preference to learn from successful rather than common behaviours in human social dilemmas. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Participants in a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.4, groups of 4, 6 rounds) were more motivated to learn from and copy successful (high-earning) behaviors than to conform to common behaviors. When shown both the average (common) contribution and the average contribution of the highest earners from a previous experiment, participants contributed significantly less than when shown only the common behavior. This decline in cooperation was not explained by simply knowing about free riders, but specifically by the desire to learn from success. Most participants preferred to see information about successful behaviors over common behaviors. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, but the lower contributions in the 'success' information condition imply lower efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games without punishment or reward, providing information about successful (high-earning) behaviors leads to lower cooperation and thus likely lower efficiency, compared to providing only information about common behaviors. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of success-based social information (even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward) can undermine efficiency. However, the study does not manipulate or analyze punishment directly, and so its direct relevance to predicting the effect of enabling punishment is limited. The findings are most relevant for understanding the role of social information and learning in public goods games, and suggest that efficiency may be lower when participants can observe and learn from successful free-riding.

Gossip is especially important when direct observation of others' behavior is limited, as it enables vicarious learning and helps participants adjust their own contributions based on information received from others. Gossip also increases social bonding and alignment of impressions between conversation partners. However, it does not address the effects of punishment or sanctions, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on punishment effects or efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it suggests that social information transmission (gossip) can substitute for formal sanctions in sustaining cooperation, but does not provide quantitative estimates for efficiency outcomes or for games with punishment enabled. The design dimensions most directly addressed are player count, number of rounds, chat/communication, and information visibility. The absence of punishment and efficiency outcomes limits its direct applicability to the prediction task.

- source: Alvarez-Rodriguez, U., Battiston, F., de Arruda, G. F., Moreno, Y., Perc, M., & Latora, V. (2021). Evolutionary dynamics of higher-order interactions in social networks. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper shows that in PGGs with higher-order interactions (hypergraphs), the critical synergy factor required for cooperation decreases as group size increases in uniform random hypergraphs, meaning larger groups can foster cooperation more easily (at a given synergy factor). Heterogeneity in group membership (hyperdegree) and group size can either facilitate or hinder cooperation, depending on the structure: scale-free (hub-dominated) hypergraphs inhibit cooperation, while uniform or less heterogeneous structures do not. The framework is validated with empirical data from scientific collaborations, showing that the synergy factor (group benefit) is a non-monotonic function of group size, with an optimal group size for maximum benefit. The paper does not study punishment, rewards, or sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting efficiency and payoff outcomes in PGG-like environments with varying group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but it does not provide any evidence about the effects of punishment or sanctions. It is directly relevant for understanding how group size and network heterogeneity affect the critical conditions for cooperation and group payoff, but cannot inform predictions about the effect of enabling punishment. Use this paper to inform baseline efficiency predictions in control (no-punishment) conditions as a function of group size, group structure, and synergy factor, but not for treatment effects of punishment.

- source: Wegmann, J., & Musshoff, O. (2019). Groundwater management institutions in the face of rapid urbanization - Results of a framed field experiment in Bengaluru, India. *Ecological Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that both externally imposed punishment and reward rules, implemented via random monitoring and sanctions/rewards for exceeding/following optimal extraction, significantly reduce over-extraction of groundwater in a framed field experiment with 600 real-world users. Social efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) increased from 86% in the control to 97% under punishment and 95% under reward, with welfare gains of 8% and 6% over control, respectively (both statistically significant at 1%). Communication alone had a much smaller effect (90% efficiency, 1% gain, not significant). The effect of punishment and reward was not significantly different between rural and urban areas. User types (myopic, rational, social optimal) persisted across treatments, but all treatments crowded out the most pro-social (social optimal) users to some extent. The main outcome is that externally imposed monitoring and sanctioning (punishment) is highly effective at increasing efficiency in this CPR context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong, directly relevant evidence that, in a dynamic CPR game with real-world users, adding an externally imposed punishment mechanism (random monitoring, monetary penalty for over-extraction) increases group efficiency (payoff/welfare) by a substantial and statistically significant margin compared to a control with no punishment. The effect is robust to user type and urbanization. The game is not a standard linear PGG but is a close CPR variant with continuous extraction, group interdependence, and profit-based payoffs. The punishment cost and technology are specified, and the efficiency gains are quantified. This evidence should increase predicted efficiency for similar games when punishment is enabled, especially when the control efficiency is below the social optimum.

- source: Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2018). Punishment and inspection for governing the commons in a feedback-evolving game. *Plos Computational Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment and inspection mechanisms can promote cooperation and prevent overexploitation in a common-pool resource game, but their ability to improve group efficiency (resource sustainability) depends critically on the intrinsic growth rate of the resource. If the resource renews too slowly, even perfect cooperation enforced by strong punishment leads to resource depletion and low efficiency. At moderate growth rates, strong punishment and inspection can achieve both high cooperation and sustainable resource levels, but weak institutions result in mixed strategy populations and depleted resources. At high growth rates, the resource is always sustainable regardless of punishment, and punishment only affects the fraction of cooperators versus defectors. Thus, the efficiency gain from punishment is context-dependent and can be absent or even negative if resource dynamics are unfavorable.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like common-pool resource games is highly contingent on the ecological context, especially the resource's intrinsic growth rate. When predicting the efficiency impact of punishment, it is crucial to consider not only the game design dimensions (such as punishment cost and effectiveness) but also the underlying dynamics of the resource. In settings where the resource cannot recover quickly, punishment may not improve efficiency even if it increases cooperation. In more favorable ecological conditions, punishment can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, but only if it is sufficiently effective (high detection probability and/or fine). Thus, the paper suggests that the efficiency effect of punishment is not universal and must be interpreted in light of resource dynamics.
