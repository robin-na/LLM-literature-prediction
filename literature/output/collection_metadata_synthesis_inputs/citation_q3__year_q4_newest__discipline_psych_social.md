- source: Spadaro, G., Graf, C., Jin, S. X., Arai, S., Inoue, Y., Lieberman, E., Rinderu, M. I., Yuan, M. L., Van Lissa, C. J., & Balliet, D. (2022). Cross-Cultural Variation in Cooperation: A Meta-Analysis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The meta-analysis finds that the presence of sanctions (punishment and/or reward) in social dilemma experiments is associated with significantly higher cooperation rates. Other situational features, such as communication and lower conflict of interest, also increase cooperation. However, the outcome measured is cooperation rate, not efficiency or group payoff. No significant cross-societal or cross-cultural differences in cooperation are found after controlling for study characteristics. The effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff is not directly analyzed or reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment or reward mechanisms increases cooperation rates in PGG and PD experiments, across a wide range of societies and experimental designs. However, because the outcome is cooperation rate rather than efficiency or group payoff, the findings should be used with caution for predicting efficiency. The paper does not address whether increased cooperation from punishment leads to higher or lower efficiency, as it does not analyze payoff-based outcomes. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment increases cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence about efficiency effects.

- source: Capraro, V. (2024). The Dual-Process Approach to Human Sociality: Meta-Analytic Evidence for a Theory of Internalized Heuristics for Self-Preservation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that promoting intuitive processing (via time pressure, ego depletion, conceptual primes, etc.) increases negative reciprocity (i.e., punishment of norm violations) in economic games, including public goods games with punishment phases and ultimatum games. This is supported by a meta-analysis showing a small but significant increase in punishment/rejection rates when intuition is promoted. However, the review does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not addressed; the focus is on the cognitive basis of the decision to punish, not on the downstream consequences for group outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that intuitive processing increases the likelihood of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, but it does not provide evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency with punishment enabled), this review is not directly informative, as it does not report or synthesize efficiency or welfare outcomes. It is useful for understanding moderators of punishment behavior (e.g., cognitive manipulation, individual differences), but not for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment. Use this paper to inform models of punishment frequency or behavioral response, not efficiency.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Burgess, J. (2023). Why do climate change negotiations stall? Scientific evidence and solutions for some structural problems. *Global Discourse*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The paper finds that climate change negotiations are structurally analogous to public goods games, with similar challenges of free-riding, group formation, and the need for institutional mechanisms to foster cooperation. It reviews experimental evidence showing that the possibility of sanctioning (punishment) increases cooperation and, over time, can improve group welfare, though some studies note that punishment can be inefficient in the short term. The authors argue that the absence of enforceable sanctions in climate agreements is a key reason for stalled progress and that introducing sanctions, improving transparency, and building trust and fairness are critical for better outcomes. However, the paper does not report new empirical data or direct efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and literature-based support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases cooperation and, in the long run, can improve group welfare and efficiency, though it acknowledges that punishment can sometimes be inefficient in the short term. It highlights the importance of institutional design features such as sanctioning, transparency, and trust. However, because it does not report new empirical data or direct efficiency outcomes, it should be used as contextual and mechanistic support rather than as a source of quantitative effect sizes for efficiency prediction.

- source: Wu, J. H., Luan, S. H., & Raihani, N. (2022). Reward, punishment, and prosocial behavior: Recent developments and implications. *Current Opinion in Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech
  findings: The review finds that both reward and punishment can promote prosocial behavior in public goods games and related social dilemmas, but their effectiveness depends on context. Punishment can sometimes reduce group welfare, especially when it leads to retaliation or is applied under noisy conditions, where it may mistakenly target cooperators and harm group payoffs. Reward is often found to be at least as effective as punishment in promoting cooperation and may be more efficient, particularly under noise. The legitimacy and source of punishment (e.g., third-party or democratic punishment) can moderate its effectiveness. Theoretical models suggest that reward is important for establishing prosociality, while punishment helps maintain it once established, but this dynamic has not been directly tested in experiments. The review also notes that indirect strategies like gossip may be more efficient than punishment in some real-world settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides useful qualitative guidance for predicting the effects of punishment and reward on prosocial behavior and, to a lesser extent, on efficiency in public goods games. It suggests that punishment does not always increase efficiency and can sometimes reduce it, especially under noise or when perceived as illegitimate. Reward may be more efficient in some contexts, particularly when monitoring is imperfect. The review also highlights the importance of the structure and legitimacy of punishment mechanisms. However, because the review does not report quantitative efficiency outcomes or systematically analyze game design dimensions, its value for direct prediction of treatment efficiency is limited to qualitative boundary conditions and theoretical expectations.

- source: Han, T. A., Lenaerts, T. C., Santos, F. C., & Pereira, L. M. (2022). Voluntary safety commitments provide an escape from over-regulation in AI development. *Technology in Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in a repeated two-player (and generalizable to N-player) innovation race game, unconditional punishment of risk-taking (unsafe) behavior can reduce social welfare by over-regulating and suppressing beneficial innovation when risk is low. Introducing voluntary, binding commitments to safe behavior—where only those who commit and then defect are punished—enables the system to achieve high efficiency: safety compliance prevails when it is collectively optimal, and innovation is not stifled when risk-taking is preferred. Both peer and institutional punishment mechanisms are effective if they are targeted only at commitment violators. The model shows that the efficiency of punishment (cost to punisher vs. impact on punished) modulates these effects, but the voluntary commitment mechanism robustly improves outcomes across parameter regimes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the design of punishment mechanisms—specifically, whether punishment is unconditional or conditional on voluntary commitment—has a major impact on group efficiency in public-goods-like innovation dilemmas. For prediction tasks, the presence of voluntary, binding commitments with targeted punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to unconditional punishment, especially in environments where the collective optimum varies with risk. The model also highlights the importance of punishment cost and effectiveness, and shows that allowing players to opt out of commitment (and thus avoid punishment) can prevent over-regulation and welfare loss. These insights are directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in games with similar structure, even if the setting is not a classic PGG.

- source: Guo, Z. Y., Guo, R., Xu, C. Y., & Wu, Z. (2022). Reflexive or reflective? Group bias in third-party punishment in Chinese and Western cultures. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that both Chinese and Western participants show ingroup favoritism in third-party punishment under reflexive (fast, high cognitive load) conditions, punishing ingroup norm violators less than outgroup violators. Under reflective (slow, low cognitive load) conditions, Western participants—especially those with high group identity—reduce ingroup favoritism and may even punish ingroup violators more, while Chinese participants continue to show strong ingroup favoritism regardless of decision mode. Group identity amplifies these effects. The main outcome is the amount of punishment assigned, not efficiency or group payoff. The game is a third-party punishment mini-dictator game, not a standard public goods game, and does not report on group efficiency or welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence about how group identity, culture, and decision-making mode moderate the behavioral use of costly punishment in a third-party punishment context. However, it does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The design is adjacent to PGGs (mini-dictator/TPP), and the outcome is punishment behavior, not payoff. The findings are useful for understanding when and how punishment is used, but not for predicting efficiency outcomes.

- source: Spadaro, G., Molho, C., Van Prooijen, J. W., Romano, A., Mosso, C. O., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2023). Corrupt third parties undermine trust and prosocial behaviour between people. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across five experimental studies, participants exposed to a corrupt (dishonest) third-party punisher (TPP) trusted strangers less and cooperated less in subsequent economic games (trust game, prisoner's dilemma, dictator game) with TPP present. The effect is robust to both second-hand and first-hand exposure to corruption, and to whether the TPP's corruption harms only the experiment or also a collective good. The negative effect of perceived TPP corruption on cooperation is mediated by reduced interpersonal trust. The studies do not report group efficiency or total payoff, so the direct effect of TPP corruption on efficiency is not measured. The design always includes a TPP; there is no comparison to a no-punishment baseline, so the effect of enabling punishment per se on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the perceived integrity of a third-party punisher moderates the effectiveness of punishment institutions in promoting trust and cooperation. However, it does not provide direct evidence on the effect of enabling punishment (vs. no punishment) on group efficiency or payoff, nor does it report efficiency outcomes. The findings suggest that if punishment is administered by corrupt or untrusted third parties, its positive effect on cooperation may be undermined or even reversed, but the paper does not quantify efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper is most informative about the moderating role of institutional trustworthiness, not about the baseline effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games.

- source: Gummerum, M., López-Pérez, B., Van Dijk, E., & Van Dillen, L. F. (2022). Ire and punishment: Incidental anger and costly punishment in children, adolescents, and adults. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that experimentally induced incidental anger increases costly punishment of unfair offers in the ultimatum game (second-party punishment) for children, adolescents, and adults. In third-party punishment games, incidental anger increases punishment severity for unfair offers in adults and adolescents, but not in children. The effect of anger is specific to unfair (unequal) offers and does not increase punishment of fair (equal) offers. The main outcomes are behavioral (punishment rates and severity), not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that the motivation for costly punishment in response to unfairness is influenced by emotional state and developmental stage, with self-relevance and norm appraisals playing key roles.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or related games. Its main contribution is to show that emotional state (incidental anger) can increase costly punishment of unfairness, especially when the violation is self-relevant (second-party) or, for adults, when it violates fairness norms (third-party). For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment rates and severity can be modulated by emotional and developmental factors, but it does not inform how these changes in punishment behavior translate into changes in efficiency or group payoff. The game designs used (ultimatum and third-party punishment games) are adjacent to, but not the same as, standard public goods games, and no efficiency or welfare outcomes are reported.

- source: Fitouchi, L., & Singh, M. (2023). Punitive justice serves to restore reciprocal cooperation in three small-scale societies. *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: Across three small-scale societies, punitive justice is primarily restorative and dyadic, not group-norm-enforcing. Third-party punishment is rare, and most interventions are aimed at mediating disputes and restoring relationships. Compensation to victims is common and often proportional to harm, especially among the Mentawai and Nuer. Kinship and interdependence reduce the likelihood or magnitude of punishment/compensation. When costs are not imposed on offenders, cooperation often breaks down and feuding may result. The paper does not use public goods games or measure efficiency, but provides evidence that punishment and compensation are more about restoring dyadic cooperation than increasing group-level efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence about the function of punishment in environments that are adjacent to public goods games: real-world small-scale societies with reciprocal obligations and norm violations. It suggests that, in such contexts, punishment is not primarily about increasing group efficiency or enforcing group-level cooperation, but about restoring dyadic relationships and preventing escalation. For prediction tasks involving efficiency in public goods games, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment may depend on whether the context is more about dyadic reciprocity or group-level cooperation. However, because the paper does not use experimental PGGs or report efficiency or payoff outcomes, its direct predictive value for efficiency outcomes in PGGs is limited.

- source: Dimant, E., & Gesche, T. (2023). Nudging enforcers: how norm perceptions and motives for lying shape sanctions. *Pnas Nexus*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that third-party punishment increases with the size of the lie and is harsher when the lie increases inequity or is for personal gain, compared to when it restores equity. Norm-nudges (empirical or normative information about others' behavior or approval) increase punishment, especially when preexisting norms are ambiguous. The effect of norm-nudges is strongest in scenarios where the social norm is less clear. These findings are robust across a behavioral experiment, a vignette experiment, and a norm elicitation study. However, the study does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality evidence on how punishment behavior is shaped by norm-nudges and the context of norm violations, but it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency in public-goods-like games, this paper is only indirectly informative: it shows that punishment is sensitive to context and can be increased by norm-nudges, especially when norms are ambiguous. However, it does not provide evidence on whether or how punishment affects group efficiency or welfare, nor does it use a public goods game structure. Thus, it should be used as indirect evidence about the determinants of punishment behavior, not about efficiency effects.

- source: Romano, A., Saral, A. S., & Wu, J. H. (2022). Direct and indirect reciprocity among individuals and groups. *Current Opinion in Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that group membership, gossip, and third-party punishment promote prosocial behavior through psychological mechanisms related to direct and indirect reciprocity. Third-party punishment is framed as a tool for upregulating reputation and deterring mistreatment, which in turn increases prosocial behavior among others. The review highlights that reputational concern and anticipation of future interaction are key drivers of both prosocial and punitive behaviors. However, the review does not report or synthesize results on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and focuses on behavioral and psychological outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and conceptual support for the idea that punishment (especially third-party punishment) can increase prosocial behavior, but it does not provide quantitative or direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games or their variants. It is useful for understanding mechanisms and moderators (e.g., reputation, observability, group membership), but does not inform direct prediction of efficiency outcomes from game design dimensions. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is best used as background on psychological mechanisms and as a pointer to empirical work, rather than as a source of quantitative or design-specific evidence.

- source: Nunney, S. J., van der Schalk, J., & Manstead, A. S. R. (2022). Emotion and intergroup cooperation: How verbal expressions of guilt, shame, and pride influence behavior in a social dilemma. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: Across two lab experiments using an intergroup centipede game, the authors find that when a group expresses shame (or guilt) after behaving uncooperatively, the opposing group is more likely to cooperate in subsequent rounds, resulting in higher group payoffs (tickets won) and more prosocial behavior. In contrast, when a group expresses pride after uncooperative behavior, the opposing group is less likely to cooperate, resulting in lower payoffs and more costly punishment. The effect of emotion expression is causal (Study 2) and operates above and beyond the effect of prior behavior. The study does not use a standard public goods game or implement a formal punishment stage as in PGGs, but does include a costly punishment measure in Study 2.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that communication—specifically, the expression of self-conscious emotions—can significantly moderate the effect of prior uncooperative behavior on subsequent group efficiency in repeated, sequential social dilemmas. While not a standard PGG, the centipede game shares key features (sequential cooperation, group payoffs, potential for exploitation). The findings suggest that, in environments where communication is possible, expressions of shame or guilt can substitute for or augment formal punishment mechanisms in restoring cooperation and efficiency, while pride expressions can undermine them. However, the study does not manipulate punishment cost, technology, or other standard PGG design dimensions, so its direct predictive value for punishment-enabled PGG efficiency is limited. It does, however, highlight the importance of communication and emotional signaling as moderators of efficiency outcomes.

- source: De Dreu, C. K. W., Gross, J., & Romano, A. (2024). Group Formation and the Evolution of Human Social Organization. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper proposes that group formation and the evolution of complex social organization are driven by fitness interdependence, reciprocity, and reputation-based partner selection. It argues that as groups grow, informal mechanisms (e.g., punishment, gossip, reward) and later formal institutions (e.g., laws, leadership) emerge to manage collective action problems and sustain cooperation. Theoretical and cited empirical work suggest that such mechanisms make cooperation more attractive and free riding more costly, but the paper does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff. The discussion is grounded in public goods and club goods dilemmas, but the focus is on behavioral and institutional mechanisms rather than direct payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical context for how punishment and other social institutions may support cooperation in public goods and club goods environments, especially as group size increases. However, it does not provide direct empirical evidence or quantitative estimates of the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff. For prediction tasks, this paper may inform mechanistic expectations (e.g., that punishment can support cooperation and group functioning), but it does not provide parameter values or direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency under specific game design dimensions. It is best used for contextual or background understanding rather than direct prediction.

- source: Singh, M., & Garfield, Z. H. (2022). Evidence for third-party mediation but not punishment in Mentawai justice. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat, punishment_cost, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that, in the Mentawai context, third-party punishment is absent: no cases were found where third parties (outside the victim, their close kin, or spouse) directly punished or demanded penalties from transgressors. Indirect or second-order sanctions by third parties are also not observed. Instead, penalties (tulou) are negotiated and paid primarily to restore dyadic cooperation, with kinship playing a major role in the likelihood and size of payments. Mediation by third parties (especially shamans and elders) is common, particularly in more severe or between-clan disputes, but the mediator's role is to facilitate negotiation, not to enforce punishment. The study suggests that, in this small-scale society, institutionalized penalties serve to repair relationships rather than to enforce group-level norms through punishment. The findings are based on retrospective interviews and ethnographic observation of 302 reliable cases of transgression and response.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides little direct support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The studied context is not a PGG or close variant, and the outcomes are not efficiency or group payoff. The findings suggest that, in small-scale, kin-based societies, third-party punishment is rare or absent, and penalties serve to restore dyadic cooperation rather than to enforce group norms or increase group efficiency. If extrapolating to PGG-like settings, the results would suggest that the presence or absence of third-party punishment may depend on the social structure and the nature of cooperation (private vs. public goods), but the paper does not provide quantitative or experimental evidence on efficiency outcomes. Thus, it is of limited use for the downstream prediction task, except as a caution that punishment mechanisms may not always function as assumed in small-scale, kin-based societies.

- source: Balliet, D., Molho, C., Columbus, S., & Cruz, T. D. D. (2022). Prosocial and punishment behaviors in everyday life. *Current Opinion in Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that prosocial and punishment behaviors in daily life are highly context-dependent, varying with relationship quality, power dynamics, and the nature of the interaction. Direct punishment (as seen in lab games) is rare in daily life, especially among strangers; indirect forms like gossip and avoidance are more common. The review emphasizes that laboratory paradigms (such as public goods games with punishment) capture only a small subset of real-world social behavior, and that field methods reveal a broader range of strategies and contexts. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or related outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar laboratory environments. It is primarily a theoretical and methodological review, arguing that lab-based findings may not generalize to real-world contexts. The paper does not report or synthesize efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate or analyze the game design dimensions relevant to the prediction task. Its main value is in highlighting the limitations of laboratory paradigms and the importance of context, but it does not inform quantitative prediction of efficiency effects from punishment in controlled games.
