- source: Spitzer, M. L. (2016). Preferences over Punishment and Reward Mechanisms in Social Dilemmas. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper does not present new empirical findings but critiques the interpretability of Kocher and Matzat's experimental results. It notes that subjects' preferences over punishment and reward mechanisms are difficult to interpret due to the multiplicity of possible utility functions and equilibria. The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. The author suggests that simpler experimental designs would make it easier to interpret the effects of institutional choices on cooperation and contributions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-good games. However, it offers important cautionary notes: (1) the effect of punishment on cooperation and efficiency is highly sensitive to the details of the punishment mechanism (e.g., cost, impact, available choices); (2) subject beliefs and the complexity of institutional choice can obscure the relationship between mechanism and outcome; (3) the presence of multiple equilibria under different utility functions makes it difficult to infer efficiency effects from observed preferences or voting patterns. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

- source: Ogaki, M., & Tanaka, S. C. (2017). Social Preferences. *Behavioral Economics: Toward a New Economics by Integration with Traditional Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The chapter reviews evidence that, in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment opportunities (as in Fehr and Gächter 2000) leads to higher contribution rates and prevents the typical decay of cooperation seen in repeated games without punishment. It explains these results using models of social preferences, especially inequality aversion. However, the chapter does not report or analyze efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes directly. The main findings are that punishment increases cooperation (contribution rates) and that social preference models can explain both altruistic and selfish behaviors depending on the context (e.g., presence of competition).
  prediction_guidance: This chapter provides indirect support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases cooperation, which is often associated with higher efficiency, but it does not provide direct evidence or quantitative estimates of efficiency or group payoff. For the downstream prediction task, the chapter supports the qualitative expectation that punishment increases cooperation, but does not allow for precise prediction of efficiency changes. The evidence is most useful for understanding mechanisms and for qualitative directionality, not for quantitative prediction.

- source: Hugh-Jones, D., & Perroni, C. (2017). The logic of costly punishment reversed: Expropriation of free-riders and outsiders. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=close | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that the threat of group expropriation (a form of punishment) can increase public goods contributions, but only when the expropriation mechanism is sufficiently difficult to coordinate (i.e., requires a large group to act together). When expropriation is too easy (requires only one or two people), it is less targeted at free-riders, more arbitrary, and leads to lower contributions and more frequent expropriation, reducing efficiency. In the baseline (punishment enabled, M=3), contributions are significantly higher than in the control (no expropriation), and expropriation is mostly targeted at unique lowest contributors. When the minimum group size for expropriation is reduced (M=2 or M=1), expropriation increases and contributions fall. In heterogeneous groups, minorities are more likely to be targeted in some treatments, especially when ambiguity or a history of targeting exists, and may respond less to punishment. However, in the main treatments, overall group efficiency is not significantly different between homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. The findings suggest that the design of the punishment mechanism (especially the ease of coordination required for punishment) is a critical moderator of its effect on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the punishment technology, specifically the minimum group size required for successful punishment (expropriation). When punishment is difficult to coordinate (higher M), it is more likely to be used as a credible threat against free-riders, increasing contributions and efficiency. When punishment is too easy (low M), it is used more arbitrarily, contributions fall, and efficiency is reduced. For prediction, the dimension 'punishment_tech' (minimum group size for punishment, or ease of expropriation) is a key moderator: higher coordination requirements for punishment (higher M) are associated with more positive effects of punishment on efficiency, while lower requirements (lower M) can make punishment detrimental. Control efficiency (without punishment) is much lower than with well-targeted, hard-to-coordinate punishment. Heterogeneity (minority status) can moderate targeting and behavioral response, but does not robustly reduce efficiency unless ambiguity or a history of targeting is present. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially punishment_tech, and provide indirect evidence for the importance of targeting and coordination in punishment mechanisms.

- source: Otto, P. E., & Bolle, F. (2016). The advantage of hierarchy: Inducing responsibility and selecting ability?. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment compares three regimes: standard PGG (voluntary contributions), AG (randomly assigned allocator determines all contributions), and PAG (principal selects allocator each round). Both AG and PAG significantly increase group production and average incomes relative to PGG. PAG (principal selection) yields the highest efficiency, with further increases in satisfaction and fairness ratings. The effect is not due to learning or order effects. The mechanism works by centralizing responsibility and leveraging selection, not by explicit punishment. In treatments with heterogeneous productivity, the most productive worker is more likely to be appointed allocator, but does not exploit the position to reduce own effort. The findings show that hierarchy can substitute for punishment in sustaining high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing hierarchical control (an allocator or principal-allocator structure) in a public goods game can substantially increase efficiency, even in the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a central authority who can determine or influence contributions should be expected to yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those typically attributed to punishment. The results are robust across group size (n=3), number of rounds (10 per regime), and both homogeneous and heterogeneous productivity. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is not available but hierarchy is, efficiency can be high. For prediction, if a game design includes a mechanism where one player can set others' contributions (with or without selection by a principal), expect efficiency to be much higher than in standard PGG, even if punishment is absent.

- source: Zhosan, D., & Gardner, R. (2013). Problems of the commons: group behavior, cooperation and sanctioning in a two-harbor experiment. *Environment and Development Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a laboratory experiment simulating a two-commons (two-harbor) resource dilemma, groups of 8 participants (students and professional fishermen) repeatedly chose how to allocate effort between two commons and a safe alternative. The experiment varied institutional features across blocks: baseline (no restrictions), enforcement (costly movement between commons), communication, and communication plus sanctioning. Efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) was the primary outcome. Communication led to large, significant increases in efficiency for all groups, with average net efficiency rising from ~0.8 to ~0.98. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings, then monetary penalties for repeated defection) further increased efficiency in most groups, sometimes reaching 1.0. The effect of sanctioning was generally positive but smaller than the effect of communication, and in one group, sanctioning led to a breakdown in cooperation. The results show that communication and sanctioning can both be effective in increasing efficiency in commons dilemmas, with communication having the largest and most consistent effect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling communication among group members in a commons dilemma can dramatically increase efficiency, even before any formal or monetary sanctioning is introduced. The addition of a sanctioning mechanism (warnings and monetary penalties) yields further, though more modest, efficiency gains. For prediction tasks, the presence of communication and/or sanctioning should be expected to increase efficiency substantially relative to a control with neither. The effect of communication is robust and large; the effect of sanctioning is positive but may be context-dependent and sometimes less pronounced. The experiment's design and reporting allow for direct mapping to game design dimensions relevant for prediction.

  findings: The endogenous-audit (collective punishment) treatment produces much higher compliance rates than both the no-audit and random-audit treatments. In the final 10 rounds, compliance rates are 0.297 (no audit), 0.758 (random audit), and 0.921 (endogenous audit). However, average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment (85.2 points) than in the random-audit treatment (92.1 points), due to the distribution of fines and discovered taxes. Audit productivity (e.g., reported tax per audit) is higher in the endogenous-audit treatment in the latter half of the game. The main effect of punishment is to increase compliance, but the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous due to the cost of monitoring and the way fines are pooled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling a collective punishment mechanism (endogenous audit) in a public-goods-like tax game increases compliance rates compared to both no-punishment and exogenous punishment (random audit) conditions. However, the effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to the full cooperation benchmark) is not directly reported and may be ambiguous, as average earnings are lower in the endogenous-audit treatment than in the random-audit treatment. For prediction tasks, this suggests that punishment can increase compliance but may not always increase efficiency, depending on the cost structure and how punishment/fines are redistributed. The game design dimensions (player count, rounds, audit cost structure, etc.) are well specified and can be mapped to the prediction task, but the lack of direct efficiency analysis is a limitation.

- source: Gaudeul, A., Crosetto, P., & Riener, G. (2017). Better stuck together or free to go? Of the stability of cooperation when individuals have outside options. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment varied the ease of exit from a public project (barriers to exit) in a repeated, two-player, stochastic public-good-like game. When exit was easier (lower barriers), participants exited more frequently, but this did not increase efficiency or average payoffs. In fact, average individual welfare was lower in treatments with lower barriers to exit. The main reason was excessive exit, driven by pessimistic beliefs about the peer's likelihood of leaving, rather than accurate cost-benefit analysis. The study finds that exit is used both as a response to perceived exploitation and as a punishment for failure, but these mechanisms do not improve group efficiency. The findings suggest that making exit easier undermines group stability and reduces welfare, contrary to some theoretical expectations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public-good-like environments with imperfect monitoring and stochastic outcomes, making exit easier (i.e., lowering barriers to exit) reduces efficiency and average payoffs. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of exit options (a form of sanction or punishment via exclusion/self-exclusion) on efficiency. The results suggest that, unless exit is tightly controlled or costly, its availability will likely decrease group welfare due to excessive and inefficient use. For prediction tasks, the presence and ease of exit should be treated as a negative moderator of efficiency, even when the control (no-exit) game already has moderate efficiency. The study does not test classic costly punishment (e.g., deduction points), but its findings on exit as a sanction are highly informative for settings where exit or exclusion is a possible response to non-cooperation.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: Camera, G., & Gioffré, A. (2017). Asymmetric social norms. *Economics Letters*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated games with heterogeneous players (random productivity), efficient outcomes may require some players to defect in certain matches. In such settings, standard public monitoring is insufficient to sustain efficiency, but contagious punishment strategies—where players switch to permanent defection after observing a deviation—can support high payoffs if the group is sufficiently patient, punishment is severe enough, and productivity differences are not too large. The paper provides explicit conditions under which these strategies are equilibrium and efficiency is achieved, and derives closed-form expressions for continuation payoffs. Thus, punishment (in the form of contagious, community-based sanctions) can enable high efficiency even in complex, asymmetric environments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment mechanisms (specifically, contagious punishment) can increase efficiency in repeated social dilemma games, even when the efficient outcome involves some defections due to heterogeneity. The results are most directly applicable to repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games with random productivity, but the mechanisms and equilibrium conditions are relevant for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public-goods-like environments with heterogeneity. The paper does not provide empirical estimates or direct mappings to standard PGG parameters, but it does clarify that punishment can sustain high efficiency if players are patient, punishment is strong, and heterogeneity is moderate. For prediction, this suggests that enabling punishment in heterogeneous repeated games is likely to increase efficiency, provided the punishment is sufficiently severe and the group is not too heterogeneous.

- source: Laclau, M., & Tomala, T. (2017). Repeated games with public deterministic monitoring. *Journal of Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, reward_exists, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper provides a general theoretical framework for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms can be used to sustain efficient outcomes in repeated games with public monitoring. It shows that the set of sustainable payoffs is characterized by admissibility and joint rationality, and that explicit punishment phases (using approachability) can deter deviations. However, the efficiency achievable in equilibrium is limited by the monitoring structure: if deviations are not detectable or not attributable, efficiency may be strictly less than the social optimum. The paper gives explicit constructions of equilibrium strategies with main (cooperation), punishment, compensation, and reward phases, and proves that for sufficiently patient players, any payoff in the interior of the admissible and jointly rational set can be supported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the efficiency of repeated games with punishment, especially in settings with imperfect public monitoring. It provides a theoretical upper bound on efficiency as a function of the monitoring structure and the ability to implement punishment phases. The explicit construction of punishment and reward phases shows how punishment can be used to sustain cooperation, but also highlights that efficiency is limited by the possibility of undetectable or unattributable deviations. For the prediction task, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the monitoring structure: when monitoring is sufficiently informative, punishment can support high efficiency; when it is not, efficiency is bounded away from the optimum. The results are general and apply to a wide class of repeated games, including but not limited to public goods games.

- source: Olcina, G., & Calabuig, V. (2015). COORDINATED PUNISHMENT AND THE EVOLUTION OF COOPERATION. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the long-run efficiency of a team trust game with coordinated punishment depends critically on the institutional parameters of punishment: the effectiveness of coordinated punishment (damage inflicted if enough punishers act), the cost of punishment, and the strength of peer punishment (peer pressure on non-punishers). If the effectiveness of coordinated punishment is high enough (damage parameter λ ≥ 0.5) and peer pressure is strong (peer punishment cost to defectors is high relative to cost of coordinated punishment), then the only stable long-run outcome is a fully cooperative, efficient culture with no observed punishment (because the threat is credible). If these conditions are not met, the system converges to a non-cooperative, inefficient culture. The model also shows that increasing the effectiveness of punishment, lowering its cost, or increasing peer pressure all make the efficient outcome more likely. The results are robust to the dynamics of preference transmission, and the model provides explicit boundaries for the basins of attraction of efficient vs inefficient cultures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling effective, coordinated punishment (with sufficient effectiveness and/or low enough cost) and strong peer punishment will increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, even when the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is low. The model is not a standard PGG but is closely related, and the mechanisms (threshold punishment, peer punishment, endogenous preference evolution) are highly relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency. The explicit parameter boundaries (e.g., λ ≥ 0.5 for effectiveness, peer punishment cost vs coordinated punishment cost) can inform quantitative predictions about when punishment will be effective. The model also highlights that if punishment is not sufficiently effective or peer pressure is weak, enabling punishment may not improve efficiency. The results are most directly applicable to trust/investment games with team-based punishment, but the logic is transferable to PGG variants with threshold or coordinated punishment.

- source: Celik, G., & Peters, M. (2016). Reciprocal relationships and mechanism design. *Canadian Journal of Economics-revue Canadienne D Economique*.

  findings: The paper finds that in environments where players cannot enforce punishments for non-cooperation, reciprocal contracting (where players can make commitments contingent on others' commitments) allows for a larger set of efficient outcomes than centralized mechanism design with the same punishment constraints. The key mechanism is that players can use contract offers to signal private information, which can lower the reservation utility of deviators and thus make more efficient outcomes individually rational and incentive compatible. The analysis is theoretical, with the Cournot duopoly serving as a worked example. The results show that, under certain conditions (private values, single crossing, transferable utility), the incentive compatibility constraints can be simplified, and efficient outcomes can be implemented with appropriate transfer schemes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that, even in the absence of enforceable punishment, mechanisms that allow for reciprocal commitments and information revelation can achieve higher efficiency than mechanisms that do not. For the downstream prediction task, the main implication is that the absence of punishment does not necessarily preclude efficient outcomes if the game design allows for reciprocal contracting and signaling. However, the results are derived in a general mechanism design context (e.g., Cournot duopoly, collusion, marriage, etc.) rather than in standard public goods games. The findings suggest that, in environments structurally similar to public goods games but with richer contracting and signaling possibilities, efficiency can be higher than predicted by models that only consider enforceable punishment or centralized mechanisms. The paper does not provide direct quantitative predictions for efficiency changes when punishment is enabled or disabled in standard PGGs, but it does clarify the theoretical boundaries of what is possible in related environments.

This leads to a lower equilibrium level of altruism and, in the long run, can reduce dynamic social welfare. The findings imply that, in environments where intrinsic motivation is important for long-run efficiency, introducing punishment may have ambiguous or even negative effects on overall efficiency, especially over evolutionary or cultural timescales. The paper is most informative for settings where the persistence of cooperation and welfare over time is of interest, rather than immediate, one-shot efficiency gains.

- source: Feinberg, Y., & Kets, W. (2014). Ranking friends. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

When punishment is available (either community or bilateral), full cooperation can be sustained if and only if incentive constraints are satisfied.   prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated helping games with sequential requests and costly helping. When the structure equalizes expected costs (Latin square), punishment is most effective at sustaining efficiency. If the structure is unbalanced, punishment is less effective, and efficiency may not be sustainable. The results are theoretical but provide explicit conditions and formulas that can be mapped to efficiency predictions in similar repeated public-goods-like environments, especially where helping is sequential and punishment is either community-wide or bilateral. However, the model is not a standard PGG, so direct quantitative predictions for standard PGGs should be made with caution.
