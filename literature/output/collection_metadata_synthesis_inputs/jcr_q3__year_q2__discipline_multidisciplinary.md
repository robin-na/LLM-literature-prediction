  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper argues that punishment is a fundamental mechanism for stabilizing cooperation in large groups, enabling the evolution and maintenance of pro-social norms and group-level adaptation. It synthesizes evidence from evolutionary theory, ethnography, and game theory to claim that humans' unique adaptive capacity is rooted in our ability to cooperate, which is supported by both formal and informal punishment of defectors. The paper does not present new empirical findings or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes, but it does highlight the importance of punishment for sustaining cooperation and adaptive capacity in complex social systems.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical support for the idea that punishment mechanisms are important for sustaining cooperation in group settings, which is relevant to public goods games and their variants. However, it does not provide empirical estimates or direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in experimental games. The arguments may inform the expectation that enabling punishment will generally increase cooperation and potentially efficiency, but the paper does not address how specific game design dimensions (such as player count, MPCR, or punishment cost) moderate these effects. Thus, its value for quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency is limited to high-level theoretical justification for including punishment as a positive moderator of cooperation.

- source: Angourakis, A., Santos, J. I., Galán, J. M., & Balbo, A. L. (2015). Food for all: An agent-based model to explore the emergence and implications of cooperation for food storage. *Environmental Archaeology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The model demonstrates that cooperation in food storage is most likely to emerge and persist when (1) cooperative storage is at least as efficient as household storage, (2) there is moderate to high dependency on storable food, and (3) social norms regulating access to the cooperative stock are neither too strict nor too lax. Too strict enforcement (high reciprocity requirement) can undermine cooperation by excluding agents who fail to contribute due to bad luck, while too lax enforcement allows free-riding to undermine the common stock. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff in the public goods sense, but shows that cooperation increases the probability of avoiding shortages and, under certain conditions, can lead to surplus. The findings suggest that moderate sanctioning (via access control) is optimal for sustaining cooperation in common-pool resource settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for the prediction task. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff in a public goods game with explicit punishment, it models a closely related common-pool resource dilemma where access to the common resource is conditional on past cooperation (a form of sanction). The results suggest that intermediate levels of sanctioning (not too strict, not too lax) maximize cooperation and system performance (in terms of shortage avoidance and potential surplus). For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment or exclusion mechanisms that are too harsh may reduce efficiency by discouraging recovery from bad luck, while too little enforcement allows free-riding. The model also highlights the importance of storage efficiency and dependency on the resource, which may map onto MPCR and baseline efficiency in public goods games. However, direct quantitative predictions of efficiency with and without punishment are not available from this model.

  findings: The paper proposes that intense human cooperation and resource sharing, conceptualized as a 'regular hexagonal pyramid' of interconnected individuals, is the most effective way to reduce CO2 emissions and achieve sustainability. It applies this model to scenarios for 2030, suggesting that targeting individual high emitters and allowing low emitters to increase emissions is a fair and effective approach. The paper is entirely theoretical and does not present data or experimental results. There is no analysis of efficiency, payoff, or the effects of punishment or sanctions.

It does not discuss punishment, sanctions, or game design dimensions such as player count, rounds, or payoff structures. Its conceptual arguments about cooperation and resource sharing may be contextually relevant to the broader literature on cooperation, but it does not inform quantitative prediction tasks involving efficiency or payoff outcomes in controlled game settings.
