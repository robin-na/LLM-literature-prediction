- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Gächter, S., Kölle, F., & Quercia, S. (2017). Reciprocity and the tragedies of maintaining and providing the commons. *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across four lab experiments, the authors compare public goods games where the group must either maintain an existing public good (maintenance) or create one from scratch (provision). Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency are systematically lower in maintenance than provision, despite identical incentives. When a costly punishment option is introduced (partners with punishment), both maintenance and provision games see a dramatic increase in the effective size of the public good, with efficiency rising to near-optimal levels and no significant difference between the two conditions. The punishment mechanism allows each player to assign up to five punishment points to each other, costing 1 MU per point and reducing the target's earnings by 3 MU per point. The paper also finds that negative reciprocity (punishment in response to low contributions) is stronger in maintenance than provision, and that the response to being punished (increased cooperation) is also stronger in maintenance. Overall, punishment is highly effective at restoring efficiency in both types of public goods dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with four players, 27 rounds, and a punishment stage (cost 1 MU, impact 3 MU), the introduction of punishment increases group efficiency from low levels (especially in maintenance) to high levels, nearly matching the social optimum. The effect is robust across both 'maintenance' and 'provision' variants, and the punishment mechanism is highly effective at overcoming the decay in cooperation seen without punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment in similar lab PGG designs will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of whether the dilemma is framed as maintenance or provision. The specific punishment technology (cost/impact) and the absence of communication or rewards are important contextual features.

- source: Oya, G., & Ohtsuki, H. (2017). Stable polymorphism of cooperators and punishers in a public goods game. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in well-mixed populations, punishment does not maintain cooperation or improve efficiency; free riders dominate unless initial conditions favor punishers, and even then, cooperation is lost. In spatially structured populations (Wright's island model and stepping stone model), if punishment is sufficiently efficient and the cost of cooperation is less than the cost of punishment, a stable coexistence of cooperators, punishers, and free riders can emerge, either as a limit cycle or a stable equilibrium (especially with mutation). In these cases, the presence of punishment can support higher average payoffs and group welfare than in the absence of punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus context-dependent: it can be positive in structured populations with the right parameters, but is neutral or negative in well-mixed populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on population structure, the relative costs of cooperation and punishment, and the efficiency of punishment. In well-mixed (random matching) environments, enabling punishment does not improve efficiency or maintain cooperation. In spatially structured environments, punishment can support higher efficiency and stable coexistence of strategies, but only if punishment is efficient and costly enough relative to cooperation. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be positive only in structured populations with high punishment efficiency and when cooperation is less costly than punishment. The model also highlights the importance of mutation and the inability to both cooperate and punish in the same round. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency outcomes in theoretical or agent-based models, and provide mechanistic insight for interpreting empirical results.

Analytical results and agent-based simulations show that the average payoff of cooperators is not increased by punishment when anti-social punishment is possible; in fact, the outcome is equivalent to the no-punishment baseline.   prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that in PGG-like environments with high exploration/mutation and the possibility of anti-social punishment, enabling punishment will not increase group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition. The effect of punishment on efficiency is neutral or negative when anti-social punishment is possible, regardless of other game parameters such as player count, rounds, or selection intensity. If the game design allows anti-social punishment, do not expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment.

- source: Zhang, J. L., Chu, T. G., & Weissing, F. J. (2013). Does insurance against punishment undermine cooperation in the evolution of public goods games?. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with punishment and an additional option for defectors to buy insurance that covers their punishment costs. It shows that if insurance is cheaper than the expected punishment, cooperation can be destabilized, leading to lower group efficiency and welfare. However, if insurance is realistically priced (at or above the expected fine), cooperation remains stable and efficient. The results are presented as a set of scenarios, with the stability of cooperation, defection, or speculation depending on the relative costs of cooperation, punishment, and insurance. The main implication is that punishment remains an effective mechanism for sustaining cooperation and efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment in public goods games are robust to the introduction of insurance against punishment, as long as the insurance is not priced below the expected punishment cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in standard public goods games with punishment, efficiency should remain high unless there are additional mechanisms (like cheap insurance) that allow defectors to avoid punishment at a lower cost. The model also clarifies that the effectiveness of punishment (and thus its impact on efficiency) depends on the relationship between the cost of cooperation, the expected punishment, and any available insurance. If the control game (without punishment) is inefficient, introducing punishment should increase efficiency unless insurance is unrealistically cheap.

- source: Sasaki, T., & Uchida, S. (2013). The evolution of cooperation by social exclusion. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that social exclusion is a more effective and robust mechanism than costly punishment for sustaining cooperation and high efficiency in public goods games. When excluders can effectively and cheaply exclude defectors, the group can reach and maintain the efficient outcome where all contribute and defectors are eliminated. Costly punishment, even when effective, is vulnerable to invasion by non-punishing cooperators (second-order freeriders), leading to a decline in punishers and eventual dominance by defectors, which collapses efficiency. The model demonstrates that social exclusion can solve both the emergence and maintenance problems of cooperation, provided the cost of exclusion is not too high. The results are robust to changes in group size and to stochastic effects. The paper also notes that if monitoring costs are high, or if exclusion is not self-serving, the advantage of social exclusion disappears.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that, in public goods games, enabling a punishment mechanism that takes the form of social exclusion (where defectors are excluded from benefit sharing and the cost of exclusion is not too high) will lead to a large increase in group efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) condition. The effect is robust across group sizes and does not require repeated interaction or reputation. In contrast, enabling costly punishment (where punishers pay to reduce defectors' payoffs) is less likely to increase efficiency, especially in the long run, due to vulnerability to second-order freeriders. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social exclusion mechanism with low cost and high effectiveness should be treated as a strong positive moderator of the efficiency effect of punishment. The specific model parameters (group size, multiplication factor, cost of exclusion/punishment, effectiveness) are all explicitly analyzed, allowing for quantitative predictions if mapped to empirical settings.

- source: Adami, C., Schossau, J., & Hintze, A. (2016). Evolutionary game theory using agent-based methods. *Physics of Life Reviews*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in agent-based simulations of the public goods game, the introduction of punishment (where cooperators can punish defectors at a cost) shifts the critical synergy parameter (r) required for cooperation to be stable, thus expanding the parameter region where cooperation (and thus higher group efficiency) is observed. The effect is quantitatively predicted by mean-field theory and confirmed by simulations: higher probability or density of punishment lowers the threshold for cooperation. However, because punishment is costly, it creates a second-order dilemma, and the evolutionary dynamics can lead to meta-stable states and hysteresis. When punishment is allowed to evolve, its effect is strongest near the critical point, and in highly cooperative or highly defective regimes, punishment is not maintained by selection. The findings are robust across different group sizes and mutation rates, and the paper provides explicit equations and simulation results for the effect of punishment on group payoff and cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in public goods games, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (payoff) in the dilemma region by shifting the critical point for cooperation to lower values of the synergy parameter (r). The effect size and direction depend on the punishment parameters (cost, effectiveness), group size, and whether punishment is fixed or can evolve. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the prediction that, controlling for game design dimensions and baseline efficiency, enabling punishment will generally increase efficiency, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold. The paper also highlights that the effect of punishment is context-dependent: it is most effective near the critical point, and less so when cooperation or defection is already dominant. The explicit equations and simulation results can inform quantitative predictions.

  findings: The paper finds that public sanctioning institutions (pool punishment) can evolve and sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games, provided their existence is publicly visible. The signaling effect of such institutions deters free-riding and enables opportunistic cooperation, leading to a stable equilibrium with a mix of pool punishers and opportunists. This equilibrium achieves higher average payoffs than regimes without visible punishment or without the option to abstain. Diminishing taxes (cost-sharing among tax payers) further increase efficiency, while second-order punishment eliminates tax evasion but does not increase average payoffs beyond the baseline with only first-order punishment. The efficiency of the punishment technology and the level of public information about the institution are critical for the emergence and stability of efficient cooperation.

The model allows direct mapping to game design dimensions such as player count, punishment cost, punishment technology, and information structure. It predicts that enabling punishment (with public visibility) will increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is sufficiently deterrent (fine > cost of cooperation + tax), and when the institution is visible to most or all players. Diminishing the per-person cost of punishment (e.g., via cost-sharing) further increases efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, given knowledge of the control efficiency and the game design parameters.

The likelihood of efficient cooperation increases with group size, sanction severity, and the public goods multiplier. The paper provides explicit payoff formulas and analyzes the basins of attraction for efficient (cooperative) equilibria under a wide range of parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling punishment in voluntary PGGs increases group efficiency, even when punishment is mild (shared-punishment). The paper gives explicit formulas for payoffs and the conditions under which cooperation (and thus efficiency) is achieved. For prediction tasks, the presence of voluntary participation, absence of antisocial punishment, and the ability to punish defectors (even with fixed, shared-cost sanctions) are sufficient to expect a large increase in efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is stronger with larger groups, higher punishment severity, and higher public goods multipliers. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in PGG-like games with similar design features.

  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with non-linear (sigmoid or step) benefit functions, stable mutualism and high group efficiency can be achieved without punishment, even in large groups and with high costs. Punishment is only necessary to maintain mutualism and efficiency in the special case of linear benefits (the N-person prisoner's dilemma). When punishment is introduced in the linear case, it drives the system to full cooperation and maximal efficiency. In non-linear cases, punishment is not required for high efficiency, as the non-linearity itself stabilizes cooperation at high levels. The paper provides analytical and numerical results for the equilibrium frequency of cooperators and the conditions for stable mutualism.
  prediction_guidance: For the downstream prediction task, this paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the shape of the benefit function. If the game is linear (standard PGG), enabling punishment will have a strong positive effect on efficiency, driving the group to full cooperation. If the game is non-linear (e.g., threshold, sigmoid, or step), high efficiency can be achieved without punishment, and enabling punishment adds little or no additional benefit. Therefore, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to know whether the benefit function is linear or non-linear. Other game design dimensions (group size, cost/benefit ratio) interact with this, but the linearity of the benefit is the key moderator identified by this paper.
