- source: Fehr, E., Gächter, S., Milinski, M., & Rockenbach, B. (2010). Institutions Fostering Public Goods Provision. *Selten School of Behavioral Economics: a Collection of Essays in Honor of Reinhard Selten*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: Punishment opportunities in public goods games increase cooperation and contributions, but typically reduce efficiency and group payoffs in standard (short) experimental settings due to the destruction of resources through costly punishment. In most experiments with 10 or fewer rounds, average payoffs are lower with punishment than without. However, in longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when punishment is combined with effective reputation mechanisms, efficiency can be higher with punishment. Endogenous institution choice leads most participants to eventually select punishment institutions, which then sustain high cooperation, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed unless the time horizon is long or reputation is leveraged. The effect of punishment on efficiency is thus mixed and highly sensitive to game design dimensions such as number of rounds and the presence of reputation mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games is not universally positive or negative, but depends critically on the number of rounds (time horizon) and the presence of reputation or endogenous institution choice. For standard short games (10 rounds or fewer), enabling punishment is likely to reduce efficiency relative to control. For longer games (e.g., 50 rounds), or when combined with reputation mechanisms, punishment can increase efficiency. The paper also highlights the importance of endogenous institution choice, which can foster high cooperation and, under the right conditions, higher efficiency. When predicting treatment efficiency from game design, the number of rounds and the presence of reputation or endogenous choice mechanisms are key moderators.

- source: Fischer, S., & Nicklisch, A. (2007). Ex interim voting:: An experimental study of referendums for public-good provision. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Unanimity voting increases contributions but leads to frequent rejections, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to majority voting or automatic provision. Majority voting does not significantly differ from automatic provision in terms of efficiency or profits. The mechanism (voting rule) is the primary driver of these outcomes, not individual voting behavior. The study provides direct evidence on how institutional design (voting rules) affects efficiency in public good games, but does not test punishment or sanctioning per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly informative for predicting efficiency in public good games with voting mechanisms but does not provide evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that mechanisms that safeguard against exploitation (like unanimity voting) can increase contributions but may reduce efficiency due to coordination failures or excessive vetoes. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

- source: English, W. (2012). Demystifying trust: Experimental evidence from Cambodia and Thailand. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in one-shot, four-player public goods games in rural Thailand and Cambodia, standard survey measures of trust (from the World Values Survey) do not predict contributions or group efficiency. Instead, expectations about others' contributions are the key driver of cooperative behavior and group payoff. When expectations are experimentally lowered (by revealing low contributions from a previous group), both contributions and group efficiency drop sharply. The distribution of player types (reciprocators, free riders, altruists) is similar to previous studies, with reciprocators being the largest group. The results suggest that interventions that shift expectations can rapidly change group efficiency, while survey-based trust is not a reliable predictor of economic cooperation in this context.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in the absence of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to participants' expectations about others' behavior, but not to standard survey measures of trust. For the downstream prediction task, this means that, for games with similar design (one-shot, no punishment, no communication, fixed group size, known MPCR), efficiency is likely to be determined by expectation management rather than by trust survey scores. The absence of punishment means the paper does not directly inform the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, but it does highlight the importance of expectation-shaping interventions. The findings are most relevant for predicting baseline (control) efficiency in PGGs without punishment, and for understanding that expectation manipulation (e.g., via feedback or communication) can have large effects on efficiency.

- source: Webb, J., & Foddy, M. (2004). Vested interests in the decision to resolve social dilemma conflicts. *Small Group Research*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The punishment rule (targeted sanctioning) leads to greater resource preservation (more trees left at the end of the game) compared to the equality rule (shared sanctioning), but the equality rule results in higher total group profit. Individual profit outcomes depend on access: high-access participants profit more under the equality rule, while low-access participants profit more under the punishment rule. Voting for structural change (switching sanctioning systems) is predicted by individual profit and fines, not by group-level resource preservation. The study highlights that the impact of sanctioning systems on efficiency and payoff is heterogeneous and depends on both the structure of the sanction and the distribution of resource access.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency and payoff in public-goods-like resource dilemmas depends on the design of the punishment system and the distribution of resource access. If efficiency is defined as resource preservation, targeted punishment (punishment rule) is more effective. If efficiency is defined as total group profit, shared punishment (equality rule) can yield higher payoffs. The results suggest that the effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or negative for efficiency; it depends on the specific sanctioning mechanism and group heterogeneity. For prediction, the design of the punishment system (targeted vs. shared), the distribution of access, and the definition of efficiency are critical moderators.

- source: McEvoy, D. M. (2012). Paying to punish on a free ride: member and nonmember punishments in voluntary coalitions. *Applied Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In a public good game with voluntary coalition formation, allowing voluntary punishment (where any player can punish any other) does not increase public good provision relative to no punishment. However, when coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance (quasi-voluntary punishment), public good provision increases significantly. Both coalition members and nonmembers are equally likely to punish noncompliance, and the extent of nonmember punishment does not change when members can credibly enforce compliance. The main outcomes are contribution levels and punishment frequencies; efficiency or group payoff is not directly reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper suggests that simply enabling voluntary punishment (without credible commitment) is not sufficient to increase efficiency or public good provision in coalition-based public good games. Only when punishment is structured so that coalition members can credibly commit to punishing noncompliance does cooperation (and likely efficiency) increase. For prediction, the presence of a credible, committed punishment mechanism is a key moderator; voluntary, non-committed punishment may have little or no effect on efficiency. The paper does not provide direct efficiency data, so predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes.

- source: Samid, Y., & Suleiman, R. (2008). Effectiveness of Coercive and Voluntary Institutional Solutions to Social Dilemmas. *New Issues and Paradigms in Research on Social Dilemmas*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=unclear
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiment shows that introducing a moderately coercive authority (with moderate fines for non-participation and moderate fees for participation) increases the rate of authority support and contributions compared to non-coercive or exploitative authority. However, when coercion becomes excessive (high fees and/or fines), groups may still converge to high authority support, but this does not necessarily improve efficiency, as the cost of coercion can outweigh the benefits. The non-coercive authority is ineffective unless social comparison is reduced (partial feedback), in which case cooperation increases. The main findings are about behavioral convergence to equilibria (all-defect, partial authority, all-authority) rather than direct efficiency or group payoff. The results highlight the importance of balancing the cost and effectiveness of coercive mechanisms to avoid undermining the benefits of cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for predicting the effect of punishment (coercive authority) on efficiency in public-goods-like games. It suggests that moderate punishment (moderate fines and fees) can increase cooperation and authority support, which are likely to improve efficiency, but excessive punishment (high fees/fines) can lead to high compliance at the cost of efficiency, as the costs may outweigh the benefits. The paper does not report direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes, so predictions must be inferred from behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, authority support) and the structure of the payoff matrices. The findings also indicate that feedback about others' payoffs (social comparison) can reduce the effectiveness of non-coercive mechanisms. For prediction, the paper supports the idea that moderate, mutually agreed punishment is most likely to improve efficiency, while excessive punishment may not.

- source: Loukopoulos, P., Eek, D., Gärling, T., & Fujii, S. (2006). Palatable punishment in real-world social dilemmas? Punishing others to increase cooperation among the unpunished. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that intermittent punishment (where only some group members are punished for defection in a given round) increases cooperation rates among those not directly punished, compared to a no-punishment baseline. This 'spillover effect' is robust to changes in the framing of the decision (individual vs. group focus) and to information about prior cooperation by others. The study does not report group efficiency or total payoff, but the payoff structure is such that increased cooperation would increase group earnings. The findings suggest that intermittent punishment can promote cooperation even among those not directly targeted by sanctions, and that this effect is not simply due to general factors that increase cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that introducing intermittent punishment in a public-goods-like sequential social dilemma increases cooperation rates among unpunished group members, compared to no punishment. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so its direct value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited. The findings suggest that even partial or probabilistic punishment can have positive behavioral effects on cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency if the payoff structure rewards cooperation. The robustness of the spillover effect to framing and prior cooperation manipulations suggests that the effect of punishment on cooperation is not easily moderated by these factors. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the idea that introducing punishment (even if not universal) can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes.

The experiment is a one-shot, anonymous, five-player VCM with no punishment or reward options. It does, however, provide contextual evidence about baseline contribution rates in a one-shot, five-player, anonymous VCM with MPCR=0.4 and no communication or sanctions. For prediction tasks, this paper is informative only for estimating control (no-punishment) efficiency in similar game designs, but provides no evidence on the effect of enabling punishment.

- source: Klempt, C. (2012). Fairness, spite, and intentions: Testing different motives behind punishment in a prisoners' dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that cooperators and defectors differ in their motives for punishment. Cooperators punish defectors more when defection is intentional, suggesting a role for retaliation, but still punish unintentional defectors, indicating some concern for payoff inequality. Defectors punish both cooperators and defectors equally, regardless of intentions, consistent with spiteful motives. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, so it does not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or prisoners' dilemma games. Its findings are relevant for understanding the motives behind punishment and how they may depend on the intentionality of actions, but it does not report or analyze efficiency or payoff outcomes. Therefore, it offers little direct support for the downstream prediction task, except to suggest that punishment behavior may depend on player type and perceived intentions, which could indirectly affect efficiency in other designs.

- source: Kodaka, F., Takahashi, H., Yamada, M., Takano, H., Nakayama, K., Ito, H., & Suhara, T. (2012). Effect of Cooperation Level of Group on Punishment for Non-Cooperators: A Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging Study. *Plos One*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that the degree of punishment assigned to a non-cooperator is significantly higher when the rest of the group is highly cooperative compared to when the group is less cooperative. This is reflected both in behavioral choices and in increased activation in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left anterior cingulate cortex. The results suggest that the social context of group cooperation modulates punitive behavior, but the study does not address efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the social context—specifically, the overall cooperation level of the group—modulates the severity of punishment assigned to non-cooperators. However, it does not provide any data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main relevance is in highlighting that punishment behavior is context-dependent, which may be important for interpreting or modeling punishment effects in environments where group cooperation levels vary.

- source: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R. (2012). Individual Differences in Foraging Strategies of Parasitic Sabre-Tooth Blennies. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that individual blennies differ markedly in their foraging strategies, including how they respond to punishment by victim fish. Some blennies are more likely to switch victim species after being punished, while others are not. Laboratory experiments show that punishment can reduce the likelihood of attack or cause switching, but these effects are highly individual-specific. The paper discusses how only certain patterns of blenny response to punishment (specifically, switching to another species) would generate a public good for the victim group, while other patterns would not. However, the study does not measure group efficiency or payoff directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, mechanistic insight into how punishment might or might not generate public goods in a natural system, depending on the behavioral rules of the punished party. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a way that would allow direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting that the effect of punishment on public good provision (and thus efficiency) may depend on the behavioral heterogeneity of the punished agents and the structure of the interaction (e.g., whether punishment causes switching to other species or not).

However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a controlled way. For prediction tasks, the main value is in highlighting that spatial structure and heterogeneity in exposure can affect the distribution and potential benefits of punishment, but the paper does not provide direct input for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in standard PGG designs.

- source: Bruttel, L., & Eisenkopf, G. (2012). No contract or unfair contract: What's better?. *Journal of Socio-economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a lab experiment comparing a sequential prisoner's dilemma (PD) to a vertically integrated version with incentive-compatible contracts (ICC), the introduction of contracts—regardless of fairness—increases joint payoffs from an average of 7.33 (PD) to 9.33 (ICC). Even unfair contracts yield higher welfare (9.02) than the best possible matching in PD. Most players cooperate under contracts, and the risk of defection in response to unfairness does not offset the efficiency gains. The study also finds that individual reciprocity types in PD do not predict negative reciprocity (defection) in ICC. Thus, the presence of incentive-compatible contracts robustly increases efficiency, even when some contracts are perceived as unfair.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player sequential prisoner's dilemma, introducing an incentive-compatible contract mechanism (analogous to a principal-agent or vertical integration structure) increases efficiency (joint payoff) compared to the baseline without contracts. The effect is robust to the fairness of the contract: even when the contract is unfair (unequal payoffs), efficiency is higher than in the baseline. The mechanism for increased efficiency is that contracts induce more cooperation, and the risk of 'punishment' via defection in response to unfairness is not large enough to negate the efficiency gains. For prediction tasks, this suggests that enabling a contract-like mechanism (even if not perfectly fair) in a similar environment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. However, the setting is a sequential PD, not a standard PGG, and punishment is not implemented as a costly action but as defection in response to unfairness.

- source: Stiff, C. (2008). Are They Bothered? How the Opportunity to Damage a Partner's Reputation Influences Giving Behavior in a Trust Game. *Journal of Social Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The opportunity to leave feedback (damage a partner's reputation) increases the amount participants are willing to give in a one-shot trust game, but only when the partner is perceived to care about their reputation. This effect is strongest when the partner's future interaction group consists of strangers rather than friends. The effect of feedback opportunity and group familiarity on giving is mediated by the participant's perceived control over the partner's reputation. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or total welfare, and the design is a dyadic trust game rather than a public goods game.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that reputation-based indirect sanctions (opportunity to leave feedback) can increase cooperative behavior (giving/trust) in dyadic trust games, especially when the target is reputation-sensitive. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, and the game is not a public goods game. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of reputation-based sanctions depends on the perceived concern for reputation and the social context of future interactions. For prediction tasks, this paper is only adjacent: it supports the idea that indirect, reputation-based sanctions can increase cooperation, but does not provide quantitative efficiency effects or direct evidence for public goods games with explicit punishment mechanisms.

- source: Kusakawa, T., Ogawa, K., & Shichijo, T. (2012). An experimental investigation of a third-person enforcement in a prisoner's dilemma game. *Economics Letters*.

  findings: The experiment shows that when a third party (M) can observe the actions of two players in a one-shot PD, the cooperation rate between those two players is significantly higher than when M cannot observe. This is attributed to the threat of future punishment by M in subsequent repeated PD games. The effect is measured in terms of cooperation rates, not efficiency or total payoff. The study provides evidence that third-party observation and the possibility of future sanctioning can facilitate cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a third-party observer with future sanctioning power can increase cooperation rates in a prisoner's dilemma setting. However, because the study does not use a public goods game or directly measure efficiency or group payoff, its value for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments is limited. The main implication is that third-party monitoring and the threat of future punishment can be a strong facilitator of cooperation, which may translate to higher efficiency in related settings, but this is not directly tested. The design is adjacent to PGGs and does not manipulate standard punishment mechanisms (e.g., costly punishment within the group), so caution is warranted in transferring the findings.

- source: Vyrastekova, J., Funaki, Y., & Takeuchi, A. (2011). Sanctioning as a social norm: Expectations of non-strategic sanctioning in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Socio-economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that the degree of experimenter-subject anonymity can affect pro- and anti-social behavior in simple allocation and destruction games. Specifically, personal disclosure of decisions to the experimenter increases pro-social transfers in the Dictator Game and reduces destructive choices in the Joy-of-Destruction game, compared to a treatment where decisions are only linked to IDs. The results suggest that experimenter demand effects can be induced by reducing anonymity, but the games used do not involve cooperation, public goods, or punishment of norm violations. No efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. The games studied (Dictator Game and Joy-of-Destruction) do not involve group cooperation, repeated interaction, or punishment/reward mechanisms. The closest relevance is that the JoD game involves costly destruction (anti-social behavior), which is adjacent to punishment, but it is not used as a sanctioning mechanism for norm enforcement. The findings may be weakly informative about how experimenter demand effects or anonymity could influence anti-social or pro-social choices, but they do not inform the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games.
