- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Gao, S. P., Du, J. M., & Liang, J. L. (2020). Impacts of preferences on the emergence of cooperation. *Physical Review E*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the introduction of punishment mechanisms—where cooperators can collectively decide to punish defectors—can make cooperation more advantageous than defection in public goods games, as measured by expected payoffs and stationary abundance. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus group efficiency) depends on the degree of cooperators' willingness to punish (PFPD), their willingness to comply with collective decisions (PFCE), and the consensus threshold (T) required to implement punishment. Lower consensus thresholds and higher willingness to punish generally increase the efficiency of the group by making cooperation more abundant. The effect of conformity (PFCE) is context-dependent: at low consensus thresholds, higher conformity increases cooperation, but at high thresholds, lower conformity can be more beneficial. The model also compares consensual (collective) and autonomous (individual) punishment, finding that consensual punishment is more effective when consensus is easy to reach and willingness to punish is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that enabling punishment in a public goods game increases group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) under a wide range of parameter settings, especially when the consensus threshold for punishment is low and the willingness to punish is high. The model allows mapping of game design dimensions (player count, group size, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, consensus threshold, etc.) to predicted efficiency outcomes. For the downstream prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that, controlling for the efficiency of the control game (no punishment), enabling punishment will increase efficiency, with the magnitude of the effect moderated by the consensus threshold, willingness to punish, and conformity. The model is most informative for settings with endogenous, collectively-decided punishment and can be used to simulate or estimate efficiency under different design parameters.

- source: Botta, R., Blanco, G., & Schaerer, C. E. (2021). Fractional Punishment of Free Riders to Improve Cooperation in Optional Public Good Games. *Games*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of punishment can be reduced by punishing only a fraction of defectors, while still achieving high cooperation and efficiency. The effect of punishment is mediated by the game parameters (group size n, multiplication factor r, loner payoff sigma), and the critical thresholds for d are derived in terms of these parameters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that, in optional public good games with loner strategies, enabling punishment (even fractional/random punishment) increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation, provided the punishment fraction is above a threshold. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

- source: Zhang, B. J., Cui, Z. G., & Yue, X. H. (2019). Cluster evolution in public goods game with fairness mechanism. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in a well-mixed PGG, adding a fairness mechanism and peer punishment (with costs shared among cooperators) can achieve full cooperation and maximum efficiency in small clusters. In larger clusters, full cooperation is difficult, but increasing the fairness factor (weight on fairness utility) and decreasing the cost of punishment both increase the equilibrium number of cooperators and group utility. The model provides explicit formulas for the utilities of cooperators and defectors with and without punishment, and shows that punishment is only effective (i.e., leads to higher group efficiency) when the number of cooperators exceeds a threshold. Simulation results confirm that lower punishment cost and higher fairness factor lead to higher group efficiency and larger stable clusters.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in PGGs with peer punishment, efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) increases when punishment is enabled, especially when punishment cost is low and the fairness factor is high. The model allows explicit calculation of group efficiency under different parameter settings, including player count, punishment cost, and fairness factor. For the prediction task, the paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) condition, with the effect size moderated by punishment cost and the fairness factor. The results are most directly applicable to well-mixed, continuous-contribution PGGs with no communication, and where punishment costs are shared among cooperators.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that altruistic punishment in a public goods game is only effective at promoting cooperation (and thus improving group outcomes) if the frequency of punishers exceeds a threshold determined by the cost of contribution and the punishment level. If this threshold is not met, defection remains the dominant strategy and group efficiency does not improve. The analysis provides explicit formulas for the returns to each strategy and the minimum frequency of punishers required for punishment to be effective. The findings imply that simply enabling punishment is not sufficient to improve efficiency; the population composition and punishment parameters are critical.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting when punishment will improve efficiency in public goods games. It shows that the effect of punishment on efficiency is conditional: punishment only increases efficiency if the proportion of punishers is above a threshold that depends on the cost of contribution and the punishment level. For prediction tasks, this means that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency; the effectiveness depends on the game parameters (contribution cost, punishment cost/impact) and the frequency of punishers. The explicit formulas can be used to estimate the required conditions for punishment to be effective, but the paper does not provide empirical efficiency values or group payoff data.

The punishment is non-deterrent and strictly efficiency-reducing by design. The findings are most relevant for settings with weak, automatic, non-peer punishment and may not generalize to peer punishment or stronger punishment regimes.

The findings suggest that punishment can increase cooperation rates under certain parameter regimes, but may also reduce payoffs and, in some cases, accelerate defection. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides only indirect, mechanistic insight rather than quantitative payoff outcomes.
