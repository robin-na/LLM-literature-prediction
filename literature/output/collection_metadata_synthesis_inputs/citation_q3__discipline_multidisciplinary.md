- source: Simpson, B., Willer, R., & Harrell, A. (2017). The Enforcement of Moral Boundaries Promotes Cooperation and Prosocial Behavior in Groups. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four-person groups, the ability to impose costly material sanctions (punishment/reward) or to make costless moral judgments (approval/disapproval) both increased contributions relative to a control with no sanctions. However, material sanctions led to efficiency losses due to their cost, so group earnings in the material sanction condition were not significantly higher than in the control. In contrast, moral judgment groups earned significantly more than both control and material sanction groups, indicating higher efficiency. The effect of both interventions was present from the first round (deterrence) and persisted across rounds (learning). Material sanctions also led to cycles of retaliation, while moral judgments did not. Downstream, moral judgment groups showed higher trust, trustworthiness, and generosity in subsequent games. Thus, punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, while moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard PGG increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency, as the cost of punishment offsets the gains from higher contributions. In contrast, costless moral judgments increase both cooperation and efficiency. For prediction, the presence of costly punishment should not be expected to increase average efficiency above control, while costless, non-material sanctions can. The specific game design (4 players, 9 rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.5, punishment cost 1:3, no chat, no knowledge of total rounds) is highly informative for similar lab PGGs. The findings suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment is sensitive to its costliness, and that non-material sanctions may be a more effective and efficient intervention.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2020). The role of peer reward and punishment for public goods problems in a localized society. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that peer reward and punishment can sustain high cooperation and group efficiency in public goods games only when local group welfare is improved by cooperation (locally efficient). In the locally inefficient condition (Local0.1), where cooperation benefits the global group but not the local group, both cooperation and group profit are significantly lower, and peer reward and punishment are less effective. In conditions where participants can reward/punish all group members (Global0.1), cooperation is sustained at a high level, but the total amount of reward is lower, possibly due to the difficulty of targeting in large groups. The study provides direct evidence that the alignment of local and global incentives, as well as the scope of peer reward/punishment, are key moderators of the efficiency gains from punishment and reward in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment (and reward) on group efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the alignment of local and global incentives (i.e., whether cooperation is locally efficient or inefficient). When local group welfare is not improved by cooperation, enabling punishment/reward does not increase efficiency, even if global efficiency would benefit. The scope of punishment/reward (local vs. global) also matters: global targeting allows for more selective rewarding but may reduce the total amount of reward given. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency will be positive only when local and global incentives are aligned, and that group size and the structure of interaction (localization) are important moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the relative differences between conditions provide guidance for predicting treatment efficiency.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2017). Punishing second-order free riders before first-order free riders: The effect of pool punishment priority on cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with a pool punishment system, prioritizing second-order punishment (punishing non-supporters before non-cooperators) leads to higher cooperation and more support for the punishment system when punishment resources are scarce (PR=1). However, this does not translate into higher group profit (efficiency), as the cost of maintaining high cooperation through punishment is high. When punishment resources are abundant (PR=3), both prioritization schemes (2To1 and 1To2) achieve high cooperation and support, and there is no significant difference in group profit between them. The presence of second-order punishment (regardless of priority) is critical for achieving high cooperation, but the efficiency gains are limited by the costliness of punishment. The system's surplus (unused punishment resources) is higher when second-order punishment is prioritized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the structure of the punishment system and the abundance of punishment resources. When punishment resources are scarce, prioritizing second-order punishment increases cooperation but does not improve efficiency due to high punishment costs. When resources are abundant, the priority does not matter, and both systems achieve high cooperation and similar efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency impact of punishment depends on both the punishment resource multiplier (punishment tech) and the priority rule. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the results show that punishment can increase cooperation but may not always increase efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

- source: Chaudhuri, A., & Paichayontvijit, T. (2017). On the long-run efficacy of punishments and recommendations in a laboratory public goods game. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated linear public goods games with groups of four, both peer punishment and exhortative recommendations (public messages) increase efficiency compared to a control with no intervention. Recommendations have a strong and immediate positive effect on efficiency, especially with fixed group composition (partners), but this effect decays over time. Punishment starts with lower efficiency due to the cost of punishing but shows an upward trend over time, eventually catching up to or surpassing recommendations in some conditions, particularly with longer time horizons. However, in these experiments, punishment never outperforms the restart recommendation treatment. The effect of punishment is stronger with fixed matching than with random rematching. The cost-effectiveness of punishment depends on the punishment technology (cost/impact ratio) and the length of the game. The study provides direct evidence on how efficiency changes when punishment is enabled, with detailed efficiency data for control, recommendation, and punishment treatments.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides high-quality, directly relevant evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear public goods games. It reports efficiency (average earnings as a fraction of the social optimum) for control and punishment treatments, with and without recommendations, across different matching protocols and time horizons. The results show that punishment can increase efficiency over time, but the effect is not immediate and depends on the number of rounds, matching protocol, and punishment cost/impact. The efficiency gain from punishment is more pronounced with longer games and fixed matching. The paper also provides detailed information on the punishment cost and technology, allowing for direct mapping to prediction dimensions. For prediction, the control efficiency and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, matching, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are all reported, making this paper a strong source for model training and validation.

- source: Salahshour, M. (2021). Evolution of prosocial punishment in unstructured and structured populations and in the presence of antisocial punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that prosocial punishment can evolve and promote cooperation (and thus high efficiency) in public goods games, even without auxiliary mechanisms like network structure or voluntary participation. The efficiency of the group (measured by which strategy dominates and thus the average group payoff) is increased when prosocial punishment dominates. The evolution of prosocial punishment (and thus efficiency) is facilitated by higher returns to the public good (mpcr), higher punishment effectiveness, and optimal punishment cost. Allowing unused punishment resources to be redistributed (non-wasteful punishment) further facilitates the evolution of prosocial punishment and efficiency. Antisocial punishment can dominate under low punishment cost or low public good return, reducing efficiency. Network structure can both hinder and facilitate the evolution of punishment, but under favorable conditions, it helps prosocial punishment dominate. The model provides phase diagrams mapping out these effects.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a PGG-like environment can increase efficiency, especially when the punishment is effective, the cost is optimal (not too low or high), and the returns to the public good are high. The model also shows that if punishment is too cheap, antisocial punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency, while if it is too costly, punishment does not evolve. The presence of a reward mechanism (redistribution of unused punishment resources) further increases the likelihood of high efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions such as mpcr, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and the presence of reward. The results are theoretical and should be interpreted as providing mechanistic and qualitative guidance rather than empirical effect sizes.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2012). The Structure of Mutations and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2019). Evolution of coordinated punishment to enforce cooperation from an unbiased strategy space. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with institutional punishment, the ability to observe the presence of punishment institutions is crucial for sustaining cooperation and high group payoff. When institutions are not observable, anti-social punishment can invade and reduce cooperation and efficiency. When institutions are observable, a small minority of pro-social institution supporters can sustain high levels of cooperation and group payoff, even in the presence of anti-social punishment strategies. The model demonstrates that the evolutionary outcome is robust to the inclusion of all possible strategies, and that the coexistence of institution supporters and opportunists is stable. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore context-dependent: positive when observability and conditional strategies are present, negative or neutral when they are absent.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on whether players can observe the presence of punishment institutions and condition their behavior accordingly. If the game design allows for observability and conditional strategies, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to the control. If observability is absent, enabling punishment—especially if anti-social punishment is possible—may reduce efficiency. The model also shows that the cost of supporting institutions and group size affect the probability of institution implementation and thus the efficiency outcome. For prediction, the presence or absence of observability/conditionality, the possibility of anti-social punishment, and the cost structure are key moderators.

- source: Ohdaira, T. (2022). The probabilistic pool punishment proportional to the difference of payoff outperforms previous pool and peer punishment. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the proposed probabilistic pool punishment mechanism, which ties the probability of paying the punishment cost to the difference in payoffs, results in a population with more punishers and fewer cooperators (who do not punish) compared to traditional pool punishment. This state is more robust to the invasion of defectors and maintains an average payoff that is comparable to or better than peer punishment. The results hold across both regular and random one-dimensional lattice structures. The study also notes that the fixed cost problem of pool punishment is solved, and that the mechanism is effective even when the number of defectors is high.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism—specifically, making punishment probabilistic and proportional to payoff differences—can improve group efficiency (average payoff) in public goods games. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling a more adaptive, probabilistic, and context-sensitive punishment regime (as opposed to fixed-cost or deterministic punishment) is likely to yield higher efficiency, especially in structured populations. The results are most directly applicable to games with 5-player groups, 1000-player populations, 600 rounds (generations), no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR and punishment cost parameters as specified. The study does not vary most game design dimensions, so its predictive value is strongest for the specific parameter regime tested.

- source: Wang, C. Q., Perc, M., & Szolnoki, A. (2024). Evolutionary dynamics of any multiplayer game on regular graphs. *Nature Communications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper analytically demonstrates that in structured populations (regular graphs), the introduction of punishment mechanisms (peer or pool) in public goods games can lead to the extinction of defection or to cyclic dominance among strategies, depending on the punishment type and strength. For peer punishment, if the punishment fine exceeds a critical threshold, defection is eliminated and only cooperators and punishers coexist, maximizing group efficiency. For pool punishment, strong punishment leads to cyclic dominance, preventing fixation of defection and maintaining higher group welfare. The paper provides explicit formulas for the critical punishment thresholds and shows that these effects are unique to structured populations and do not occur in well-mixed populations, where defection always retains a basin of attraction. The results are presented in terms of group payoff, mean payoff, and phase diagrams, all directly related to efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for predicting that, in public goods games on regular graphs, enabling punishment (peer or pool) will increase group efficiency and welfare, provided the punishment strength exceeds a critical threshold. The analytical results specify how the effect depends on the number of neighbors (player_count), the punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech), and the structure of the game (e.g., mpcr, payoff function). The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency, especially in settings with structured populations and multi-strategy dynamics. The explicit threshold formulas for punishment strength can be used to estimate when punishment will be effective in increasing efficiency.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2016). The dynamics of human behavior in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Scientific Reports*.

  findings: In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The group average payoff is strongly positively correlated with b1 (self-regarding preference) in punishment and combined conditions.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with four players, 50 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, adding institutional punishment (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases group payoffs and average contributions compared to control. The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Conditional punishment is a double-edged sword in promoting cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.
