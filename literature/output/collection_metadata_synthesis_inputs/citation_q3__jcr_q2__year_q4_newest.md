- source: Shen, Y., Yin, W. K., Kang, H. W., Zhang, H. G., & Wang, M. (2022). High-reputation individuals exert greater influence on cooperation in spatial public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The paper develops and analyzes a theoretical model of a public goods game with tax-based punishment and reward mechanisms. It shows that: (1) Tax-based rewards given directly to cooperators are more effective at promoting cooperation and group payoff than rewards given to punishers. (2) When rewards are used to support punishers, this can undermine the effectiveness of punishment and reduce overall cooperation and efficiency, as punishers and cooperators end up in competition. (3) The effect of punishment and reward on efficiency is highly sensitive to the way these mechanisms are implemented, with some parameter regimes leading to lower group payoff than the baseline. (4) Increasing taxes (used as rewards for cooperators) generally increases the proportion of cooperators and system efficiency, but excessive rewards to punishers can be detrimental.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical evidence that the efficiency impact of punishment in public goods games depends critically on the design of the reward and punishment mechanisms, especially whether rewards are given to cooperators or punishers. For prediction, it suggests that enabling punishment does not always increase efficiency: if rewards are used to support punishers, efficiency may decrease compared to control. If tax-based rewards are given to cooperators, efficiency is more likely to increase. The model also provides explicit payoff functions and equilibrium conditions that can be used to estimate efficiency under different parameterizations (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost, reward structure). However, the results are theoretical and may not capture all behavioral nuances seen in experiments.

- source: Xiao, J. F., Liu, L. J., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2023). Evolution of cooperation driven by sampling punishment. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that sampling punishment can dramatically increase the average cooperation level in both linear (PGG) and nonlinear (CRD) social dilemmas, provided the punishment intensity is above a threshold. Low punishment thresholds and small sample sizes are optimal, as they maximize cooperation and minimize punishment cost. The effect of punishment intensity is nonlinear: below a threshold, it is ineffective, but above the threshold, further increases yield little additional benefit. These findings are robust across both PGG and CRD models. However, the paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so the direct effect on efficiency cannot be determined.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that institutional punishment mechanisms—specifically, sampling punishment—can increase cooperation rates in PGG-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently intense, the threshold for triggering punishment is low, and the sample size is small. However, because the paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation rates, not payoffs. If the downstream prediction task assumes a strong link between cooperation and efficiency, these results may be indirectly informative, but caution is warranted.

- source: Frey, U. J., & Burgess, J. (2023). Why do climate change negotiations stall? Scientific evidence and solutions for some structural problems. *Global Discourse*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: chat
  findings: The paper finds that climate change negotiations are structurally analogous to public goods games, with similar challenges of free-riding, group formation, and the need for institutional mechanisms to foster cooperation. It reviews experimental evidence showing that the possibility of sanctioning (punishment) increases cooperation and, over time, can improve group welfare, though some studies note that punishment can be inefficient in the short term. The authors argue that the absence of enforceable sanctions in climate agreements is a key reason for stalled progress and that introducing sanctions, improving transparency, and building trust and fairness are critical for better outcomes. However, the paper does not report new empirical data or direct efficiency outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and literature-based support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases cooperation and, in the long run, can improve group welfare and efficiency, though it acknowledges that punishment can sometimes be inefficient in the short term. It highlights the importance of institutional design features such as sanctioning, transparency, and trust. However, because it does not report new empirical data or direct efficiency outcomes, it should be used as contextual and mechanistic support rather than as a source of quantitative effect sizes for efficiency prediction.

- source: Sun, X. P., Han, L. F., Wang, M., Liu, S. X., & Shen, Y. (2023). Social exclusion with antisocial punishment in spatial public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment by investors can induce a stable coexistence of punishing investors and trustworthy trustees in a hierarchical N-player trust game. The effectiveness of punishment in promoting trust depends on both the intensity of punishment and the fraction of investors in the population. For low punishment intensity, an intermediate fraction of investors is optimal for promoting trust, while for high punishment intensity, a higher fraction of investors is more effective. Increasing punishment intensity generally enlarges the attraction domain of the coexistence state, but only up to a point. The model does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but interprets the stable coexistence of trust and trustworthiness as a positive social outcome.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that punishment can promote trust and trustworthiness in a hierarchical trust game, with the effect moderated by punishment intensity and the fraction of investors. However, it does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, so its value for predicting efficiency outcomes in public goods games is indirect. The findings suggest that the presence and intensity of punishment, as well as the composition of the population, are important moderators of the effect of punishment on cooperative behavior. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper provides indirect support for the idea that punishment can improve group outcomes under certain conditions, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data.

- source: Chen, L., Sun, J. J., Li, K., & Li, Q. R. (2022). Research on the effectiveness of monitoring mechanism for yield to pedestrian based on system dynamics. *Physica A-statistical Mechanics and Its Applications*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Without external control, the system exhibits persistent oscillations in the probability of drivers yielding to pedestrians, and TMD monitoring disappears over time. (2) Introducing dynamic punishment (fines that increase with non-compliance) suppresses oscillations and increases compliance, but does not fully eliminate violations. (3) Combining dynamic punishment and reward (penalty-incentive control) leads to full compliance (all drivers yield) and eliminates the need for monitoring or pedestrian supervision. (4) Alternatively, a sufficiently high static monitoring rate by TMD (above a threshold) can also achieve full compliance. The results are framed in terms of behavioral probabilities and system stability, not aggregate efficiency or payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that dynamic punishment and/or reward mechanisms can stabilize and maximize compliance in a multi-agent system with monitoring and sanctioning, analogous to public goods games with punishment and reward. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes. The findings are most useful for understanding the qualitative effect of punishment and reward on stabilizing cooperation and eliminating violations, especially in systems with endogenous monitoring and third-party enforcement. The model is adjacent to PGGs, but the outcome mapping to efficiency is indirect.

- source: Wang, Q. E., Lai, W., Ding, M. M., & Qiu, Q. (2022). Research on Cooperative Behavior of Green Technology Innovation in Construction Enterprises Based on Evolutionary Game. *Buildings*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that stronger punishment mechanisms (higher penalty and indirect punishment) increase the likelihood that both parties will adopt positive cooperation strategies, thus reducing opportunistic behavior. Similarly, higher willingness to cooperate, greater positive spillover (sharing), and more favorable income distribution also promote positive cooperation. However, the outcomes are reported in terms of the probability or stability of cooperation strategies, not in terms of group efficiency or total payoff. The model is adjacent to public goods games, as it involves cooperation with spillovers and punishment, but is tailored to the context of construction enterprise innovation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for the prediction task: it shows that increasing punishment intensity (both direct and indirect) makes positive cooperation more likely in a repeated game with spillovers, which is structurally similar to public goods games. However, it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, so its value is in supporting the mechanism that punishment can deter defection and promote cooperation under certain parameter regimes. The findings are most useful for understanding how punishment, sharing, and income distribution interact to affect cooperation, but less directly useful for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.
